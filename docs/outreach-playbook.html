Backlinks, explained simply

A backlink is a link to Ochorus from someone else’s website. Each good one is a public recommendation — and Google listens to recommendations.

Why they matter

  1. Search ranking. Google treats links from trusted sites as votes of confidence, so Ochorus shows up higher when people search for “Spurgeon sermons” or “Christian books in Luganda”.
  2. Direct visitors. People click the link and start reading today.
  3. Trust. A link from a church or a college tells readers the library is safe and sound.

What makes a link valuable

  1. Relevance — a Christian site, a school or a library.
  2. Trust — an established organization, a real website people visit.
  3. Placement — inside a resource list or article, not hidden in a footer.
  4. Words — clear link text such as “free Christian classics in Luganda”.

Four words you will hear

The outreach method

Use these seven steps for every church, school or website. They turn cold contacts into partners and keep our reputation clean.

  1. Find the prospect. Look for organizations whose readers would love Ochorus: churches, schools, homeschool groups, ministries, Christian bloggers. Use Google, Facebook, denominational directories and your own network.
  2. Check the fit. Is the website active (updated in the last two years)? Does it already link to resources or books? Is it clearly Christian, educational or family-focused? If not, skip it.
  3. Find the right person. The pastor, communications officer, librarian, head teacher or blog author — a named person, not “info@”. A warm introduction through someone you know is best of all.
  4. Find the gap. What would Ochorus add for them? A Luganda edition for their members, a free copy of a book on their syllabus, a reading plan for their youth group, a working link to replace a broken one.
  5. Make the ask — short and personal. Under 120 words. Use their name, mention their page, name one specific Ochorus page, and suggest exactly where it could go. Templates are on page 9.
  6. Follow up once. If there is no reply after 7 days, send one polite reminder. After that, let it go and move on — never pester.
  7. Record everything. Log each contact in the shared tracker the same day, including “no” answers. When a link goes live, check it and record its address.

Working with churches

Churches are our most natural partners: they want their members reading good books, and Ochorus gives them a free library in their own language.

A story that opens doors in Uganda

Ochorus carries the East African Revival — Simeon Nsibambi, Festo Kivengere, Janani Luwum, William Nagenda and others — alongside the classics. For Anglican and evangelical congregations this is their own heritage, freely available. Lead with it.

How to approach a church

  1. Start with a person you know. A member, a youth leader or a choir member can introduce you to the pastor or the communications team. Introductions work far better than cold emails.
  2. Meet or call, then follow with a message. Show Ochorus on your phone in two minutes: open a Luganda or Swahili book, a reading plan, and an author’s life story.
  3. Offer three ways to use it (see the list below), and let them choose.
  4. Ask for the website link. “Would you add Ochorus to your website’s resources page so members can find it?” Offer the exact wording (page 9).
  5. Thank them and stay in touch. Send a new reading plan or a seasonal book every few months.

Ways a church can use Ochorus

  • A “Resources” page link on the church website
  • A reading plan for small groups or a fasting season
  • A “book of the month” announced from the pulpit
  • A QR code in the bulletin or on a notice board
  • Links shared in the church’s WhatsApp groups and Facebook page
  • Sermon study questions for Bible study leaders

Who to approach

  • Large city churches with active websites
  • Diocese and denominational offices — one link can reach many parishes
  • Churches with founders on Ochorus: Methodist (Wesley), Baptist (Spurgeon), Salvation Army (the Booths)
  • Youth and campus fellowships
  • Women’s and men’s ministries
Tip

Many church websites have no resources page yet. Offer to help: a short paragraph about Ochorus plus a link is often welcome — and Odyssey’s web skills can make that easy for them.

Schools, colleges & seminaries
Section 5

Christian schools, colleges and seminaries

Links from schools and universities (.ac.ug, .edu and similar) are among the most trusted on the internet. Teachers and librarians link to anything that saves their students money.

Who to approach

  • University and seminary librarians — they keep online lists of free study resources
  • Lecturers in church history, theology, missions and Christian ministry
  • Bible colleges and pastors’ training schools
  • Christian secondary schools — chaplains and Christian Religious Education teachers
  • Christian student unions on campus

What to offer them

  • Free primary sources: Augustine, Athanasius, Luther, Calvin, Wesley and more, readable on a phone
  • Reading lists by subject: early church, Reformation, revival, missions, African Christianity
  • African voices: Samuel Ajayi Crowther and the East African Revival
  • Downloads students can keep for offline study

Step by step

  1. Find the library’s online resource pages. Search: “[college name] library e-resources” or “[college name] theology free resources”. Note pages that already list free websites.
  2. Find the course outlines. Which classic texts appear on theology or history reading lists? Check that Ochorus has them.
  3. Write to the librarian or lecturer by name. Say which course or page it helps, and include direct links to the exact books.
  4. Offer a short demo for staff or students — 15 minutes in a library session or a chapel announcement.
  5. Ask for a place on the resources page and the course reading list. Thank them, then check and record the link.
Timing matters

Reading lists are written before each term or semester begins. Contact lecturers and librarians 4–6 weeks before the term starts, when they are choosing materials.

Tip

Ask international contacts too. Seminaries in Kenya, Tanzania, Nigeria, the UK and the USA keep the same kind of library guides. One well-placed email to a librarian can earn a link that other libraries copy.

Homeschool families and organizations

Christian homeschoolers love the classics and are always looking for free, wholesome books. They also blog and share generously — which makes them excellent sources of links.

What makes Ochorus a good fit

  1. Classic literature for every age. Children’s and teens’ editions of works like The Pilgrim’s Progress, plus the full originals for older students.
  2. Free and ad-free — safe for children to use.
  3. Life stories of missionaries and preachers for history and character lessons.
  4. Daily reading plans that fit a school-day rhythm.

Who to approach

GroupWhere to find themWhat to ask for
Homeschool associations & co-opsNational association websites, Facebook groupsA spot on their “free resources” page or newsletter
Curriculum bloggersSearch: “Christian homeschool free books”, “classical education reading list”A mention in a reading-list post
Charlotte Mason & classical communitiesBlogs, forums and podcasts on “living books”A link beside the classics they recommend
Christian parenting sitesFamily ministries and magazinesA short guest article on reading the classics with children
Local familiesChurches, school networks, WhatsApp groupsShare it and tell friends; feedback for us

Step by step

  1. Search for existing “free books” lists on homeschool sites (search strings above). These pages exist to link out.
  2. Read the list first. Note which books they recommend that Ochorus offers free, or in another language.
  3. Write a friendly note naming their list and one or two specific Ochorus books that would fit it.
  4. Offer something extra. A short guest post such as “Five Christian classics to read aloud with your children.” A guest post with a link is often easier to say yes to than a bare link.
Tip

Homeschool audiences are worldwide and mostly in English. Pitch the children’s editions and missionary biographies first — they are the easiest “yes”.

More partners and online link sources

Beyond churches and schools, these partners and methods each bring links, readers or both.

Christian bookshopsCustomers who cannot afford every bookA poster or QR code; a link on their site
Tech & startup mediaA Ugandan team supporting a free reading app is a good storyA short feature or interview

Three online methods

1. Fix broken links (our highest success rate)

  1. Find old Christian websites that have shut down (pages now showing “Page not found”).
  2. Use a backlink checker tool to list the pages that still link to them.
  3. Where we host the same book, write: “Your link to [book] is broken — here is a free working copy.”

This works because we are fixing a real problem for the website owner. Expect a much higher reply rate than ordinary outreach.

2. Resource pages

Search Google for pages that list free Christian books, for example “free Christian classics online”, “Spurgeon sermons free” or “vitabu vya Kikristo bure”. Point out what is missing from their list — usually a language or a book they do not have — and offer our page.

3. Directories and app listings

Submit Ochorus to genuine, human-curated Christian resource directories and app review sites. Use the same short description each time. Skip any directory that charges for a listing.

Wikipedia: please leave this to us

Wikipedia has strict rules about people promoting their own websites, and breaking them can get Ochorus blocked. Please do not add Ochorus links to Wikipedia. If you spot a Wikipedia article where Ochorus would genuinely help, note it in the tracker and we will handle it.

The 90-day plan, reporting and ground rules

A steady weekly rhythm beats a big burst. Aim for quality: one link from a real church beats ten from nowhere.

WeeksFocusGoal by the end
1–2Set up the tracker; build a list of 100 prospects; learn the site; directory listings100 prospects listed; 10 listings submitted
3–6Churches and Ugandan schools; broken-link and resource-page outreach begins60 contacts made; first 15 links live
7–10Seminaries abroad, homeschool groups, bloggers, radio; follow-ups150 contacts total; 40 links live
11–13Double down on whatever is working best; thank partners60+ links live; a short lessons-learned report

Weekly rhythm

  1. Monday — find 20 new prospects
  2. Tue–Thu — send 15–20 personal messages; make 2–3 visits or calls
  3. Friday — follow-ups, check live links, update the tracker, send the weekly report

Weekly report (5 lines)

  • Contacts made this week
  • Replies received
  • New links live (with addresses)
  • Best conversation or lesson
  • Help needed from Ochorus

Ground rules

  • Never pay for a link or accept payment for one
  • No link swaps, spam comments or mass emails
  • Do not add links to Wikipedia — flag them to us
  • Never promise features Ochorus does not have
  • Do not describe how books were translated; just share the book
  • Be honest that you are helping Ochorus
  • Respect a “no” — one follow-up only
