# Faithful With Little

*Money, Wisdom, and Treasure That Lasts*

Scripture quotations are from the Open Word Bible (OWB), Balanced edition, dedicated to the public domain.

---

## Introduction: Why Jesus Talked About Money

Most of us would rather talk about almost anything than money. We will tell a friend about our health, our marriage, even our doubts about God, long before we tell them what we earn, what we owe or what we give. Money is the last private room in the house. We keep the door shut.

Jesus kept opening it.

He told a story about a farmer who built bigger barns and died the same night. He watched a widow drop two small coins into the temple box and said she had given more than everyone else. He looked at a rich young man, loved him, and told him to sell everything. He said it was easier for a camel to squeeze through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom. He talked about lost coins, hidden treasure, unpaid debts, day wages, talents of silver, taxes and tithes. Read through the Gospels with a pencil and mark every place money or possessions come up. You will wear the pencil down.

Why? Jesus was not short of funds and He was not running a building campaign. He had nowhere to lay His head. He talked about money because He knew where it leads. In the middle of the Sermon on the Mount He said it in one line:

> "Your heart will always be where your treasure is." (Matthew 6:21)

Notice the order. We usually think it works the other way round: first we care about something, then we put our money there. Jesus says the money leads and the heart follows. Where we put our treasure, our hearts go after it. Money doesn't just reveal what we love. It trains what we love.

That is why money is not a side issue in the Christian life. It is one of the main places where discipleship happens, or doesn't. You can sing on Sunday that Jesus is Lord of all, and on Monday treat your bank account as the one corner of your life where He has no say. Most of us have done exactly that. This book is an invitation to open that door and let Him in.

### What this book is not

It is not a promise that God will make you rich. Some preachers teach that if you give enough, or believe enough, God is bound to repay you in cash, with interest. The Bible does say God blesses generosity, and you will see that many times in these pages. But Jesus was poor. Paul knew hunger. The heroes of faith in Hebrews 11 "roamed about wearing the skins of sheep and goats, lacking everything" (Hebrews 11:37). Any gospel that would embarrass the apostles is not the gospel.

Nor is it a book that shames you for having money. Abraham was wealthy. So were Job, Joseph of Arimathea and Lydia, the businesswoman who opened her home in Philippi to Paul and the first believers there. The Bible never says money is evil. It says "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10). The problem is not in your wallet. It is in the heart that holds it.

And it is not a technical finance manual. You will find practical wisdom here about budgets, debt, saving and work, because the Bible has a great deal to say about all of them. But there are better places to learn how a pension works. This book is about the heart behind the numbers.

### What this book is

It is a short guide to money for people who want to follow Jesus with all of their life, including the part that pays the bills. It walks through the whole Bible, from the garden of Eden to the day we give an account, and it gathers what God says about money around thirty key verses.

It has five parts:

- **Whose Is It?** lays the foundation. God owns everything, and we are His managers.
- **The Heart Behind the Wallet** looks at what money does inside us: covetousness, worry, contentment and the danger of riches.
- **Wisdom for Everyday Money** covers honest work, planning, saving, debt and providing for a family.
- **Generous Like God** is about firstfruits, cheerful giving, the poor, and giving all we can.
- **Treasure That Lasts** looks ahead: faithfulness in little things, money and the mission of God, teaching our children, and the day we hear "Well done."

### Voices from the past

You will not walk this road alone. Along the way you will meet men and women who wrestled with money long before we did, and whose books you can read in full in the Ochorus library. John Wesley, who preached "Gain all you can, save all you can, give all you can," and lived it. George Müller, who cared for thousands of orphans in Bristol without ever asking anyone for money. Charles Spurgeon, whose plain-spoken ploughman sayings are as sharp today as when he wrote them. Hudson Taylor and his last half-crown. A. W. Tozer on what he called "the tyranny of things." Thomas Watson, the Puritan who could take covetousness apart like a watchmaker. Their words are quoted exactly as they wrote them, so some of the English is old. It is worth the effort.

### How each chapter works

There are twenty chapters, and each one takes about ten minutes to read. Every chapter has the same shape:

- **A story** to open it, from the Bible, from history or from everyday life.
- **A key verse**, one of the thirty.
- **The teaching**, usually two or three clear points.
- **A voice from the past**, a short quotation from one of the classic writers.
- **Practice this week**, one small, concrete thing to do.
- **For groups**, a few questions to talk through with others.

You can read the book alone, but it was written with small groups in mind. Two chapters a week will carry a group through it in ten weeks. At the back you will find a thirty-day reading plan built on the thirty key verses, a one-page money plan, and a guide for group leaders.

### Before you begin

Take an honest look at where you are. You might be buried in debt and ashamed of it. You might be comfortable and quietly afraid that Jesus would ask you to give something up. You might be generous already and wondering how to be wise about it. You might be poor, and tired of being told what to do with money you do not have.

Wherever you are, the first word of this book is not a rule. It is grace. Before the Bible tells us anything about our money, it tells us about God's riches toward us:

> "After all, you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ: rich as He was, He became poor for your sake, so that His poverty might make you rich." (2 Corinthians 8:9)

Everything else in these pages grows out of that. We do not give to earn God's love. We give because we have already been given more than we could ever repay. We do not manage money well to impress God. We manage it well because it is His, and He has trusted us with it.

Jesus said, "Someone who is faithful in the smallest matter is faithful in large matters too" (Luke 16:10). Money is one of the smallest matters. It will all be gone one day. But how we handle it shapes who we become, and who we become lasts forever.

So let's open the door.

## It All Belongs to God

King David was an old man. He had wanted all his life to build a house for God, a temple in Jerusalem, and God had told him no. His son Solomon would build it. So David did the next best thing. He spent his last years gathering the materials: gold, silver, bronze, iron, timber and precious stones, in quantities that are hard to imagine. Then he gave his own private fortune on top. The leaders of Israel saw it and followed his lead, and the gifts poured in until the treasuries overflowed.

If ever a man had the right to feel proud of his generosity, it was David that day. Instead, he stood up in front of the whole assembly and prayed. And in the middle of the greatest offering of his life, he said something that turned it inside out.

> "To You, LORD, belong the greatness, the power, the glory, the victory and the majesty; indeed everything in heaven and on earth is Yours. The kingdom is Yours, LORD, and You are lifted high as head over all. Riches and honor come from You; You rule over everything. In Your hand are power and might, and it is in Your hand to make anyone great and to give strength to all. And now, our God, we give You thanks and praise Your glorious name. But who am I, and who are my people, that we should have the means to give as freely as this? Everything comes from You; what we have given You came from Your own hand." (1 Chronicles 29:11-14)

David had given away a fortune, and he knew he had not given God anything that wasn't already God's.

### God is the owner

The Bible's teaching about money starts here, and if we get this wrong, nothing else will come out right. Everything belongs to God.

> "The earth is the LORD's, and all that fills it, the world and everyone who lives there." (Psalm 24:1)

Not some of it. All of it. The land, the oceans, the cattle, the bank in your town and the money in it. God says through the prophet Haggai, "The silver belongs to Me, and the gold belongs to Me, declares the LORD of Armies" (Haggai 2:8). In Psalm 50 He gently mocks the idea that He could ever need anything from us: "Were I ever hungry, you are not the one I would tell, since the world and all it holds are Mine" (Psalm 50:12).

When God gave Israel the promised land, He made a striking rule. Land could be leased, but never sold forever. "No land is to be sold for good, for the land belongs to Me. You are only outsiders and residents living with Me" (Leviticus 25:23). The Israelites lived on the land, farmed it and passed it down to their children. But the title deed was always in God's name.

That is still true. Your house, your car, your savings, your income, your skills, your time: you hold them, but you do not own them. You are a resident in God's world, living on what He provides.

### We are managers

If God is the owner, what are we? The Bible's word is *steward*, an old word for a manager. A steward ran a household or an estate on behalf of the master. Joseph was a steward in Potiphar's house. He had keys to everything and authority over everything, and he owned none of it.

That is our position. Paul says, "Now what is looked for in household managers is this: that each one proves trustworthy" (1 Corinthians 4:2). Peter says the same about every gift God gives: "Each of you has received a gift; use it to serve one another, as good managers of the richly varied grace of God" (1 Peter 4:10).

This changes the question we ask about money. An owner asks, "What do I want to do with my money?" A manager asks, "What does the owner want done with His money?" Those are two very different questions. The first one puts me at the centre. The second puts God there.

It also changes how we think about giving. Most of us quietly assume that the ninety percent we keep is ours, and the ten percent we give is God's. But if everything belongs to God, then the question is not how much of my money I should give to God. It is how much of God's money I should keep for myself. The whole hundred percent is His, and all of it is to be used in a way that pleases Him: the rent and the groceries and the holiday, as well as the offering.

### The danger of forgetting

Moses saw a danger coming. Israel had spent forty years in the wilderness, eating manna one day at a time, completely dependent on God. Now they were about to enter a rich land of wheat and vines and copper mines. Moses warned them that prosperity would bring a temptation that hardship never had:

> "And you may say to yourself, 'My own power and the strength of my own hands have won me this wealth.' Remember the LORD your God instead. It is He who gives you the ability to grow wealthy, in order to carry out the covenant He swore to your ancestors, as He is doing today." (Deuteronomy 8:17-18)

That is the self-made man's prayer: "My own power and the strength of my own hands." It sounds like common sense. We did work hard. We did take risks. But who gave us the strength to work? Who gave us a mind that could learn a trade, a body that could get up in the morning, a country with roads and a market, parents who fed us? Paul asks it bluntly: "What do you have that was not given to you? And if it was given to you, why do you take pride in it as though it were not a gift?" (1 Corinthians 4:7).

Forgetting God is rarely a dramatic decision. It is a slow drift that comes with comfort. The more we have, the easier it is to think we did it ourselves.

### Freedom in being a manager

This might sound like bad news, as if God is taking something away from us. In fact it is wonderfully freeing.

If I own everything, then everything depends on me. I have to protect it, grow it, worry about it. Every loss is my loss. But if I am a manager, the weight sits on the owner's shoulders. My job is to be faithful with what He has given me, and to trust Him with the rest. When the market falls, it is His money that fell. When a bill arrives that I cannot pay, I can bring it to the One who owns the cattle on a thousand hills.

Job understood this when he lost everything in a single day: his flocks, his servants and his children. He fell to the ground and worshiped:

> "I came naked from my mother's womb, and naked I will go back there. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; may the name of the LORD be blessed." (Job 1:21)

Only a steward can pray like that. An owner who loses everything has lost everything. A steward who loses everything still has the Master.

### A voice from the past

George Müller never asked anyone for money. He believed that the orphan houses he ran in Bristol belonged to God, and so did every penny that came in to feed the children. His biographer, Arthur Pierson, summed up Müller's view of giving like this:

> "Enlightened believers look on all things as inalienably God's, and, even in the voluntary diversion of money into sacred rather than selfish channels, still remember that they give to Him only what is His own!"
> Arthur T. Pierson, *George Müller of Bristol*

### Practice this week

Write a short list of everything you think of as "mine": your home, your car, your phone, your savings, your income, your skills. Then at the top of the page write the words of Psalm 24:1. Take a few minutes to go through the list in prayer, giving each thing back to God by name. You might pray something like this: "Lord, this is Yours. Thank You for trusting me with it. Show me how You want it used."

### For groups

- What is something you own that you would find hardest to think of as God's rather than yours? Why?
- How would your spending change this week if you really believed you were managing someone else's money?
- Read Deuteronomy 8:17-18 together. Why do you think prosperity makes us forget God more easily than hardship does?
- Is the idea of being a manager rather than an owner a relief to you, or a burden? Why?

## Made to Work, Called to Keep

The first job in the Bible was gardening.

Before there was sin, before there was a curse, before a single thorn grew, God planted a garden in Eden and put the man in it. He didn't put him there to lie in a hammock. He put him there to work.

> "The LORD God took the man and settled him in the garden of Eden to work it and take care of it." (Genesis 2:15)

Two jobs: work it and take care of it. Develop it and protect it. Make it fruitful and keep it safe. That was humanity's calling from the very beginning, and it was given in paradise.

### Work is not a curse

Many people think of work as a punishment, something we have to put up with until the weekend or retirement. There is a curse on work in the Bible, but it comes later, in Genesis 3, after the fall. God told Adam, "Through painful work you will eat from it all the days of your life. It will grow thorns and thistles for you" (Genesis 3:17-18). Since then work has been hard. It is frustrating and tiring, and sometimes it seems pointless.

But the curse made work painful. It did not make it bad. Work itself was God's idea, and it was part of the world He called "very good." When God first blessed the man and the woman, He gave them work to do: "Be fruitful and multiply. Fill the earth and subdue it. Rule over the fish in the sea, the birds in the sky, and every living creature that moves along the ground" (Genesis 1:28). Theologians call it the creation mandate. It means that ordinary work, farming, building, cooking, teaching, coding, cleaning, caring for children, is a way of carrying out God's first command.

God Himself is a worker. He worked six days to make the world, and Jesus said, "My Father has been at work right up to now, and I am at work as well" (John 5:17). Jesus spent most of His life in a carpenter's shop. When we work, we are doing something God does.

### Why work matters for money

This book is about money, so why start with work? Because in the Bible, the normal way God provides for us is through work. Money is not meant to fall from the sky. It is meant to be earned.

Paul is blunt about it. Some believers in Thessalonica had stopped working, perhaps because they thought Jesus was coming back any day. Paul wrote:

> "For even while we were with you, we gave you this instruction: 'Anyone who will not work should not eat.' We hear that some among you are living undisciplined lives: they are not busy working, they are busy with everyone else's business. We command such people, and we urge them in the Lord Jesus Christ, to go about their work quietly and to eat bread they have earned themselves." (2 Thessalonians 3:10-12)

That is not cruelty to people who cannot work. The same Bible commands generous care for the sick, the old, the widow and the orphan. It is a word to people who could work and choose not to. Work is part of our dignity. To earn your bread is to stand on your own feet and not be a burden on others. Paul told the Thessalonians to "earn your living with your hands, as we instructed you, so that outsiders will see you live rightly, and you will not need to rely on anyone" (1 Thessalonians 4:11-12).

Proverbs puts it in a single line: "A slack hand leads to poverty, but hard-working hands bring wealth" (Proverbs 10:4). It isn't a guarantee. Some people work very hard and stay poor, and the Bible knows that. But as a general rule, diligence and provision go together.

### Work as worship

Here is the great secret of Christian work. Paul wrote to slaves, people with the hardest, least rewarding jobs in the Roman world, and told them their work could be offered to Jesus:

> "Whatever you are doing, put your whole self into the work, as for the Lord and not for people, knowing that you will receive from the Lord the inheritance as your reward." (Colossians 3:23-24)

If that was true for a slave, it is true for you. The spreadsheet, the shift at the till, the lesson plan, the nappy, the night on the ward: all of it can be done "for the Lord." Your boss may never notice. Jesus will.

This means there is no such thing as a secular job for a Christian. There are only jobs done for Christ and jobs done for ourselves. A plumber who fixes a leak honestly and well, and charges a fair price, is serving God just as truly as a preacher in a pulpit.

### Keeping as well as working

Remember the second half of Genesis 2:15: "to work it and take care of it." Adam was not only to develop the garden. He was to guard it. Stewardship includes both.

In money terms, working is earning, and keeping is managing what we earn. Some people work hard and then let it all leak away through carelessness. Others are careful keepers but never really work. God's design is both: to produce and to protect, to earn and to manage.

That second half is what most of this book is about. But it starts here, with the dignity of work. You can't manage what you haven't earned, and you can't give what you haven't got. Paul said that one of the best reasons to work is to have something to share: "Thieves must steal no more. Instead they must work hard, doing something good with their own hands, so that they have something to share with anyone in need" (Ephesians 4:28).

Look at that. The ex-thief does not just stop taking. He starts working, and he works so that he can give. That is the whole Christian view of money in one verse.

### When work becomes an idol

There is a warning here too. Work is good, but it can become a god. Some of us work far too much, not because God has called us to it but because we are afraid, or ambitious, or we get our sense of worth from our job. The psalmist says, "It does you no good to get up early and stay up late, eating food earned by anxious work; He gives sleep to the one He loves" (Psalm 127:2).

God built a day of rest into the week right from creation. Rest is a weekly reminder that the world does not depend on our labour. We work hard, then we stop, and we trust God with what we didn't finish.

### A voice from the past

The Puritan William Law asked his readers to look honestly at how they spent their money, as plainly as a farmer can tell you where his fields are:

> "Every one also can as easily tell how he lays out his money, and whether he considers how to please God in it, as he can tell where his estate is, and whether it be in money or land."
> William Law, *A Serious Call to a Devout and Holy Life*

### Practice this week

Before you start work each day this week, whatever your work is, pray Colossians 3:23 over it: "Lord, I'm doing this for You." At the end of the week, notice whether it changed how you worked, or how you felt about your work.

### For groups

- Did you grow up thinking of work as a blessing or a curse? Where did that idea come from?
- What would it look like to do your particular job "for the Lord" this week?
- Read Ephesians 4:28. What does it tell us about the purpose of earning money?
- Are you more likely to make an idol of work, or to avoid it? What would balance look like for you?

## Two Masters

Jesus didn't say, "You should not serve God and money." He said, "You cannot."

> "No one can serve two masters. Either they will hate one and love the other, or they will be loyal to one and look down on the other. You cannot serve both God and money." (Matthew 6:24)

Think about what that means. It is not a rule. It is a description of reality, like saying you cannot walk north and south at the same time. Sooner or later, one master or the other will give an order the other one won't, and you will have to choose. When that moment comes, you will find out which one you have really been serving.

### Money is not neutral

Most people today think of money as a neutral tool, like a hammer. It is neither good nor bad. It all depends on how you use it. There is truth in that, and John Wesley said something like it, as we will see. But Jesus said something more. In Matthew 6:24 He doesn't put God up against a hammer. He puts God up against a master.

The word in the older translations is *mammon*, an Aramaic word for wealth or property. Jesus speaks of it almost as a person, a rival lord who wants your loyalty. Money is not just a tool. It has a pull. It makes promises. It offers security, status, comfort, freedom and control. And it asks to be trusted.

That is why Paul calls greed "idolatry" (Colossians 3:5), and why he warns that "no one who is greedy (for the greedy person is an idolater), has any inheritance in the kingdom of Christ and of God" (Ephesians 5:5). An idol is anything we look to for what only God can give. Money is the idol of choice for respectable people, because you can worship it without ever going near a shrine.

### How do I know who I'm serving?

If money is a master that can quietly take over, how can I tell whether it has? Here are some honest questions to ask.

**Where does my mind go when it is free?** When you are lying awake at night, or driving, or waiting in a queue, what do you think about? If it is mostly money, getting it, spending it, worrying about it, that tells you something.

**What makes me anxious?** We worry about what we trust in. If a fall in the stock market or a big bill throws you into panic, it may be because your security is resting there.

**What would I not give up?** Jesus told the rich young ruler to sell everything and follow Him, and the man went away sad, "for he owned a great deal of property" (Mark 10:22). Jesus doesn't ask that of everyone, but He did put His finger on the one thing that man would not let go of. What would you refuse to give up if Jesus asked?

**What does my spending say?** A bank statement is a kind of autobiography. It tells the story of what we value. If someone read yours, who would they say your master is?

**What gets cut first?** In a sermon on this very verse, the Welsh preacher Martyn Lloyd-Jones gave a test that is hard to wriggle out of: "When money becomes difficult, the first thing we economize on is our contribution to God's work." When times get tight, what goes first?

### Hate one, love the other

Jesus says we will "hate one and love the other." That sounds strong. But think about it. If money is your master, you will resent God whenever He asks you to give, or to trust Him, or to be honest when lying would pay better. You may never say it out loud, but there will be a quiet irritation every time the offering comes round or a needy person crosses your path.

And if God is your master, you will start to see money for what it is. Not a god, but a servant. Not something to love, but something to use for the One you do love.

This doesn't mean Christians should despise money in the sense of being careless or ungrateful for it. It means money loses its throne. It goes back to being a tool in the hand of a servant of God.

### Choosing a master

The good news is that you can choose. Joshua stood before Israel and told them to "make your choice today. Whom will you serve?" (Joshua 24:15). That choice is in front of every Christian, and we often have to make it more than once.

Here is how to make it. You can't drive out one master by simply trying harder. You need a better master to take its place. Money is a hard master. It always wants more, and it never says "Well done." Jesus is a kind master. He says, "My yoke is easy to wear, and the load I give is light" (Matthew 11:30). When we see how good He is, money's promises start to look thin.

The writer of Hebrews says it beautifully:

> "Do not let love of money rule the way you live; be satisfied with what you have. For God Himself has said, 'I will not ever let you go, and I will never, never abandon you.'" (Hebrews 13:5)

Why can we be free from the love of money? Because God has promised never to leave us. Money promises security, but it can be gone in a day. God is the security money only pretends to be.

### A voice from the past

A. W. Tozer saw that the trouble often doesn't show until a real choice arrives:

> "Let him be forced into making a choice between God and money, between God and men, between God and personal ambition, God and self, God and human love, and God will take second place every time."
> A. W. Tozer, *The Pursuit of God*

And the Puritan Thomas Watson put the whole matter into a single sentence:

> "If we trust in our riches, we make riches our god."
> Thomas Watson, *The Ten Commandments*

### Practice this week

Get out your last month's bank statement or spending record. Read it slowly, as if it belonged to someone else. Ask, "What does this person love? What does this person trust?" Then talk to God honestly about what you see.

### For groups

- Why do you think Jesus said "cannot" rather than "should not"?
- Which of the questions under "How do I know who I'm serving?" was hardest for you to answer? Why?
- Can you remember a time when you had to choose between God and money? What happened?
- Read Hebrews 13:5. How does God's promise in the second half of the verse make the command in the first half possible?

## The Riches of Christ

Paul had a fundraising problem. The church in Jerusalem was poor and suffering, and Paul was collecting money from the Gentile churches to help them. The Corinthians had promised to give a year earlier, but they hadn't followed through. So Paul wrote to them about it.

What is striking is how he did it. He didn't guilt them. He didn't promise them that God would pay them back double. He didn't set a target. Instead, he pointed them to Jesus:

> "After all, you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ: rich as He was, He became poor for your sake, so that His poverty might make you rich." (2 Corinthians 8:9)

That is the deepest reason a Christian gives. Not because we have to, but because of what Jesus did.

### He was rich

Think about how rich Jesus was. Before He came to earth He shared the glory of the Father. He made the stars. Every angel worshiped Him. Everything in heaven and earth belonged to Him, because it was made through Him and for Him. There has never been wealth like the wealth of the eternal Son of God.

### He became poor

And He gave it all up. Paul says in Philippians that "He emptied Himself, taking on the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:7). He was born not in a palace but in a borrowed stable, to parents who could only afford the poor family's offering of two birds when they presented Him at the temple. He grew up in a small town with a bad reputation. He worked with His hands. During His ministry He said, "Foxes have their holes and the birds in the sky their nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to rest His head" (Matthew 8:20). He was buried in a borrowed tomb.

But His poverty went much deeper than money. On the cross He became poor in a way we can hardly understand. He lost the sense of His Father's presence. He was stripped of His clothes, His friends, His dignity and finally His life. He cried out, "My God, My God, why have You abandoned Me?" (Matthew 27:46). He became as poor as it is possible to become.

### So that we might become rich

Why? "For your sake, so that His poverty might make you rich." Not rich in money. Paul is not promising the Corinthians a bigger income. He is talking about a far greater wealth: forgiveness, adoption into God's family, the Holy Spirit living in us, eternal life, a share in Christ's own inheritance. Paul calls it "the riches of Christ, riches no one can ever fully trace" (Ephesians 3:8).

Everything we most need, Jesus bought for us at the cost of everything He had. We came to Him spiritually bankrupt, with nothing to offer. He paid our debt and then made us His heirs.

### Why this changes how we give

This is the foundation for everything this book says about generosity. If you skip it, giving becomes a duty, a tax, a way to feel good about yourself, or worst of all, a way to try to earn God's favour. All of those will wear you out in the end.

But when you see what Jesus gave for you, giving becomes a response. It is the natural overflow of a heart that has been made rich. Paul says the Macedonian churches, who were desperately poor themselves, begged him for the privilege of giving: "Hardship has put them to a severe test, yet their joy has overflowed, and their deep poverty has spilled over into riches of generosity" (2 Corinthians 8:2). How? "Before anything else they gave themselves to the Lord" (2 Corinthians 8:5). When the heart belongs to Jesus, the wallet follows.

At the end of his long appeal, Paul doesn't finish with a demand. He finishes with worship:

> "Thanks be to God for His gift, which no words can describe!" (2 Corinthians 9:15)

Every gift we give is a small echo of that indescribable gift.

### Grace, not guilt

This means this whole book is about grace. If you are reading these chapters and feeling a growing weight of shame, about debts you have run up, about money you have wasted, about how little you have given, come back to this verse. The good news is not "Try harder with your money." The good news is that Jesus became poor to make you rich. Your failures with money are among the sins He carried. Your worth to God does not rise or fall with your bank balance or your giving record.

Start there. Receive His riches first. Then, as a forgiven and rich child of God, begin to learn how to handle the smaller riches He has put in your hands.

### Rich toward God

There is one more thing. Jesus once told a parable about a rich farmer who had a bumper harvest and decided to build bigger barns and retire in comfort. That night God said to him, "You fool! This very night your life is being asked back from you." Then Jesus added: "That is how it is for anyone who piles up wealth for themselves but is not rich in God's sight" (Luke 12:20-21).

There are two ways to be rich. You can be rich in yourself, or you can be rich toward God. The farmer had full barns and an empty soul. The Christian may have small barns, or none at all, and still be infinitely rich, because his treasure is in Christ.

### A voice from the past

Charles Spurgeon, meditating on the love of Christ, marvelled at how much He gave:

> "Although a tithe of his possessions would have made a universe of angels rich beyond all thought, yet was he not content until he had given us all that he had."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Morning by Morning*

Read Philippians 2:5-11 slowly each day this week. Each time, list one thing Jesus gave up for you, and one thing He has given you. At the end of the week, give a gift, of money, time or help, as a simple way of saying thank you for His.

- Why do you think Paul pointed the Corinthians to Jesus rather than simply telling them to keep their promise?
- What is the difference between giving out of guilt and giving out of gratitude? Have you experienced both?
- Read 2 Corinthians 8:1-5. What do you find most surprising about the Macedonians?
- What does it mean to you, practically, to be "rich toward God"?

# Part 2 · The Heart Behind the Wallet

## Covetousness, the Respectable Sin

When Israel crossed the Jordan and Jericho's walls fell, God gave a clear command. The city's silver and gold were devoted to Him. No one was to take any of it.

Achan took some anyway. Later, when he was found out, he explained exactly how it happened:

> "Among the spoil I saw a beautiful cloak from Babylonia, about 5 pounds of silver, and a bar of gold weighing about 1 1/4 pounds. I coveted them and took them, and now they are buried in the ground in the middle of my tent." (Joshua 7:21)

I saw. I coveted. I took. I hid. That is the anatomy of a hundred sins. But notice where it starts. Not with the taking, but with the wanting.

### The tenth commandment

Of the Ten Commandments, nine deal mostly with what we do or say. The tenth goes straight to the heart:

> "You shall not covet your neighbor's household: you shall not set your heart on his wife, his male or female servant, his ox or his donkey, or anything else your neighbor has." (Exodus 20:17)

To covet is to want something that isn't yours so much that it takes hold of you. It is not the same as simply wanting things. You can want a new coat because your old one has worn through, and there's nothing wrong with that. Coveting is wanting that has become a fixation, a restless "if only" that eats away at contentment. It looks at someone else's house, job, car, holiday or life and says, "I deserve that more than they do."

What makes covetousness so dangerous is that nobody sees it. You can be arrested for theft. You will never be arrested for coveting. It lives quietly in the heart, and it is perfectly respectable. In fact, much of modern advertising exists to stir it up. We live inside a machine designed to make us discontented.

### More than enough

The great Puritan pastor Thomas Watson, writing about the tenth commandment, gave a definition that is hard to improve on. He borrowed it from Augustine, who called covetousness *Plus velle quam sat est*. In Watson's words, that means "to desire more than enough."

More than enough. That is the heart of it. Covetousness is not about having a lot or a little. It is about never being able to say "enough." A poor person can be eaten up with covetousness. A rich person can be free of it. It is a condition of the heart, not of the bank account.

Solomon knew this from the inside. He was the richest man of his age, and he wrote:

> "Whoever loves money never has enough money, and whoever loves wealth is never satisfied with what comes in. This too is a breath." (Ecclesiastes 5:10)

The word translated "breath" is the famous word of Ecclesiastes, often rendered "vanity" or "meaningless." It means something that vanishes like your breath on a cold morning. Love of money is a hunger that grows the more you feed it. Bernard of Clairvaux saw the same thing nine hundred years ago: "whatever riches he may have, his nature is to want more."

### Life is not things

One day a man in the crowd called out to Jesus, "Teacher, tell my brother to share our father's property with me" (Luke 12:13). It was a reasonable request. Perhaps the brother was being unfair. But Jesus refused to get involved. Instead, He turned to the crowd:

> "Then He said to them, 'Take care! Keep yourselves from every kind of greed. Even when someone has more than enough, life does not come from the things a person owns.'" (Luke 12:15)

Jesus saw something the man didn't. Under the legal complaint there was a heart problem. The man thought that if he got his share, his life would be better. Jesus said life doesn't work that way. Your life is not made up of what you own.

That is the great lie covetousness tells: that the next thing will satisfy. The next promotion, the next house, the next upgrade. And when it comes, it doesn't. So we look for the next thing after that.

### Why covetousness is so serious

Thomas Watson showed that covetousness breaks every one of the Ten Commandments. It breaks the first, because "the covetous man has more gods than one; Mammon is his god." It breaks the eighth, because it is "the root of theft." It broke the sixth when King Ahab wanted Naboth's vineyard so badly that he let his wife have Naboth killed to get it. It breaks the ninth whenever someone lies to get ahead.

The Bible's most solemn warning on money comes from Paul:

> "Godliness is indeed great gain, when it comes with contentment. For we brought nothing into the world, and we can take nothing out of it. If we have food and clothing, we will be content with that. Those who want to get rich fall into temptation and a trap, and into many foolish and harmful desires that drag people down into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil. Some people, eager for money, have wandered from the faith and pierced themselves with many griefs." (1 Timothy 6:6-10)

Notice the picture at the end: people who "pierced themselves with many griefs." Covetousness doesn't just hurt others. It wounds the person who covets. It costs sleep, relationships, peace and sometimes faith itself.

### How to fight it

You can't fight covetousness by simply trying not to want things. A heart cannot be emptied. It can only be filled with something better. Here are four weapons the Bible gives us.

**Gratitude.** Covetousness looks at what we don't have. Gratitude looks at what we do. It is very hard to be thankful and covetous at the same time. Make a habit of naming what God has given you.

**Generosity.** Giving is the direct opposite of grasping. Every time you open your hand to give, you loosen money's grip on your heart. That is one reason Paul tells Timothy to command the rich "to give generously and to share" (1 Timothy 6:18).

**Guarding your eyes.** Achan's sin started with "I saw." What are you looking at? Shop windows, catalogues, social media feeds, house listings, all of it can stir up desire. Sometimes the wisest thing is simply to stop looking.

**Treasuring God.** In the end, covetousness is a worship problem. The heart was made to delight in God, and when it doesn't, it reaches for other things. Seek more of Him, and you will find you need less of everything else.

### A voice from the past

Thomas Watson had a gift for pictures. Here is one of his best:

> "As a ferry man takes in so many passengers to increase his fare, that he sinks his boat; so a covetous man takes in so much gold to increase his estate, that he drowns himself in perdition."
> Thomas Watson, *The Ten Commandments*

### Practice this week

For one week, notice every time you feel the "if only" of covetousness: when you see someone else's house, car, holiday, phone or salary and feel a pang. Each time, stop and name one specific thing God has already given you, and thank Him for it. If a certain shop, website or app keeps stirring up discontent, take a break from it for the week.

### For groups

- What is the difference between wanting something and coveting it?
- Watson's definition is "to desire more than enough." What does "enough" look like for you?
- Read Luke 12:13-15. Why do you think Jesus refused to settle the inheritance dispute?
- Which of the four weapons against covetousness do you most need right now?

## The Learned Secret of Contentment

Paul wrote one of the happiest letters in the Bible from prison. He was in chains, waiting to hear whether he would be executed. His friends in Philippi had sent him a gift of money, and he wrote to thank them. In the middle of his thank-you note he said something remarkable:

> "I do not say this because I am going short. However things stand with me, I have learned to be content. I know how to get by on little, and I know how to handle having plenty. In each and every situation I have been let into the secret: how to eat my fill and how to go hungry, how to have more than enough and how to do without. I have strength for every situation in the one who makes me strong." (Philippians 4:11-13)

Twice Paul uses the language of learning. "I have learned to be content." "I have been let into the secret." Contentment was not something Paul was born with. It wasn't a personality type. It was a skill he had learned, and it was learned in a hard school.

### Contentment is not

It is worth being clear about what contentment is not.

It is not laziness. Paul worked hard, often making tents through the night so he wouldn't be a burden on the churches. A contented person can still have goals and work toward them.

It is not pretending. Paul didn't pretend he wasn't hungry when he was. He names his hunger plainly. Contentment is not putting on a brave face.

It is not settling for injustice. The Bible is full of cries for justice for the poor. A contented Christian can still work to change unfair systems, and should.

And it is not only for hard times. Notice that Paul says he learned to be content in plenty as well as in need. Plenty has its own temptations: pride, self-reliance, forgetting God. Being content with much means not being owned by it, and not needing more.

### Contentment is

So what is it? Contentment is a settled peace in God that doesn't depend on circumstances. It is the ability to say, whatever my bank balance says, "I have enough, because I have God."

The writer of Hebrews links it directly to God's presence: "Be satisfied with what you have. For God Himself has said, 'I will not ever let you go, and I will never, never abandon you'" (Hebrews 13:5). We can be content because we are never alone. Whatever we lack, we have Him.

There is an old prayer in Proverbs that says this beautifully:

> "Keep falsehood and lies far away from me; give me neither poverty nor wealth, but feed me with the bread I need each day, so that I do not have too much and disown you, asking, 'Who is the LORD?' or have too little and steal, and so bring disgrace on my God's name." (Proverbs 30:8-9)

Look at that request: "Give me neither poverty nor wealth." How many people have ever prayed that? Most of us pray to be spared poverty, but we would quite like to try wealth. The writer is wise enough to see that both are dangerous. Too much, and he might forget God. Too little, and he might be tempted to steal. So he asks for just enough: "the bread I need each day." It is the same prayer Jesus taught us: "Give us today the bread we need" (Matthew 6:11).

### How Paul learned it

How does anyone learn contentment? Paul tells us the secret in the next sentence: "I have strength for every situation in the one who makes me strong." People often quote that verse as if it were about winning a race or passing an exam. But in context, it is about being content when you're hungry. Christ is the one who gives the strength to be at peace with little, and the strength to stay humble with much.

Here are some of the lessons in that school.

**Remember you will leave it all behind.** "For we brought nothing into the world, and we can take nothing out of it" (1 Timothy 6:7). The old saying is that a funeral hearse never tows a trailer. If you can't keep it, don't let it keep you.

**Compare downward, not upward.** Most of our discontent comes from comparing ourselves with people who have more. Most of the world lives on far less than most readers of this book. Comparison with those who have less turns discontent into gratitude.

**Count your blessings, literally.** Write them down. The very act of naming God's gifts reorders the heart.

**Pray your worries.** "Do not worry about anything. Instead, in everything, bring your requests to God through prayer and petition, with thanksgiving. And God's peace, which is beyond all our understanding, will stand guard over your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:6-7). Notice that contentment and peace come through prayer with thanksgiving.

**Simplify.** Sometimes the way to contentment is to need less. Every possession needs to be stored, cleaned, insured, repaired and eventually thrown away. Fewer things can mean more freedom.

### Godliness with contentment

Paul told Timothy that "godliness is indeed great gain, when it comes with contentment" (1 Timothy 6:6). There is a kind of religion that is used to get rich. Paul had just warned against people who "think godliness is a way to make money" (1 Timothy 6:5). He turns it around. Godliness really is great gain, but not financial gain. The real profit is a godly life with a contented heart. That is a wealth no market crash can touch.

A well-known psalm says it another way: "The little that the righteous own is worth more than the plenty of many wicked people" (Psalm 37:16). And Proverbs: "Better to have little and fear the LORD than great riches with trouble" (Proverbs 15:16).

### A voice from the past

In *The Pilgrim's Progress*, John Bunyan has his pilgrims pass through the Valley of Humiliation, a lowly place most travellers fear. There they meet a shepherd boy in poor clothes with a fresh, happy face, singing to himself. Bunyan's guide says that boy "wears more of that herb called heart's-ease in his bosom" than people dressed in silk and velvet. This is the boy's song:

> "I am content with what I have, Little be it or much; And, Lord, contentment still I crave, Because thou savest such."
> John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*

### Practice this week

Take Proverbs 30:8-9 and pray it every day this week, slowly, meaning it. Then, at the end of each day, write down three things God gave you that day that you would not want to lose. By the end of the week you will have twenty-one.

### For groups

- Paul says he "learned" contentment. What experiences in your life have taught you contentment, or failed to?
- Is it harder for you to be content with little or with much? Why?
- Read Proverbs 30:8-9. Could you honestly pray "Give me neither poverty nor wealth"? What makes it hard?
- What is one thing you could simplify in your life to need less?

## Daily Bread, Not Daily Worry

For forty years in the wilderness, God fed Israel with manna. Every morning it lay on the ground like frost, and every family gathered what they needed for the day. God gave them a strict rule: don't keep any of it overnight. Some people didn't trust Him and kept extra anyway. By morning it was crawling with worms and stank.

The rule had a purpose. God was teaching His people to depend on Him one day at a time. And the miracle was that it always worked out:

> "The Israelites did this. Some of them gathered a lot, and others only a little. Yet when they measured it with a 2-quart measure, the one who gathered much had nothing left over, and the one who gathered little had no shortage. All of them had gathered as much as they could eat." (Exodus 16:17-18)

Centuries later, Jesus taught His disciples to pray, "Give us today the bread we need" (Matthew 6:11). Not this year's bread. Not a lifetime supply. Today's.

### Jesus on worry

In the Sermon on the Mount, straight after saying "You cannot serve both God and money," Jesus turns to the most common money emotion there is. Not greed but worry.

> "That is why I tell you not to worry about your life, about what you will eat, or about your body, about what you will wear. Is life not more than food, and the body more than clothes? Look at the birds in the sky. They do not plant or harvest or store food in barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not worth far more than they are?" (Matthew 6:25-26)

Notice the word "That is why." Worry is connected to the two masters. When money is our master, it doesn't just make us greedy. It makes us anxious. If money is your security, then every threat to your money is a threat to you. And there is always a threat.

Jesus gives several reasons not to worry. Life is more than food. Your Father feeds the birds, and you are worth far more. Worry can't add a single hour to your life. Your Father already knows what you need. And then He gives the great alternative:

> "Above all, seek the kingdom and His righteousness, and you will be given all these things as well." (Matthew 6:33)

### Seek first

This is one of the most important verses in this book. Jesus doesn't say, "Don't think about food and clothes." He knows we need them. He says, put God's kingdom first, and trust your Father to provide what you need.

This is not a bargain, as if God owes us whatever we want because we went to church. It is a matter of priorities. What gets your first attention, your best energy, your first money? Jesus says it should be God's kingdom: His rule in your life, His purposes in the world, His righteousness. When God is first, everything else falls into its proper place, and our Father takes responsibility for our needs.

Notice too that it says needs, not wants. God has promised to give us what we need. He hasn't promised to give us everything we want, and a good Father wouldn't.

### Is planning a lack of faith?

Someone might ask, "Then should Christians not save, or plan, or buy insurance? Isn't that a lack of faith?" No. The same Bible tells us to learn from the ant, which "gets its food ready in summer" (Proverbs 6:8), and Jesus Himself spoke of counting the cost before building a tower. We will look at that in Part 3.

The difference is between planning and worrying. Planning is responsible. Worrying is taking a weight that only God can carry. You can make a budget, put money aside and then sleep peacefully, because you have done what you can and trusted God with the rest. Or you can have plenty in the bank and lie awake in fear. The amount in your account is not what decides it. Trust is.

### Trust as a daily habit

How do we move from worry to trust? Not all at once. It is like the manna: one day at a time.

**Turn worries into prayers.** Paul says, "Do not worry about anything. Instead, in everything, bring your requests to God" (Philippians 4:6). Every worry is an invitation to pray. Name the bill, the debt, the job you're afraid of losing, and hand it to your Father.

**Remember His record.** Look back at how God has provided in the past. The psalmist wrote, "Young I was, and now I am old, and in all those years I have not known the righteous to be deserted, or their children to go hunting for bread" (Psalm 37:25).

**Live in today.** Jesus said, "So do not be troubled about tomorrow; tomorrow can take care of its own worries" (Matthew 6:34). Most of our money worries are about the future: what might happen. God gives grace for today, not for imaginary tomorrows.

**Do the next right thing.** Trust doesn't mean doing nothing. It means doing what you can and leaving the outcome with God. Apply for the job. Talk to the bank. Cut the unnecessary spending. Then rest.

### A story of trust

George Müller ran orphan houses in Bristol in the nineteenth century, caring for thousands of children over many years. He made it his rule never to ask anyone for money, but to tell his needs to God alone. Many times there was not enough food for the next meal, and he and his staff prayed. Again and again, help came, sometimes at the last moment. His diaries are full of entries recording what came in and when.

Hudson Taylor, the missionary to China, learned the same lesson as a young man. He once gave away his last coin, a half-crown, to a starving family. The next morning a letter arrived with no name and no message, just a pair of gloves with half a sovereign inside, four times what he had given. He wrote that he "then and there determined that a bank which could not break should have my savings or earnings."

These stories are not formulas. God does not always answer the way He did for Müller and Taylor, and both of them went through long times of want. But they show us a God who can be trusted. He is our Father, and He knows what we need.

### A voice from the past

Charles Spurgeon, writing on God's promises, put the choice in a single sentence:

> "Covetousness is poverty, and anxiety is misery: trust in God is an estate, and likeness to God is a heavenly inheritance."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith*

### Practice this week

Write down your three biggest money worries right now. Each morning this week, pray over each one, using Matthew 6:33 and Philippians 4:6-7. Then for each worry, write one small, practical thing you can do about it, do it, and leave the rest with God.

### For groups

- Why do you think Jesus put the teaching on worry right after "You cannot serve both God and money"?
- What is the difference between planning and worrying? Where is the line for you?
- Can you share a time when God provided for you in a way you didn't expect?
- What would it look like for you to "seek first the kingdom" with your money this month?

## The Danger of Riches

A rich young man ran up to Jesus and knelt in front of Him. He was eager, sincere and morally upright. He had kept the commandments since boyhood. He asked the most important question there is: "Good Teacher, what should I do to inherit eternal life?" (Mark 10:17).

Mark tells us something lovely: "Jesus looked at him and loved him." And then Jesus said the one thing the young man didn't want to hear:

> "'You are missing one thing,' He told him. 'Go and sell everything you own, and give the money to the poor. Then you will have treasure in heaven. After that, come and follow Me.' At these words his face clouded over, and he went away sad, for he owned a great deal of property." (Mark 10:21-22)

He went away sad. He wanted eternal life, but he wanted his property more. As he walked away, Jesus turned to His disciples and said something that astonished them: "a camel could pass through the eye of a needle more easily than a rich person could enter the kingdom of God" (Matthew 19:24).

### Why riches are dangerous

The disciples were amazed because in their culture, wealth was seen as a sign of God's blessing. If the rich can't be saved, who can? Jesus answered that with God all things are possible. A rich person can be saved. Abraham, Job, Zacchaeus and Joseph of Arimathea all prove it. But it is hard, and Jesus wanted His disciples to know why.

**Riches compete for trust.** It is very hard to depend on God when you don't seem to need Him. Proverbs says, "Those who put their trust in riches will fall" (Proverbs 11:28). The more we have, the more tempting it is to trust what we have.

**Riches choke the word.** In the parable of the sower, some seed falls among thorns: "the cares of the present age and the false promises of wealth smother it, so that it yields no crop" (Matthew 13:22). Notice "false promises." Wealth promises what it can't deliver.

**Riches make us proud.** Paul told Timothy to command the rich "not to be proud and not to put their hope in wealth, which is so uncertain" (1 Timothy 6:17). Money can make us feel superior, as if we are worth more because we have more.

**Riches blind us.** Jesus spoke to the church in Laodicea, which said, "I am rich. I have built up my wealth, and I lack nothing." His reply was devastating: "You do not see that it is you who are wretched and to be pitied, poor and blind and naked" (Revelation 3:17).

**Riches make us forget others.** In Jesus' story of the rich man and Lazarus, the rich man's sin isn't that he was rich. It is that a beggar lay at his gate every day, and he never saw him.

### The tyranny of things

A. W. Tozer saw that the problem goes deeper than money. It is about possessing. In his classic *The Pursuit of God*, he wrote a chapter called "The Blessedness of Possessing Nothing." He described the human heart this way:

> "There is within the human heart a tough fibrous root of fallen life whose nature is to possess, always to possess."

He said that before the fall, things were meant to be "external to the man and subservient to him." But now, he wrote, "The roots of our hearts have grown down into things, and we dare not pull up one rootlet lest we die."

That is the danger. Not that we have things, but that things have us. You don't have to be a millionaire for that to be true. You can be gripped by a phone, a car, a wardrobe or a savings account of any size.

Tozer pointed to Abraham as the answer. When God asked Abraham to give up Isaac, his son, the most precious thing he had, Abraham obeyed, and God gave Isaac back. "He had everything," Tozer wrote, "but he possessed nothing." Abraham was a rich man, but he held it all with an open hand.

### If riches increase

The Bible's answer to the danger of riches is not that every Christian must take a vow of poverty. Most believers in the Bible were not commanded to sell everything. Zacchaeus gave away half, not all. Jesus didn't tell Joseph of Arimathea to sell his tomb. But to the one man for whom wealth was the idol that kept him from Jesus, He said, "Sell everything."

The general rule is in the Psalms: "If wealth multiplies, do not give your heart to it" (Psalm 62:10). Riches may come. They are not sinful in themselves. But do not give your heart to them.

How do you keep your heart from riches when they increase? Paul gives Timothy a four-part answer for the rich:

> "Tell those who are rich in this present world not to be proud and not to put their hope in wealth, which is so uncertain, but in God, who richly gives us everything to enjoy. Tell them to do good, to be rich in good deeds, to give generously and to share. In this way they will store up treasure for themselves as a firm foundation for the age to come, so that they may take hold of the life that is truly life." (1 Timothy 6:17-19)

Don't be proud. Don't trust your wealth. Enjoy God's gifts with thanks. And give generously. That last one is the key. Giving is how a rich person keeps riches from becoming a master.

### Are you rich?

It is easy to read this chapter and think it's about someone else, the billionaires and celebrities. But if you have a roof over your head, food in the cupboard, clothes for more than one season and some money left at the end of the month, you are richer than most people who have ever lived, and many who are alive today. This chapter is for you. It is for me.

### A voice from the past

John Wesley saw many of his early converts grow rich through hard work and thrift, and then grow cold toward God. He did not tell them to stop working. He warned them that riches had to be given away to be safe. In one of his sermons he asked:

> "Suppose ye that money, though multiplied as the sand of the sea, can give happiness?"

And elsewhere, plainly:

> "Money seldom brings happiness, either in this world or the world to come."
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

### Practice this week

Pray Tozer's prayer from *The Pursuit of God* each day this week, naming specific possessions: "O God, be Thou exalted over my possessions." Then choose one possession you hold too tightly and decide what it would mean to hold it with an open hand: lend it, share it, or give it away.

### For groups

- Why were the disciples so surprised by what Jesus said about the rich?
- Read Tozer's idea that the problem is "possessing." What is the difference between having things and possessing them?
- By world standards, are you rich? How does that change how you read 1 Timothy 6:17-19?
- What is one thing you hold too tightly? What would holding it loosely look like?

# Part 3 · Wisdom for Everyday Money

## Gain All You Can, Honestly

In the eighteenth century, John Wesley preached a sermon called "The Use of Money." It became one of the most famous sermons on the subject ever preached, because it boiled the Bible's teaching down to three rules that anyone could remember:

Gain all you can. Save all you can. Give all you can.

The surprise is the first rule. Wesley was a man of deep holiness who lived very simply. You might expect him to warn people off making money altogether. Instead he started with "Gain all you can," and he meant it. He said Christians should work hard and earn well, using "all the understanding which God has given you." The fault, he said, "does not lie in the money, but in them that use it."

But he attached conditions, and they matter. We are to gain all we can without hurting our own body or health, without hurting our mind or soul, and without hurting our neighbour. In other words: gain all you can, honestly.

### Honest scales

The Bible has a great deal to say about how money is made, not just how it is spent. One of its most repeated commands concerns weights and measures. In the ancient world, buying and selling involved weighing out goods or silver on a balance. A dishonest trader could keep two sets of weights, a heavy one for buying and a light one for selling, and cheat every customer a little.

God hates it:

> "The LORD detests dishonest scales, but an honest weight pleases Him." (Proverbs 11:1)

The prophet Amos denounced the merchants of his day who couldn't wait for the holy days to end so they could get back to business, "making the ephah smaller and the shekel heavier, and cheating with dishonest scales, so that we can purchase the poor with silver" (Amos 8:5-6). They went to worship, but their hearts were in their shops, and their shops were built on cheating.

Today we don't use balances, but the principle is the same. Dishonest scales look like an inflated invoice, a hidden fee, a misleading advert, a fiddled expense claim, an unpaid tax, a "cash job" to avoid declaring income, or selling something with a fault you know about but don't mention. They also look like an employee who takes a full wage for half the work. God sees all of it, and He cares.

### Better a little honestly

Proverbs gives the honest worker a promise and a warning:

> "Money that comes easily soon dwindles, but whoever gathers it bit by bit makes it grow." (Proverbs 13:11)

There is a kind of wealth that comes fast: the lottery win, the get-rich-quick scheme, the shady deal. And there is a kind that comes slowly, a little at a time, through honest work and patient saving. The Bible is wary of the first and commends the second. "A faithful person will be richly blessed, but one who rushes to get rich will not escape punishment" (Proverbs 28:20).

Better still: "Better a little gained honestly than a fortune made by injustice" (Proverbs 16:8). Integrity is worth more than income. If you have to choose between losing money and losing your honesty, lose the money.

### What honest work looks like

Here are some marks of honest work for a Christian.

**Do a full day's work.** Whether you work for an employer or for yourself, give what you are paid for. Paul tells workers, "Do not work only while they are watching, to win their favor, but with an undivided heart" (Colossians 3:22).

**Charge a fair price.** Don't exploit people's ignorance or desperation. Zacchaeus the tax collector, when Jesus came to his house, promised to pay back four times anything he had taken by cheating (Luke 19:8).

**Pay fairly.** If you employ others, pay them promptly and fairly. James warns rich landowners, "The pay you held back from the laborers who mowed your fields is crying out, and the shouts of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of Armies" (James 5:4). God hears the cry of underpaid workers.

**Pay your taxes.** Jesus said, "Then pay Caesar what you owe Caesar, and pay God what you owe God" (Matthew 22:21). Paul says the same: "Pay everyone what you owe them: taxes to the one who collects taxes" (Romans 13:7).

**Choose your work wisely.** Wesley warned against any trade that harms the body or soul of the worker or the customer. Some ways of making money are simply not open to a Christian, however profitable they are.

**Be content with your pay, and still ask for what is fair.** When soldiers asked John the Baptist what repentance looked like for them, he said, "Do not shake anyone down for money or make false charges, and be satisfied with your pay" (Luke 3:14). There is a godly contentment that doesn't constantly grumble. That doesn't mean you can never ask for a raise or look for a better job. It means you don't use your position to grab what isn't yours.

### Why honesty is a witness

Honest dealing is one of the most powerful ways a Christian can show the world what God is like. People may never read your Bible, but they will read your invoices. When a Christian plumber refuses to overcharge, when a Christian employee owns up to a mistake that cost the company money, when a Christian business owner pays suppliers on time, people notice.

The opposite is also true. Few things damage the reputation of Jesus more than Christians who cheat in business. "A good reputation is worth choosing over great wealth" (Proverbs 22:1).

### Gain all you can

Don't miss the positive side. Wesley really did say, "Gain all you can." There's nothing holier about being poor through laziness or carelessness. If God has given you ability, use it. Work hard. Learn new skills. Build a business if you have the gift for it. Ask for a fair wage. A Christian who earns well and gives generously can do enormous good.

Just remember that the first rule is only the first rule. Gaining is never the goal. It is the starting point for saving and giving, as we will see. Wesley warned that people who obey the first rule and stop there are in great danger.

### A voice from the past

Charles Spurgeon, reading the proverb about hastening to be rich, wrote this:

> "He that hasteth to be rich shall not be innocent nor sure; but steady perseverance in integrity, if it do not bring riches, will certainly bring peace."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith*

And Wesley, in "The Use of Money":

> "Gain all you can by honest industry."
> John Wesley, *The Use of Money*

### Practice this week

Do an honesty check on your work and your finances. Is there anything you are doing at work that you would be ashamed for Jesus to see? Any tax you have avoided paying, any expense claim you have stretched, any customer you have overcharged? If so, put it right this week, even if it costs you. If you can, pay it back, as Zacchaeus did.

- What are some modern forms of "dishonest scales"?
- Read Proverbs 13:11 and 28:20. Why is the Bible so wary of getting rich quickly?
- How has someone's honesty in business, or lack of it, affected your view of their faith?

## Count the Cost

Jesus was a builder's son. He knew what an unfinished building looks like.

> "If any of you planned to build a tower, would you not sit down first and add up the expense, to find out whether you could afford to finish it?" (Luke 14:28)

In the story, the man who doesn't plan lays a foundation, runs out of money, and becomes a joke. Everyone who walks past laughs: "Here is someone who started to build and could not finish" (Luke 14:30).

Jesus told this story to make a point about discipleship. Following Him costs everything, so count the cost before you start. But He used an illustration from everyday money because the principle is so obviously true. Wise people plan before they spend.

### Wisdom plans

The book of Proverbs, which is full of practical wisdom about money, keeps returning to this idea:

> "The plans of the diligent bring nothing but gain, but all who rush in haste come to nothing but need." (Proverbs 21:5)

Planning and diligence lead to plenty. Haste leads to poverty. That's as true now as it was three thousand years ago. Impulse buys, unplanned spending and decisions made in a hurry are some of the main reasons people end up in financial trouble.

Proverbs sends us to the smallest of teachers:

> "You lazy one, go and look at the ant; watch how it lives, and learn wisdom. It has no chief, no overseer and no ruler, yet it gets its food ready in summer and gathers its supplies at harvest time." (Proverbs 6:6-8)

The ant plans ahead. When there's plenty, it stores some away for winter. It doesn't need a boss to tell it to. It is simply wise.

### Know your flocks

Here is another proverb that could have been written for anyone with a bank account:

> "Know how your flocks are doing, and keep your mind on your herds, for riches do not last forever, and a crown does not pass down through every generation." (Proverbs 27:23-24)

In an agricultural world, flocks and herds were your wealth. A wise farmer knew how many sheep he had, which ones were sick, and how the lambing was going. He didn't leave it to chance.

Today that means knowing what comes in, what goes out, and what you owe. Many people, including many Christians, have no idea where their money goes each month. They are surprised when it runs out. Knowing the state of your flocks is not unspiritual. It is simple faithfulness. You can't manage what you don't measure.

### A budget is a plan

A budget is just a plan for your money. It is deciding ahead of time where God's money will go, instead of finding out afterwards where it went. It doesn't need to be complicated. At its simplest, it has four parts:

- **What comes in.** Your income after tax.
- **What goes to God first.** Your giving, decided in advance (see chapter 13).
- **What you need to live.** Housing, food, utilities, transport, debt payments.
- **What's left.** Saving, then other spending.

There's a simple one-page money plan at the back of this book (Appendix 2). If you have never made a budget, start there. It might feel restrictive at first, but most people find it brings freedom. Instead of vague guilt and anxiety about money, you have a plan. You can spend what is in the plan without worrying, because you know it's covered.

### Save all you can

Wesley's second rule was "Save all you can." By this he mainly meant: don't waste money. Don't spend it on things that just feed pride, vanity or the desire to be seen. Live simply, so that more is left over.

But the Bible also commends saving in the modern sense of putting some aside for the future. "Valuable treasure and oil are stored in the home of the wise, but a fool gulps it all down" (Proverbs 21:20). The fool spends everything as soon as he gets it. The wise person keeps something in reserve.

Joseph saved Egypt from famine by storing up grain in the seven good years. Paul told the Corinthians to set aside money regularly, "On the first day of each week, every one of you should put something aside at home, saving as much as you have gained" (1 Corinthians 16:2), so that it would be ready when it was needed.

Saving gives you margin: room to breathe when the car breaks down or the boiler fails, so that a small crisis doesn't become a debt. It means you can say yes when God puts a need in front of you. And it means your family won't be a burden on others.

### When saving becomes hoarding

There's a line, though, and Jesus drew it sharply in the parable of the rich fool. The man's problem wasn't that he saved. It was that he saved everything for himself, built bigger and bigger barns, and planned to spend his life eating, drinking and having a good time. He saved out of selfishness and a desire for security apart from God. And he died that night.

How do you know if you have crossed the line from saving to hoarding? Ask yourself why you're saving. Saving for a specific, wise purpose, such as an emergency fund, a home, your children's needs or your old age, is good stewardship. Saving endlessly with no purpose except feeling safe can be a sign that money has become your security instead of God. A good test is whether your giving grows as your savings grow. If you are saving more and more but giving the same or less, something may be wrong.

### Spend well

Finally, planning includes spending. Spending money is not wrong. God "richly gives us everything to enjoy" (1 Timothy 6:17). A holiday, a meal out, a gift for someone you love, a beautiful thing for your home: these can all be received with thanks. The question is not whether you spend but whether you spend wisely and gratefully, as a steward.

Spurgeon, in his folksy *John Ploughman's Talk*, put it like this:

> "Economy is half the battle in life; it is not so hard to earn money as to spend it well."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *John Ploughman's Talk*

### A voice from the past

John Wesley taught that once you have gained all you can, "by honest wisdom and unwearied diligence," the second rule of Christian prudence is "Save all you can." He meant it as a guard against waste, so that there would be more to give. He was not commending hoarding but freeing money for the third rule.

### Practice this week

Track every penny you spend this week. Write it all down, even the coffee. At the end of the week, sort it into categories and look at it honestly. Then fill in the one-page money plan in Appendix 2. If you are married, do it together.

- Do you find budgeting freeing or restrictive? Why?
- Read Proverbs 27:23-24. What would "knowing the state of your flocks" look like in your life?
- Where is the line between wise saving and hoarding? How can you tell which side of it you are on?
- What is one area of your spending that needs a plan?

## The Borrower's Chains

Elisha the prophet was approached one day by a desperate widow. Her husband had been one of the prophets, a man who feared God. He had died in debt, and now the man he owed was coming to take her two sons as slaves to pay it off.

Elisha asked her what she had in the house. Nothing, she said, except a small jar of oil. He told her to borrow empty jars from all her neighbours, as many as she could, shut the door and start pouring. The oil kept flowing until every jar was full. Then Elisha said, "Go and sell the oil, and pay off your debt. You and your sons can live on the rest" (2 Kings 4:7).

It's a story of God's miraculous provision. But it is also a story about how terrible debt can be. In the ancient world, debt could cost you your children's freedom.

### Debt is a kind of slavery

> "The rich lord it over the poor, and whoever borrows becomes the lender's slave." (Proverbs 22:7)

That's a hard word, but it's true. When you owe money, you are not completely free. Part of your future income already belongs to someone else. Every month a portion of your work goes to pay for the past instead of building the future or giving to God. Debt limits your choices. You may not be able to change jobs, move, give generously, or answer a call from God, because you have to keep up the payments.

Debt is also expensive. Interest means you pay far more for something than it costs. With high-interest credit cards or payday loans, that can be ruinous.

And debt often brings anxiety, conflict in marriages and shame. Many people carry debt like a weight they never talk about.

### Is all debt sin?

The Bible doesn't say that all borrowing is a sin. The law of Moses gave rules about lending, which assumes that borrowing happened, and it told the Israelites to lend generously to their poor neighbours. Jesus said, "Give to anyone who asks you, and do not turn your back on someone who wants to borrow from you" (Matthew 5:42). Most people need to borrow to buy a home, and a carefully planned mortgage can be wise.

But the Bible clearly treats debt as something to avoid where possible and get out of as soon as you can. Paul's command is: "Do not stay in debt to anyone, except for the debt of love you owe one another" (Romans 13:8). The only debt we should carry forever is the debt of love.

And the Bible is clear that we must repay what we borrow. "The wicked borrow and never pay back, but the righteous are kind and give freely" (Psalm 37:21). Failing to repay, when you are able, is a form of theft.

### Be careful with guarantees

Proverbs repeatedly warns against another kind of debt: putting up security for someone else's loan.

> "Do not be one of those who shake hands on a pledge and guarantee other people's loans. If you cannot pay, why let them take even the bed you sleep on?" (Proverbs 22:26-27)

That sounds harsh. Shouldn't we help our friends? Yes, but Proverbs is wise. If you guarantee someone else's debt, you are promising to pay if they can't, and you lose control over your own finances. Many friendships and families have been broken this way. If you want to help someone, it is often better to give what you can afford to give, rather than to guarantee what you can't afford to lose.

### Getting free

If you are in debt, don't despair. Many people have walked out of debt with God's help, and so can you. Here are some steps.

**Stop digging.** The first rule of getting out of a hole is to stop digging. Don't take on any new debt. That might mean cutting up a card or closing an account.

**Face the facts.** Write down every debt you have: who you owe, how much, the interest rate and the monthly payment. It can be frightening, but you can't defeat an enemy you won't look at.

**Make a plan.** Use the money plan in Appendix 2. Find every bit of money you can free up. Many people pay off their smallest debt first, to build momentum, then roll that payment into the next. Others start with the highest interest rate, to save money. Either way, have a plan.

**Get help.** There are free, trustworthy debt advice services in many countries, and many churches have people who can help. There's no shame in asking. Pride keeps more people in debt than poverty does.

**Keep giving.** It might seem strange, but don't stop giving altogether, even if your gift has to be small for a season. Giving keeps your heart free from money's grip, and it reminds you who your Provider is.

**Pray.** Ask God for help, for wisdom and sometimes for provision. The widow in Elisha's day did exactly that, and God answered.

**Be patient.** You probably didn't get into debt overnight, and you won't get out overnight. Celebrate every debt you pay off.

### Freedom

The Bible's picture of the good life includes freedom from debt. Picture what it would mean to owe nothing to anyone. Every pound or dollar you earn would be yours to manage for God: to live on, to save and to give. You would be free to answer God's call wherever it led. That freedom is worth working for.

And remember the gospel. We were all deep in a debt we could never repay, a debt of sin. Jesus paid it all. He taught us to pray, "Forgive us what we owe You, as we too have forgiven those who owe us" (Matthew 6:12). The deepest freedom is already ours in Christ. Getting out of financial debt is a small picture of that great freedom.

### A voice from the past

Spurgeon's John Ploughman gave this advice about lending and guaranteeing:

> "Very often he that his money lends loses both his gold and his friends, and he who is surety is never sure."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *John Ploughman's Talk*

### Practice this week

Make a complete list of every debt you have. Pray over it. Then decide on one concrete step toward freedom you can take this week, and take it. If you have no debt, thank God, and ask whether there's someone you could help, perhaps by giving rather than lending.

- Why does Proverbs compare the borrower to a slave? Have you experienced that?
- Read Romans 13:8. What is "the debt of love"?
- Why do you think Proverbs warns so strongly against guaranteeing other people's loans?
- What is one thing that keeps people from getting help with debt? How can a church help?

## Provide for Your Own

Some people who come to faith suddenly want to give everything away. That can be a beautiful impulse. But it can also go badly wrong.

William Seymour, the African-American preacher at the centre of the Azusa Street revival in Los Angeles in 1906, saw both sides. In a short teaching called "In Money Matters," he warned against teachers who told new believers to sell everything, with terrible results. "Under false teaching," he wrote, "children have been left to go half naked, women have left their husbands, and husbands leave their wives to wash and scrub, and the Bible says that is worse than infidelity."

Then he quoted the verse that sets a limit on that kind of zeal.

### Worse than an unbeliever

> "Anyone who does not provide for their relatives, and especially for their own household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever." (1 Timothy 5:8)

That's one of the strongest statements in the New Testament, and it's about money. A Christian who refuses to look after their own family, when they're able to, is behaving worse than an unbeliever. Even people who don't know God know that you look after your family.

This verse is mainly about caring for elderly relatives, especially widows, in a world without pensions. But it applies to all our family responsibilities: to our spouse, to our children, to our ageing parents. Providing for them is not a distraction from serving God. It is part of serving God.

### Balance

Seymour didn't then tell people to hoard. He went on to warn just as strongly against the opposite error: "It does not mean for you to have great real estate and money banked up while your brothers and sisters are suffering." And he taught that the Spirit would lead each believer in their giving. It is not either/or. It is both: provide for your family, and give generously.

That balance runs right through the Bible. The Proverbs 31 woman is a capable businesswoman who buys a field, plants a vineyard and makes sure her household is clothed for winter. And "Her hand is open to the poor; she reaches out to help the needy" (Proverbs 31:20). She provides and she gives.

### What providing looks like

Providing for your family means more than just paying the bills. Here are some areas to think about.

**Daily needs.** Food, clothing, shelter, health care and education. This is the basic level, and most of our income goes here.

**Margin for emergencies.** We looked at this in the last chapter. An emergency fund protects your family from small crises becoming large ones.

**Protection.** Depending on where you live and your circumstances, this might mean insurance, so that your family isn't left destitute if you become ill or die. Some Christians worry that insurance shows a lack of faith. But Joseph stored grain for the famine, and nobody accuses him of unbelief. Wise provision is a form of love.

**The future.** Proverbs says, "A good person leaves an inheritance to their grandchildren" (Proverbs 13:22). That doesn't have to mean a fortune. It means thinking beyond yourself and your own lifetime. It can include saving for old age, so that you are not a burden on your children, and making a will, so that your family isn't left in confusion.

**Time and presence.** Providing isn't only about money. Many parents work so hard to provide things for their children that they have no time left to give them themselves. Children need a parent more than they need a bigger house.

### Ageing parents

In many cultures, caring for elderly parents is simply expected. In others, it has become optional. The Bible is clear. Jesus rebuked religious leaders who found a loophole to avoid supporting their parents by declaring their money "devoted to God." He said they were cancelling the word of God for the sake of their tradition. Honouring your father and mother includes providing for them when they can no longer provide for themselves.

That can be costly, in money and in time. But it is one of the clearest ways the Bible asks us to show our faith.

### Providing is not hoarding

How do you tell the difference between providing for your family and hoarding for yourself? Here are some questions to ask.

- Am I providing for real needs, or am I building a bigger and bigger cushion out of fear?
- Is my giving growing along with my provision for my family?
- Am I using "providing for my family" as an excuse to avoid giving?
- Am I teaching my children to trust God, or to trust money?

### When you can't provide

Some readers will feel the weight of this chapter painfully. Perhaps you have lost your job, or you are ill, or you are a single parent working two jobs and still can't make ends meet. 1 Timothy 5:8 is not a word against you. It is aimed at people who could provide and won't.

If you are struggling, the same chapter tells the church to care for those who have no one else. It is not shameful to receive help. The early Christians shared so that "No one among them lacked anything" (Acts 4:34). Let your church family help carry the load, and remember that your heavenly Father knows what you need.

### A voice from the past

William Seymour, after warning against careless teaching, gave this balanced word about property:

> "It is right for you to have property, but if the Lord says, take $200 or $500 or $1,000 and distribute here or there, you do it."
> William J. Seymour, *In Money Matters*

### Practice this week

Sit down, alone or with your spouse, and ask: are there any gaps in how we provide for our family? Do we have an emergency fund? A will? Do our ageing parents need help? Choose one gap and take a first step toward closing it this month.

### For groups

- Why do you think Paul uses such strong language in 1 Timothy 5:8?
- What does the Proverbs 31 woman teach us about balancing providing and giving?
- How can "providing for my family" become an excuse not to give?
- How can a church help those who are struggling to provide for their families?

## Firstfruits

In ancient Israel, harvest was a time of joy and of anxiety. A farmer waited all year for the crops to ripen. Would there be enough? Would the rains come? Would locusts arrive? When the first sheaves of barley were finally cut, the temptation was to keep them all, just in case.

God told His people to do the opposite. The very first of the harvest, the firstfruits, were to be brought to Him before anything else was eaten.

> "Honor the LORD with what you own, with the first and best of everything your land produces; then there will be more than enough to fill your barns, and new wine will burst out of your vats." (Proverbs 3:9-10)

The first and the best. Not the leftovers. Not what's left at the end of the month. The first.

### Why first?

Giving first is an act of faith. When you give from the first of your income, before you know whether the rest will stretch far enough, you are saying, "God, I trust You to provide for the rest." When you give from the leftovers, you are saying, in effect, "I'll trust myself first, and give You what I don't need."

Giving first is also an act of worship. It declares that God comes first in your life, not just in your words but in your bank account. It puts Matthew 6:33 into practice: "seek the kingdom" first.

And giving first is wise. If you wait until the end of the month to see what's left, you will almost always find there's nothing. Spending expands to fill whatever is available. If you decide your giving first, it happens.

### The tithe

The Old Testament gave a specific measure for this firstfruits giving: the tithe, which means a tenth. Abraham gave a tenth to the priest Melchizedek. Jacob promised God a tenth of all he received. The law of Moses required Israel to bring a tenth of their crops and flocks:

> "Year after year, be sure to set aside a tithe of all the crops that grow from the seed you sow in the field." (Deuteronomy 14:22)

Notice the purpose, which comes in the next verse: "In this way you will learn to fear the LORD your God at all times" (Deuteronomy 14:23). The tithe was a way of training the heart to put God first.

Centuries later, when Israel had grown slack, the prophet Malachi brought God's challenge:

> "Bring the full tithe into the storehouse, so that there will be food in My house. Put Me to the test in this, says the LORD of Armies, and see if I do not open the windows of heaven for you and pour out on you a blessing until you have more than enough." (Malachi 3:10)

It's the only place in the Bible where God invites His people to test Him. He had just accused them of robbing Him: "Will a mortal rob God? But you are robbing Me. Yet you ask, 'In what way do we rob You?' In tithes and offerings" (Malachi 3:8).

### Do Christians have to tithe?

This is a question on which faithful Christians disagree. Some believe the tithe is still the standard for believers today. Others note that the New Testament never commands it directly. Jesus mentions it once, approving it in passing while rebuking the Pharisees for neglecting justice and mercy. The New Testament's teaching on giving focuses instead on generosity, proportion, cheerfulness and the example of Christ.

This book takes the view that the tithe is a wonderful starting line but not a ceiling. Here's why. If Israel, under the old covenant, was asked to give a tenth, it is hard to imagine that Christians, who have received so much more in Christ, should be content to give less. And the New Testament pattern seems to push us beyond a tenth rather than below it: the Macedonians gave "more than they could," Zacchaeus gave half, the widow gave everything.

So if you have never given a tenth, it is a good goal to work toward. If you already give a tenth, don't stop there. Ask God whether He wants you to grow in giving as He grows your income.

What matters most is not the exact percentage. It is the heart: giving first, giving regularly, giving cheerfully, and giving in proportion to what God has given you.

### Proportional giving

That last idea, proportion, comes straight from Paul. When he organised the collection for Jerusalem, he told the Corinthians: "On the first day of each week, every one of you should put something aside at home, saving as much as you have gained" (1 Corinthians 16:2). Each person should give in proportion to how they have been prospered.

That means a student with a part-time job and a business owner with a large income are not expected to give the same amount. But both are expected to give, and both are expected to give in proportion. As Paul says elsewhere, "a gift is welcomed for what the giver has, not judged by what the giver lacks" (2 Corinthians 8:12).

### What about the promise?

Proverbs 3 and Malachi 3 both promise blessing to those who give. Does that mean God will always pay you back in money?

Be careful here. These promises are real. God does bless generosity, and many Christians can tell stories of His provision after they began to give. But the Old Testament promises to Israel were often tied to their life in the land, and the blessing God gives His people is not always financial. Jesus was the most generous person who ever lived, and He died owning only the clothes He wore.

The right way to hold these promises is this: give because you love God and trust Him, not as an investment scheme. God will make sure you never lose by being generous, but He will decide how He repays you, whether in this life or the next.

### Starting

If you have never given regularly, or have only given what's left over, here's a simple way to begin.

**Decide a percentage.** Pray about it. If a tenth seems impossible right now, choose a percentage you can manage, and plan to increase it by one percent every year.

**Give it first.** Set it up to happen as soon as you are paid. Treat it as the first line in your budget.

**Give it to your church first.** Most Christians believe that our regular giving should begin with the local church where we are fed, taught and cared for. "Those who are being taught the word must share all their good things with the one who teaches them" (Galatians 6:6).

**Watch what happens.** Not to see if you get rich, but to see how God provides and how your heart changes.

### A voice from the past

George Müller, who gave away almost everything he received, gave this simple advice to Christians who wanted to begin:

> "Only fix even the smallest amount you purpose to give of your income, and give this regularly; and as God is pleased to increase your light and grace, and is pleased to prosper you more, so give more."
> George Müller, quoted in *George Müller of Bristol*

And F. B. Meyer saw that giving a fixed proportion protects the heart:

> "In order to guard against the love of money, we should be careful to give a stated proportion to the cause of Christ."
> F. B. Meyer, *Our Daily Walk*

### Practice this week

Decide what percentage of your income you will give to God, starting with your next pay. Set it up so it happens first. If you already give, pray about whether God is asking you to take a step up.

- What is the difference between giving firstfruits and giving leftovers? Which have you mostly done?
- Do you think Christians are required to tithe? Why or why not?
- Read 1 Corinthians 16:2. What does "in proportion" mean in your situation?
- How would you answer someone who says, "I can't afford to give"?

## Cheerful, Regular, Quiet Giving

The apostle Paul once gave a teaching on giving that has shaped the church ever since. It came in a letter to a church that was not giving well.

The Corinthians had been enthusiastic about the collection for Jerusalem at first, but their enthusiasm had faded. Paul didn't scold. He taught them three things about how to give: generously, cheerfully and willingly.

> "Keep this in mind: sow sparingly and you will reap sparingly; sow generously and you will reap generously. Each of you should give the amount you have settled on in your own heart, not with regret and not because you feel you must. God loves a giver who gives gladly. God is able to make all grace abound for you, so that in every way and at all times you will have everything you need, with plenty to spare for every good work." (2 Corinthians 9:6-8)

### Generous

Paul starts with a farming picture. A farmer who sows only a few seeds will get a small harvest. A farmer who sows generously will get a large one. Giving is like sowing. It is not throwing money away. It is planting it.

Proverbs says the same, in a way that seems upside down:

> "One person gives freely and gains even more; another holds back what should be given and ends up in need. A generous person will prosper, and whoever refreshes others will be refreshed." (Proverbs 11:24-25)

The world says you get rich by keeping. God says you become truly rich by giving. Again, this isn't a formula for financial gain. But it is a principle God has built into His world: generous people are refreshed, and stingy people shrink.

### Cheerful

"God loves a giver who gives gladly." Older translations say "a cheerful giver." God doesn't want our money if it comes with a scowl. He isn't short of funds. What He wants is our hearts, and gladness is the sign that the heart is in it.

That doesn't mean you have to feel happy every time you give. Sometimes giving is a costly act of obedience, and the joy comes later. But it does mean that giving should never be squeezed out of us by guilt, pressure or manipulation. "Not with regret and not because you feel you must," Paul says.

How do you become a cheerful giver? Remember chapter 4. "You know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ." Cheerful giving flows from a heart that knows how much it has been given. Paul ends this section with "Thanks be to God for His gift, which no words can describe!" (2 Corinthians 9:15). Gratitude is the soil cheerful giving grows in.

Jesus Himself said, "It is more blessed to give than to receive" (Acts 20:35). Paul quotes this saying, which isn't recorded in the Gospels, to the elders of Ephesus. Giving is not a loss. It is a blessing, often a greater one than receiving.

### Willingly and deliberately

"Each of you should give the amount you have settled on in your own heart." Giving should be decided in advance, thoughtfully and prayerfully. It shouldn't be an impulse driven by an emotional appeal, nor an afterthought. Paul had asked them to set money aside every week (1 Corinthians 16:2) so that when he arrived there would be no need for a last-minute collection.

This is one of the most practical things you can do. Decide what you will give, regularly, and give it regularly. Then you are free to respond to special needs as well, out of what you have planned to keep in reserve. George Müller warned that giving only from impulse is a loss:

> "If you neglect an habitual giving, a regular giving, a giving from principle and upon scriptural ground, and leave it only to feeling and impulse, or particular arousing circumstances, you will certainly be a loser."

### Quietly

Jesus added one more quality: secrecy.

> "Take care not to put your righteousness on show for others to see. If you do, your Father in heaven has no reward for you. So when you give to the poor, do not announce it with trumpets, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and on the streets to win praise from others. Truly I tell you, they have already been paid in full. But when you give, keep it so quiet that your left hand does not know what your right hand is doing. Your gift will be hidden, and your Father, who sees what is hidden, will repay you." (Matthew 6:1-4)

Notice what Jesus says about the people who give for show: "they have already been paid in full." If you give to be seen, you'll get exactly what you wanted: to be seen. That is all you will get. But if you give quietly, your Father sees, and He will reward you.

This doesn't mean all giving must be anonymous. The early church knew that Barnabas sold a field and gave the money. Sometimes giving is public, and an example can inspire others. The question is motive. Am I giving to be seen by people, or by God?

George Müller made it his rule not to publish the names of those who gave to the orphan houses. His annual reports gave the sum and the date of each gift, but not the giver. He wanted the glory to go to God alone.

### God supplies the giver

Don't miss the end of Paul's teaching: "God is able to make all grace abound for you, so that in every way and at all times you will have everything you need, with plenty to spare for every good work" (2 Corinthians 9:8). And a few verses later: "You will be made rich in every way so that you can be generous in every way" (2 Corinthians 9:11).

Here is the purpose of God's provision. He gives to us so that we can give to others. We are not reservoirs where blessing collects. We are channels it flows through.

### A voice from the past

Charles Spurgeon, reflecting on how God treats generous people, wrote:

> "Men trust good stewards with larger and larger sums, and so it frequently is with the Lord; he gives by cartloads to those who give by bushels."

He added a word of balance: "Where wealth is not bestowed the Lord makes the little much by the contentment which the sanctified heart feels in a portion of which the tithe has been dedicated to the Lord."

And A. B. Simpson saw the deepest reason of all:

> "This is why the Master teaches us to give, because giving means loving, and love is but another name for life."
> A. B. Simpson, *Days of Heaven Upon Earth*

### Practice this week

Do one act of secret giving this week. Give something, money, a meal, a gift card, a bag of groceries, to someone in need, and tell no one. Don't mention it to your friends, don't post about it. Let it be just between you and your Father.

- Read Proverbs 11:24-25. How have you seen this principle at work?
- Why do you think Jesus put such emphasis on giving secretly?
- What does it mean that God gives to us so that we can give to others?

## Lending to the Lord

There is a verse in Proverbs that ought to change the way we look at every poor person we meet:

> "Whoever is kind to the poor is lending to the LORD, and He will pay them back for what they have done." (Proverbs 19:17)

When you give to someone in need, God takes it as a loan to Himself. And God always repays His debts.

### God cares about the poor

From one end of the Bible to the other, God shows His special care for the poor, the widow, the orphan and the foreigner. These were the people with no land, no protector and no voice in the ancient world, and God said again and again that He was their defender.

The law of Moses built care for the poor into everyday life. Farmers were not to harvest right up to the edges of their fields or pick up what they dropped: "Leave them for the poor and for the outsider. I am the LORD your God" (Leviticus 19:10). Every third year the tithe was to go to the Levites, the foreigners, the orphans and the widows. Debts were to be cancelled every seventh year. And Moses told Israel how to treat a neighbour in need:

> "Do not harden your heart or keep your hand closed against that needy person. Instead, open your hand to him generously and lend him as much as he needs to meet his want." (Deuteronomy 15:7-8)

"Open your hand." What a picture. A closed fist holds on. An open hand gives.

The prophets kept returning to this. Amos thundered against those who "trample on the needy." Micah summed up what God requires: "to do justice, to love loyalty, and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). Proverbs says, "Whoever oppresses the poor insults the One who made them, but kindness to the needy honors Him" (Proverbs 14:31).

### Jesus and the poor

Jesus began His ministry by announcing that He had come "to bring good news to the poor." He was born poor, lived among the poor and constantly drew attention to them. He told a host not to invite his rich friends to his feasts, but to "ask the poor to come, and those who are maimed, lame, or blind. And you will be blessed, for they have no way to pay you back" (Luke 14:13-14).

And in one of His most solemn teachings, He identified Himself with the poor completely. At the final judgment, He said, the King will say to the righteous:

> "For when I was hungry, you gave Me food; when I was thirsty, you gave Me a drink; when I was a stranger, you took Me into your home; when I had no clothes, you dressed Me; when I was sick, you looked after Me; when I was in prison, you came to see Me." (Matthew 25:35-36)

When they ask when they ever did that, He answers: "Truly I tell you, each time you did it for one of these My brothers and sisters, even the least of them, you did it for Me" (Matthew 25:40).

When you feed a hungry person, you feed Jesus.

### Faith that works

The early church took this seriously. James wrote:

> "Suppose a brother or sister has no proper clothes and not enough food for the day, and one of you tells them, 'Go in peace; keep warm and eat well,' but you do not give them what the body needs. What use is that?" (James 2:15-16)

And John:

> "Suppose someone has enough of this world's goods and notices a brother or sister in need, yet shuts their heart against them. How can the love of God remain in such a person? Dear children, let us love not by what we say or with our tongues, but by what we do and in truth." (1 John 3:17-18)

Words are cheap. Love that never opens its wallet is not the love of God.

### Giving wisely to the poor

Caring for the poor is not always simple. It takes wisdom as well as compassion. Here are some principles.

**Start close.** Paul says, "So whenever we have the chance, we should do good to everyone, and above all to those who belong to the family of faith" (Galatians 6:10). Begin with needs in your own church, your neighbourhood, your extended family.

**Give in ways that help.** Sometimes cash is the best gift. Sometimes it can do harm. A meal, a bill paid directly, a bag of groceries or a lift to a job interview may be better. Ask what will truly help.

**Respect dignity.** The poor are not projects. They are people made in God's image. Listen to them. Learn their names. Where you can, help in ways that let people work and provide for themselves, as Boaz did for Ruth by letting her glean in his fields.

**Support those who do it well.** Many churches and Christian organisations have experience and wisdom in helping the poor. Giving through them can multiply your impact.

**Don't let complexity become an excuse.** It is easy to say, "I don't know if they'll use it well," and so never give at all. Better to err occasionally on the side of generosity than to harden your heart.

### Justice as well as charity

The Bible calls us not just to give to the poor but to act justly. That means paying fair wages, refusing to exploit people, speaking up for those who are oppressed and working, where we can, for a fairer society. The prophets condemned not only stinginess but also injustice: rigged courts, cheating merchants and landowners who swallowed up their neighbours' fields. Christians who handle money should handle it justly.

### A voice from the past

John Wesley once told a story against himself. At Oxford, on a cold winter's day, a young maid from a school he helped to run called at his room. She was wearing only a thin linen gown. He reached into his pocket to give her something and found he had almost nothing left. He had just spent it on pictures for his walls. It struck him like a blow:

> "Will thy Master say, 'Well done, good and faithful steward?' Thou hast adorned thy walls with the money which might have screened this poor creature from the cold!"
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

Elsewhere he put it more sharply:

> "When you are laying out that money in costly apparel which you could have otherwise spared for the poor, you thereby deprive them of what God, the proprietor of all, had lodged in your hands for their use."

### Practice this week

Find one practical way to help someone poor this week: buy groceries for a food bank, pay a bill for a struggling family, take a meal to a neighbour, or give to a church fund for those in need. If possible, do it in person, and learn the name of someone you help.

### For groups

- What does it mean that giving to the poor is "lending to the LORD"?
- Read Matthew 25:31-40. How does this passage change the way you see poor people?
- What are some wise and unwise ways to help people in need?
- What injustices in your community might God be calling Christians to address?

## Give All You Can

Jesus sat down opposite the temple treasury and watched people put in their offerings. Many rich people came by and threw in large amounts. It must have made a satisfying noise as the coins clattered in.

Then a poor widow came and dropped in two tiny coins, the smallest coins there were, worth less than a penny. Jesus called His disciples over.

> "Truly I tell you, this widow, poor as she is, has given more than all the others who are giving to the offering boxes. The others all gave from what they had left over. But she, in her need, gave everything she had, the whole of what she had to live on." (Mark 12:43-44)

### How God counts

In God's arithmetic, the widow gave more than all of them. How? Because God doesn't measure a gift by its size. He measures it by its cost. The rich gave out of their surplus. They had plenty left. The widow gave out of her poverty. She had nothing left.

That is good news for people with little. Your small gift, given sacrificially, may be worth more in heaven than a large gift given from comfort. God is not impressed by size. He looks at the heart and at what the gift costs the giver.

The American revivalist Charles Finney said the same thing to the wealthy church members of his day:

> "Perhaps that poor woman, who puts in twelve and a half cents at the monthly concert, has exercised more self-denial, than they have in giving thousands."

And it is a challenge to people with much. If God measures what we keep as well as what we give, many of us who give generous-sounding amounts are still giving from our surplus. We give what we will never miss.

### Wesley's third rule

We have seen John Wesley's first two rules: "Gain all you can" and "Save all you can." His third rule is where they lead:

> "Having, First, gained all you can, and, Secondly saved all you can, Then 'give all you can.'"
> John Wesley, *The Use of Money*

Wesley warned that a person who gained and saved but did not give was in great danger. Gaining and saving without giving just make you a richer miser. The whole point of gaining and saving is to give.

And he lived it. The story often told of him goes back to his early biographers. As a young man at Oxford he found he could live on about twenty-eight pounds a year, and he gave away what was left. When his income doubled, and then rose further, he kept living on the same twenty-eight pounds and gave all the rest away. His income grew, but his standard of living stayed simple. Late in life, his books earned a great deal, and almost all of it went to the poor and to the work of the gospel.

Wesley's challenge to us is not "give a tenth." It is "give all you can." That doesn't mean give everything and leave your family destitute. It means: provide for your real needs, then ask, how much more can I give?

### A ceiling on spending

One practical way to follow Wesley is to set a limit on your own lifestyle. Decide what you and your family actually need to live on, simply but not miserably. Then, as your income grows, let your giving grow instead of your spending.

Most of us do the opposite. When our income goes up, our lifestyle goes up to match. A bigger house, a newer car, more expensive holidays. Before long, we feel just as stretched on the higher income as we did on the lower one. This is sometimes called lifestyle creep, and it is one of the main reasons people who earn more do not give more.

Setting a ceiling on spending is a way of saying, "Enough." It's the opposite of covetousness. It frees more money every year for God's work.

### The call to the rich

Paul gave Timothy specific instructions for the wealthy members of his church:

> "Tell them to do good, to be rich in good deeds, to give generously and to share. In this way they will store up treasure for themselves as a firm foundation for the age to come, so that they may take hold of the life that is truly life." (1 Timothy 6:18-19)

Notice: "rich in good deeds." The rich are not told to stop being rich. They are told to become rich in a different currency, and to use the first kind of riches to gain the second.

And notice the result: "so that they may take hold of the life that is truly life." Giving is not a loss of life. It is how we grab hold of real life. Stinginess shrinks the soul. Generosity enlarges it.

### Sacrificial giving

The Macedonian churches that Paul praised in 2 Corinthians 8 were extremely poor, and yet "they gave all they could, and more than they could, and they did it of their own free will" (2 Corinthians 8:3). That is sacrificial giving: giving that costs. It's giving that means you go without something you would like.

For most of us, sacrificial giving won't mean giving away everything. But it should mean giving enough that we feel it. If our giving never affects our lifestyle at all, it may be that we are only ever giving from our surplus.

### Everything

The widow gave "the whole of what she had to live on." In the end, that is what God asks of every disciple. Not necessarily all our money, but all of ourselves. The Macedonians "gave themselves to the Lord" first. When we have given ourselves, our money follows.

### A voice from the past

Amy Carmichael, missionary to India, wrote a little book of searching questions about what she called "Calvary love." One of them goes straight to the heart of giving:

> "If there is any reserve in my giving to Him who loved me enough to give His dearest for me... then I know nothing of Calvary love."
> Amy Carmichael, *If*

### Practice this week

Work out roughly what you and your family need to live on, simply but not miserably. Write it down. Then pray about this question: if my income goes up next year, could I let my giving rise instead of my spending?

### For groups

- Why did Jesus say the widow gave more than all the others?
- Is it possible to give a large amount and still give from surplus? How would you know?
- What do you think of Wesley's practice of capping his living costs? Could you do something similar?
- What is the difference between giving sacrificially and giving irresponsibly?

## Faithful in Little

Hudson Taylor was a young medical assistant in Hull, preparing to go to China as a missionary. He had decided he needed to learn to trust God for money before he left England. If he couldn't trust God in Hull, he reasoned, how would he trust Him in China? So when his employer forgot to pay his salary, Taylor didn't remind him. He prayed instead.

One Saturday night he found he had only a single coin left, a half-crown. That Sunday evening a poor man asked him to come and pray for his wife, who was dying. Taylor followed him to a wretched room where a mother lay exhausted next to a newborn baby, and several children stood around with the hollow cheeks of slow starvation.

Taylor began to tell them about a loving Father in heaven, and his own words condemned him. He could trust God, he realised, "plus one-and-sixpence," but not with nothing in his pocket. He tried to pray, and his conscience said, "Dare you kneel down and call Him Father with that half-crown in your pocket?" At last he gave the man the coin, his last money in the world. "The joy all came back in full flood-tide to my heart," he wrote.

The next morning a letter came with no name and no message inside, only a pair of gloves. As he unfolded them, half a sovereign fell to the floor, four times what he had given. Years later, after a life of trusting God through great difficulty in China, he looked back on that night and wrote:

> "If we are faithful to God in little things, we shall gain experience and strength that will be helpful to us in the more serious trials of life."

### Little things, large things

Taylor was echoing Jesus, who said:

> "Someone who is faithful in the smallest matter is faithful in large matters too, and someone who is dishonest in the smallest matter is dishonest in large matters too. So if you have not been faithful in handling unjust wealth, who will put real wealth in your care?" (Luke 16:10-11)

Jesus calls money "the smallest matter." To us money feels enormous. It decides where we live, what we eat, how secure we feel. But from heaven's point of view, money is one of the smallest things in life. It will all be gone one day. And yet, Jesus says, how we handle this small thing reveals and shapes our character for large things.

What are the large things? Jesus calls them "real wealth," or as older translations say, "true riches." These are the things that last forever: people, souls, the kingdom of God, character, eternal reward, responsibility in the age to come. Money is the training ground. If we can't be trusted with the passing wealth of this world, why would God trust us with the riches of the next?

### Money is a test

This means that every financial decision, however small, is part of our discipleship. The way you handle a few pounds of change in your pocket, the way you fill in an expense form, the way you treat a waiter, the way you respond when someone asks for help, are all part of God's training.

That is both sobering and encouraging. It is sobering because it means there is no such thing as a small compromise. If you will cheat on a little, you will cheat on a lot, given the chance. But it is encouraging because it means you don't have to be rich to be faithful. A widow with two coins and a millionaire with a fortune face the same test. Faithfulness with little is just as precious to God as faithfulness with much.

### The parable of the talents

Jesus told a story about a man who went on a long journey and entrusted his property to his servants. "He gave five bags of silver to one, two to another and one to a third, according to what each could manage" (Matthew 25:15). The first two servants put the money to work and doubled it. The third buried his in the ground.

When the master returned, he said to each of the first two exactly the same words:

> "Well done! You are a good and faithful servant. You proved faithful in small matters; I will put you in charge of great ones. Come in and share the joy of your master." (Matthew 25:21)

The servant with five bags and the servant with two received identical praise. The master didn't compare them with each other. He only asked whether each had been faithful with what he was given.

But the third servant, who had been given one bag, hid it. He said he was afraid of his master. The master called him wicked and lazy, and took away even what he had.

The lesson is plain. God gives different people different amounts, of money, talents, opportunities and time. He doesn't expect everyone to produce the same. He expects each of us to be faithful with what we have, and to put it to work for Him. The great sin in the parable is not losing money. It is doing nothing with it out of fear.

### Faithfulness in practice

What does faithfulness in little look like day to day?

**Honesty in small things.** Give back the extra change. Report the mistake in your favour. Pay for the thing that broke.

**Care in small things.** Look after what you have. Don't waste food, energy or possessions. Fix things instead of throwing them away when you can.

**Generosity in small things.** You don't have to wait until you are rich to be generous. Give what you can now. Many people say, "When I have more, I'll give more." But if you aren't generous with little, you probably won't be with much.

**Diligence in small things.** Keep track of your spending. Pay your bills on time. Keep your promises.

**Prayer in small things.** Bring even little money matters to God. Taylor learned faith by praying over a few shillings of salary. God cares about the details.

### Unjust wealth

One more thing. Just before His saying about faithfulness, Jesus told one of His strangest parables, about a dishonest manager who, about to be fired, used his master's money to make friends who would take him in. Jesus doesn't praise his dishonesty. He praises his shrewdness: the man used money, which he was about to lose anyway, to prepare for his future.

Then Jesus applies it: "use the wealth of this unjust world to make friends for yourselves, so that when it is gone, they will welcome you into the eternal homes" (Luke 16:9). In other words, use money, which you can't keep, for things you can keep: people and eternity. Use it to bless others, to support the gospel, to help the poor. One day the money will be gone. The people you helped, and the welcome they give you, will last forever.

### A voice from the past

Charles Spurgeon put this principle in two memorable sentences:

> "God's gold is not miser's money, but is minted to be traded with."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Morning by Morning*

> "Your possessions are never so safe as when you are willing to resign them, and you are never so rich as when you put all you have into the hand of the Lord."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Gleanings Among the Sheaves*

### Practice this week

Look for one "little thing" each day this week where you can be faithful with money: returning extra change, paying a small debt you had forgotten, giving a small gift, keeping careful track of a purchase. At the end of the week, reflect on how these small choices shaped you.

### For groups

- Why do you think Jesus calls money "the smallest matter"?
- What are the "real wealth" or "true riches" Jesus talks about in Luke 16:11?
- In the parable of the talents, why did the servants with five and two bags receive the same praise?
- Where is it most tempting for you to be unfaithful in little things?

## Money and the Mission

When Jesus sent out His first disciples to preach, He told them not to take money with them. "A worker has earned their pay" (Luke 10:7), He said. They were to rely on the hospitality of the people they served. From the beginning, the spread of the gospel depended on God's people opening their homes and their purses.

It has been that way ever since. Luke tells us that as Jesus travelled from town to town, a group of women went with Him, including Mary Magdalene and Joanna, the wife of Herod's household manager. "These women were using their own resources to provide for them" (Luke 8:3). The ministry of the Son of God was funded, in part, by these women's generosity.

### Partners in the gospel

Paul's letter to the Philippians is, among other things, a thank-you letter for money. The church at Philippi had supported Paul again and again, sending gifts while he was planting churches elsewhere and while he was in prison. Paul calls them partners in the gospel. He writes:

> "It is not the gift I am after. What I am after is the profit that keeps adding up in your account. Everything has been paid to me in full, and I have more than enough. Since Epaphroditus brought me what you sent, I have all I need. Your gift is a sweet-smelling offering, a sacrifice God accepts, one that pleases Him." (Philippians 4:17-18)

Notice two things. First, Paul says their giving was building up "profit" in their own account. When we give to support the gospel, we share in the fruit of the work. Every person who comes to faith through a missionary we support is, in a sense, credited to our account too.

Second, Paul calls their gift "a sweet-smelling offering, a sacrifice God accepts." Giving to support God's work is not just a financial transaction. It is an act of worship, as much as any song or prayer.

Then comes the promise: "And my God will fill every need you have, in keeping with His glorious wealth in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:19). This famous verse was not written to people who hoarded. It was written to people who had given generously to the mission of God.

### Supporting those who teach

The New Testament is clear that churches should support those who teach and preach. "Those who are being taught the word must share all their good things with the one who teaches them" (Galatians 6:6). Paul argued at length in 1 Corinthians 9 that those who preach the gospel should get their living from the gospel, even though he himself often chose not to use that right.

That means our giving should begin with our own church, where we are taught, fed and cared for. But it shouldn't stop there. The mission of God stretches to the ends of the earth.

### To the ends of the earth

Jesus' last command to His disciples was to go and make disciples of all nations. Some are called to go. Many more are called to send. Without senders, there are no goers.

Today there are still billions of people who have never heard a clear presentation of the gospel, many of them in places with few believers and little Christian witness. There are still languages without a Bible. There are believers in poorer countries working with very few resources. Our money can help.

Think of what giving can do: support a missionary family, pay for Bible translation, train pastors in places where there are few theological colleges, print and distribute Scripture, help a persecuted church survive. A small gift from a comfortable Christian can make an enormous difference elsewhere.

### Money without prayer

But money is not enough. E. M. Bounds, who wrote some of the greatest books on prayer, warned in *The Essentials of Prayer* that money given without prayer can do little:

> "Money is important, but money without prayer is powerless in the face of the darkness, the wretchedness and the sin in unchristianised lands."

And he noticed something about the church that is still true:

> "As poor as our giving is, our contributions of money exceed our offerings of prayer."
> E. M. Bounds, *Purpose in Prayer*

The mission of God is spiritual. It is advanced by the Holy Spirit through prayer, and money is a servant of that work, never a substitute for it. The best givers are also the best pray-ers. If you support a missionary, pray for them by name. If you give to a cause, pray for the people it serves.

### When times are hard

Here is a hard question. When money gets tight, what goes first? For many Christians, the answer is giving. As Martyn Lloyd-Jones observed, "When money becomes difficult, the first thing we economize on is our contribution to God's work."

That's understandable. But think about what it says. It says that God's work is the least important thing in our budget, the first to be cut. Some of the most inspiring giving in history has come from Christians in hard times who refused to cut God's share first. Paul said of the Macedonian churches, "Hardship has put them to a severe test, yet their joy has overflowed, and their deep poverty has spilled over into riches of generosity" (2 Corinthians 8:2).

Of course there are times when real hardship means our giving must shrink for a while. God knows that and doesn't condemn us. But it should be the last thing we cut, not the first.

### Investing in eternity

Jesus said, "pile up treasures for yourselves in heaven, where no moth or rust can ruin them and no thief can dig through and steal" (Matthew 6:20). Giving to the mission of God is one of the clearest ways to do that. Money given to spread the gospel is the only money you will ever see again. It is invested in people, and people are eternal.

Every gift we receive comes from God, and every gift we pass on can be given in fellowship with Him.

### A voice from the past

Andrew Murray, the South African pastor and writer on prayer, described what George Müller believed about money:

> "Money was a Divine stewardship, and... all money had therefore to be received and dispensed in direct fellowship with God Himself."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

### Practice this week

Find out about one missionary, mission organisation or church in another part of the world. Learn their names and their needs. Then begin to pray for them regularly, and if you can, give to support them. If you already support someone, write to them this week and tell them you are praying.

### For groups

- Read Philippians 4:14-19. What do you notice about Paul's attitude to the Philippians' gift?
- Why do you think Paul called giving "a sweet-smelling offering"?
- How do prayer and giving belong together?
- If money became tight for you, what would you cut first? What would you protect?

## Passing It On

Every parent teaches their children about money, whether they mean to or not. Children watch how we spend. They listen to how we talk about money: whether we worry about it, argue about it, complain about it, or thank God for it. They notice whether we give, and how cheerfully. By the time they are adults, many of their money habits will have been formed at home.

That's a sobering thought. It is also a great opportunity.

### Teach them at home

Moses told Israel to make God's commands part of everyday family life:

> "Keep these words that I am commanding you today close to your heart. Teach them carefully to your children, and speak of them as you sit in your house and as you go on your way, as you lie down and as you rise." (Deuteronomy 6:6-7)

Notice where the teaching happens: at home, on the road, at bedtime, at breakfast. Not mainly in formal lessons, but in the ordinary rhythms of life. That's how children learn about money too. They learn in the supermarket, in the car, when the bills come in, when the offering plate goes round.

And notice the order: "Keep these words that I am commanding you today close to your heart" first, then "Teach them carefully to your children." You can't pass on what you don't have. The best thing you can do to teach your children about money is to live it yourself.

> "Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not leave it." (Proverbs 22:6)

### What to teach

Here are some lessons every child needs to learn about money.

**God owns everything.** Start with Psalm 24:1. Even a small child can understand that the world belongs to God and that He has given us what we have.

**Work is good.** Let children earn money by doing jobs around the house or neighbourhood. Help them feel the dignity and satisfaction of earning.

**Give first.** When a child receives money, help them set aside a portion for God first. Many families use three jars or envelopes: Give, Save, Spend. Let children choose where their giving goes, and let them see the difference it makes.

**Save for something.** Help them save up for something they want, so they learn patience and the joy of waiting.

**Spend wisely.** Let them make choices, and sometimes mistakes, with small amounts. A child who wastes ten pounds on something that breaks the next day learns a lesson that will save them much more later.

**Contentment.** Talk about adverts and how they try to make us want things. Practise gratitude together. Help them see how much they already have.

**Debt is dangerous.** Older children and teenagers need to understand how interest works and how quickly debt can grow. Many young adults fall into debt simply because nobody ever explained it to them.

**Care for the poor.** Involve children in serving and giving to people in need. Let them see poverty, gently and appropriately, and see your compassion.

### Model it

Children learn more from what we do than from what we say. If you tell your children to give but they never see you give, they will learn that giving is something you talk about, not something you do. If you tell them not to worry but they hear you worrying about money every night, they will learn to worry.

Consider letting your children see your giving. That might seem to go against Jesus' teaching about secrecy, but there is a difference between showing off to the world and training your children. Explain to them why you give, how you decide how much, and what it means to you. Let them see you pray about money. Let them see you choose not to buy something because it isn't in the plan.

And if your own money habits have been poor, it isn't too late. Your children can learn a great deal from watching you change. Tell them honestly: "I haven't always handled money well, but I'm learning to do it God's way." That might be the most powerful lesson of all.

### Wesley's advice

John Wesley spoke directly to parents in his sermons. His advice was strong, but wise:

> "Instil into them, as early as possible, a fear and contempt of pomp and grandeur; an abhorrence and dread of the love of money; and a deep conviction; that riches cannot give happiness."

Notice what he wants children to learn: not fear of money, but fear of the love of money. Not contempt for work, but contempt for showing off. And a deep conviction that riches can't make you happy.

### Leave an inheritance

"A good person leaves an inheritance to their grandchildren" (Proverbs 13:22). For some families, that will include money or property, and it is wise to plan for it carefully, with a will and with prayer. Too much inheritance, received too early, can harm a child: "An inheritance grabbed too early will not be blessed in the end" (Proverbs 20:21). Many wise Christian parents leave a good share of their estate to God's work, as a final act of stewardship.

But the greatest inheritance is not money. It is faith. Paul reminded Timothy of the sincere faith that had lived first in his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice, and now in him. That is the inheritance that matters most. A child who learns to trust God, to work hard, to give generously and to be content has been left something worth far more than any estate.

### Not only parents

This chapter isn't just for parents. Grandparents, aunts and uncles, godparents, youth leaders, teachers and older friends can all help young people learn to handle money God's way. The church is a family, and every older believer has something to pass on to someone younger.

### A voice from the past

Susanna Wesley gave birth to nineteen children, and nine of them died as babies. She raised the others in a poor country rectory, with debts that never seemed to go away and a fire that burned the house down. She knew money trouble from the inside, and she taught her children about it. When her son John was a young student at Oxford, she wrote to him:

> "I wish you would save all the money you can conveniently spare, not to spend on a visit, but for a wiser and better purpose — to pay debts, and make yourself easy."
> Susanna Wesley, in a letter to her son John

Out of that struggling home came John and Charles Wesley, who helped change a nation. John grew up to be the man who preached "Give all you can," and he gave away a fortune.

### Practice this week

If you have children at home, introduce the Give, Save, Spend jars or envelopes this week, and talk about why you give. If you don't, think of one young person you could encourage or mentor in handling money, and look for a natural opportunity to share something you have learned.

### For groups

- What did you learn about money from your parents, for good or bad?
- Which lessons in this chapter do you most wish you had learned as a child?
- How can parents show their children their giving without breaking Jesus' teaching about giving secretly?
- What is the most valuable inheritance you could leave the next generation?

## Well Done

One day every one of us will stand before God and give an account of our lives. The Bible says it plainly:

> "For all of us must be laid open before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive what matches the things done in the body, whether good or worthless." (2 Corinthians 5:10)

"So each of us will have to give an account of ourselves" (Romans 14:12).

For a Christian, this is not a judgment about whether we will be saved. That was settled at the cross. Paul says that "those who are in Christ Jesus now face no sentence of condemnation" (Romans 8:1). But it is a judgment of our works, our faithfulness, the way we used what God entrusted to us. And that includes our money.

### Give an account

Jesus' parable of the unjust manager begins with these words from the master: "What is this that I keep hearing about you? Hand in a report on how you have managed my affairs, because you cannot be my manager any longer" (Luke 16:2).

That day is coming for every steward. John Wesley preached a sermon on exactly this, called "The Good Steward." He reminded his hearers that our time as stewards will end, and then we will give an account. Our bodies, our minds, our time, our talents, our money: all of it was lent to us by God, and we will report on how we used it.

That should not terrify a Christian. But it should sober us. It means our choices matter. It means that what we do with money today has eternal consequences.

### The joy of the master

Remember the words of the master in the parable of the talents: "Well done! You are a good and faithful servant. You proved faithful in small matters; I will put you in charge of great ones. Come in and share the joy of your master" (Matthew 25:21).

Imagine hearing that from Jesus. Imagine the One who gave everything for you looking at your life, including the way you handled your money, and saying, "Well done." All the small acts of faithfulness: the tithes given when it was hard, the honest invoices, the secret gifts, the debts paid off, the poor neighbour helped, the missionary supported, the child taught to give. All of it seen, all of it remembered, all of it rewarded.

"For God is not so unfair as to forget your work and the love you have shown for His name by serving His holy people, and by serving them still" (Hebrews 6:10).

### Treasure in heaven

Jesus urged us to "pile up treasures for yourselves in heaven" (Matthew 6:20). What does that mean? It means that what we give away in this life, for the sake of Christ and His kingdom, isn't lost. It is sent ahead. Every gift to the poor, every offering to God's work, every sacrifice for the gospel, is stored up where moths and rust and thieves can't touch it.

C. T. Studd, the English cricketer who gave away a fortune to become a missionary, once wrote a little verse that many people have repeated since: "Only one life, 'twill soon be past, only what's done for Christ will last." It's true of our money too. Only what is given for Christ will last.

### Not earned, but rewarded

It is important to be clear here. We are not saved by our giving. Salvation is a free gift. You can't buy your way into heaven, and you don't need to. Jesus paid it all.

But the Bible does teach that God rewards His children for faithfulness, and that there are degrees of reward. Jesus spoke of some who would be put in charge of ten cities and others of five. Paul spoke of works that survive the fire and works that are burned up. We don't fully understand what these rewards will be. But we know that God is generous, and that He delights to reward His servants far beyond what they deserve.

That changes how we think about money. Every pound or dollar that passes through our hands is an opportunity to lay up treasure that will last forever.

### Living with the end in view

Here is one way to make decisions about money. Before you spend a large amount, or decide what to give, or take on a debt, ask yourself: "How will this look on the day I give an account?" Not with fear, but with honesty.

That question has a way of clearing the fog. It shows us what really matters. It makes the new car or the bigger house look less important, and the struggling family or the missionary or the church plant look more important. It helps us to live, as Paul said, so that we "take hold of the life that is truly life."

### Grace to the end

If you have read this far and feel that you have been a poor steward, don't despair. Every one of us has failed with money in some way. The thief on the cross had nothing to give and no time to give it, and Jesus welcomed him into paradise. God's grace is bigger than our failures.

Start where you are. Repent of what needs repenting of. Receive His forgiveness. Then, from today, begin to be faithful with what He has given you. It is never too late to start laying up treasure in heaven.

### A voice from the past

In his sermon "On Riches," John Wesley held out this hope for every believer:

> "Be a good steward of the manifold gifts of God; that when you are called to give an account of your stewardship, he may say, 'Well done, good and faithful servant, enter thou into the joy of thy Lord!'"

And Thomas à Kempis, centuries earlier, pointed beyond all gifts to the Giver:

> "If you have wealth, do not glory in it, nor in friends because they are powerful, but in God Who gives all things and Who desires above all to give Himself."
> Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*

### Practice this week

Take an hour this week to think and pray about this question: "If I gave an account of my stewardship today, what would I be glad of? What would I wish I had done differently?" Write down one change you want to make, and make it.

### For groups

- How do you feel about the idea of giving an account of your money to God? Why?
- What is the difference between being saved by works and being rewarded for works?
- Read Matthew 25:21. What would it mean to you to hear those words?
- What is one decision you face right now that would look different "with the end in view"?

## Conclusion: A Rule of Life for Money

We began with a door. Money is the last private room in the house, the place where many Christians quietly keep Jesus out. This book has been an invitation to open that door.

We have seen that everything belongs to God and that we are His managers. We have looked at the heart behind the wallet: covetousness, worry, contentment and the danger of riches. We have thought about everyday money: honest work, planning, debt and providing for a family. We have explored generosity: firstfruits, cheerful giving, the poor, and giving all we can. And we have looked ahead, to faithfulness in little, the mission of God, the next generation, and the day we hear "Well done."

Now what?

### A rule of life

In the history of the church, many Christians have found it helpful to write down a "rule of life," a simple set of commitments that shape how they live. It is not a set of laws to earn God's favour. It is more like a trellis that helps a plant grow in the right direction.

Here is a suggested rule of life for money, built on John Wesley's three rules and the teaching of this book. Read it slowly. Change it to fit your own situation. Then pray over it, and consider writing your own version and putting it somewhere you will see it.

**I will remember that it all belongs to God.**
Everything I have is His. I am a manager, not an owner.

**I will gain all I can, honestly.**
I will work hard, as for the Lord. I will deal fairly. I will not cheat, lie or harm others to make money.

**I will give first.**
Before I spend anything else, I will give a planned proportion of my income to God, starting with my local church, and I will grow that proportion as God allows.

**I will save all I can.**
I will live simply and avoid waste. I will plan my spending, keep some money aside, and know the state of my flocks.

**I will stay free.**
I will avoid unnecessary debt, repay what I owe, and work toward owing nothing but love.

**I will provide for my own.**
I will care for my family's real needs, including my ageing parents, and plan wisely for the future.

**I will give all I can.**
I will set a limit on my lifestyle, so that when my income grows, my giving grows. I will give cheerfully, regularly and quietly, to the poor and to the mission of God.

**I will guard my heart.**
I will fight covetousness with gratitude and generosity. I will learn contentment. I will bring every money worry to God in prayer.

**I will pass it on.**
I will teach the next generation, by word and example, to handle money God's way.

**I will live with the end in view.**
I will remember that I will give an account, and I will seek to hear "Well done."

### Small steps

Don't try to do all of this at once. Pick one or two steps, perhaps the ones God has pressed on your heart while reading. Take them this month. Then take another. Faithfulness grows one small step at a time.

And remember that you are not alone. Talk to your spouse, your friends, your small group or your pastor. Ask someone to pray with you and hold you accountable. Money is the private room, but it doesn't have to stay private.

### Treasure

Jesus said, "Your heart will always be where your treasure is" (Matthew 6:21). We began with that verse, and we end with it.

Where is your treasure? If it is in your bank account, your heart will follow it there, and one day your heart will be left with nothing. But if your treasure is in Christ, then your heart is safe. Nothing can take it away.

Jesus once told a story about a man who found treasure hidden in a field. In his joy he went and sold everything he had and bought that field. He didn't feel he was making a sacrifice. He knew he had found something worth more than everything else.

That is what it means to know Christ. He is the treasure. When you have found Him, the rest falls into place. You can hold money loosely, give it freely, manage it faithfully and enjoy it gratefully, because your heart is already full.

May God give you grace to be faithful with little, and to hear one day the words every disciple longs to hear: "Well done! You are a good and faithful servant... Come in and share the joy of your master."

## Appendix 2: A One-Page Money Plan

This is a simple monthly plan. You don't need special software. A sheet of paper, a pencil and an honest hour are enough. If you are married, do it together.

### Step 1: What comes in

Write down your total income for a typical month, after tax. Include wages, benefits, pensions and any other regular income. If your income varies, use a careful average, or plan on your lowest typical month.

### Step 2: What goes to God first

Decide the percentage of your income you will give. Write down the amount. This goes first, before anything else.

### Step 3: What you need to live

List your essential monthly costs:

- Housing (rent or mortgage)
- Utilities (energy, water, phone, internet)
- Food and household supplies
- Transport
- Insurance
- Minimum debt payments
- Health, childcare and education costs
- Other essentials

### Step 4: Saving

Decide how much you will put aside each month. A good first goal is a small emergency fund. Then build toward three to six months of essential costs, and then save for future needs such as a home, old age or your children's needs.

### Step 5: Freedom from debt

If you have debts beyond your mortgage, list them here, smallest to largest, with their interest rates. Decide how much extra you will pay each month, and which debt you will pay off first.

### Step 6: Everything else

Whatever is left can be spent on other things: clothing, gifts, eating out, hobbies, holidays. Decide amounts for each in advance.

### Step 7: Check the balance

Add up Steps 2 to 6. The total should equal Step 1. If it is more, you are spending more than you earn: go back and cut until it balances. If it is less, decide where the extra goes, perhaps to giving, saving or paying off debt faster.

### Step 8: Review

At the end of the month, compare what you planned with what actually happened. Adjust the plan for next month. It usually takes three or four months to get a plan that works. Don't give up.

### A prayer for your plan

Lord, all of this is Yours. Thank You for what You have given me. Help me to give first, to live wisely, to stay free, to provide for those I love, and to give all I can. Make me faithful with little, for Jesus' sake. Amen.

## Appendix 3: A Ten-Week Group Guide

This book was written to be read with others. Money is easier to talk about than most people think, once someone goes first. Here is a simple plan for a group meeting once a week for ten weeks.

### Before you begin

**Keep it safe.** Agree together that what is shared in the group stays in the group. Nobody should ever be pressured to share figures, such as their income or how much they give. Talk about attitudes and decisions, not amounts, unless people choose otherwise.

**Keep it honest.** Leaders should be willing to go first and share their own struggles. If the leader pretends to have money all worked out, nobody else will be honest either.

**Keep it gracious.** Some people in the group may be in debt or under real financial strain. Others may be wealthy and quietly uncomfortable. Make room for both. The goal is not guilt, but grace and growth.

### Each week

Ask everyone to read the two chapters before the meeting. When you meet:

- **Open** with prayer and a quick check-in: How did last week's "Practice this week" go?
- **Read** the key verse from each chapter aloud.
- **Discuss** the "For groups" questions. You don't have to cover them all. Choose the ones that spark the most conversation.
- **Commit** to one "Practice this week" for the coming week, and pray for one another by name.

### The plan

- **Week 1.** Introduction and chapter 1: It All Belongs to God.
- **Week 2.** Chapters 2 and 3: Made to Work, Called to Keep; Two Masters.
- **Week 3.** Chapters 4 and 5: The Riches of Christ; Covetousness, the Respectable Sin.
- **Week 4.** Chapters 6 and 7: The Learned Secret of Contentment; Daily Bread, Not Daily Worry.
- **Week 5.** Chapters 8 and 9: The Danger of Riches; Gain All You Can, Honestly.
- **Week 6.** Chapters 10 and 11: Count the Cost; The Borrower's Chains. (You may want to give out copies of the money plan in Appendix 2 this week.)
- **Week 7.** Chapters 12 and 13: Provide for Your Own; Firstfruits.
- **Week 8.** Chapters 14 and 15: Cheerful, Regular, Quiet Giving; Lending to the Lord. (Consider doing a practical act of service together for people in need.)
- **Week 9.** Chapters 16 and 17: Give All You Can; Faithful in Little.
- **Week 10.** Chapters 18, 19 and 20, and the Conclusion. Spend time this last week writing personal rules of life for money, and praying over them together.

### For leaders

You don't need to be a financial expert to lead this group. You only need to be honest, to love the people in your group, and to point them to Jesus. If someone shares a serious money problem, such as heavy debt, help them find good advice, and keep praying for them after the group ends.

Above all, keep coming back to grace. We don't handle money well to earn God's love. We handle it well because we already have His love, and because everything we have is His.
