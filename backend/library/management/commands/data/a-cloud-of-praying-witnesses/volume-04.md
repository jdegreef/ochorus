## Introduction: Where We Are in the Story

> "The world did not deserve such people. They wandered over deserts and mountains, and sheltered in caves and in holes in the earth." (Hebrews 11:38)

The New Testament ends, but the story does not.

By about the year 100, the apostles had died. Peter and Paul had given their lives in Rome. John, the last of them, had died an old man, the story is told, in Ephesus. The people who had walked with Jesus were gone. Their letters were still being read aloud in the churches, and their students were still alive. But from now on the church would have to pray without anyone who had seen the Lord with their own eyes.

How would it do that? This book is about the answer.

### Where we are in the series

This is the fourth volume of *A Cloud of Praying Witnesses*, a series that follows the people of God at prayer through the whole story of the church. The name comes from Hebrews 12:1, which pictures all the faithful who went before us as "so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us."

Volume 1, *The Prayers Jesus Learned*, listened to the prayers of the Old Testament. Volume 2, *Jesus, Teacher of Prayer*, sat with the disciples as Jesus taught them to pray. Volume 3, *Devoted to Prayer*, followed the apostles and the first church from the upper room to the golden bowls of Revelation.

This volume is the first to step outside the pages of the Bible. Its witnesses are the children and grandchildren of the church in Acts, and the generations after them, up to about the year 500. Many of them knew one another, or knew someone who had known an apostle. Polycarp, whom we meet in chapter 4, had learned from John himself, his student Irenaeus remembered.

### The world of these witnesses

For most of these years, the church lived inside the Roman Empire, and for much of that time being a Christian was against the law. Persecution came and went. In some places and years believers lived in peace; in others they were arrested, tortured and killed. In the year 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone in the empire to offer sacrifice to the gods, and the persecution under Diocletian, which began in 303, was the worst of all.

The Greek word for "witness" is *martys*. The church began to use it for those who gave their lives rather than deny Christ, and so it gave us the word "martyr." The first half of this book belongs to them. They prayed for the rulers who killed them. They prayed on the road to the lions, in prison cells, and at the stake.

Then, early in the 300s, everything changed. The emperor Constantine turned to the Christian faith, and by 313 the persecution had ended across most of the empire. Within a few generations the church went from being hunted to being honored. That was a great gift, but it brought a new danger: it became easy to be a Christian. And so thousands of men and women went out into the deserts of Egypt, Palestine and Syria, to pray and to fight against sin with all their strength. The second half of this book belongs to them, and to the bishops, mothers, poets and missionaries who prayed in the great cities and at the edges of the known world.

These witnesses come from many lands: Syria and Asia Minor (today's Turkey), Egypt and North Africa, Italy, Gaul (today's France), Mesopotamia, Britain and Ireland. They spoke Greek, Latin, Syriac and Coptic. They are honored today by Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant Christians alike. Where later churches came to differ about some of the things they did, we will describe their practice simply, and listen for what all of us can learn about prayer.

They were not perfect people, and we will not pretend they were. But they were faithful, many of them to death, and they knew how to pray.

### What we will see

This volume has eighteen chapters, in three movements:

- **The age of the martyrs** (chapters 1 to 8). We learn to pray three times a day from the *Didache*, one of the oldest Christian writings outside the New Testament. We hear Clement of Rome pray for rulers who persecute, walk with Ignatius on the road to the lions, and stand with old Polycarp at the stake. We sit with Perpetua and Felicity in their prison cell, and learn from three teachers of prayer: Tertullian and Cyprian of Carthage, and Origen of Alexandria.
- **Prayer in the desert** (chapters 9 to 13). We follow Antony into the desert of Egypt and listen to the mothers and fathers who came after him. John Cassian teaches us one short verse to pray all day long. Macrina turns her family's home into a house of prayer, and Ephrem teaches the church to pray in song.
- **Teachers, mothers and missionaries** (chapters 14 to 18). John Chrysostom calls prayer the light of the soul. Monica weeps and prays for her son for years, and that son, Augustine, discovers that our hearts are restless until they rest in God. Patrick learns to pray as a slave in Ireland and goes back there to preach. And the volume ends with one of the simplest prayers ever prayed: "Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy."

### How each chapter works

Every chapter in this series has the same shape:

- **A key verse** opens the chapter, the Scripture it rests on.
- **A story** sets the scene.
- **The teaching** looks at what the story shows us about prayer.
- **Voices from the past** quote men and women of prayer, often the witnesses themselves, word for word from their own books in the Ochorus library.
- **Three key insights** sum up what the chapter teaches.
- **For us today** brings the lesson into ordinary life, wherever in the world you live.
- **Try this** gives one simple practice for the coming week.
- **A closing prayer** and **Questions for reflection** help you respond, alone or with others.
- **Read more on Ochorus** points you to classic books where you can go deeper.

At the end of the book, two appendices gather all the key insights by theme and list every Scripture quoted, in Bible order, with the chapters where it appears.

### A word about these stories

The stories in the first three volumes came from the Bible. The stories in this volume come from history: from letters, diaries, sermons and the lives that friends wrote soon after a person died. Most of these sources are very old and very good. Some details were added later by people who loved these witnesses and wanted to honor them. In each chapter we tell you where the story comes from, and when a detail is only a tradition, we say so.

### How to read this book

One chapter a day will carry you through the book in eighteen days. With a small group, one or two chapters a week works well.

If you live where it is costly to follow Jesus, this book is especially for you. The believers in these pages knew prison, loss and danger, and they found that God was faithful in all of it. If you live where faith is easy, they have something to teach you too: how to keep praying when nothing forces you to. Either way, they are part of the great cloud of witnesses around you. Listen to them, and learn to pray.

## Three Times a Day

> "At evening, at morning and at noon I pour out my troubles and groan aloud, and He hears me." (Psalm 55:17)

By about the year AD 100, the apostles were gone.

James, the brother of John, had been put to death by King Herod in Jerusalem (Acts 12:2). An old tradition says that Peter and Paul died in Rome under the emperor Nero. John, the last of them, lived to be very old. Irenaeus, who wrote about eighty years later, said that John stayed with the church in Ephesus "until the time of Trajan," the emperor who began to rule in AD 98. After that, no one was left who had walked with Jesus by the Sea of Galilee.

The temple in Jerusalem was gone too. The Roman armies had burned it in AD 70. In Volume 3 we watched Peter and John climb its steps for the afternoon prayer at three o'clock. Now there were no steps to climb and no hour announced from the temple courts.

And yet the church was growing, in Syria and Egypt, Asia Minor and Greece, Rome and North Africa. Believers met in houses. Many had grown up worshiping idols and had never heard the Psalms. They needed to be taught how a Christian lives, and how a Christian prays.

So someone wrote a short handbook, simple enough to read aloud to a new believer. It taught the "two ways," the way of life and the way of death. It explained baptism, fasting and the Lord's Supper. And in its eighth chapter, it gave a new believer a rhythm for every day. It wrote out the Lord's Prayer, word by word, and then it said:

"Thrice in the day thus pray."

"Thrice" is an old English word for "three times." Three times every day, at home or at work, in the field or at the market, the new Christian was to stop and pray the prayer Jesus taught.

### What we know, and what we do not

This little book is called the *Didache* (say it DID-a-kay), a Greek word that means "teaching." In the old manuscript its full title reads, in one common English translation, "The Lord's Teaching Through the Twelve Apostles to the Nations." It is one of the oldest Christian writings we have outside the New Testament.

We should be honest about how much we do not know.

We do not know **who wrote it**. The title speaks of the twelve apostles, but almost no one thinks the twelve wrote it themselves. It claims to pass on what they taught, much as a teacher today might say, "This is what the church has always believed."

We do not know **exactly when** it was written. Some scholars place it before AD 70. Many think it was written near the end of the first century. Others place it in the early second century. Many also think it grew in stages, with later hands adding to an older core.

We do not know **where** it was written. Many think of Syria, perhaps near Antioch. Others suggest Egypt. The truth is that we cannot be sure.

What we do know is that the early church valued it. Eusebius, the church historian who wrote in the early 300s, knew it by name, though he did not count it as Scripture:

> "Among the rejected writings must be reckoned also the Acts of Paul, and the so-called Shepherd, and the Apocalypse of Peter, and in addition to these the extant epistle of Barnabas, and the so-called Teachings of the Apostles"
> Eusebius, *The Church History*

After that, the book was nearly lost. For centuries scholars knew its name but not its words. Then in 1873 a Greek Orthodox scholar, Philotheos Bryennios, who later became a bishop, found a copy in a library in Constantinople, the city we call Istanbul. It was in a small handwritten book that a scribe named Leo had finished in the year 1056. Bryennios published the *Didache* ten years later, in 1883. A voice from the first Christians had come back.

### How they prayed

Here is what the *Didache* says about prayer, in chapter 8. It tells believers not to fast on the same days as "the hypocrites," but to fast on Wednesdays and Fridays. Then it says not to pray like the hypocrites either, "but as the Lord commanded in His Gospel." It writes out the Lord's Prayer, almost word for word as it stands in Matthew 6. At the end it adds words of praise: "for Yours is the power and the glory forever." In Volume 2 we saw that this is one of the earliest places where those words of praise appear. Then comes the simple command: three times a day, pray like this.

The idea was not new. God's people had long prayed at set times. David wrote our key verse: "At evening, at morning and at noon." Daniel in Babylon "got down on his knees and prayed" three times a day, "just as he had always done" (Daniel 6:10). The first Christians kept this old rhythm. But they put the prayer of Jesus at its center.

Notice what the *Didache* does not say. It does not name the hours or ask for a special building. A slave could keep this rhythm, and so could a mother with small children. A person who could not read could learn the prayer by heart.

Notice too that the prayer is in the plural: "Our Father," "Give us," "Forgive us." In chapter 8, Cyprian of Carthage will show us how much that little word "our" means. Elsewhere in the *Didache*, in a prayer of thanks at the Lord's Supper, the church prays, "Remember, Lord, Your Church, to deliver it from all evil and to make it perfect in Your love." Those first Christians were scattered and small, but in prayer they were one family.

Centuries later, the English Puritan Thomas Watson wrote a whole book on the Lord's Prayer. He explained why this one prayer can guide all the others:

> "The ten commandments are the rule of our life, the creed is the sum of our faith, and the Lord's prayer is the pattern of our prayer."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

Watson added that we are not tied only to these words. Jesus said, "This is how you should pray" (Matthew 6:9). The prayer is a pattern, not a cage. When our prayers wander, it brings us back to what matters most: God's name, God's kingdom, God's will, and our daily need.

The *Didache* has one more line about prayer that we should not miss. In its very first chapter, it gives the teaching of Jesus to new believers: "Bless those who curse you, and pray for your enemies, and fast for those who persecute you." That was not an easy word in a time when Christians could be arrested for their faith. In the chapters ahead we will meet believers who lived it.

### Three key insights

1. **A simple rhythm of prayer can carry a whole life.** The *Didache* did not give new believers a long program. It gave them one prayer and three times a day. Small, steady habits of prayer can hold us up for years, through good times and hard ones.
2. **The Lord's Prayer is a pattern to live inside, not a formula to rush through.** The first Christians prayed the words Jesus gave them, and those words shaped everything else they asked. When we pray the Lord's Prayer slowly, we learn what to care about most.
3. **Set times of prayer join us to God's people across the ages.** David prayed evening, morning and noon, and Daniel knelt three times a day. The first Christians kept that old rhythm and put the prayer of Jesus at its center. When we keep it, we pray alongside them.

### For us today

The world of the *Didache* was not so different from ours. Many believers were poor. Some were slaves. Some lived in homes where no one else believed. In many countries today, believers live in the same way.

Three times a day is possible for almost anyone. A factory worker in Dhaka can pray the Lord's Prayer before the first shift, at the noon meal and on the walk home. A grandmother in Malawi can pray it at sunrise, at midday and when the lamp is lit. A believer in a country where Christians cannot meet openly can pray it silently, and no one need know.

You may already pray the Lord's Prayer in church each week. The *Didache* invites us to make it part of every day. Some of us fear that praying the same words again and again will make our prayer empty. But the danger is not in the words. It is in a heart that is not paying attention. The same words, prayed slowly and honestly, can become deeper every time we pray them. We will come back to this question in chapter 18.

### Try this

For one week, follow the rhythm of the *Didache*.

- **Choose three times.** Pick a morning, a midday and an evening moment that fit your day. Tie each one to something you already do, such as waking, eating, or turning off the light.
- **Pray the Lord's Prayer slowly.** At each time, pray it once, pausing after each line. Let one line stay with you until the next time of prayer.
- **Remember the church.** Before you finish, pray one sentence for the whole church: "Remember, Lord, Your Church." Name one country where believers are suffering.
- **Pray for one enemy.** Once a day, as the *Didache* teaches, pray a blessing on someone who has hurt you.

### A closing prayer

Our Father in heaven, thank You for the believers who came before us, who prayed the words of Your Son three times a day when the apostles were gone and the temple had fallen. Teach me to pray as they prayed. Give me a steady rhythm of prayer, morning, noon and evening. When I pray, "Our Father," remind me that I belong to a family in every nation. Remember Your Church, Lord. Deliver it from all evil and make it perfect in Your love. Help me to bless those who curse me and to pray for my enemies. For Yours is the kingdom, the power and the glory forever. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- The *Didache* gave new believers a simple rhythm instead of a long list of rules. Why do you think this was wise?
- Which line of the Lord's Prayer is hardest for you to pray honestly? Why?
- What would change in your week if you prayed the Lord's Prayer three times a day?

## A Prayer for Rulers Who Persecute

> "First of all, then, I urge that requests, prayers, intercessions and thanks be offered for everyone: for kings and for all who hold high office, so that we may live peaceful and quiet lives, fully devoted to God and worthy of respect." (1 Timothy 2:1-2)

The church in Rome had good reason to fear the emperor.

About thirty years earlier, in AD 64, a great fire had burned much of the city, and the emperor Nero had blamed the Christians. Many believers were arrested and killed in cruel ways. The church in Rome still remembered them. When its leaders later wrote a letter, they spoke of Peter and Paul, who had "suffered martyrdom," and of "a great multitude of the elect" who had died with them.

Now it was about AD 96. A new letter had to be written, and it was not an easy one. News had come from the church in Corinth, in Greece. Some members there had risen up against their elders and pushed them out of office. The church was divided. So the believers in Rome sat down to write to their brothers and sisters in Corinth, urging them to make peace.

The letter begins with an apology. The Romans say they have been slow to write because of "the sudden and successive calamitous events which have happened to ourselves." They do not say what those events were. Many scholars think they mean a time of trouble for Christians under the emperor Domitian, who ruled until AD 96. Others think the words point to something smaller. We cannot be sure. But we know this was a church that had buried martyrs, and that knew it could suffer again.

The letter is long. It calls the Corinthians back to humility, love and order. Then, near the end, it stops teaching and begins to pray, for three whole chapters. And in that prayer, from a church that had seen its leaders killed by the government, comes this:

"To our rulers and governors on the earth... to them, Lord, give health, peace, concord, stability, that they may exercise the authority given to them without offense."

Three men named in its last lines, Claudius Ephebus, Valerius Bito and Fortunatus, carried the letter to Corinth. There the believers heard their brothers in Rome asking God to bless the rulers of Rome.

### Who he was

This letter is usually called *First Clement*. But the name Clement does not appear anywhere in it. It is written from "the Church of God which sojourns at Rome" to the church at Corinth.

Very early, though, Christians said that a leader named Clement wrote it. Eusebius, the church historian, tells us that Clement was the third bishop of Rome after the apostles, following Linus and Anencletus. Eusebius also thought he was the Clement whom Paul calls one of his "fellow workers" (Philippians 4:3), though we cannot know that for sure. Of the letter itself, Eusebius wrote:

> "There is extant an epistle of this Clement which is acknowledged to be genuine and is of considerable length and of remarkable merit. He wrote it in the name of the church of Rome to the church of Corinth, when a sedition had arisen in the latter church."
> Eusebius, *The Church History*

"Sedition" means a rebellion. Eusebius adds that the letter was read aloud in "a great many churches" in his own day.

One more thing a reader should know. For centuries the great prayer near the end of the letter was lost, because the oldest copy then known, in a famous Bible manuscript called the Codex Alexandrinus, had a leaf missing just there. The older English translation in the Ochorus library was made from that copy, so it lacks the prayer. Then, in 1875, Philotheos Bryennios, the scholar who later gave the world the *Didache* (chapter 1), published a full copy of Clement's letter from the same manuscript of 1056. We quote the prayer here from John Keith's English translation of that newly found text, published in 1896.

### What they prayed

In Volume 3 we looked at Paul's command to pray "for kings and for all who hold high office." Here we can hear one of the first churches actually doing it. Three things stand out.

**First, the prayer begins with God, not with the rulers.** Before it mentions any emperor, it praises the God "who layest low the insolence of the haughty," who "settest the low on high and bringest low the exalted." The church in Rome was small and weak, and the emperor was the most powerful man in the world. But in prayer they remembered who was truly in charge. Only a church that knows God is King can pray for earthly kings without fear and without flattery.

**Second, it prays first for those who suffer.** Before the rulers, the prayer turns to the weakest people in the church: "Those of us in affliction save, on the lowly take pity; the fallen raise." It asks God to "fill the hungry" and to "redeem those of us in bonds," that is, those in prison. It asks God to "deliver us from those who hate us wrongfully." This is an honest prayer. It does not pretend that all is well. It names the danger.

**Third, it asks for the good of the rulers themselves.** It does not ask God to make the emperor kind to Christians only. It asks God to give rulers "health, peace, concord, stability." "Concord" means agreement and harmony. It also asks God to "direct their counsel according to that which is good and well-pleasing in Your sight." The church believed what Proverbs says: "A king's heart is like streams of water in the hand of the LORD, who directs it wherever He pleases" (Proverbs 21:1). So they asked God to guide the minds of the very men who might one day order their arrest.

Clement's letter ends with a blessing that is also a prayer. Here it is in the library's translation:

> "May God, who seeth all things, and who is the Ruler of all spirits and the Lord of all flesh, who chose our Lord Jesus Christ and us through Him to be a peculiar people, grant to every soul that calleth upon His glorious and holy Name, faith, fear, peace, patience, long-suffering, self-control, purity, and sobriety"
> Clement of Rome, *The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians*

"A peculiar people" is an old way of saying "a people God has chosen as His own."

This way of praying did not stop with Clement. Some years later, Polycarp of Smyrna, whom we will meet in chapter 4, wrote to the church in Philippi:

> "Pray also for kings, and potentates, and princes, and for those that persecute and hate you, and for the enemies of the cross, that your fruit may be manifest to all, and that ye may be perfect in Him."
> Polycarp, *The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp*

### Three key insights

1. **Praise of God's greatness frees us to pray for the powerful without fear.** Clement's church began with the God who lifts up the lowly and brings down the proud. When we remember who really rules, we can pray for rulers calmly, neither flattering them nor fearing them.
2. **Honest prayer names the suffering before it asks for anything else.** The prayer from Rome speaks of the hungry, the fallen and those in prison, and it asks to be delivered from those who hate without cause. God does not ask us to hide our pain when we pray for those in power.
3. **We can ask God to guide the hearts of rulers who are against us.** The church prayed that God would direct the counsel of the emperor's men. No ruler's heart is beyond the reach of God's hand, or of our prayers.

### For us today

Some readers of this book live where the government watches the church and meetings must be held quietly. For them, the prayer from Rome is a gift. It shows that praying for rulers is not weakness or surrender. It is faith. The church in Rome did not stop telling the truth about its suffering, and it did not stop praying for the men who caused it.

Other readers live where leaders are chosen by vote, and where it is easy to speak against them in anger. Clement's prayer asks something of us too. Do we pray for the health and wisdom of leaders we did not choose? Do we ask God to guide the counsel of the officials whose decisions we dislike?

Jesus said, "Love your enemies, and pray for the people who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). The first Christians believed Him and obeyed, in a city where Christians had been burned.

### Try this

This week, pray in the order of the prayer from Rome.

- **Begin with God.** Read Psalm 113 or 1 Samuel 2:1-10, and praise the God who lifts up the poor and brings down the proud.
- **Pray for those who suffer.** Name the hungry, the sick, the fallen and the imprisoned that you know of, near and far.
- **Pray for your rulers by name.** Ask God to give them health, peace and wisdom, and to direct their counsel toward what is good.
- **Pray for one leader who opposes the church.** Ask God to guide that person's heart and to bring him or her to know Christ.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You bring down the proud and lift up the lowly. You see all things, and You rule every ruler on the earth. Save those of us who suffer. Raise those who have fallen. Feed the hungry, and set free those in prison for Your name. Deliver us from those who hate us without cause. And for the rulers of my land and of every land, I ask You: give them health, peace, harmony and stability. Direct their counsel toward what is good and pleasing in Your sight. Turn the hearts of those who oppose Your church, and keep Your people faithful and at peace. Through Jesus Christ our High Priest. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think the church in Rome praised God's power before it prayed for the emperor's men?
- Is it hard for you to pray for a ruler's health and peace? Why or why not?
- How might your words about your leaders change if you prayed for them by name every week?

## Prayer on the Road to the Lions

> "For me, living is Christ, and dying is gain." (Philippians 1:21)

The prisoner walked in chains, with ten soldiers around him.

He was no longer young. He was the bishop of the church in Antioch, in Syria. His name was Ignatius. He had been arrested for his faith and condemned to be thrown to wild animals in Rome, for the crowds to watch. Rome was more than a thousand miles away. So the soldiers marched him there, across the land we now call Turkey, from city to city, toward the sea. Tradition places this in the reign of the emperor Trajan, around AD 108, though the date is not certain.

The soldiers were hard men. Ignatius described them in a letter: "From Syria even unto Rome I fight with wild beasts, by land and sea, by night and by day, being bound amidst ten leopards, even a company of soldiers, who only wax worse when they are kindly treated." "Wax worse" means "grow worse." The kinder the Christians were to them, the crueler they became.

But Ignatius was not alone on that road. As news of his journey spread, churches sent people to meet him. At the city of Smyrna, Polycarp, the bishop there, welcomed him, and messengers came from the churches of Ephesus, Magnesia and Tralles. They brought him comfort, and they took his letters home. Later, at the port of Troas, he wrote again. Seven of his letters have survived, written in short stops along the road.

What did a man on his way to die write about? He wrote about Christ. He wrote about the unity of the church. And again and again, in nearly every letter, he wrote about prayer.

"Remember me in your prayers," he asked the church at Magnesia, "that I may attain unto God." To the Romans he wrote, "Supplicate the Lord for me," that is, plead with the Lord for me. And in letter after letter, he asked them to pray for the people he had left behind: "Remember in your prayers the church which is in Syria, which hath God for its shepherd in my stead." He was being taken away from his flock. He could not protect them anymore. But he could ask others to pray for them, and he could trust God to be their shepherd.

### Who he was

Almost everything we know about Ignatius comes from his own seven letters. Later writers add a little. An old tradition says he was a disciple of the apostle John, but we cannot be sure of that. Other stories about him, like the legend that he was the child Jesus once took in His arms, are almost certainly not true.

What we do know is enough. Ignatius belonged to the generation just after the apostles. He was the leader of the church in Antioch, the city where the followers of Jesus were first called Christians (Acts 11:26). He called himself "Theophorus," which means "God-bearer," because he believed that Christ lived in him. And he was ready to die rather than deny that Christ.

His letter to the church in Rome is the most famous. He feared that the Roman Christians, out of love, would try to use their influence to save him. So he begged them not to. He wrote one of the most remembered lines in Christian history: "I am God's wheat, and I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts that I may be found pure bread."

We should read those words with care. Ignatius was a man facing death, and he wrote with the heat of that hour. He was not teaching that every Christian should seek to die. In chapter 4 we will see that the church in Smyrna warned against people who gave themselves up to the authorities. But Ignatius had already been condemned. His choice was not between life and death. It was between faithfulness and fear.

### What he asked for

Here is the heart of it. What did Ignatius ask the churches to pray for? He did not ask them to pray that he would escape. He asked them to pray that he would stay faithful to the end:

> "Only pray that I may have power within and without, so that I may not only say it but also desire it; that I may not only be called a Christian, but also be found one."
> Ignatius of Antioch, *The Epistles of Ignatius and His Life's Story*

Look at what he wanted: strength "within and without." Strength in his body to face the animals, and strength in his heart to want what God wanted. He knew that it is one thing to say brave words, and another thing to mean them when the moment comes. So he asked for prayer.

The apostle Paul had walked a similar road. Paul wrote our key verse from prison, not knowing whether he would live or die. And just before it he wrote, "I know that through your prayers and the help supplied by the Spirit of Jesus Christ, this will turn out for my salvation" (Philippians 1:19). Paul's hope was "that I will not be put to shame in anything" and that "Christ will be shown to be great in my body, whether through my life or through my death" (Philippians 1:20).

E. M. Bounds, the American Methodist pastor who wrote so much about prayer, noticed how often Paul asked others to pray for him. Writing about these very verses, he said:

> "Shame was taken away, holy boldness secured, and life and death made glorious by the prayers of the saints at Philippi for Paul."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

Ignatius knew this too. A bishop who had prayed for others all his life now leaned on the prayers of others. He did not think it was weakness to ask. He thought it was the way God gives strength.

And then something happened. When Ignatius reached Troas, news came from home. The church in Antioch was at peace. Ignatius did not call it luck. He wrote to the church in Philadelphia that it had been reported to him "in answer to your prayer" that the church in Antioch "hath peace." The believers along the road had prayed for his flock, and God had answered. Ignatius asked them to send a deacon to Antioch to rejoice with the church there.

### Three key insights

1. **Asking others to pray for us is strength, not weakness.** Ignatius was a bishop and a hero of the faith, yet he asked every church he wrote to for prayer. Those who are strongest in God are often the quickest to say, "Pray for me."
2. **In suffering, the deepest prayer is for faithfulness, not only for escape.** Ignatius asked to be "found" a Christian, not only called one. When we cannot change our circumstances, we can still ask God to keep our hearts true.
3. **When we can no longer care for those we love, we can still pray for them.** Taken away from his church, Ignatius placed it in God's hands and asked others to pray. God heard, and gave the church in Antioch peace.

### For us today

Most readers of this book will never be marched in chains to a foreign city. But some will. Today, in parts of Africa, Asia and the Middle East, pastors are arrested and taken far from their churches. Families are separated. Believers wait for trials whose end they cannot know. For them, the letters of Ignatius speak directly. You may not be able to protect your flock. But God is their shepherd, and the prayers of the church can reach where you cannot.

For the rest of us, Ignatius gives two tasks. First, ask for prayer. Many of us find it hard to tell others we are struggling. We pray for others, but we do not let them pray for us. Ignatius teaches us to say, simply and without shame, "Remember me in your prayers."

Second, pray for those who are suffering for Christ. "Keep in mind those in prison," says Hebrews, "as if you were in chains alongside them" (Hebrews 13:3). Ignatius also told the church in Ephesus to pray "without ceasing" for those outside the church, "for there is in them a hope of repentance." Even the soldiers who mistreated him were not beyond hope.

### Try this

This week, practice prayer on the road.

- **Ask for prayer.** Tell one or two trusted believers one thing you are facing, and ask them, "Will you pray for me?"
- **Pray for faithfulness.** Pray Ignatius's request for yourself: that you may not only be called a Christian, but also be found one, at work, at home and in hard moments.
- **Pray for a persecuted pastor.** Find the name of one church leader in prison for his or her faith, and pray for that person and for the church they had to leave.
- **Pray for those who are hard on you.** Like the "ten leopards," some people grow harsher when we are kind. Pray for them by name, that God may still bring them to repentance.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, You are my life, and to be with You is gain. Thank You for Ignatius, who prayed and was prayed for on the road to death. Give me strength within and without. Let me not only be called a Christian, but be found one. Teach me to ask for prayer without shame and to pray for others without ceasing. Be the shepherd of every church whose pastor has been taken away. Strengthen those in chains for Your name, and soften the hearts of those who guard them. Keep me faithful to the end, whether by my life or by my death. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Ignatius asked so often for others to pray for him?
- Is it easy or hard for you to ask others to pray for you? What makes it so?
- What is the difference between praying to escape a hard situation and praying to be faithful in it? When might each be right?

## "Eighty and Six Years Have I Served Him"

> "Have no fear of anything you are going to suffer. Look: some of you the devil is about to throw into prison, so that you will be tested, and you will face affliction for ten days. Stay faithful even to death, and I will give you the crown of life." (Revelation 2:10)

The crowd in the stadium of Smyrna wanted blood.

It was around AD 155, in a rich port city on the west coast of what is now Turkey. Christians had been brought before the Roman governor, the proconsul, and some had been thrown to wild animals. After a believer named Germanicus faced the beasts with great courage, the crowd began to shout: "Away with the Atheists; let Polycarp be sought out!"

They called Christians "atheists" because they would not worship the gods of Rome. And they wanted Polycarp most of all, the old bishop of Smyrna.

At first Polycarp wanted to stay in the city, but his friends persuaded him to go to a farmhouse in the countryside. The letter that tells his story says what he did there. He was "engaged in nothing else night and day than praying for all men, and for the Churches throughout the world, according to his usual custom."

"His usual custom." Polycarp did not begin to pray because danger came. Danger came and found him praying, as he always had.

His pursuers came at last, with horsemen, led by a young man of his own household who had been tortured until he told where Polycarp was. It was evening. Polycarp could have slipped away to another house, but he refused, saying, "The will of God be done." He came down to meet them. Then he did something none of them expected. He ordered food and drink to be set before the men who had come to arrest him. And he asked them for one thing: an hour to pray without being disturbed.

They agreed, and he stood and prayed, the letter says, "being full of the grace of God, so that he could not cease for two full hours." He prayed for everyone he had ever known, "both small and great, illustrious and obscure," and for the whole church throughout the world. Some of the men who heard him began to be sorry they had come to arrest such a godly old man.

In the stadium, the proconsul tried to save him. "Have respect to thy old age," he said. "Swear, and I will set thee at liberty, reproach Christ." To "reproach" Christ meant to curse Him.

Polycarp answered: "Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?"

### Who he was

We know this story because the church in Smyrna wrote it down soon after his death, in a letter to another church, at Philomelium. It is usually counted the oldest account we have of a Christian martyr after the New Testament. The writers tell of some things they saw as wonders they could not explain. They also say plainly that they did not praise believers who gave themselves up to the authorities, "seeing the Gospel does not teach so to do." Polycarp did not seek death. He waited, and when it came, he did not run.

Polycarp's life reached back to the apostles. Irenaeus, who later became a bishop in Gaul (modern France), had listened to him as a boy. Years later he wrote to an old friend:

> "I am able to describe the very place in which the blessed Polycarp sat as he discoursed, and his goings out and his comings in, and the manner of his life, and his physical appearance, and his discourses to the people, and the accounts which he gave of his intercourse with John and with the others who had seen the Lord."
> Eusebius, *The Church History*

"Intercourse" here simply means time spent together. Polycarp had known John and others who had seen Jesus. In chapter 3 we saw him welcome Ignatius at Smyrna, nearly fifty years before his own death. Ignatius later wrote him a letter full of advice for a bishop, including this: "Give thyself to unceasing prayers." Polycarp did.

His "eighty and six years" may mean his whole life, or the years since his baptism. We cannot be sure.

Our key verse was written to this very city. In Revelation, the risen Jesus sent a message to "the church in Smyrna" (Revelation 2:8). It promised suffering, and it promised a crown: "Stay faithful even to death, and I will give you the crown of life." About sixty years later, the bishop of that church did exactly that.

### How he prayed

When the fire was ready, they wanted to nail Polycarp to the stake. He told them God would give him strength to stand still, so they only tied him. Then he looked up to heaven and prayed. The letter gives us his prayer. Here is part of it:

> "O Lord God Almighty, the Father of thy beloved and blessed Son Jesus Christ, by whom we have received the knowledge of Thee, the God of angels and powers, and of every creature, and of the whole race of the righteous who live before thee, I give Thee thanks that Thou hast counted me worthy of this day and this hour, that I should have a part in the number of Thy martyrs, in the cup of thy Christ, to the resurrection of eternal life"
> Polycarp, *The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp*

It is **full of who God is**. Polycarp does not begin with the fire, but with the "Lord God Almighty," the God of angels and of every creature.

It is **a prayer of thanks**. "I give Thee thanks." He is about to die, and he thanks God for counting him worthy. He speaks of "the cup" of Christ, remembering Jesus' prayer in the garden: "Yet let Your will be done, not mine" (Luke 22:42).

It is **full of hope**. He speaks of "the resurrection of eternal life." Death is not the end of the story.

And it ends in **praise**. "I praise Thee for all things, I bless Thee, I glorify Thee," he prayed, giving glory to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Then he said "Amen," and the fire was lit.

Many scholars notice that this prayer sounds like the prayers of thanksgiving the church used in its worship. Polycarp had prayed the church's prayers for most of a century. When his last hour came, the words were already in him. He did not have to search for them. A lifetime of prayer had prepared his heart for this one.

The letter adds that the believers gathered his bones and met each year to remember the day of his death with joy. Christians of different traditions honor the martyrs in different ways, but all of us can learn from the faith they kept.

### Three key insights

1. **A lifetime of prayer prepares us for the hour of testing.** Polycarp was praying "according to his usual custom" when danger came, and the prayer he prayed at the stake grew out of decades of daily prayer. What we practice in ordinary days is what we will have in hard ones.
2. **A praying heart remembers everyone, the small and the great.** In his last two hours, Polycarp named the famous and the forgotten alike, and the whole church throughout the world. True intercession has room for every person God loves.
3. **Thanksgiving can be our last word, even in suffering.** At the stake, Polycarp's prayer was not a cry of fear but a song of thanks. Those who have known Christ's faithfulness for many years can thank Him in every hour.

### For us today

Polycarp's words, "He never did me any injury," are a testimony. Many older believers who have walked with Christ through poverty, war, sickness and loss would say the same. If you know such a person, honor them. Ask them to tell you their story. If you are one of them, tell it. The psalmist prayed, "Now that I am old and gray, God, do not abandon me, not until I have told the generation after me what Your arm has done" (Psalm 71:18).

Polycarp also fed the men who came to arrest him, and he prayed for "all men." In many places today, Christians are betrayed by neighbors or even family, as Polycarp was by his own household. His example invites us to pray for them too.

Polycarp did not become brave in one day. Whatever your age, you can begin today.

### Try this

This week, pray like Polycarp in the farmhouse.

- **Keep your usual custom.** Choose a daily time of prayer and keep it every day this week, so that it becomes your custom too.
- **Pray for the small and the great.** List people you have known, famous and forgotten, and pray for a few of them by name each day.
- **Pray for the whole church.** Each day, pray for believers in one other part of the world, especially where the church suffers.
- **Thank God for His faithfulness.** Write down three ways God has been faithful to you, and thank Him for each.

### A closing prayer

Lord God Almighty, Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, thank You for Your servant Polycarp, who found You faithful to the end. Make prayer my usual custom, so that when trouble comes, it finds me already speaking with You. Teach me to remember the small and the great, and Your whole church throughout the world. Give me grace to feed my enemies and to pray for them. When my last hour comes, let me meet it with thanks and not with fear. Keep me faithful even to death, and give me the crown of life. I praise You for all things, I bless You, I glorify You, through Jesus Christ, with the Holy Spirit. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Polycarp was praying "according to his usual custom" when danger came. What are your usual customs of prayer?
- Why do you think Polycarp asked to pray before he was taken away, and what do you notice about what he prayed for?
- Who in your life could say, "He never did me any injury"? What could you learn from their story?

## Prayer in a Prison Cell

> "Even if my path goes down through a valley of deepest darkness, I will not be afraid of any harm, because You are there with me; Your rod and Your staff give me courage." (Psalm 23:4)

The young mother had never known darkness like it.

Her name was Vibia Perpetua. She was about twenty-two years old, from a respected family in Carthage, the great Roman city on the coast of North Africa, near where Tunis stands today. She had a baby son who was still nursing. In the year 203, during the reign of the emperor Septimius Severus, she was arrested with a small group of new believers who were preparing for baptism. Among them were two slaves, a man named Revocatus and a young woman named Felicity, who was eight months pregnant.

Perpetua wrote down what happened next. "We are taken into the dungeon," she wrote, "and I was very much afraid, because I had never felt such darkness." The prison was hot and crowded, and the soldiers pushed the prisoners about. And she was sick with worry for her baby.

Two deacons, Tertius and Pomponius, paid for the prisoners to spend a few hours in a better part of the prison, and Perpetua nursed her hungry son. Later she was allowed to keep him with her. Then, she wrote, "the dungeon became to me as it were a palace, so that I preferred being there to being elsewhere."

Her brother, visiting, asked her to pray for a vision, to learn whether she would be set free or put to death. Perpetua wrote that she "knew that I was privileged to converse with the Lord," and so she promised him boldly: "Tomorrow I will tell you." She asked, and in a vision she saw a golden ladder reaching up to heaven, with a dragon lying beneath it. She stepped on the dragon's head and climbed. At the top she saw a garden and a white-haired shepherd who welcomed her: "You are welcome, daughter." She and her brother understood that she would die.

Her father, who was not a Christian, begged her again and again to give up her faith. At her trial he brought her baby and pleaded with her in front of everyone. The Roman official Hilarianus said, "Spare the grey hairs of your father, spare the infancy of your boy, offer sacrifice for the well-being of the emperors." She replied, "I will not do so." He asked, "Are you a Christian?" And she said, "I am a Christian."

The whole group was condemned to be thrown to wild animals in the arena. And Perpetua wrote, "we went down cheerfully to the dungeon."

### Who they were

We know this story from an ancient account called *The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity*. ("Passion" here means suffering.) It has three parts.

The middle part is **Perpetua's own prison diary**, written in her own words. It tells of her arrest, her father, her baby, her trial and her visions, up to the day before she died. Many scholars think it is the oldest writing we have from a Christian woman. Beside it is a vision written by Saturus, their teacher, who gave himself up to share their suffering.

Around these is a frame by **an editor**, a Christian in Carthage who gathered the pieces soon after their deaths and described what Perpetua could not: the last days in prison and the arena. Some have guessed that the editor was Tertullian, the Christian writer of Carthage, but no one knows. The quotations above are all from Perpetua's own part. What follows about Felicity and the arena comes from the editor.

Tertullian did live in Carthage then. A few years earlier he had written a defense of the Christians to the Roman rulers:

> "The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed."
> Tertullian, *The Apology*

Perpetua and Felicity were part of that harvest.

### How they prayed

**They prayed in the darkness.** Perpetua did not pretend to be brave. She was "very much afraid." But God met her there. The prison was still a prison, but in it she found peace, and the dungeon became "as it were a palace." That is what Psalm 23:4 promises. It does not say we will never walk through the valley of deepest darkness. It says, "You are there with me."

**They prayed boldly.** Perpetua asked God for a vision because she knew she could speak with Him as a friend. She was a new believer, not yet baptized when she was arrested. Yet she came to God with confidence. She also prayed for others. One day, while the prisoners were praying together, the name of her little brother Dinocrates came to her mind. He had died of a terrible illness when he was seven years old. She prayed for him "day and night, groaning and weeping," and she believed that God showed her in a vision that he was at peace. Christians of different traditions understand this part of her story in different ways. All can see in it a sister's love that would not stop praying.

**They prayed together for one another.** The editor tells us that Felicity was afraid. Roman law did not allow a pregnant woman to be put to death. She feared that she would be left behind and die later among criminals. Her friends did not want to leave her either. So, three days before the games, "joining together their united cry, they poured forth their prayer to the Lord." Right after their prayer, her labor began. One of the prison servants mocked her: if she cried out now, what would she do when she faced the beasts? Felicity answered:

"Now it is I that suffer what I suffer; but then there will be another in me, who will suffer for me, because I also am about to suffer for Him."

She meant Christ, who would be with her and in her in the hour of her death. She gave birth to a baby girl, and a Christian sister in the church took the child to raise as her own.

The day before the games, Perpetua had one last vision. The deacon Pomponius seemed to come to the prison gate and lead her into the arena. He said to her, "Do not fear, I am here with you, and I am labouring with you." She woke knowing that her real fight was against the devil, and that the victory would be hers.

On the day of the games, the editor writes, Perpetua walked into the arena calm and singing psalms. When Felicity was thrown to the ground, Perpetua went to her, "gave her her hand, and lifted her up." A noblewoman and a slave stood side by side. Before they died by the sword, Perpetua called her own brother and a young believer to her side and said: "Stand fast in the faith, and love one another, all of you, and be not offended at my sufferings."

### Three key insights

1. **God meets us in the darkness before He brings us out of it.** Perpetua was afraid, and her prison stayed a prison. But with God there, the dungeon became like a palace. The Shepherd of Psalm 23 walks with us through the valley.
2. **Even a new believer may come to God with bold confidence.** Perpetua had not yet been baptized when she was arrested, yet she knew she could speak with the Lord. Boldness in prayer rests on God's welcome, not on how long we have been Christians.
3. **Praying together keeps anyone from being left alone.** Felicity's friends would not let her face her fear by herself. Their united prayer carried her, and Christ Himself, she said, would suffer in her.

### For us today

There are prisons today where Christians sit in the dark, sometimes for years without a trial. Elsewhere a young woman may be locked in a room by her own family because she has turned to Christ. Perpetua's father loved her and still tried to pull her from her faith. Many believers know that pain.

If you are in a dark place, Perpetua's diary tells you that fear is not failure. She was afraid, she prayed, and God was there. The promise is the same for you: "You are there with me."

And if you are free, remember those who are not. About fifty years after Perpetua, Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage, wrote to Christians in prison in Rome:

> "Confession shuts you up in prison; affection shuts me up there. And I indeed, remembering you day and night, both when in the sacrifices I offer prayer with many, and when in retirement I pray with private petition, beseech of the Lord a full acknowledgment to your crowns and your praises."
> Cyprian, *The Epistles of Cyprian*

"Confession" here means standing up for Christ.

### Try this

This week, pray from the valley and for those in it.

- **Pray Psalm 23 slowly each day.** Stop at verse 4 and tell God honestly where you feel darkness or fear.
- **Ask boldly.** Bring God one request you have been afraid to ask, trusting His welcome.
- **Pray with others for one who is afraid.** Join one or two believers to pray for someone facing a hard day.
- **Remember a prisoner.** Learn the name of one Christian imprisoned for the faith, and pray for him or her each day this week, "day and night," as Cyprian did.

### A closing prayer

Lord, my Shepherd, You were with Perpetua in the darkness of her cell, and with Felicity in the pain of her labor. Be with me in every valley I walk through. When I am afraid, remind me that You are there with me. Teach me to come to You boldly, as Your welcome child. Help me to pray with others, so that no one faces their fear alone. Strengthen every believer in prison today, make their cells places of Your peace, and give me love that remembers them day and night. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Perpetua admitted she was "very much afraid." Why is it important that she was honest about her fear?
- What do you learn from the way Felicity's friends prayed for her?
- Who do you know, near or far, who is in a "valley of deepest darkness"? How could you stand with them in prayer?

## Prayer, the Wall of Faith

> "Pray in the Spirit at every opportunity, with every kind of prayer and request. To that end, keep watch, never giving up, and make requests for all God's holy people." (Ephesians 6:18)

Around the year AD 200, Carthage was one of the great cities of the Roman world. It stood on the coast of North Africa, in what is now Tunisia, with a busy harbor and an arena where wild animals were set loose on condemned prisoners. In the last chapter we were in that arena with Perpetua and Felicity, and we heard a few words from Tertullian, a Christian teacher who lived in the city at the same time.

Tertullian knew how the crowds of Carthage spoke about Christians. When things went wrong, they had someone to blame. In a book he wrote to the Roman governors around AD 197, called the *Apology* (a "defense"), he described it:

> "If the Tiber rises as high as the city walls, if the Nile does not send its waters up over the fields, if the heavens give no rain, if there is an earthquake, if there is famine or pestilence, straightway the cry is, “Away with the Christians to the lion!”"
> Tertullian, *The Apology*

So what did the Christians of Carthage do, in a city that wanted them dead? Tertullian tells us. They met together to pray. "We meet together as an assembly and congregation," he wrote, "that, offering up prayer to God as with united force, we may wrestle with Him in our supplications." And he added, "This violence God delights in."

He also described how they prayed for the emperor. They stood, looked up, and spread out their hands:

> "Thither we lift our eyes, with hands outstretched, because free from sin; with head uncovered, for we have nothing whereof to be ashamed; finally, without a monitor, because it is from the heart we supplicate."
> Tertullian, *The Apology*

"Without a monitor" means that no priest had to tell them what words to say, as in the pagan temples. And Tertullian says they prayed for the emperor: for his long life, a safe empire and a world at peace. The emperor was the man whose laws could send them to the lions.

Tertullian saw something else in that posture. A Christian standing with arms spread wide has nothing to defend himself with. He wrote that "the very attitude of a Christian praying is one of preparation for all punishment." The Christians of Carthage had no weapons and no friends in high places. They had prayer.

A few years later Tertullian wrote a short book for new believers, *On Prayer*. At the end of it, after explaining the Lord's Prayer line by line, he praised prayer with a long list of all it can do. Then he gave it a name: "Prayer is the wall of faith."

### Who he was

Tertullian was born in Carthage, probably around AD 160, and grew up a pagan. He was very well educated and a powerful writer. He came to faith in Christ as an adult, probably in the 190s. He was one of the first Christians to write in Latin, the language of the western half of the empire. He is the first writer we know of who used the Latin word for "Trinity" to speak of God.

His *On Prayer* is the oldest explanation of the Lord's Prayer that has come down to us. Tertullian loved its shortness. He said that "in the Prayer is comprised an epitome of the whole Gospel," that is, a short summary of the whole good news. He also taught about set times of prayer, as we saw in Volume 3, and about the fast days on Wednesdays and Fridays, which the *Didache* also taught (chapter 1). He called these days "Stations," a word Roman soldiers used for a guard post. "For we withal are God's military," he explained.

We should also be honest about Tertullian. Later in life, perhaps around AD 207, he joined a movement called the New Prophecy, which claimed new revelations from the Spirit and demanded a much stricter life. He became harsh toward the main church. Christians since have been sad about this, as we are when a gifted friend walks away from the family. Yet the church kept reading his book on prayer. A generation later, Cyprian, the great bishop of Carthage, loved his writings, as we will see in chapter 8.

### The armor that holds the rest together

Our key verse comes at the end of Paul's famous picture of the armor of God. Paul wrote this letter as a prisoner, probably chained to a Roman soldier (Ephesians 6:20). He may have looked at his guard as he wrote: belt, breastplate, shield and helmet. He told the believers in Ephesus to put on God's armor:

> "For this reason, take up God's complete armor. Then you will be able to resist on the evil day and, having carried everything through, remain standing." (Ephesians 6:13)

Then, after the sword of the Spirit, Paul does not stop. He goes straight on: "Pray in the Spirit at every opportunity." Prayer is not one more piece of armor. It is the way the soldier wears all the rest. E. M. Bounds, whom we heard in chapter 3, saw this clearly:

> "Note carefully that the Christian's armour will avail him nothing, unless prayer be added. This is the pivot, the connecting link of the armour of God. This holds it together, and renders it effective."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Necessity of Prayer*

Long before Bounds, Tertullian used the same picture. Read his closing words in *On Prayer*:

> "Prayer is the wall of faith: her arms and missiles against the foe who keeps watch over us on all sides. And, so never walk we unarmed. By day, be we mindful of Station; by night, of vigil. Under the arms of prayer guard we the standard of our General; await we in prayer the angel’s trump."
> Tertullian, *On Prayer*

The "standard" was an army's flag, our "General" is Christ, and the "angel's trump" is the trumpet that will sound when He returns. Until then we stand guard, as Paul asks: "keep watch, never giving up."

Notice what Tertullian did *not* promise. In the old days, he wrote, prayer had shut the mouths of lions and kept men safe in the fire. But Christian prayer "has no delegated grace to avert any sense of suffering; but it supplies the suffering, and the feeling, and the grieving, with endurance." Prayer is a wall that keeps faith standing when pain comes.

And he listed what prayer does for others: it "keeps bivouac on behalf of personal enemies, makes supplication on behalf of persecutors." A bivouac is a soldier's camp for the night. Prayer keeps watch even over our enemies. He ends the whole book by looking at Jesus: "Even the Lord Himself prayed; to whom be honour and virtue unto the ages of the ages!"

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer is not an extra piece of armor but the strength of all the rest.** Paul ends his picture of God's armor with prayer, and Tertullian called it the wall of faith. Without prayer, the believer stands unarmed.
2. **Christians answer hatred by praying for those who hate them.** The believers of Carthage were blamed for every disaster, and they prayed for the emperor. Paul wrote, "Bless the people who persecute you. Bless them, and do not curse them" (Romans 12:14).
3. **A soldier keeps watch at set times, and so should we.** Tertullian's "Stations" were guard posts in the week. Prayer that waits for a free moment rarely comes, but prayer that keeps its set times stays strong.

### For us today

Many readers of this book live where Christians are still blamed when things go wrong. In some villages, when the rains fail or sickness comes, believers are accused of angering the old gods. In some cities, Christians are called enemies of the nation. Tertullian's church knew that cry: "Away with the Christians to the lion!" They did not answer with anger. They lifted open hands to God and prayed for the very rulers who threatened them.

Others of us are under attack in quieter ways: fear, temptation, discouragement. Tertullian says no one should walk out "unarmed." Before you go to the market, the office, the field or the school, put on God's armor in prayer.

Notice, too, that Paul's prayer is not only for ourselves. He asks us to "make requests for all God's holy people." When you pray, you stand guard over brothers and sisters you have never met, in prisons, refugee camps and hidden house churches. They are praying for you too.

### Try this

This week, try praying the way the Christians of Carthage prayed.

- **Open your hands.** Stand, if you can, and lift your hands with palms open. Tell God you have nothing to bring and nothing to hide.
- **Put on the armor.** Read Ephesians 6:13-18 slowly each morning. Ask God for each piece: truth, righteousness, peace, faith, salvation and His word. End with prayer "in the Spirit."
- **Keep a Station.** Choose one or two set times in the week, perhaps on a fast day, when you will stand guard in prayer for the persecuted church.
- **Pray for an opponent.** Name one person who makes life hard for you or for other believers, and ask God to bless them and bring them to Himself.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You are the wall around Your people. Thank You for the believers of Carthage, who lifted open hands to You when the crowds cried out against them. Teach me to pray at every opportunity, with every kind of prayer. Clothe me in Your armor, and let prayer hold it all together. When suffering comes, give me endurance. When I am wronged, teach me to pray for those who wrong me. Keep me on watch, never giving up, until the trumpet sounds and Your Son returns. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Paul ends his picture of God's armor with prayer?
- Tertullian said prayer does not always take away suffering but gives endurance in it. Have you known this to be true?
- Who are the "persecutors" or opponents you find hardest to pray for? What might happen if you prayed for them each day?

## Prayer Rising Like Incense

> "Let my prayer stand before You like incense, my lifted hands like the offering at evening." (Psalm 141:2)

In the year AD 202, a wave of persecution reached Alexandria, the great city at the mouth of the Nile in Egypt. Christians from all over Egypt were being brought there to be tried and killed.

One of those arrested was a believer named Leonides. He had a son who was not yet seventeen, the oldest of seven boys. The son's name was Origen.

We know the story from Eusebius, a bishop who wrote the first great history of the church about a hundred years later. He collected Origen's letters and learned from people who remembered him. He tells us that young Origen wanted to die for Christ with his father. So his mother did the one thing she could think of:

> "For, at first, entreating him, she begged him to have compassion on her motherly feelings toward him; but finding, that when he had learned that his father had been seized and imprisoned, he was set the more resolutely, and completely carried away with his zeal for martyrdom, she hid all his clothing, and thus compelled him to remain at home."
> Eusebius, *The Church History*

A boy without clothes could not go out into the street. So Origen sat at home, and he did what he could. He wrote a letter to his father in prison. Eusebius quotes one line of it: "Take heed not to change your mind on our account." In other words: do not deny Christ to save us.

Leonides did not change his mind. He was beheaded. The government took the family's property, and Origen was left with his mother and six younger brothers, and nothing to live on.

Eusebius adds a tender detail from earlier years. Leonides had taught his son the Scriptures every day, and "they say," Eusebius writes, that the father would often stand by the sleeping boy, uncover his chest, and kiss it, as if the Holy Spirit lived there.

That boy became one of the most brilliant teachers of the early church. Some thirty years later, he wrote a book called *On Prayer*.

### Who he was

Origen did not die as a martyr in 202. Instead he went to work to feed his family. Eusebius says that "he was in his eighteenth year when he took charge of the catechetical school," the school in Alexandria that taught new believers the faith. He lived very simply. He fasted and "never lay upon a bed, but upon the ground." Later he wrote explanations of almost every book of Scripture.

Around AD 231 he moved to Caesarea in Palestine. There, about AD 233, he wrote *On Prayer* for two friends, a man named Ambrose and a woman named Tatiana, who had asked him questions about prayer.

In AD 250, under the emperor Decius, Origen was arrested at last. He was an old man by then. Eusebius tells us that he suffered "bonds and bodily tortures and torments under the iron collar and in the dungeon," and that his feet were stretched in the stocks for many days. His judge was careful not to let him die, perhaps hoping to break him. Origen did not deny Christ. He was released, but his body was broken, and he died a few years later, probably around AD 253 or 254.

We should be honest about Origen too. He was a daring thinker, and he sometimes guessed where Scripture is silent. He wondered, for example, whether souls existed before they were born into bodies, and whether in the end all might be restored to God. Centuries later, some of these ideas were condemned. Even in *On Prayer* he argued that prayer in the fullest sense should be made only to the Father, through the Son. The church did not follow him there: from Stephen, who prayed, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Acts 7:59), Christians have always prayed to Christ. Yet Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant teachers alike have learned from Origen's love for Scripture and for prayer. We can honor a teacher without agreeing with every word.

### The whole life as a prayer

Psalm 141 is an evening prayer. In Israel's worship, incense was burned and an offering was made each evening, and David asked that his own prayer would rise to God in the same way. Origen loved this verse. He quoted it when he taught that Christians should pray at least three times a day, morning, noon and evening, like Daniel (Daniel 6:10). The evening prayer, he said, is the one David spoke of: "the lifting up of my hands in evening sacrifice."

But set times are not the whole story. Paul wrote, "Never stop praying" (1 Thessalonians 5:17). How can anyone do that, when we must sleep, work and cook? Origen answered that good actions done for God are themselves part of prayer. He wrote that "he prays without ceasing who combines prayer with right actions, and becoming actions with prayer." The only way the command makes sense, he said, is "if we may speak of the whole life of a saint as one great continuous prayer."

Here is a fresh way to read our verse. A believer's whole life can rise to God like incense. The set times of prayer are when the smoke is thickest, but the fire need never go out.

Fourteen hundred years later, a humble cook in a monastery in Paris, known as Brother Lawrence, discovered the same thing in his kitchen:

> "The time of business," said he, "does not with me differ from the time of prayer; and in the noise and clatter of my kitchen, while several persons are at the same time calling for different things, I possess God in as great tranquility as if I were upon my knees at the blessed sacrament."
> Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*

### Lifting the heart before the hands

Origen also wrote carefully about how to pray. Many believers in his day stood to pray, with their hands stretched out and their eyes lifted up, as David describes. Paul also wrote, "I want the men everywhere to pray, lifting up holy hands, free from anger and quarreling" (1 Timothy 2:8).

But Origen said the body is not the main thing. Before we begin, we should pause, put away distractions, remember how great God is, and let go of every grudge. Then, he wrote, a person should come "with his soul, as it were, extended before his hands, and his mind intent on God before his eyes." The heart must reach up before the hands do.

Posture can also bend to need: a sick person may pray lying down, and a traveler may pray "without any outward sign of doing so." And Origen taught that "every place is rendered fit for prayer by one who prays rightly."

Most beautiful of all, Origen taught that we never pray alone. Jesus Himself joins our prayer. "He prays for those who pray," Origen wrote, "and pleads along with those who plead." The letter to the Hebrews says the same: Jesus "is always alive to plead on their behalf" (Hebrews 7:25). Origen also believed that the angels and the saints in heaven pray with the church. Christians understand this in different ways, but all can rejoice that Christ prays with us.

### Three key insights

1. **Our whole life can become one long prayer.** Set times of prayer matter, but they are like the thickest smoke of the incense. When our work and our kindness are done for God, the fire on the altar never goes out.
2. **The heart must be lifted before the hands.** Posture can help us pray, but God looks first at the soul. Pause, put away distractions and grudges, and remember whom you are speaking to.
3. **When we pray, Christ prays with us.** Jesus, our great High Priest, takes up our weak prayers and pleads for us before the Father. Even our poorest prayer is carried by His.

### For us today

Many of us think our lives are too full for prayer. A mother in Nairobi with small children, a farmer in Bolivia working from dawn, a factory worker in Vietnam on a long shift: all may feel there is no room. Origen would tell them to keep a few set times, and then to offer the rest of the day as incense too.

Remember that Origen wrote about prayer as a man who had lost his father to the sword, and who would one day wear the iron collar himself. If you live under pressure or danger, your lifted hands, even hidden ones, rise before God like the evening offering.

### Try this

Origen suggested an order for prayer that you can use this week. Begin and end with praise, and in the middle:

- **Give glory.** Start by praising God for who He is, through Christ and in the Holy Spirit.
- **Give thanks.** Thank Him for blessings given to all people, and then for the ones He has given to you.
- **Confess.** Name your sins honestly and ask for healing from the habits that lead you into them, as well as forgiveness.
- **Ask.** Pray for great and heavenly things, for yourself and for those you love. Then end by giving glory to God again.

At three set times each day, lift your hands, even for one minute, and say Psalm 141:2 as your prayer.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, let my prayer stand before You like incense, and my lifted hands like the offering at evening. Thank You for Origen, who learned to pray in a house of grief and who stayed faithful under torture. Teach me to lift my heart before my hands. Make my work, my words and my kindness part of my prayer, so that my whole life rises to You. And thank You that I never pray alone, because Your Son prays with me and for me. In His name I pray. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Origen said the whole life of a believer can be "one great continuous prayer." What part of your ordinary day could you offer to God as prayer?
- Why do you think it matters to prepare our hearts before we begin to pray?
- How does it change your prayer to know that Jesus prays with you?

- Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*, especially "A Life of Prayer"

## Why We Say "Our" Father

> "And since you are children, God has sent out the Spirit of His Son into our hearts, and the Spirit cries, 'Abba, Father!'" (Galatians 4:6)

On September 13, AD 258, two officers came for the bishop of Carthage. They put Cyprian between them in a chariot and drove him to a country estate called Sexti, a few miles outside the city, where the Roman governor, Galerius Maximus, had gone to recover from an illness.

We know what happened next because the church of Carthage kept a careful account. It includes the official record of the trial, and believers treasured and copied it. We call it the *Acts of Cyprian*.

Cyprian spent the night under guard in an officer's house, but he was not alone. The account says that the whole crowd of believers came and waited in the street outside the gate. Even then, thinking of his flock, he gave orders that the young women in the crowd should be protected.

In the morning a great crowd gathered at Sexti. The governor asked, "Are you Thascius Cyprianus?" Cyprian answered, "I am he." Told that the emperors commanded him to take part in the Roman rites, Cyprian answered, "I refuse to do so." "Take heed for yourself," said the governor. Cyprian replied, "Execute the Emperor's orders; in a matter so manifest I may not deliberate." He meant: the choice is clear, and I do not need time to think.

The governor spoke with his advisers and then read the sentence from a tablet: Cyprian was to be beheaded. Cyprian answered, "Thanks be to God."

Then the crowd of believers cried out with one voice, "We will be beheaded with him."

They followed him to an open field. Cyprian took off his cloak, and the account says that "he kneeled down, and addressed himself in prayer to the Lord." The believers spread cloths on the ground around him. He told his friends to give the executioner twenty-five gold coins. Then Cyprian covered his eyes with his own hands, and he died for Christ.

That night the believers carried his body to its grave with torches and prayers.

Notice how often Cyprian is surrounded by his people in this account. They waited at the gate. They cried out together. They followed him to the field. Six years earlier, in a little book on the Lord's Prayer, Cyprian had taught them why.

### Who he was

Cyprian was born in North Africa early in the 200s, into a wealthy family. He became a famous teacher of public speaking in Carthage. He was already a grown man, around AD 246, when an older Christian named Caecilius led him to Christ. After his baptism he gave much of his wealth to the poor. Within about two years the church of Carthage chose him as its bishop.

He did not have long in peace. In AD 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone in the empire to offer sacrifice to the gods. Cyprian went into hiding and led his church by letters. Some criticized him for this, but he believed his people needed him alive. Many believers gave in and sacrificed, and afterward Cyprian worked hard to bring them back without making light of their fall. Then a terrible plague struck Carthage. His deacon Pontius, who wrote his life, says Cyprian urged the believers to care for the sick, and that good was done "to all men, not to those only who are of the household of faith."

Cyprian loved the writings of Tertullian, whom we met in chapter 6. The scholar Jerome later heard from a man who had worked for Cyprian that the bishop read Tertullian every day and would say, "Give me the master." Cyprian's book *On the Lord's Prayer*, written around AD 252, follows Tertullian's pattern. But Cyprian added something of his own.

### "Our," not "my"

Before he explains a single line, Cyprian stops at the very first word:

> "Before all things, the Teacher of peace and the Master of unity would not have prayer to be made singly and individually, as for one who prays to pray for himself alone. For we say not “My Father, which art in heaven,” nor “Give me this day my daily bread;” nor does each one ask that only his own debt should be forgiven him; nor does he request for himself alone that he may not be led into temptation, and delivered from evil. Our prayer is public and common; and when we pray, we pray not for one, but for the whole people, because we the whole people are one."
> Cyprian, *The Treatises of Cyprian*

In Volume 2 we saw that "Our Father" means we never pray alone. Cyprian goes further. Every request for ourselves is plural: our bread, our debts, lead *us*, deliver *us*. So even in an empty room, you pray for the whole family. When you ask for daily bread, you ask it for hungry believers in lands you have never seen.

Cyprian points to the three young men in the fiery furnace in Babylon, who praised God together "as if from one mouth." And he points to the first believers in Jerusalem, who prayed together with one mind after Jesus returned to heaven. Prayer that is shared, he says, is prayer God loves to hear.

He knew the opposite too. During the persecution of 250, he wrote a letter to his clergy describing a vision in which the people prayed, but with "dissonant voices, and wills disagreeing," and this displeased the Lord. A family that fights at the table cannot pray "Our Father" with one heart.

### The Spirit who cries "Abba"

Our key verse tells us where this shared prayer comes from. "Abba" is an Aramaic word, the word a child in Jesus' land used for his father. It is the word Jesus Himself used in Gethsemane: "Abba, Father, for You all things are possible" (Mark 14:36). Paul says that God has sent the Spirit of His Son "into our hearts," and the Spirit cries out the Son's own word in us.

Notice Paul's word: "*our* hearts." The same Spirit lives in every believer. He cries "Abba" in a house church in Beijing, a village in Nigeria and a city in Peru. That is why we can say "our" Father: we have one Father, because one Spirit cries in all of us.

Cyprian was amazed that we are allowed to speak this way at all. Calling God "Father," he wrote, is "a name which none of us would dare to venture on in prayer, unless He Himself had allowed us thus to pray!" And he drew a lesson from it: "when we call God Father, we ought to act as God's children."

In 257 the emperor Valerian sent Cyprian into exile in a small town called Curubis. He could not gather his people, but he could still pray "Our Father" with them. Thomas Watson, the Puritan we met in chapter 1, put it simply:

> "A prison cannot keep thee from thy God; the way to heaven can never be blocked up."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

### Three key insights

1. **We call God "Father" only because His Spirit gives us the right.** Cyprian said none of us would dare to use that name unless God allowed it. The Spirit of the Son cries "Abba" in our hearts, so we come not as strangers but as children.
2. **The word "our" makes every prayer a prayer for the whole family.** Even when we pray alone, the Lord's Prayer asks for our bread and our forgiveness. Each time we pray it, we carry the whole people of God with us.
3. **Quarrels among believers weaken prayer, and unity strengthens it.** Cyprian saw that "dissonant voices" displeased the Lord. When we make peace with one another, our prayers rise together "as if from one mouth."

### For us today

Many of us think of faith as a private matter: "my" relationship with God, "my" prayer life. Each of us must come to God personally. But the Lord's Prayer has no "my" in it. If your culture prizes the family and the village, you already understand this. If it prizes the individual, Cyprian's words may be a needed correction.

Think of the believers who waited in the street outside the house where Cyprian was held. Today, Christians in prison for their faith are strengthened by knowing that others are praying. When you pray "give us our daily bread," you are praying for them. When you pray "forgive us," you are standing beside believers you have never met.

And if you are in a quarrel with another believer, Cyprian would tell you to make peace. "Our Father" is a prayer for people who belong to one another.

### Try this

This week, pray the Lord's Prayer slowly each day, paying attention to the plural words.

- **Pause at "our."** Before you go on, name a few believers far away: a persecuted church, a country at war, a family in need. Bring them with you into the prayer.
- **Pray "us" with faces in mind.** At "give us," think of someone hungry. At "forgive us," think of a believer who has fallen.
- **Make peace.** If you are in a quarrel with another believer, take one step toward peace before Sunday.
- **Pray it together.** Pray the Lord's Prayer aloud with your family, friends or church, "as if from one mouth."

### A closing prayer

Abba, Father, thank You that You have sent the Spirit of Your Son into our hearts. I would never dare to call You Father unless You had invited me. Thank You for Cyprian and the believers of Carthage, who prayed together and stood together to the end. Teach me to pray not "my Father" but "our Father." Give us, all Your children everywhere, our daily bread. Forgive us, and help us forgive one another. Heal the quarrels in Your church, and make us one, so that our prayers rise to You as from one mouth. In the name of Jesus. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Jesus gave us a prayer with "our" and "us" in every request, and no "my" or "me"?
- Cyprian said that quarrels weaken the church's prayers. Have you seen this happen? Have you seen the opposite?
- Who are the brothers and sisters you could carry with you when you pray "Our Father" this week?

- Cyprian, *The Epistles of Cyprian*, especially "Epistle VII: To the Clergy, Concerning Prayer to God"
- Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*, especially "The Fourth Petition: Give Us This Day Our Daily Bread"

## Into the Desert to Pray

> "Therefore I am going to woo her; I will lead her into the wilderness and speak tenderly to her." (Hosea 2:14)

Around the year AD 270, in a farming village beside the Nile in Egypt, a young man walked to church. His name was Antony. He was about eighteen or twenty years old. His parents had died less than six months before, leaving him with a younger sister to care for and a large farm of good land.

As he walked, he thought about the apostles, who left everything to follow Jesus, and about the first believers in Jerusalem, who sold their land to share with the poor. We have the story from Athanasius, the bishop of Alexandria, who knew Antony well:

> "Pondering over these things he entered the church, and it happened the Gospel was being read, and he heard the Lord saying to the rich man, ‘If thou wouldest be perfect, go and sell that thou hast and give to the poor; and come follow Me and thou shalt have treasure in heaven.’"
> Athanasius, *The Life of Antony*

These are the words of Jesus in Matthew 19:21: "go, sell your property, and give the money to people who are poor, and treasure in heaven will be yours. After that, come and follow Me." Antony felt, Athanasius says, as if "the passage had been read on his account." He went straight out of the church. He gave his family's land, about three hundred acres, to the people of the village. He sold the rest and gave the money to the poor, keeping a little for his sister.

Some time later he was in church again and heard Jesus say, "do not be troubled about tomorrow" (Matthew 6:34). This time he gave away even what he had kept. He placed his sister with a group of faithful Christian women and left home to give himself to God.

He did not go far at first. In those days, Athanasius says, "no monk at all knew of the distant desert." Antony lived near his village and learned from an old man who had lived alone with God since his youth. Later he went to live among the tombs outside the village, and later still to an empty old fort in the desert across the Nile. Finally he traveled three days into the desert to a mountain with a spring of cold water and a few palm trees. Most think it was the mountain near the Red Sea where a monastery bearing his name still stands. He lived there for the rest of his long life.

Antony died in AD 356, aged one hundred and five. By then many men and women had followed him into the deserts of Egypt to pray.

### Who told his story

Athanasius was one of the great leaders of the church in the 300s. He was driven into exile five times for defending the truth that Jesus is fully God. Soon after Antony died, monks in other lands wrote and asked Athanasius to tell them about him. He wrote that he had seen Antony "many times," and that he had been his helper "for a long time, and poured water on his hands." So his *Life of Antony* is a friend's story.

Athanasius tells us that Antony did not want to go to school as a boy, and he may never have learned to read well. But he listened in church so carefully that "none of the things that were written fell from him to the ground." His memory, Athanasius says, "served him for books."

Athanasius also tells of Antony's long struggles with demons. He tells them plainly, as real fights, and always gives the victory to Christ, not to Antony. In the next chapter we will see how the monks who followed him understood that battle.

### How he prayed

From the very beginning, prayer was the heart of Antony's new life:

> "And he was constant in prayer, knowing that a man ought to pray in secret unceasingly."
> Athanasius, *The Life of Antony*

He worked with his hands, and later grew a garden, and gave part of what he earned to the needy. He often prayed through the whole night.

In the tombs, Athanasius says, the demons beat him so fiercely one night that a friend found him lying as if dead and carried him to the village church. When Antony woke at midnight, he asked his friend to carry him back. He was too weak to stand, so "he prayed as he lay." Then he shouted to his enemies that nothing would "separate me from the love of Christ" (see Romans 8:35), and he sang a psalm: "If an army sets up camp against me, my heart will not be afraid" (Psalm 27:3).

At last, Athanasius writes, Antony saw a ray of light come down, and the attack ended. Antony asked, "Where wert thou? Why didst thou not appear at the beginning to make my pains to cease?" A voice answered him: "Antony, I was here, but I waited to see thy fight." God had been there all along.

After nearly twenty years alone in the old fort, his friends broke down the door, and Antony came out "as from a shrine," calm and healthy, neither too thin nor too fat, his soul at peace. Many came to him for help, and he prayed for them. But he never made himself great:

> "And with those who suffered he sympathised and prayed. And oft-times the Lord heard him on behalf of many: yet he boasted not because he was heard, nor did he murmur if he were not."
> Athanasius, *The Life of Antony*

Those who were healed, Athanasius says, "were taught not to give thanks to Antony but to God alone."

### Into the wilderness

Hosea's words were first spoken to Israel. God's people had been unfaithful to Him, like a wife who had left her husband. You might expect God to send her away forever. Instead He says, "I am going to woo her." To "woo" is to win someone's love gently, the way a young man speaks to the woman he hopes to marry. And God says He will do it in the wilderness, where Israel first learned to trust Him after leaving Egypt (Hosea 2:15).

The wilderness in the Bible is a hard place, but also a place of meeting. Moses met God at the burning bush in the desert. Jesus "kept slipping away to places in the wilderness to pray" (Luke 5:16). In the wilderness, the noise of the world falls away, and God can "speak tenderly."

Antony went into a real desert. Most of us will not. Orthodox and Catholic Christians honor the monastic life of prayer that grew from his example. Many Protestants have questioned some later forms of it, while still loving Antony's devotion. All of us can learn from what he sought there: not escape from people, but closeness to God.

Notice what happened: Antony went away from people to pray, and people came to him. Andrew Murray, the South African pastor, explained why this happens:

> "Separation from men, in solitude with God is the sure, and the only way to live in oneness with men in the power of God's blessing."
> Andrew Murray, *The Inner Chamber*

### Three key insights

1. **God leads us into quiet places to speak tenderly to us, not to punish us.** Hosea's wilderness is a place of love. When God draws us away from the noise, He wants to win our hearts.
2. **One word from Scripture, heard with an open heart, can change a whole life.** Antony heard a Gospel reading he had perhaps heard many times, and he obeyed it that same day. God still speaks through His word to those who listen.
3. **Time alone with God makes us more useful to others, not less.** Antony went into the desert to pray, and he came out able to comfort, heal and guide many. Solitude with God is the root of love for people.

### For us today

Few believers are called to move to a desert mountain. But all of us need a "wilderness": a place and a time away from noise, where God can speak tenderly. For a mother in Accra it may be ten minutes before the children wake. For a student in Delhi it may be a walk with no phone. For a prisoner for Christ it may be the stillness of the heart, which no guard can take away.

Our wilderness may also be a season we did not choose: illness, loss, loneliness. The dry place may be where God speaks to us most tenderly.

And Antony reminds us to listen when the Bible is read, as if Jesus were speaking to us, and to obey at once.

### Try this

This week, make a small wilderness for prayer.

- **Find your desert.** Choose a quiet place and time each day, even fifteen minutes, with no phone, no radio and no screen.
- **Listen to one word.** Read Matthew 19:16-22 slowly. Ask, "Lord, is there one thing You are asking me to let go of?" Write down what comes to mind.
- **Pray Antony's counsel.** Antony told the monks who came to him, "Pray continually; avoid vainglory; sing psalms before sleep and on awaking." Sing or say a psalm before you sleep and when you wake.
- **Come back to serve.** Afterward, do one kind thing for someone in need.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You led Your people into the wilderness to speak tenderly to them. Draw me away from the noise, and speak to my heart. Thank You for Antony, who heard Your Son's words and obeyed at once, and who prayed in secret all his life. Show me what I must let go of to follow You. Give me a quiet place to meet You, and when I come out, make me gentle and useful to others. In the dry places I did not choose, let me hear Your voice. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Antony felt that a Bible reading "had been read on his account." Has a passage of Scripture ever spoken to you like that? What did you do?
- Where is your "wilderness," the place where God can speak to you without interruption?
- How can time alone with God make us better at loving other people?

## A Struggle to the Last Breath

> "Our fight is not with flesh and blood. It is with the rulers, with the authorities, with the world rulers of this darkness, with the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places." (Ephesians 6:12)

West of the green land of the Nile Delta, the ground turns to sand and stone. In the 300s this empty country was called Scetis. There was no town there and very little water. Yet by the time Antony died, many Christians were living in it. Each had a small cell of mud brick or stone. They wove baskets and ropes from palm leaves and sold them to buy bread. On Saturdays and Sundays they gathered to worship together, and then went back to their cells to pray.

One of these monks was a man named Agathon. In time other monks called him "Abba," which means "father," and came to him for advice. Once, in town to sell his baskets, he found a sick traveler lying alone in the street. Agathon rented a room and cared for him for four months, paying with his own work, until the man was well.

One day some of the brothers came to Agathon with a question. Of all the good things a Christian can do, they asked, which one takes the greatest effort? Fasting? Night watches? Giving everything away?

Agathon's answer surprised them. He told them that, as far as he could see, no labor is as great as prayer to God. When a person wants to pray, he said, the enemies of the soul try to stop him, because they know that if they can turn him away from prayer, they can stop his journey to God. With every other good work, if a person keeps at it, there comes a time of rest. But prayer is not like that. Prayer, he told them, is a battle that lasts to our last breath.

The story is told that as he lay dying, he stayed three days with his eyes open, as if looking at something. The brothers asked where he was. He told them he was standing before the judgment seat of God. He would not put his confidence in all his years of hard work, because, he said, the judgment of God is not like the judgment of people. And the brothers who were there said that he died with joy, like a man greeting his dearest friends.

### A desert full of praying people

These stories come from the *Sayings of the Desert Fathers*. For many years the monks passed them on by word of mouth, until they were written down in the late 400s and 500s, in Greek and soon in Latin, Syriac and Coptic. (A word of care: many sayings found online under the name of a desert father are not in these collections.)

After Antony, the desert filled with people. In Egypt there were great centers of monks at Nitria, Kellia ("the Cells") and Scetis. In the south, a former soldier named Pachomius gathered large numbers of monks into communities that lived and prayed together under a rule. In Palestine, a monk named Hilarion settled in the dry country near Gaza, and Jerome wrote his life. In Syria, a hermit named Simeon lived for many years on top of a stone pillar east of Antioch, and crowds came to hear him. Bishop Theodoret, who visited him, wrote about him while he was still alive.

And there were women. The collections call them "Amma," which means "mother," and they were honored as teachers. Amma Syncletica of Alexandria gave away her wealth to live a hidden life of prayer, and other women gathered around her. Amma Sarah, the sayings tell us, lived for sixty years beside a river. Amma Theodora discussed the Scriptures with the bishop of Alexandria, and many monks sought her advice.

### How they prayed

The desert mothers and fathers believed Paul's words about this fight. They spoke often of demons, and they meant real spiritual enemies, as Paul did. But most of the battles in their sayings are very ordinary: anger, pride, impure thoughts, laziness, discouragement, the wish to give up. They went to the desert to face these things honestly, before God.

So their first lesson was: **expect prayer to be hard.** Amma Theodora told of a monk who felt cold and feverish and had headaches every time he began to pray. At last he told himself that since he was ill and might soon die, he had better get up and pray before he died. So he forced himself to pray, and when he finished, the fever had gone. The things that keep us from prayer are often weaker than they seem.

Their second lesson was: **ask for strength, not only for escape.** Amma Sarah, the sayings say, struggled for many years against impure desires. But she never prayed that the battle would stop. She prayed only that God would give her strength.

Their third lesson was: **keep prayer simple.** When the brothers asked Abba Macarius how they should pray, he told them there was no need for long speeches. It is enough, he said, to stretch out your hands and ask the Lord to have mercy, as He wills and as He knows. And if the struggle grows fierce, simply cry to Him for help.

Their fourth lesson was: **keep going.** Abba Arsenius, who had once lived at the emperor's court, would stand on Saturday evening with the setting sun behind him and his hands raised toward heaven, and pray until the rising sun shone on his face. Few could do that, but all of them believed in returning to God again and again. Abba Poemen said that water is soft and stone is hard, yet water dripping on a stone wears it away. In the same way, he said, our hearts are hard, but "if it heareth continually the Word of God, the heart will be opened."

Many centuries later, E. M. Bounds wrote words the desert would have understood. By "closet" he meant a private room for prayer:

> "The closet is not an asylum for the indolent and worthless Christian. It is not a nursery where none but babes belong. It is the battlefield of the Church; its citadel; the scene of heroic and unearthly conflicts."
> E. M. Bounds, *Purpose in Prayer*

### Three key insights

1. **If prayer feels like a struggle, you are not failing; you are fighting.** Agathon, who spent his life in prayer, called it the hardest work of all. The tiredness and wandering thoughts that come when we kneel are part of the battle Paul describes.
2. **God's grace can be enough for a struggle He does not take away.** Amma Sarah did not pray for an easy life. She asked only for strength, and she kept standing. God may not remove every struggle, but His grace is enough for it (2 Corinthians 12:9).
3. **Coming back to God again and again slowly softens a hard heart.** Abba Poemen's water does its work drop by drop. A few humble words, such as "Lord, have mercy" or "Lord, help!", prayed day after day, keep turning us back to God, and He does the rest.

### For us today

Most of us will never live in a desert. We live in crowded cities, in villages, in small rooms full of family. But the desert mothers and fathers did not think the desert was magic. Amma Syncletica warned that a person can live on a mountain and still have a mind full of the noise of the town, while another can live in a crowd and keep a quiet heart before God.

In chapter 6 we saw that Paul's teaching on the armor of God ends with prayer, and that he asks us to keep watch, never giving up (Ephesians 6:18). Keep watch. Never give up. That is the whole teaching of the desert.

This is a word for believers who suffer for their faith today. Where Christians are watched, threatened or put in prison, the real fight is not with the officials or the neighbors who hate them, who are only flesh and blood. It is a spiritual fight, and it is fought on our knees, often with very few words. Peter wrote to suffering Christians: "Stand up to him and hold firm in your faith. You know that your brothers and sisters all over the world are enduring these same sufferings" (1 Peter 5:9). You are not alone in the fight.

And if you find prayer hard, take comfort: so did Agathon, to his last breath. Do not wait until prayer feels easy. Pray now, as you are.

### Try this

This week, practice the simple prayers of the desert.

- **Begin with open hands.** When you sit down to pray, open your hands in front of you and use Macarius's words as your own: Lord, as You will, and as You know, have mercy.
- **Use a short cry in the battle.** When anger, fear or temptation rises during the day, pray at once, "Lord, help!"
- **Ask for strength.** Name one struggle that God has not yet taken away. Pray as Amma Sarah did, and ask God for strength to stand.
- **Notice your excuses.** When you feel too tired or too busy to pray, pray for just two minutes anyway, as Theodora's monk did. Notice what happens.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, You fought the great battle for us on the cross, and You won. Teach me to stand in Your strength and not my own. When prayer is hard, keep me from giving up. When my thoughts wander, bring them back to You. When temptation comes, I do not ask only for an easy road; give me strength for the fight. Thank You for the mothers and fathers of the desert who prayed to their last breath. Make me faithful like them, until I see Your face with joy. Lord, as You will, and as You know, have mercy on me. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What usually happens when you try to pray? What gets in the way?
- Why do you think Agathon called prayer the hardest of all good works?
- Is there a struggle in your life where you could pray for strength instead of only for escape?

## "God, Come and Rescue Me"

> "God, come and rescue me! LORD, be quick to help me!" (Psalm 70:1)

Two young men sat in a cell in the Egyptian desert, deeply troubled. Their names were John Cassian and Germanus. They had come from a monastery in Bethlehem to learn from the old monks of Egypt how to pray.

A few days before, they had visited a wise old man named Abba Isaac, and he had spoken to them about prayer so beautifully that they could not stop thinking about it. He had described a heart always turned toward God. It sounded wonderful, and they had no idea how to begin.

Then something happened that troubled them even more. Every year the bishop of Alexandria sent a letter to all the churches and monasteries of Egypt, announcing the date of Easter. That year, the letter also taught that God does not have a human body. Many simple monks had always pictured God in their prayers as a great man, and they were upset. One of them was an old monk named Sarapion, who had lived a strict and holy life in the desert for fifty years. A visiting deacon patiently explained from the Scriptures how the church understood our being made in God's image. Sarapion accepted the teaching, and the brothers stood up to give thanks.

But as they prayed together, the old man suddenly fell to the ground, weeping. The picture of God that he had always held in his mind when he prayed was gone, and he did not know how to pray without it. Cassian later wrote down his cry: "Alas! Wretched man that I am! They have taken away my God from me, and I have now none to lay hold of; and whom to worship and address I know not."

The two young men were shaken. If a man who had prayed for fifty years could feel so lost, what hope was there for them? So they went back to Abba Isaac and asked him plainly: how can a person keep God in mind all the time? Where do we start?

Isaac's answer was surprisingly simple. He gave them a short prayer to carry everywhere, which he had received from a few of the oldest fathers still living. It was one verse from the Psalms. In the old English translation of Cassian it reads: "O God, make speed to save me: O Lord, make haste to help me." Say it, Isaac told them, in every situation, and never stop.

### Who he was

John Cassian was born around 360, most likely in the region near the mouth of the river Danube, in what is now Romania and Bulgaria. With Germanus he spent many years among the monks of Egypt. Later he went to Constantinople, where the great preacher John Chrysostom ordained him a deacon, and then to Rome.

Around 415 he settled in Marseille, a port city in the south of what is now France. There he founded two communities of prayer, one for men and one for women. And there, at the request of bishops in the region, he did something that would change the Western church. He wrote down what he had learned in Egypt. His *Institutes* describe how the desert monks lived. His *Conferences* tell of his conversations with the old men of the desert. Abba Isaac's teaching on this verse is in the tenth Conference.

We should be honest about how these books were made. Cassian wrote them some twenty-five years or more after the conversations, and he shaped them as a teacher. But they carried the wisdom of the desert to the Latin-speaking West. About a century later, an Italian monk named Benedict told his monks to read Cassian's *Conferences*, and in his Rule (chapter 18) he told them to begin the hours of prayer with this same verse from Psalm 70. We will meet Benedict in Volume 5. Through him, this little prayer has been said by monks and nuns, morning and evening, for fifteen hundred years.

Not everyone agreed with everything Cassian taught: some followers of Augustine questioned what he wrote about grace and free will. But on prayer, churches of East and West alike have gladly learned from him.

### How to pray one verse

Why this verse? Isaac said it fits every feeling and every situation a human being can face. In Cassian's words, "it contains an invocation of God against every danger." It is a cry for help, a confession of weakness, and a statement of trust, all at once. And it is never out of date, "for one who is constantly calling on his protector, is certain that He is always at hand."

Isaac then gave a long list of moments when the verse should be prayed. When we are tempted to eat too much, and when we have no appetite at all. When we cannot sleep, and when sleep comes too soon over our reading. When we are angry or proud or discouraged. When our thoughts wander and we cannot pray at all. But also, and this is the surprise, when things are going well: when we feel joy in God, when we have won a battle against sin, when the Scriptures suddenly open to us. Then too, he said, we must pray, "O God, make speed to save me," so that we do not become proud and fall.

He wanted the verse to fill the whole day. "When you wake let it be the first thing to come into your mind," he said. Pray it while working, traveling and eating, and fall asleep still saying it.

Isaac also saw a deeper gift in it. A person who prays this verse all day becomes, as he put it, content with "the poverty of this one verse." Psalm 70 ends the same way it begins: "As for me, I am poor and in need; God, come to me quickly!" (Psalm 70:5). To pray it honestly is to admit that we have nothing of our own, and to receive everything from God. That is the poverty Jesus blessed: the poor in spirit.

Centuries later, in London, the preacher Charles Spurgeon gave his busy hearers the same advice:

> "While your hands are busy with the world, let your hearts still talk with God; not in twenty sentences at a time, for such an interval might be inconsistent with your calling, but in broken sentences and interjections."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Mighty Power in Prayer*

### Three key insights

1. **One short verse can carry us through the whole day.** Abba Isaac did not tell the young monks to find more time for long prayers. He gave them a few words they could take everywhere: into work, travel, meals and sleep.
2. **We need God's help in our joys as much as in our troubles.** Isaac taught that the verse is for good days too. When things go well, we are in danger of forgetting God, so we cry, "Be quick to help me," even then.
3. **Prayer is learned from those who walked the road before us.** Isaac received this verse from "the oldest fathers," Cassian carried it to the West, and Benedict passed it on. We do not have to invent prayer. We can receive it.

### For us today

Many people think prayer belongs only in church or in a quiet room. But most of the world's believers have very little quiet. A mother in Nairobi with a baby on her back, a factory worker in Dhaka, a taxi driver in São Paulo, a nurse on a long night shift: all of them can pray, "God, come and rescue me! LORD, be quick to help me!" It takes three seconds, whispered or said in the heart.

This is a prayer for frightened people. Believers who are threatened for their faith, or who wait for news in a hospital corridor, or who lie awake worrying about money, do not need many words. Isaac called this verse "an impregnable wall" for those under attack. It is also a prayer for proud people, which means all of us. When our work goes well and others praise us, it reminds us whose help we depend on.

Old Sarapion's tears also hold a gentle lesson. When our picture of God is too small, it may break, and that can be painful. But the God who is greater than all our pictures is still near. We can always call on Him, even when we do not understand Him. The verse does not need a picture. It needs only a cry.

### Try this

For one week, make Psalm 70:1 your companion.

- **Learn it by heart.** Say it in your own language, as it is in your own Bible. Write it on a card or on the wall by your door.
- **Make it your first words.** Before you get out of bed, say it once, slowly, as Isaac advised.
- **Pray it in the hard moments.** When you feel anger, fear, temptation or tiredness, pray it at once, silently.
- **Pray it in the good moments.** When something good happens, thank God, and then pray the verse again, asking Him to keep you humble and close to Him.

### A closing prayer

God, come and rescue me! LORD, be quick to help me! I am poor and in need, and I have no strength of my own. Be my help when I am tempted and when I am tired, when I am afraid and when I am glad. Keep my heart turned toward You through every hour of this day. When I wake, let my first thought be of You, and when I lie down, let my last word be Yours. Thank You for the old fathers who handed down this prayer. Teach me to pray it until it becomes the cry of my whole life, through Jesus Christ my Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Abba Isaac gave the young monks one short verse instead of a long method?
- Why might we need to ask for God's help when things are going well?
- What short prayer or verse could you carry with you through the day?

- Francis de Sales, *Introduction to the Devout Life*, especially "Aspirations, Ejaculatory Prayer and Holy Thoughts"
- Jeremy Taylor, *The Rule and Exercises of Holy Living*, especially "Of Prayer"

## A Home Made a House of Prayer

> "I put my spirit into Your hand for safekeeping; You have redeemed me, LORD, God who is faithful." (Psalm 31:5)

In the year 379, or very soon after, a bishop named Gregory was traveling home through the hills of Pontus, in the north of what is now Turkey. He was grieving. His older brother Basil, the famous bishop of Caesarea, had died about nine months before. Troubles in the church, including exile under an emperor who opposed his faith, had kept Gregory from visiting his eldest sister for nearly eight years. Now he turned aside to see her.

Her name was Macrina. She lived on the family's old country estate in Pontus, near the river Iris, at a place usually called Annisa, where she led a community of women who had given their lives to prayer. As he drew near, a servant told him that she was very ill. He hurried on.

When he arrived, the men of the community came out to greet him, and the women waited quietly in the church. Then someone led him to the house where Macrina lay. She was not on a bed. She was lying on the floor, on a sack spread over a board, with another board to support her head. When she saw her brother at the door, she could not stand, but she raised herself on her elbow to greet him. Then she lifted her hand to God and prayed aloud. Gregory remembered her words: "This favour also Thou hast granted me, O God, and hast not deprived me of my desire, because Thou hast stirred up Thy servant to visit Thy handmaid."

For the rest of that day, though her breathing was hard, she talked with him about the soul, about death and about the resurrection. When Gregory was sad, she gently scolded him for forgetting how good God had been to their family.

The next day it was clear that she was dying. Her bed had been turned to face the east, and as the sun went down, she stopped speaking to those around her and spoke only to God, in a low whisper, with her hands lifted. Gregory wrote down her prayer. She thanked Christ for freeing us from the fear of death and for opening the way of resurrection. She asked Him to forgive her sins, and to remember her, as He remembered the thief on the cross. And she ended with words that echo Psalm 31: "may my soul be received into Thy hands spotless and undefiled, as an offering before Thee."

As night came, a lamp was brought in. Macrina opened her eyes and looked toward the light. She wanted to join in the evening thanksgiving that the community sang when the lamps were lit, but her voice had gone. So she prayed it in her heart, moving her hands and her lips. When she finished, she made the sign of the cross. Then, Gregory wrote, "she drew a great deep breath and closed her life and her prayer together."

### Who she was

We know Macrina's story because Gregory, who became bishop of Nyssa and one of the great teachers of the Eastern church, wrote her *Life* soon after her death. In another book he calls her simply "the Teacher."

She was born around 327, the eldest of nine children, into a family that had suffered for Christ. Gregory tells us that one set of grandparents lost all their property for confessing Christ, and their mother's father was put to death by the emperor. As a girl Macrina learned the Psalms by heart. Gregory says that when she got up, worked, rested, ate or went to bed, "the Psalter was her constant companion, like a good fellow-traveller that never deserted her."

When she was about twelve, her father arranged for her to marry a fine young man, but he died suddenly. Macrina chose not to marry anyone else. Christians of many traditions honor that kind of choice, and she made it freely. She stayed at her mother's side, cooked for her with her own hands, and after her father's death helped her run the family's lands.

Then came her brother Basil, home from his studies in Athens, very proud of his gifts as a speaker. Gregory says that Macrina "took him in hand," and drew him toward a humble life of prayer and work. Basil later became a great bishop, and his writings for monks still shape Orthodox monastic life today. But it was his sister who turned him first.

### How she prayed

Slowly, Macrina persuaded her mother to change the way their household lived. They gave up their comfortable life and lived with their servants and slave girls as if they were sisters, eating the same food and sleeping on the same kind of bed. The family estate became a community of women, and later a community of men grew up beside it, where her youngest brother Peter, whom she had raised herself, played a leading part. When a terrible famine came, so many hungry people crowded in for food that, Gregory says, "the desert seemed a city."

At the center of this home was prayer. Gregory describes their life as "the unceasing round of prayer and endless hymnody, co-extensive with time itself, practised by night and day." He adds, with a play on words, that for them prayer was their work, and what others called work was their rest.

That is why her death was not a sudden change. She had prayed the Psalms all her life, so she died with the Psalms on her lips. Her last prayer echoes Psalm 31:5, the words Jesus Himself prayed from the cross: "Father, I place My spirit in Your hands" (Luke 23:46). Through the centuries, Christians have learned to pray this verse every night, and the night prayers of many churches still use it. The English writer Jeremy Taylor gave his readers this prayer for bedtime:

> "Into thy hands, most blessed Jesus, I commend my soul and body, for thou hast redeemed both with thy precious blood. So bless and sanctify my sleep unto me that it may be temperate, holy, and safe; a refreshment to my wearied body, to enable it so to serve my soul, that both may serve thee with a never-failing duty."
> Jeremy Taylor, *The Rule and Exercises of Holy Living*

Notice, too, Macrina's thankfulness. When Gregory told her of his exile and his troubles, she did not pity him. She asked him whether he could not see the grace of God in all that had happened to him, and told him that it was their parents' prayers "that are lifting you up on high." Even while dying, she was teaching her brother to give thanks.

### Three key insights

1. **Any home can become a house of prayer.** Macrina did not build a great monastery. She began with her own family, her own kitchen and her own daily duties, and slowly filled them with prayer and Psalms.
2. **Psalms learned by heart stay with us when our strength is gone.** Macrina prayed the Psalms every day for fifty years, so when her strength failed, they were still there. The verses we learn now will carry us when we can no longer carry ourselves.
3. **Thanksgiving is a medicine for grief.** Grieving for Basil and burdened by his own troubles, Gregory found his sister counting God's blessings. Gratitude did not remove his sorrow, but it lifted his eyes.

### For us today

Most of us will not turn our homes into monasteries. But every Christian home can become a house of prayer. In many parts of Africa, Asia and Latin America, families still pray together in the morning or evening, sometimes with grandparents, cousins and neighbors in one room. That is a treasure. Where it has been lost, it can begin again with one person, as it did with Macrina.

Her home was also a place where rank did not matter. Rich and poor, family and servants, prayed and ate together. In a world that still divides people by wealth, tribe and class, a praying home can show a different way.

And Macrina teaches us how to face death. Many readers of this book have sat beside a dying parent, or will. Some live where death comes early, through illness, poverty or violence. Psalm 31:5 is a prayer for those moments. It is short enough to whisper when there is no strength left. "I put my spirit into Your hand for safekeeping." Christians do not pray it in despair, but in trust, because the hand we place ourselves into belongs to the God who has redeemed us.

### Try this

This week, make your home a little more like Macrina's.

- **Pray Psalm 31:5 every night.** Before you sleep, say: "I put my spirit into Your hand for safekeeping; You have redeemed me, LORD, God who is faithful."
- **Light a lamp and give thanks.** At sunset or when you turn on the lights, stop for one minute and thank God for the day, as Macrina's community did.
- **Pray with your household.** Gather whoever lives with you, even just one other person, and read one psalm aloud together.
- **Name God's gifts.** When you feel sorry for yourself, write down three good things God has given you or your family, as Macrina helped Gregory to see his.

### A closing prayer

Faithful God, I put my spirit into Your hand. You have redeemed me through Jesus Christ, who prayed these words on the cross. Make my home a house of prayer. Fill our kitchens and our work, our mornings and our evenings, with Your praise. Teach me the Psalms, so that they become my companions for life. Give me a thankful heart in times of grief. And when my last day comes, may I close my life and my prayer together, resting in Your hands. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What would it take for your home to become a little more of a house of prayer?
- Why do you think Macrina could pray so calmly at the end of her life?
- Gregory was grieving, and Macrina helped him give thanks. When has thankfulness helped you in a time of sorrow?

- Thomas Watson, *A Body of Divinity*, especially "A Believer's Privilege at Death"

## Prayer in Song

> "Let the word of Christ make its home among you richly, in all wisdom. Teach and warn one another with psalms, hymns and Spirit-given songs, and sing to God, thankful from the heart." (Colossians 3:16)

Edessa was a busy city on the eastern edge of the Roman Empire, in what is now southeastern Turkey. Its people spoke Syriac, a form of Aramaic, the language Jesus Himself had spoken. And in the 360s, Edessa was full of songs.

Not all of them were Christian. Some teachers in the city spread ideas that the church believed were false: that the stars rule our lives, or that the body will not be raised. Years before, a clever poet named Bardaisan and his son Harmonius had set such teaching to beautiful tunes. People sang their songs in the streets and at home, and the ideas went down with the melody. Ephrem later said of Bardaisan, "He blended for the simple poison with sweetness."

Into this city came a refugee. His name was Ephrem. He was a deacon from Nisibis, a frontier city about 200 kilometers to the east. Nisibis had been attacked again and again by the armies of Persia. In 363, after a Roman emperor died in battle, the new emperor gave the city to the Persians to save his army, and many Christians had to leave their homes. Ephrem, now nearly sixty years old, was one of them.

Ephrem was a poet, and he understood the power of a song. A Greek church historian named Sozomen, writing about seventy years later, says that when Ephrem saw how the people of Edessa were charmed by the melodies of Harmonius, he studied those same tunes and wrote new songs to them, filled with the true faith. Soon, Sozomen says, the Syrians were singing Ephrem's songs instead.

But who would sing them? Here Ephrem did something new. A later Syriac poet, Jacob of Serugh, who wrote a poem in his honor, tells us that Ephrem gathered the "daughters of the covenant," women in the church who had given their lives to prayer and service. He taught them his hymns, and they sang them in the church. Picture it: a choir of women singing one verse after another about Christ, the Word made flesh, the Cross, the Resurrection, while the whole congregation joined in the short refrain. Many in the crowd could not read. But they could sing, and as they sang, they learned what the church believed.

### Who he was

Ephrem was born around 306 in Nisibis. He grew up under its bishop, Jacob, who made him a teacher in the church. He was ordained a deacon, and as far as we know he never became a priest. Most of his life he lived simply, without marrying, teaching and writing.

He lived through three sieges of Nisibis by the Persian king Shapur. In his *Nisibene Hymns*, written in those years, he speaks to the city itself, and tells her why God saved her: "The prayer of thy inhabitants, sufficed for thy deliverance; it was not that they were righteous, but that they were penitent."

In Edessa he wrote hundreds of hymns, and he taught in the church there for the last ten years of his life. He wrote about Christ's birth, His death and His resurrection, about the Church, about faith and about the beauty of God's world. Again and again he ends a verse with a refrain of praise. One hymn ends, "Blessed be He Who has made me His harp!" Ever since, Syriac Christians have called him "the Harp of the Spirit."

He did not only sing. Sozomen tells that shortly before Ephrem died, a famine struck Edessa. Ephrem left his quiet cell and rebuked the rich for letting the poor die around them. They answered that they did not know anyone honest enough to share out their gifts. Ephrem offered to do it himself. He set up about three hundred beds in the covered walkways of the city and cared for the sick and starving, both local people and strangers. When the famine ended, he went back to his cell, and a few days later he died. The year was 373.

### How he prayed

For Ephrem, a hymn was a prayer. Many of his hymns speak directly to God, and the people who sang them were praying as they sang. They also taught. That is exactly what Paul describes in Colossians 3:16: the word of Christ living richly among believers, as they "teach and warn one another with psalms, hymns and Spirit-given songs."

Ephrem was humble about his songs. He knew that no human words could ever praise God enough. At the end of one Christmas hymn he prays: "Ocean of glory Who needest not to have Thy glory sung, take in Thy goodness this drop of praise." That is a beautiful picture of prayer. God is an ocean. Our praise is one drop. And still He receives it gladly.

His hymns spread far beyond Edessa. They were translated into Greek, Armenian and other languages, and Ephrem became so loved that, in later centuries, many writings were attached to his name that he did not write. One of them may be the short prayer that Orthodox Christians pray every weekday in Lent, called the Prayer of St Ephrem. No one can be sure it comes from him, but it is full of his humble spirit. In one common English form it ends: "Yes, O Lord and King, grant me to see my own sins and not to judge my brother."

Singing hymns soon spread to the Western church too. Augustine of Hippo was baptized in Milan in 387, the year after the church there began to sing hymns and psalms "after the manner of the Eastern Churches." He never forgot it:

> "How did I weep, in Thy Hymns and Canticles, touched to the quick by the voices of Thy sweet-attuned Church! The voices flowed into mine ears, and the Truth distilled into my heart, whence the affections of my devotion overflowed, and tears ran down, and happy was I therein."
> Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions*

### Three key insights

1. **What we sing, we remember, and what we remember shapes what we believe.** Ephrem knew that a melody carries words deep into the heart. That is why he filled his songs with the truth about Christ, and why we should choose our songs with care.
2. **Singing together is praying together.** When a congregation sings to God, every voice becomes part of one prayer. Ephrem's choirs of women show that this prayer belongs to the whole church, not only to its leaders.
3. **True praise leads to love for the poor.** The poet who sang of Christ's coming to the poor and humble spent his last strength caring for starving strangers. Songs of praise that never move our hands are not yet complete.

### For us today

All over the world, the church still learns its faith by singing. In villages across Africa, believers who cannot read sing long songs that tell the whole story of the gospel. In Korea and China, house churches have learned hymns by heart. In Latin America, new songs carry the Psalms into everyday language. Christians in prison have sung in their cells, as Paul and Silas did at midnight (Acts 16:25). And in Iraq, Syria, southern India and wherever their people have scattered, churches of the Syriac tradition still sing Ephrem's own hymns today.

So sing your prayers. You do not need a good voice. You need only a thankful heart, as Paul says: "sing to God, thankful from the heart." E. M. Bounds saw how closely the two belong together:

> "Praise is so distinctly and definitely wedded to prayer, so inseparably joined, that they cannot be divorced. Praise is dependent on prayer for its full volume and its sweetest melody."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Essentials of Prayer*

Take care, too, with what you sing. The people of Edessa learned false ideas from beautiful tunes. A song with a lovely melody and empty words can still shape our hearts. Ask of every song: Does it tell the truth about God? Does it lead me to love Him and my neighbor?

### Try this

This week, let your prayers become songs.

- **Sing a psalm.** Choose a short psalm, such as Psalm 100 or Psalm 121, and sing it to any simple tune you know.
- **Pray a hymn.** Take a favorite hymn or worship song and read the words slowly as a prayer, without music.
- **Make a refrain.** Like Ephrem, choose one short line of praise, such as "Blessed be the Lord!", and sing or say it after each thing you thank God for.
- **Let praise move your hands.** Do one practical thing for someone in need this week, as Ephrem did in the famine.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, You are worthy of every song in heaven and on earth. Thank You for Ephrem, the harp of Your Spirit, and for all who have taught Your truth in song. Let Your word make its home in me richly. Put a new song in my mouth, and teach me to sing with thanks in my heart. Ocean of glory, receive my small drop of praise. Keep me from songs that lead away from You, and make my praise reach my hands, so that I love the poor as You do. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Which songs or hymns have taught you the most about God? What have they taught you?
- Why do you think Ephrem chose to teach the faith through songs instead of only through sermons?
- How could your singing on Sunday become a real prayer, and not only music?

## Prayer, the Light of the Soul

> "You make my lamp shine; the LORD my God brightens my darkness." (Psalm 18:28)

In the summer of the year 407, an old man was being marched along the roads of Pontus, a hilly country on the southern shore of the Black Sea. He was thin and often ill. The soldiers who guarded him had orders to take him to Pityus, a lonely place at the far eastern edge of the empire, and many believed they had been told to make the journey hard. They walked him through blazing sun and heavy rain, and would not let him rest in the towns.

The old man was John, the bishop of Constantinople, the capital of the eastern Roman Empire. A few years before, crowds had filled the great church to hear him preach. Now he was a prisoner with no city, no church and no rights.

We know how his journey ended because Palladius, a bishop who was John's friend, wrote an account of his life soon after his death. Palladius tells us that the guards brought John to a town called Comana and led him on, past the town, to a small chapel built over the grave of a martyr named Basiliscus. That night, Palladius says, John saw the martyr in a dream. Basiliscus told him to take courage, because the next day they would be together.

In the morning John begged to rest there a few more hours. The soldiers refused and set off again. But after a few miles he was so ill that they had to turn back to the chapel. There John asked for white clothes. He took off the clothes he was wearing, put on the white ones, and gave the rest of his things away to those who were there. He received the bread and cup of the Lord's Supper. Then he prayed in front of those who stood around him.

Palladius says he used his customary words, the phrase he had spoken again and again through all his troubles: "Glory to God for all things." He said "Amen," and he died. It was September 407. He was about sixty years old.

Long after his death, the Greek-speaking church gave him a new name: *Chrysostom*, which means "Golden Mouth." We still know him as John Chrysostom.

### Who he was

John was born around the year 347 in Antioch in Syria, one of the leading cities of the Roman East. His father, a high-ranking army officer, died soon after John was born. His mother was left a widow while still very young, and she did not marry again, so that she could give herself to raising her son. In that home a young widow taught a boy to love God.

He trained in public speaking under a famous pagan teacher, Libanius, and seemed set for a career in the law courts. But the Scriptures drew him away. After his baptism he lived for some years as a hermit in the hills near Antioch, praying and learning the Bible by heart, until his health broke and he came back to the city.

In 386 he was made a priest in Antioch, and for twelve years he preached there, explaining whole books of the Bible verse by verse in plain words that craftsmen and widows could follow. He spoke strongly against the luxury of the rich and the neglect of the poor. You can read his *Homilies on Matthew* and *Homilies on Romans* on Ochorus today.

In 398 he was taken, almost by force, to Constantinople and made its bishop. There he cut back the costly household of the bishop's palace, used the money to build hospitals for the sick, and kept preaching as plainly as before, even against the sins of the powerful. He made enemies, among them the empress Eudoxia and a rival bishop, Theophilus of Alexandria. In 403 they had him condemned and sent away. The people protested, and he was brought back, but in 404 he was exiled for good.

Even in exile he kept praying and writing. Many of his letters from those years went to Olympias, a widow and deaconess in Constantinople who was suffering for her loyalty to him. Orthodox Christians still pray Sunday by Sunday with the Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom. Scholars debate how much of it goes back to him, but its name shows how the church of the East honors him as a teacher of prayer.

### What he taught about prayer

There is a beautiful homily on prayer that has come down to us under John's name. It calls prayer the light of the soul. It describes prayer as a conversation with God, and it urges believers not to keep prayer for special hours only, but to turn to God through the whole day and night. We should be honest: it is printed among the writings whose authorship is doubtful, and many scholars think a later preacher wrote it. But the picture is true to the Bible. David sang, "You make my lamp shine; the LORD my God brightens my darkness" (Psalm 18:28). When we pray, we bring our darkness to the One who can light it.

We do not need to depend on that homily to hear John himself. In his sermons on Matthew, preached in Antioch, he explained Jesus' words about praying in secret (Matthew 6:5-8). He told the noisy, crowded church that God listens to the heart:

> "Let us not then make our prayer by the gesture of our body, nor by the loudness of our voice, but by the earnestness of our mind: neither with noise and clamor and for display, so as even to disturb those that are near us, but with all modesty, and with contrition in the mind, and with inward tears."
> John Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew*

He reminded them that Hannah prayed without a sound, and God heard her, "forasmuch as her heart cried out." Then he asked a question every praying person asks. If God already knows what we need, why should we pray at all? His answer is one of the wisest things ever said about prayer:

> "Not to instruct Him, but to prevail with Him; to be made intimate with Him, by continuance in supplication; to be humbled; to be reminded of thy sins."
> John Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew*

Prayer does not give God information. It draws us near to Him. It makes us His close friends. That is why John could pray in a full cathedral and in a cold chapel at the end of the road.

E. M. Bounds copied out a passage that he credited to Chrysostom. Bounds gives no source for it, but it shows how later Christians heard him:

> "Prayer is an all-efficient panoply, a treasure undiminished, a mine which is never exhausted, a sky unobscured by clouds, a heaven unruffled by the storm. It is the root, the fountain, the mother of a thousand blessings."
> E. M. Bounds, *Purpose in Prayer*

(A "panoply" is a soldier's complete set of armor.)

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer brings God's light into our darkest places.** John's last journey was dark in every way: sickness, injustice, loneliness and the end of his life. Yet he died praising God. Prayer did not take the darkness away, but it let God's light shine in it.
2. **We do not need a loud voice or a quiet room for God to hear us.** We do not need loud words, a special posture or a crowd to watch us. Like Hannah, we can pray with our lips barely moving, and God hears the cry of the heart.
3. **We pray not to inform God but to become His close friends.** God already knows what we need. In prayer we are humbled, we remember our sins and His mercy, and we are drawn into friendship with Him.

### For us today

Many readers of this book live where John lived at the end: far from home, under the power of people who do not wish them well. Some are refugees. Some are in prison, or pushed out of their families, for following Jesus. John's story says that no road is so dark that God cannot meet us on it. A guard may take away your freedom and your friends, but not your prayer.

Others live in noisy, crowded places: one room for a whole family, a busy market, a full bus. John told people like that that God hears "even before the voice." You can pray in your heart while you cook, sell or walk to the field.

And John's last words are a gift to us all. He said "Glory to God for all things" after losing his church, his health and his home. To thank God in all things is not to pretend that evil is good. It is to trust that God is still good in the middle of it.

### Try this

This week, bring your darkness into the light of prayer.

- **Light a lamp.** One evening, light a lamp or a candle, read Psalm 18:28 slowly, and tell God plainly about one dark place in your life.
- **Pray from the heart, without a sound.** Choose a busy moment in your day and pray silently, like Hannah. Let your heart speak to God while your hands keep working.
- **Say John's prayer.** When something goes wrong, stop and say, "Glory to God for all things." Then tell God honestly how you feel about it.
- **Write to an Olympias.** Pray for one believer who is suffering for their faith, and send them a short message of encouragement.

### A closing prayer

Lord my God, You make my lamp shine. You brighten my darkness. Thank You for Your servant John, who prayed to You to the end of a hard road. Teach me to pray from my heart and not for show. Draw me near to You, not because You need to hear my news, but because You want me as Your friend. When I am far from home, be my home. And in every joy and every sorrow, give me grace to say: glory to God for all things. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- John Chrysostom died far from home, praising God. What do you think made that possible?
- Why do you think God wants us to pray, when He already knows what we need?
- Where is the darkest place in your life right now? What would it mean to bring it to God in prayer?

- John Chrysostom, *On the Priesthood*, especially "Book VI"

## The Child of Those Tears

> "Get up and cry aloud in the night, as each watch of the night begins; empty out your heart like water before the Lord's face. Raise your hands to Him for the lives of your little ones..." (Lamentations 2:19)

It was night at the harbor of Carthage, the great city on the coast of North Africa, in the year 383. A ship was waiting for the wind to sail for Rome. A young teacher named Augustine, about twenty-nine years old, meant to be on it. His mother, Monica, had followed him down to the sea. She held on to him, begging him to stay or to take her with him.

He did neither. He told her a lie. He said that a friend of his was sailing and that he only wanted to keep the friend company until the wind was right. He persuaded her to spend the night in a small chapel near the ship, built in memory of Cyprian, the martyred bishop of Carthage, whom we met in chapter 8.

So Monica went into the chapel to pray, and Augustine slipped away in the dark. Years later Augustine wrote it down in his *Confessions*, a long prayer to God telling the story of his life:

> "That night I privily departed, but she was not behind in weeping and prayer."
> Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions*

What was she asking for, with so many tears? That God would not let him sail. The wind rose, the sails filled, and the shore disappeared behind him. In the morning Monica stood at the water's edge, overcome with grief, and found the ship gone. She had prayed all night for one thing, and God had not given it.

And yet Augustine says she did not stop. After she had grieved, and complained of his cruelty, she "betook herself again to intercede to Thee for me." She went home and kept praying.

### Who she was

Monica was born around the year 331 in Thagaste, a small town in the Roman province of Numidia, in what is now Algeria. She grew up in a Christian home. Augustine tells us that an old servant woman of the family trained her in self-control and the fear of God.

She was married young to Patricius, a town councillor who was not a Christian and who had a hot temper. Other women in the town were often beaten by their husbands, and they wondered why Monica never was. She told them her secret: she never answered her husband while he was angry, but waited until he was calm. She bore his unfaithfulness patiently and prayed for him. Toward the end of his life, Augustine says, she won him to Christ.

Augustine was born in 354. He was brilliant and restless. As a student in Carthage he lived with a woman outside marriage and had a son by her. He joined a sect called the Manichees, whose teaching was far from the Christian faith. Monica grieved over him as if he had died. Augustine says she wept for him "more than mothers weep the bodily deaths of their children," and that her tears "watered the ground under her eyes in every place where she prayed."

At one point she begged a bishop in Africa to talk with her son and argue him out of his errors. The bishop wisely refused. Augustine was too proud to listen yet, he said; "only pray God for him." When she would not stop pleading with him, and her tears kept falling, he sent her away with words she never forgot:

> "Go thy ways and God bless thee, for it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish."
> Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions*

Augustine says she took this answer "as if it had sounded from heaven." It would be years more before her son came home to God.

### How she prayed

Monica's prayers were not elegant. They were tears. Augustine pictures her coming to church "twice a day, morning and evening," not for gossip, "but that she might hear Thee in Thy discourses, and Thou her in her prayers." She asked God, he says, not for gold or silver, but for the salvation of her son's soul.

The strangest part of her story is that night in Carthage. Her prayer was refused. But Augustine, looking back as a bishop, saw that the refusal was the answer. If he had stayed in Africa, he would never have gone to Milan, never have heard the great bishop Ambrose preach, and never have found Christ there. He wrote this to God:

> "But Thou, in the depth of Thy counsels and hearing the main point of her desire, regardest not what she then asked, that Thou mightest make me what she ever asked."
> Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions*

Arthur T. Pierson, telling the story of George Müller, put it in one plain sentence:

> "God fulfilled the mother's desire while denying her request."
> Arthur T. Pierson, *George Müller of Bristol*

Monica followed him across the sea to Milan. Bishop Ambrose came to love her, and would congratulate Augustine on having such a mother. And in the summer of 386, in a garden in Milan, the son of those tears gave his life to Christ. We will stand in that garden in the next chapter. Here we only need to see Monica's face when Augustine and his friend Alypius came in to tell her. "She leaps for joy," he wrote, "and triumpheth, and blesseth Thee." God had given her more than she had asked.

The next year they set out for home together. At Ostia, the port of Rome, while they waited for a ship, Monica and Augustine stood alone at a window looking into a garden, talking about the life of the saints with God. In that conversation, Augustine says, they seemed for a moment to reach up toward God Himself. Then Monica said:

> "One thing there was for which I desired to linger for a while in this life, that I might see thee a Catholic Christian before I died. My God hath done this for me more abundantly, that I should now see thee withal, despising earthly happiness, become His servant: what do I here?"
> Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions*

A few days later she fell ill with a fever. She asked her sons not to worry about where her body was buried: "this only I request, that you would remember me at the Lord's altar, wherever you be." (Christians of different traditions understand prayer for those who have died in different ways. All of us can see in her words a heart that wanted to stay close to her family in Christ.) When someone asked if she was afraid to die so far from home, she answered, "Nothing is far to God." She died in the fifty-sixth year of her age, in 387. Augustine was thirty-three.

### Three key insights

1. **Tears poured out before God are a kind of prayer.** Monica did not have fine words. She emptied out her heart like water before the Lord, as Lamentations says. God saw every tear.
2. **God may refuse our request in order to grant our deepest desire.** Monica asked that her son would not sail. God let him sail, so that he would become what she always asked. A "no" from God may be part of a greater "yes."
3. **Prayer for a wandering child is never wasted.** Monica prayed for many years, and for a long time nothing seemed to change. But God was at work the whole time, and her prayers were answered beyond what she imagined.

### For us today

Some of you are praying for a son or daughter who has walked away from God, or for a husband who is harsh, as Patricius was. Some have children who have gone far away to find work, and you do not know how they are living. Monica's story is for you.

R. A. Torrey, the American Bible teacher, saw in her story a lesson every praying parent needs:

> "How utterly all of Monica's efforts and entreaties failed with her son, but her prayers prevailed with God, and the dissolute youth became St. Augustine, the mighty man of God."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

Notice what the wise bishop told her: talk less, pray more. There is a time to speak to our children about God. There are also long seasons when our words only push them away, and the best thing we can do is keep loving them and keep praying.

And do not be afraid of God's "no." When a door closes or a plan falls apart, God has not stopped listening. He may be carrying your prayer somewhere you could not have planned.

### Try this

This week, pray like Monica for someone you love who is far from God.

- **Name them before God, morning and evening.** Like Monica, come to God twice a day, even for one minute, and say their name to Him.
- **Pour out your heart.** Read Lamentations 2:19 aloud. Then tell God everything you feel about this person, even if it comes out as tears instead of words.
- **Speak less, love more.** Ask God to show you whether your words are helping or hurting. Choose one act of quiet love for this person this week.
- **Ask for the deepest thing.** Pray, "Lord, do not give me only what I ask. Give them what I truly desire: that they would know You."

### A closing prayer

Father, You saw every tear Monica wept for her son, and You did not despise them. I bring You the people I love who are far from You. I name them before You now. I do not know how to reach them, but You do. When You do not give me what I ask, help me trust that You are working toward what I most desire. Keep me praying through the long years. Let my tears be prayers, and let them not be wasted. Bring my loved ones home to You, as You brought Augustine home. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Who are you praying for, as Monica prayed for Augustine? How long have you been praying?
- Can you remember a time when God's "no" turned out to be a deeper "yes"?
- Why do you think the bishop told Monica to talk less and pray more? When might that be wise for you?

- R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*, especially "The Importance of Prayer"

## "Our Heart Is Restless"

> "I am quiet before God alone; my rescue comes from Him." (Psalm 62:1)

The house in Milan had a small garden. The owner was away, so the two friends who were staying there could use it as they liked. On a summer day in the year 386, one of them rushed out into it as if he were running from a fire.

His name was Augustine. He was thirty-one years old, a teacher of public speaking with a good post in one of the most important cities of the Roman Empire. He had friends and a bright future. And he was in agony.

Through the preaching of Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, he had come to see that the Christian faith was true. But he could not let go of his old life, especially his desires for sexual pleasure. A visitor had just told him and his friend Alypius about Antony, the Egyptian monk who gave up everything to follow Christ, and about two officials who had read Antony's story and given their lives to God at once. Augustine was ashamed. Simple people were taking hold of heaven, and he, with all his learning, still could not move.

Some ten years later he told what happened next in his *Confessions*.

He threw himself down under a fig tree and wept. He cried out to God in the words of the Psalms, and then in his own: "How long, how long, 'to-morrow, and tomorrow?' Why not now?"

Then he heard a voice from a house nearby, a child's voice, singing again and again, "Take up and read; Take up and read." He knew no children's game with words like that. He remembered that Antony, as we saw in chapter 9, had heard a reading from the Gospel and taken it as God's word to him. So Augustine got up, went back to the bench where he had left a book of Paul's letters, opened it, and read the first words his eyes fell on. They were these:

> "Let us live decently, as people do in the daytime: not in wild parties and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and shameless excess, not in quarrels and envy. Instead, put on the Lord Jesus Christ like clothing, and do not make plans to satisfy the desires of the flesh." (Romans 13:13-14)

He read no further. He did not need to. "By a light as it were of serenity infused into my heart," he wrote, "all the darkness of doubt vanished away." He went in with Alypius to tell his mother, whose long years of prayer we followed in the last chapter.

### Who he was

As we saw in the last chapter, Augustine was not a good young man. He was proud, ambitious and restless. The woman he lived with for many years had borne him a son, Adeodatus. And as a follower of the Manichees, who taught that the world was a battle between light and darkness, he had looked down on the Bible as crude.

He was also honest, and that honesty finally brought him home. At Easter in 387 he was baptized by Ambrose in Milan, together with Alypius and his son. Back in Africa, he hoped for a quiet life of prayer and study, but in 391 the church in the port town of Hippo seized him and asked their bishop to make him a priest. A few years later he became bishop of Hippo himself, and he served there for the rest of his life. He preached, cared for the poor and wrote books that have shaped Christian thinking ever since.

He never forgot what he had been. His most famous book is not a book of theology at all. It is a confession, spoken to God, of his sins and of God's mercy. And it begins with a sentence that Christians all over the world know by heart:

> "Thou awakest us to delight in Thy praise; for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee."
> Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions*

### Prayer as holy desire

That sentence is the key to Augustine's teaching on prayer. Every human heart is hungry. We look for rest in money, pleasure or praise, and none of it is enough, because we were made for God. Prayer, for Augustine, is that hunger turned toward the One who can fill it.

So he talked much about desire. Our desires are not the enemy; the problem is that they are divided. Augustine admitted that as a young man he had prayed, "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet." He wanted God, but he wanted other things more. Real prayer begins when our deepest desire becomes God Himself.

Once he preached on the words "we will be like Him, for we will see Him as He truly is" (1 John 3:2). We cannot see God fully now, he said. So what are we to do in the meantime? Desire Him:

> "The whole life of a good Christian is an holy desire. Now what thou longest for, thou dost not yet see: howbeit by longing, thou art made capable, so that when that is come which thou mayest see, thou shall be filled."
> Augustine of Hippo, *Homilies on the First Epistle of John*

He compared it to stretching open a sack so that it can hold more. "So God, by deferring our hope, stretches our desire; by the desiring, stretches the mind; by stretching, makes it more capacious." When God makes us wait, He is not ignoring us. He is making room in us for what He wants to give.

Years later, a wealthy Roman widow named Proba wrote to ask him how she ought to pray. She had fled to Africa after the city of Rome was sacked by the Goths in 410. His long letter to her is not yet in the Ochorus library, but here are its main lines. Pray for a happy life, he told her, and know that true happiness is found only in God. God does not need us to tell Him what we need. He asks us to pray so that our desire may grow, and we may become able to receive what He is ready to give. The Lord's Prayer, he wrote, holds everything we may rightly ask. And to "never stop praying" (1 Thessalonians 5:17) is to keep this longing for God alive in faith, hope and love all through the day, even when our lips are silent.

There is one more lesson. Augustine knew that God sometimes refuses what we ask, as He refused his mother's prayer that he would not sail. So he wrote:

> "He is Thy best servant who looks not so much to hear that from Thee which himself willeth, as rather to will that, which from Thee he heareth."
> Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions*

That is where the restless heart finds rest. "I am quiet before God alone; my rescue comes from Him" (Psalm 62:1). Augustine died in 430, while an army of Vandals was besieging Hippo. His friend Possidius, who wrote his life, tells us that in his last illness he had the psalms of repentance copied out and fixed to the wall where he could see them from his bed, and he read them and wept. The great teacher ended his life as he had begun his new one: asking for mercy, and resting in God.

### Three key insights

1. **We were made for God, and our hearts find rest only in Him.** Every restless longing in us is, underneath, a longing for God. Prayer is where we bring that hunger to the only One who can satisfy it.
2. **God stretches our hearts by making us wait.** Unanswered prayer is not wasted time. While we keep desiring and asking, God is enlarging our hearts to receive more of Him.
3. **The best prayer learns to want what God wants.** We begin by asking God for our wishes. We grow by asking Him to make His will our wish.

### For us today

Augustine's restlessness is not only a problem of the rich West. A young man in Nairobi scrolling his phone at midnight, a businesswoman in Shanghai who has everything except peace, a farmer in Bangladesh who feels his life is going nowhere: all of them know the ache he described. Augustine tried money, pleasure and success to quiet it. None of them worked.

The good news is that the ache itself can become a prayer. Bring your desires to God, the good and the confused alike, and ask Him to sort them out. Ask Him to make Himself your deepest desire.

If you are praying "but not yet," be honest with God about that too. He heard that half-hearted prayer and kept working until the day in the garden. He can do the same for you.

### Try this

This week, let Augustine teach you to pray your desires.

- **Pray his first sentence.** Each morning, say slowly in your own words: *You made me for Yourself, and my heart is restless until it rests in You.* Then sit quietly before God for a minute, as Psalm 62:1 says.
- **Name your restlessness.** Write down three things you have been looking to for happiness. Hand each one to God in prayer and ask Him to be your true joy.
- **Pray the Lord's Prayer slowly.** Augustine told Proba that it holds everything we may rightly ask. Pray it one line at a time, and let each line shape your own requests.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You made me for Yourself, and my heart is restless until it rests in You. You know how divided my desires are. You know the things I still say "not yet" to. Have mercy on me. Stretch my heart while I wait. Teach me to want You more than Your gifts, and to want Your will more than my own. Let me be quiet before You alone, for my rescue comes from You. Clothe me with the Lord Jesus Christ, and give me rest in Him. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Where do you most often look for rest instead of looking to God?
- Augustine prayed, "only not yet." Is there anything you are saying "not yet" to God about?
- How might it change your prayers to think of waiting as God stretching your heart?

## Christ Be with Me

> "And remember this: I Myself am with you every day, until the close of the age." (Matthew 28:20)

Before the sun came up, the boy was already awake. Outside it might be snowing, or frosty, or raining hard, as it often is on the hills of Ireland. He went out anyway, to the woods and the mountainside where he looked after another man's animals. And there, before anything else, he prayed.

He was about sixteen when raiders from across the sea had attacked his home in Roman Britain and carried him off with many others to be sold as a slave. Now he was perhaps seventeen or eighteen, hungry, poorly dressed and far from his family, at what he called the ends of the earth. He had grown up in a Christian home. His father was a deacon and his grandfather a priest. But by his own account he had not known the true God, and he had not cared.

On those hills everything changed. Years later, as an old man, he wrote down what happened in a short book he called his *Confession*. The love of God and the fear of God grew in him, he says, and his faith grew, until in a single day he would say "as many as a hundred prayers," and almost as many at night. Out in the woods and on the mountainside he was up to pray before daybreak, through snow and frost and rain, and he says he felt no harm from it.

His name was Patrick.

He was not counting carefully. A lonely slave boy had discovered that he was not alone. The God he had ignored at home had been watching over him the whole time, and now Patrick could not stop talking to Him.

After six years, he says, a voice in the night told him that his ship was ready. He walked a long way to the coast, perhaps two hundred miles, and found a ship about to sail. The captain refused him at first. Patrick turned away and began to pray, and before he had finished his prayer, one of the sailors shouted to him to come back. He reached home at last.

But one night he dreamed that he heard the voices of Irish people calling him to come and walk among them again. He could not forget it. In time he went back to the land of his slavery, this time as a bishop, to bring the good news of Christ to the people who had once owned him. He never came home to Britain again.

### Who he was

We know very little about Patrick for certain, and almost all of it comes from two short writings of his own: the *Confession* and a fierce *Letter* to the soldiers of a British warlord, Coroticus, who had killed and enslaved some of Patrick's newly baptized converts. Scholars agree that these two writings really are his. He wrote them in plain, rough Latin, because his schooling had been cut short when he was taken captive, and he was embarrassed by it all his life.

He lived in the fifth century, as the Roman Empire was falling apart in the west. The dates often given for his life, such as 432 for the start of his mission, come from later Irish books, and historians still argue about them. Many famous stories about Patrick, such as the snakes he drove out of Ireland and the shamrock he used to explain the Trinity, are legends that were told long after his death.

What we do know is enough. He traveled through a land with no Roman law to protect him. He baptized, he says, many thousands of people, and he ordained clergy to care for them. He was seized more than once, and once kept in chains for fourteen days. He refused gifts so that no one could say he had come to Ireland to get rich. He cared especially for slaves, and for enslaved women most of all, because he knew what their life was like. And to the end he called himself a sinner, astonished that God would use someone like him.

In his *Confession*, Patrick quotes the last command Jesus gave His disciples, to go and teach all nations. He believed that he had been sent to the very ends of the earth to obey it. And he leaned with all his weight on the promise that came with it: "I Myself am with you every day, until the close of the age" (Matthew 28:20).

### How he prayed

Patrick's *Confession* is full of prayer. He tells of a terrible night when he felt as if a huge rock had fallen on him, and he could not move. He called out, and as the sun rose its brightness lifted the weight from him. He believed that Christ had come to his help.

He also tells of a night when he seemed to hear someone praying inside him, with words he could not understand. At the end of the prayer, he says, it was shown to him that it was the Spirit. Paul wrote of this mystery: "What we should pray for, and how, we do not know. But the Spirit Himself pleads on our behalf, with groans that no words carry" (Romans 8:26). Patrick, the unlearned slave, knew from experience that he did not pray alone.

Andrew Murray, whom we heard in chapter 9, preached on the promise Patrick leaned on. He said it is as true for us as it was for the first disciples:

> "And just as really as Christ was with Peter in the boat, just as Christ sat with John at the table, as really can I have Christ with me."
> Andrew Murray, *Jesus Himself*

### The Breastplate

The prayer most often linked with Patrick is called *Saint Patrick's Breastplate*, or in Irish *The Deer's Cry*. A "breastplate" is the piece of armor that protects a soldier's chest. The prayer asks for God's protection by binding oneself to the Trinity, to Christ's life, death and rising, and to the power of God in all creation. Its best-known part names Christ again and again: with me, before me, behind me, in me, beneath me, above me.

We should be honest about it. The prayer is traditionally attributed to Patrick, and a later story says he sang it while his enemies lay in wait for him. But the oldest copies we have were written down centuries after his death, and most scholars think it was composed later, perhaps in the 600s or 700s, by Irish Christians who loved his memory. It is not in his own writings.

Even so, it sounds like the faith of the *Confession*. Many English-speaking Christians know it through a hymn version written in 1889 by Cecil Frances Alexander, a hymn writer in Ireland. Its best-known lines are these: "Christ be with me, Christ within me, Christ behind me, Christ before me." Whoever first prayed those words, they are Matthew 28:20 turned into a prayer.

### Three key insights

1. **The place of our deepest suffering can become the place where we learn to pray.** Patrick found God not in his comfortable home but as a slave on a cold hillside. God met him where he was lowest.
2. **Christ's promise to be with us is the strength of our mission.** Patrick went back alone to the land of his slavery. He could do it because Jesus had promised to be with His people every day, to the close of the age.
3. **When we do not know how to pray, the Spirit prays within us.** Patrick heard words he could not understand and learned that they were the Spirit's. Even when we have no words, God's Spirit is praying in us and for us.

### For us today

Patrick's story speaks to many readers of this book. Millions of people around the world today are trafficked, forced to work for others, or held far from home. Patrick knew that life from the inside. He shows that God sees the slave on the hillside, and that no one is too low or too far away to be found by Him.

His story also speaks to those whom God is calling to cross borders with the gospel: a Nigerian nurse in the Gulf, a Brazilian family in North Africa, a Korean student in Central Asia. Patrick went back to the people who had wronged him. That is one of the hardest things God ever asks. It is possible only because Christ goes with us.

And Patrick's hundred prayers a day are a word to busy people everywhere. He had animals to watch and hard work to do. He prayed anyway, in the cold, before dawn, in short prayers all through the day and night.

### Try this

This week, let Patrick teach you to pray in ordinary places.

- **Pray before dawn.** Get up a little earlier one morning, go outside if you can, and pray in whatever weather you find.
- **Pray a hundred small prayers.** Through one day, turn to God again and again with short words: "Lord, be with me," "Thank You," "Help me." Do not count them. Just keep turning.
- **Pray the Breastplate lines.** As you leave home, pray slowly: Christ be with me, Christ before me, Christ behind me, Christ within me. Picture Him all around you.
- **Pray for someone who is enslaved or trafficked.** Ask God to free them, to comfort them, and to send them people like Patrick.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, You promised to be with us every day, until the close of the age. Thank You for Your servant Patrick, who found You as a slave on a lonely hill and went back to serve the people who had wronged him. Be with me, before me, behind me and within me. When I am far from home, hold me. When I do not know how to pray, let Your Spirit pray within me. Give me courage to go wherever You send me, and to love even those who have hurt me. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Patrick found God in the hardest years of his life. Has God ever met you in a time of suffering?
- Why do you think Patrick was willing to go back to Ireland? What would it take for you to serve someone who had wronged you?
- What difference would it make in your day to remember that Christ is with you, before you and behind you?

## Lord Jesus Christ, Have Mercy

> "So he shouted, 'Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!'" (Luke 18:38)

Every day the blind man sat in the same place, beside the road outside Jericho, with his cloak spread out to catch the coins of travelers. He could not see their faces. He knew the world by its sounds: footsteps, donkeys, voices, the clink of a coin.

One day the sounds were different. A great crowd was passing, louder than usual. He asked what was happening, and someone told him: "Jesus of Nazareth is going by" (Luke 18:37).

He had only a moment. So he shouted:

> "Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" (Luke 18:38)

The people at the front of the crowd were embarrassed and annoyed. They "told him sternly to be quiet, but he cried out all the louder, 'Son of David, have mercy on me!'" (Luke 18:39). Mark tells us his name was Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46).

Notice what he did. He did not make a speech. He did not explain who he was or why he deserved help. He did not change his words to sound more impressive. He said the same short prayer again, louder. And Jesus "stood still" (Luke 18:40). The Lord of heaven and earth stopped on a crowded road for one blind beggar who would not stop calling His name.

A few verses earlier, in the story Jesus told about the temple, a tax collector had prayed, "God, be merciful to me, the sinner!" (Luke 18:13). In Volume 2 we stood beside him. Now Luke shows us a beggar on the road praying almost the same prayer. Both were heard.

Some four hundred years later, in the deserts of Egypt and Palestine, men and women who had left everything to seek God took these two cries and joined them. Out of them came one of the best-loved prayers in Christian history. In the form most commonly used today it runs: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner." It is called the Jesus Prayer.

### From the road to the desert

We have met the desert fathers and mothers already in this book. In chapter 9 we saw Antony learning, as a young man, to pray in secret without ceasing. Paul had written, "Never stop praying" (1 Thessalonians 5:17). The monks tried to obey that command. But how can anyone pray without stopping while they weave baskets, carry water and work in the fields?

Part of their answer was short prayers, repeated again and again through the day. In chapter 11 we met John Cassian and the verse he learned from the monks of Egypt: "God, come and rescue me! LORD, be quick to help me!" (Psalm 70:1). Augustine, in his letter to Proba, mentioned that the brothers in Egypt prayed very often, but in very short prayers, so that their attention would stay awake. And in chapter 10 we heard Abba Macarius tell the brothers that there was no need for long speeches: it was enough to stretch out their hands and ask the Lord for mercy.

In the 400s, a Greek bishop named Diadochus of Photice taught believers to keep their minds fixed on the name of the Lord Jesus, calling on it quietly in their hearts. Over the next two hundred years, in the monasteries of Gaza and Sinai, the cry of the blind man and the tax collector settled into the words we know. We cannot say exactly when the full form took its final shape; it grew slowly, the way a path is worn by many feet.

In the centuries that followed, the Jesus Prayer became the heart of prayer in the Orthodox churches of Greece, Russia, Romania, Georgia, Serbia and many other lands. Orthodox Christians often pray it with a prayer rope, a cord of knots, moving one knot at a time. Some pray it slowly with their breathing. In Volume 5 we will meet the monks of Mount Athos who made this prayer the center of their lives.

### Is this "vain repetition"?

Some Christians worry about repeating a prayer. Jesus said, "When you pray, do not keep piling up empty words as the pagans do. They think they will be heard because they say so much" (Matthew 6:7). Is the Jesus Prayer that kind of praying?

Notice what Jesus warned against: empty words, and the idea that we will be heard because we say so much. The Jesus Prayer is the opposite. It is very few words, and it does not try to earn anything. It simply asks for mercy. Jesus Himself, in the garden of Gethsemane, "prayed a third time, saying the same words" (Matthew 26:44). And He heard Bartimaeus when he cried the same words twice.

George Whitefield, the great preacher of the English and American revivals, preached a whole sermon on Bartimaeus. He saw this very point:

> "This was not a vain repetition, but a devout reiteration of his request. We may sometimes repeat the same words, and yet not be guilty of that battalogia, or vain speaking, which our Lord condemns."
> George Whitefield, *Selected Sermons of George Whitefield*

("Battalogia" comes from the Greek word behind "piling up empty words" in Matthew 6:7.) What matters is the heart. Repeated words can become empty if the heart wanders. But they can also become a road the heart walks on, back to Jesus again and again.

Christians in the West have known this too. Jeremy Taylor, the English bishop whose bedtime prayer we read in chapter 12, gave this advice to busy people:

> "In the midst of the works of thy calling, often retire to God in short prayers and ejaculations; and those may make up the want of those larger portions of time, which, it may be, thou desirest for devotion, and in which thou thinkest other persons have advantage of thee."
> Jeremy Taylor, *The Rule and Exercises of Holy Living*

("Ejaculations" here is an old word for short prayers that are "thrown up" to God.)

### Three key insights

1. **The shortest prayer can hold the whole gospel.** In a few words the Jesus Prayer confesses who Jesus is, Lord, Christ and Son of God, and who we are: sinners who need His mercy.
2. **Repeating a prayer from the heart is not vain repetition.** Bartimaeus cried the same words again and Jesus stood still. God is not tired of hearing us call on His Son's name.
3. **Calling on the name of Jesus is a prayer for every place and every hour.** A short prayer can be prayed while we work, walk, wait or lie awake. In this way ordinary believers can learn to "never stop praying."

### For us today

You do not need to be Orthodox, or a monk, to pray the Jesus Prayer. Its words come straight from the Gospels, and believers of every tradition pray it: Catholic and Protestant, Pentecostal and Anglican, in villages and in great cities. You can pray it in Arabic or Swahili, Korean or Quechua. You can pray it in a hospital bed, on a crowded train, in a prison cell, or at a market stall.

It is especially a gift to people who cannot read, or who have no Bible of their own, or whose lives leave no time for long prayers. A mother with a sick child at night, a soldier on duty, a worker in a factory with the noise all around: each of them can pray, "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me." It is also a gift when we are overwhelmed and have no words left. When grief or fear or temptation fills the mind, these few words are like a handhold on a cliff.

Some people pray it on a prayer rope, as the Orthodox do. Others pray it in time with their breathing: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God" as they breathe in, "have mercy on me, a sinner" as they breathe out. Others simply pray it whenever they remember. There is no special technique that makes it work. It is not a magic phrase. It is the cry of a beggar to a Savior who stops for beggars.

### Try this

This week, learn the blind man's prayer by heart and let it go with you.

- **Begin slowly.** Sit quietly for five minutes in the morning. Pray the Jesus Prayer gently, again and again: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner." When your mind wanders, simply come back.
- **Carry it through the day.** Choose a few daily moments, such as washing your hands, waiting for a bus or starting a new task, and pray it silently each time.
- **Pray it for others.** Change the words for someone you love: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on my brother," or "on my village," or "on Your persecuted church."
- **Pray it in the night.** If you wake and cannot sleep, pray it quietly instead of worrying, until you rest again.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner. You stood still on the road for a blind man who would not stop calling Your name. Stand still for me. I have no great words to offer You, only my need. Teach me to call on Your name all through my day, at work and at rest, in joy and in fear. Open my eyes, as You opened his, so that I may follow You on the road, giving glory to God. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Bartimaeus kept crying out when the crowd told him to be quiet? What "crowds" try to silence your prayers?
- What is the difference between empty repetition and repeating a prayer from the heart?
- When in your day could you pray a short prayer like the Jesus Prayer?

## Conclusion: Passing the Torch

> "And what you heard from me before many witnesses, pass on to trustworthy people who will be able to teach others as well." (2 Timothy 2:2)

Paul wrote those words to Timothy near the end of his life. He was in prison in Rome, and he knew he would soon die. His concern was not for himself. It was that what he had received would be handed on: from Paul to Timothy, from Timothy to trustworthy people, and from them to others.

That is what we have watched happen in this book. The apostles taught Polycarp and Clement. Polycarp taught Irenaeus. Antony taught the monks who came to him in the desert, and they taught John Cassian, who carried their wisdom to the West. Macrina taught her brothers. Monica's prayers, Ambrose's preaching and a child's voice in a garden brought Augustine to Christ, and Augustine's *Confessions* have taught millions to pray ever since. A British slave boy learned to pray on an Irish hillside, and came back to teach a whole people to pray.

In four hundred years the church went from a few small, hunted communities to the great church of the Roman world, and beyond it, into Persia, Ethiopia, Armenia and Ireland. Through all of it, again and again, it was carried by people who prayed.

### What the witnesses taught us

Here, in a few lines, is some of what they taught us about prayer:

- A simple rhythm of prayer, built around the Lord's Prayer, can carry a whole life (chapter 1, the *Didache*).
- Praise of God's greatness frees us to pray for the powerful without fear (chapter 2, Clement).
- Asking others to pray for us is strength, not weakness, and in suffering the deepest prayer is for faithfulness (chapter 3, Ignatius).
- A lifetime of prayer prepares us for the hour of testing, and thanksgiving can be our last word (chapter 4, Polycarp).
- God meets us in the darkness before He brings us out of it (chapter 5, Perpetua and Felicity).
- Prayer is not an extra piece of armor but the strength of all the rest (chapter 6, Tertullian).
- Our whole life can become one long prayer, and when we pray, Christ prays with us (chapter 7, Origen).
- The word "our" makes every prayer a prayer for the whole family (chapter 8, Cyprian).
- God leads us into quiet places to speak tenderly to us, not to punish us (chapter 9, Antony).
- If prayer feels like a struggle, you are not failing; you are fighting (chapter 10, the desert mothers and fathers).
- One short verse can carry us through the whole day (chapter 11, John Cassian).
- Any home can become a house of prayer (chapter 12, Macrina).
- What we sing, we remember, and singing together is praying together (chapter 13, Ephrem).
- Prayer brings God's light into our darkest places (chapter 14, John Chrysostom).
- Prayer for a wandering child is never wasted (chapter 15, Monica).
- We were made for God, and our hearts find rest only in Him (chapter 16, Augustine).
- Christ's promise to be with us is the strength of our mission (chapter 17, Patrick).
- The shortest prayer can hold the whole gospel (chapter 18, the Jesus Prayer).

You will find every one of these lessons gathered by theme in Appendix 1, and every Scripture in this book listed in Bible order in Appendix 2.

### Faithful unto death

The title of this book comes from the words of Jesus to the church in Smyrna, where Polycarp was later bishop: "Stay faithful even to death, and I will give you the crown of life" (Revelation 2:10).

Many of the people in this book were faithful to death in the plainest sense. Ignatius, Polycarp, Perpetua, Felicity and Cyprian gave their lives rather than deny Christ. But faithfulness to death also took other shapes. Antony prayed in the desert for more than eighty years. Monica prayed for her son for many years before she saw her prayer answered. John Chrysostom died in exile, worn out by a forced march, still giving glory to God. Patrick spent the rest of his life among the people who had once enslaved him. Each of them kept praying to the end.

That is the gift of this volume. It shows us that prayer is not only for the good days. It is how believers have held on to God in prison, in loss, in exile and in old age, and found that He was holding on to them.

The book of Revelation shows what all this looks like from heaven. John hears a loud voice in heaven speak of God's suffering people, and say that "they have overcome him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony; they did not hold their lives dear, even in the face of death" (Revelation 12:11). The world did not see them as winners. Heaven did.

### The torch is passed

When this book ends, around the year 500, the old Roman world in the West is breaking apart. Rome itself was captured by the Goths in 410, and as Augustine lay dying in 430, an army was besieging his city of Hippo. To many people it seemed the end of the world.

It was not the end of the church. In **Volume 5, *Seven Times a Day***, we will follow the monks, mystics and missionaries of the next thousand years. We will meet Benedict, who taught his monks to pray and work, and to stop for prayer seven times every day. We will find the ancient church of Ethiopia, the Church of the East carrying the gospel along the Silk Road to China, and Celtic believers who prayed over every ordinary task. We will kneel with Gregory of Narek in Armenia, Anselm in England, Bernard in France and Francis in Italy. We will hear Julian of Norwich say that all shall be well, watch the hesychasts of Mount Athos wait on God in stillness, and stand with Jan Hus as he prays for those who are burning him.

They were the heirs of the witnesses in this book. They prayed the same Lord's Prayer, sang the same psalms and, many of them, repeated the same short prayer: "Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy."

### Your turn

The torch has been handed on for two thousand years, and now it is in your hands.

So pray the Lord's Prayer three times a day, as the *Didache* taught. Pray for the rulers of your country, even those who oppose the faith, as Clement did. When you suffer, ask others to pray for you, as Ignatius did, and give thanks to the end, as Polycarp did. Make some quiet room for God, even in a crowded house. When prayer is hard, keep fighting. Carry one short verse or one short prayer with you through the day. Sing your prayers. Pray for the child, or the friend, who has wandered far from God, and do not give up. And remember that Christ is with you, before you and behind you, every day, until the close of the age.

Then hand it on. Teach someone else to pray. That is how the torch has always been passed.

### A closing prayer

Faithful God, thank You for the martyrs who loved You more than their lives, for the mothers and fathers of the desert who fought to pray, and for the teachers, poets and missionaries who taught Your church to call You Father. Thank You for Ignatius and Polycarp, for Perpetua and Felicity, for Antony and Macrina, for Monica and Augustine, for Patrick and for all whose names only You know. Make me faithful as they were. When I suffer, keep me praying. When my heart is restless, let it rest in You. Give me courage to pray for those who oppose me, and love to pray for those who wander. Help me to pass on what I have received, so that others may learn to pray. And keep me faithful, even to death, until I receive the crown of life. Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me. Amen.
