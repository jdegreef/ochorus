## Introduction: Where We Are in the Story

> "As soon as he took the scroll, the four living creatures and the twenty-four elders bowed low before the Lamb. Every one of them had a harp, and they held gold bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of God's holy people." (Revelation 5:8)

Near the end of the Bible, John is shown the throne room of heaven. Around the throne, the elders hold bowls of gold, and the bowls are full. They are full of incense, and the incense, John is told, is "the prayers of God's holy people."

Think of what those bowls hold. Abraham's pleading for Sodom is there, and Hannah's silent prayer at Shiloh. The prayers of the first church in Jerusalem are there, and the songs of Paul and Silas at midnight. So are the prayers of martyrs in the arena and of monks in the desert, the psalms sung seven times a day in the monasteries, the cries of reformers and of slaves, the prayers of missionaries in China and of George Müller for the orphans of Bristol, of revival meetings in Wales and Korea, and of believers in the prisons of our own day. Not one of those prayers was lost. God kept them all.

And there is room in the bowls for yours.

### Where we are in the series

This is the eleventh and last volume of *A Cloud of Praying Witnesses*. The name of the series comes from Hebrews 12:1, which pictures all the faithful who went before us as "so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us."

Volumes 1 to 10 told a story. We began with the Old Testament, with Abraham, Moses, Hannah, David and the prophets. We followed Jesus as He prayed and taught His friends to pray, and the apostles as they prayed the church into being. We met the martyrs and the desert fathers, the monks and the mystics, the reformers and the Puritans, the leaders of the great awakenings, the missionaries of the nineteenth century, the revivals and the wars of the twentieth, and finally the worldwide praying church of our own day. Volume 10, *Every Tribe and Tongue*, ended with Christians praying in more languages than ever before, and with a promise: there would be one more volume, and it would be about you.

### What is different about this book

This volume does not tell the story again. Instead, it steps back to look at the whole cloud of witnesses together, from Abraham to today, and it asks a simple question: what do they teach us about our own life of prayer?

Each chapter takes one great theme. It opens with a scene from one or two witnesses we have already met, and tells you which volume they come from. Then it gathers the same thread through the centuries, from different eras, traditions and parts of the world, and turns to the plain teaching of Scripture. If you have read the earlier volumes, you will meet old friends again. If you have not, each chapter points you to the place where their full story is told.

This is the most practical book in the series. Its heart is the section called **Try this** at the end of each chapter. Every practice there is simple and can be done anywhere in the world: by a busy parent, a student, a worker on a long shift, someone who has no quiet room of their own, or someone who does not own a Bible.

### What we will see

This volume has twelve chapters, in three movements:

- **The prayer meeting we join** (chapters 1 to 4). We look at the whole cloud of witnesses as one prayer meeting that has lasted for thousands of years, and ask what its members agree on. Then we learn two of their oldest practices: praying with the Bible open, and fasting.
- **The life of prayer** (chapters 5 to 8). We learn to pray alone and with others, to wait on the Lord, to keep trusting when heaven seems silent, and to pray for all people, near and far.
- **Prayer that shapes a life** (chapters 9 to 12). We pray for revival, build prayer into our homes, and shape a simple rhythm of prayer for our own days. And we end where the Bible ends, with the prayer of the whole church: "Come, Lord Jesus!"

### How each chapter works

Every chapter in this series has the same shape:

- **A key verse** opens the chapter, the Scripture it rests on.
- **A story** sets the scene, from a witness we met earlier in the series.
- **The teaching** follows the theme through the centuries and into the Bible.
- **Voices from the past** quote men and women of prayer word for word from books in the Ochorus library.
- **Three key insights** sum up what the chapter teaches.
- **For us today** brings the lesson into ordinary life, wherever in the world you live.
- **Try this** gives one simple practice for the coming week.
- **A closing prayer** and **Questions for reflection** help you respond, alone or with others.
- **Read more on Ochorus** points you to books where you can go deeper.

### The appendices to the whole series

Because this is the last volume, its appendices gather the whole series, not just this book:

- **Appendix A** lists every key insight in all eleven volumes, by theme.
- **Appendix B** is a Scripture index to the whole series, from Genesis to Revelation.
- **Appendix C** is a timeline of every person and movement in Volumes 1 to 10.
- **Appendix D** is a toolkit of simple ways to pray, gathered from the witnesses, each with the place in the series where you can read its story.
- **Appendix E** lists the books on Ochorus where you can keep reading, volume by volume.

### How to read this book

One chapter a week will carry you through the book in three months, with time to practise each **Try this** before you move on. With a small group, meet once a week, read one chapter together, and come back the next week to tell each other how the practice went.

The witnesses in this series were ordinary people. They were tired, busy, afraid and sometimes doubting, as we are. What made them witnesses was not that they were great, but that they prayed to a great God. As you read, do not only admire them. Join them. Their prayers are already in the bowls of gold. Yours can be too.

## One Prayer Meeting, Two Thousand Years Long

> "So then, since we too have so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us, let us strip off every burden, and the sin that wraps itself around us so readily, and let us run the race laid out ahead of us, and run it with endurance." (Hebrews 12:1)

In the winter of 1858, the city of Boston, on the east coast of the United States, was full of prayer meetings. At twelve o'clock each day, businessmen crowded into the chapel of the Old South Church to pray, so many that some could not get in. Across the city, at Park Street Church, Elizabeth Finney led a daily meeting for women. It grew so full that women stood outside the doors, listening as well as they could. In Volume 8 we saw how this wave of prayer began, with one man waiting alone in an empty room in New York.

The preacher Charles Finney was in Boston that winter, and years later he remembered one meeting in his *Memoirs*. A stranger stood up to speak. He said that he came from Omaha, in Nebraska, then a small new town on the Missouri River, far to the west. On his journey east, the man said, he had found "a continuous prayer-meeting all the way." By his reckoning it was about two thousand miles from Omaha to Boston. So this, he said, was a prayer meeting two thousand miles long.

Finney did not record his name. But we can picture his journey. In town after town he found believers gathered for the same purpose. Wherever he stopped, the meeting was already going on. When he reached Boston, he simply walked into one more room of it and sat down.

That traveler is a picture of this whole series. For ten volumes we have walked the length of a prayer meeting. The church's part of it began in an upper room in Jerusalem nearly two thousand years ago, and older prayers stand behind it, reaching back to Abraham. In all that time, it has never closed its doors.

### Walking the length of the meeting

The meeting began before the church. Abraham stood before the LORD and pleaded for a city (Volume 1, chapter 1). Hannah poured out her soul at Shiloh (Volume 1, chapter 5). Daniel knelt three times a day at his open window in Babylon (Volume 1, chapter 14). Then the Son of God Himself came into the room. Jesus rose very early to pray, and spent a whole night on the mountain before He chose the Twelve (Volume 2, chapters 1 and 2). He taught His friends to say "Our Father."

After He rose and returned to the Father, about a hundred and twenty believers waited in an upper room in Jerusalem. "All of these were devoted to prayer and of one mind in it" (Acts 1:14; Volume 3, chapter 1). From that room the meeting spread, and it has gone on ever since.

Polycarp, hiding on a farm outside Smyrna, prayed night and day for the churches throughout the world (Volume 4, chapter 4). Benedict's monks prayed the psalms seven times a day (Volume 5, chapter 1). The hidden Christians of Japan kept praying in secret for about two hundred and fifty years (Volume 6, chapter 10). At Herrnhut, in 1727, a village of refugees began a watch of prayer that went on day and night, with each person taking one hour (Volume 7, chapter 4). Clerks prayed at noon in New York (Volume 8, chapter 8). A whole church prayed aloud together in Pyongyang (Volume 9, chapter 4). And tonight, believers will pray until dawn in the cities of Africa (Volume 10, chapter 17), while others pray quietly in homes where it is dangerous to be a Christian (Volume 10, chapter 19).

They prayed in Hebrew and Greek, Latin and Ge'ez, Korean and Yoruba. Some knelt, some stood. Some prayed in cathedrals and some in prison cells. Yet it was one meeting, because they all prayed to the one God, through the one Lord Jesus, by the one Spirit.

### A cloud of witnesses

Many people picture the "cloud of witnesses" as a crowd in the seats of a great stadium, watching the runners below. There is truth in that picture. But in this letter, a witness is first of all someone who gives testimony. All through Hebrews 11, God bears witness about His people. Of Abel the writer says that "God bore witness about his gifts," and then, "though he died, he is still speaking" (Hebrews 11:4). The chapter ends:

> "Through their faith all of these were attested by God, and yet they did not receive the promise. God had in view something better for us: they were not to be made perfect apart from us." (Hebrews 11:39-40)

So the witnesses are not mainly watching us. They are speaking to us. Their lives say: God is real, God keeps His promises, and prayer to Him is never wasted. (The Greek word for witness, *martys*, later gave us the word "martyr.") And notice the last words: "not to be made perfect apart from us." The meeting is not complete without you.

E. M. Bounds, the American pastor whom we met in Volume 8, believed that the prayers of the past are still at work:

> "A man can pray better because of the prayers of the past; a man can live holier because of the prayers of the past, the man of many and acceptable prayers has done the truest and greatest service to the incoming generation."
> E. M. Bounds, *Purpose in Prayer*

Christians understand the link between the saints in heaven and the church on earth in different ways. Orthodox and Catholic believers ask the saints to pray for them; most Protestants speak to God alone. But many churches confess together, in the Apostles' Creed, that they believe in "the communion of saints." And all agree with Jesus that God "is not a God of dead people but of living people" (Luke 20:38).

### Your place in the meeting

Our key verse holds two small words: "we too." The writer does not leave us in the seats. He puts us on the track.

He tells us to "strip off every burden." What weighs down your prayer? It may be a sin you will not give up, bitterness against someone, or a day so full that God gets only the last tired minutes. Put it down.

He tells us to "run it with endurance." The meeting is long. You will not finish it, and you do not have to. At Herrnhut no one prayed all night, every night. Each person took one hour, and then slept while another took up the watch. Your part is to take your hour.

And he tells us where to look: "Let us keep our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer who leads the way in faith and brings it to perfection" (Hebrews 12:2). The witnesses are not the center of the meeting. Jesus is. He leads it, and He still prays for us: "He is always alive to plead on their behalf" (Hebrews 7:25).

Charles Spurgeon used a picture to show that believers on earth and in heaven belong to one company. In Venice, he had been told, when the fishermen were far out at sea, their wives would go down to the shore in the evening and sing a hymn:

> "They sing the first stanza in the shrill silvery notes of woman's voice, and then they wait. They cannot see a single boat upon the sea, the blue Adriatic is not dotted with a sail; but, presently, mysteriously wafted across the waters, comes the second stanza."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *An All-Round Ministry*

One part of the choir cannot see the other. But they are singing the same song.

### Three key insights

1. **Whenever we pray, we join a meeting that began long before us and has never closed its doors.** From Abraham to the believers praying tonight, God's people have gathered to call on Him. No one who prays is starting something new or praying alone.
2. **The cloud of witnesses is a cloud of testimony, telling us that God is faithful and prayer is never wasted.** Hebrews 11 shows God bearing witness to His people, and their lives now speak to us. They are not there to judge our running but to tell us that the race can be finished.
3. **Our eyes belong on Jesus, who leads the whole meeting and prays for us still.** We honor the witnesses, but they all looked to Him, and He holds the meeting together.

### For us today

Perhaps you are the only believer in your family or your village. Perhaps you are in a hospital bed, or in prison, or in a land where meeting with other Christians is dangerous. It is easy to feel that your prayer is small and lonely.

It is not. When you pray, you take your seat in the oldest meeting in the world. A grandmother in Kenya, a student in Manila, a farmer in Bolivia and a pastor in Seoul are praying to the same Father. At every hour of the day and night, somewhere on earth, the church is praying.

This last volume is about your place in that meeting. In the chapters ahead we will learn from the witnesses how to pray with Scripture, how to fast, how to wait, how to pray for the nations, and how to build a rhythm of prayer for your own life.

### Try this

This week, take your seat in the meeting.

- **Choose your hour.** It may be ten minutes, not sixty. Choose one time each day, and keep it, as the believers at Herrnhut kept their hour.
- **Pray with one witness.** Each day, remember one person from this series and pray as they prayed: with Hannah's honesty, Polycarp's thanks or the words of the Lord's Prayer.
- **Pray around the world.** Picture believers praying at that same moment in another land, and pray for them.
- **Put down one burden.** Name one thing that slows your prayer, and ask God to help you lay it aside.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You lead the great meeting of Your people, and You live to pray for us. Thank You for the cloud of witnesses: for Abraham and Hannah, for the first church, for martyrs and monks, for mothers and missionaries. Thank You that I am not praying alone. Help me to put down every burden that slows me, and to run my part of the race with patience. Keep my eyes on You. And let my small prayers rise with all the prayers of Your people, until we stand together before Your throne. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- How does it change your prayer to think of it as one seat in a meeting that is thousands of years old?
- Which witness from this series has spoken most clearly to you, and what has their life told you about God?
- What "burden" most often keeps you from praying, and what would it mean to lay it aside?

## What the Witnesses Agree On

> "And no one can please God without faith, since anyone who approaches God has to believe both that He exists and that He repays those who search for Him." (Hebrews 11:6)

On 3 November 1854, a small steamer called the *Pleiad* anchored off Agberi, a village in the swampy delta of the river Niger. The crew needed firewood for the engine, and the village had plenty, because its people kept fires burning in their houses to dry them against the damp. On board was Samuel Ajayi Crowther, the Yoruba clergyman whom we met in Volume 8. While the wood was loaded, he sat down to talk with one of the village's two headmen, a younger man named Agbekun.

Agbekun had just come home from a long journey. He had no children, and he had gone all the way to Aro, to ask the great god of the Igbo people, whom Crowther calls Tshuku, about it. There he had gone through many ceremonies and made many sacrifices. Yet he had never seen the god he came to ask. Every question had to pass through the priests. He showed Crowther three small images he had brought back, as proof that he had truly been there.

Crowther did not mock him. He saw a man who was searching, and he spoke to him about the true God, the giver of every good thing. Then, he wrote in his journal, Agbekun asked a question: "He wanted to know how to pray to the Great God, whom he knew to be greater than Tshuku."

It is the oldest question in this whole series. How do I pray to the Great God?

Crowther's answer was simple. Pray, he told him, just as a little child would ask his father for what he needs. No priest to stand between, no long journey, no sacrifice to buy a hearing. The Great God is a Father.

We do not know what Agbekun did after the *Pleiad* steamed away. But his question still stands in our own day, asked by people in villages and cities in every land. And if we could gather the witnesses of all ten volumes into one room and put his question to them, Crowther's answer would be repeated, in a hundred languages, from Abraham to the believers of today.

### One answer in many voices

The witnesses in this series did not agree on everything. But on the heart of prayer they speak with one voice.

**God is, and God hears.** Our key verse says that anyone who comes to God "has to believe both that He exists and that He repays those who search for Him" (Hebrews 11:6). Abraham pleaded under the stars because he believed the Judge of all the earth would do right (Volume 1, chapter 1). George Müller cared for thousands of orphans to show that God still hears prayer (Volume 8, chapter 5). Andrew Murray, writing on Jesus' words about the secret room, quoted our verse and added:

> "Not on the strong or the fervent feeling with which I pray does the blessing of the closet depend, but upon the love and the power of the Father to whom I there entrust my needs."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

The "closet" is the private room where we pray. Faith does not look at itself. It looks at the Father.

**We come as children to a Father.** Cyprian of Carthage taught that the first word of the Lord's Prayer makes us one family (Volume 4, chapter 8). Mary Slessor, afraid on a dark forest path in Nigeria with the children in her care, found that the single cry "Father!" was a whole prayer (Volume 8, chapter 18). "How much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him!" (Matthew 7:11).

**We come through Jesus.** The verb "approaches" in our key verse is used again and again in Hebrews. We "approach the throne of grace" with confidence (Hebrews 4:16), because Jesus "is able to save completely those who approach God through Him" (Hebrews 7:25). The monks of the deserts of Egypt and Palestine gave the church the Jesus Prayer, in the form most used today, "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me" (Volume 4, chapter 18). Calvin taught that only Christ turns God's throne of glory into a throne of grace (Volume 6, chapter 3). Cyprian loved to pray the very words Jesus gave:

> "It is a loving and friendly prayer to beseech God with His own word, to come up to His ears in the prayer of Christ."
> Cyprian, *The Treatises of Cyprian*

**We keep on asking.** Jesus taught "about their need to pray at all times and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1). Monica prayed for years for her wandering son (Volume 4, chapter 15). Catherine of Siena gave God no rest (Volume 5, chapter 14). John Hyde wept and prayed over the Punjab (Volume 9, chapter 6).

**We pray with others.** From the church at Antioch (Volume 3, chapter 9) to the Olney prayer meeting (Volume 7, chapter 13) and the students who prayed beside a haystack in the rain (Volume 8, chapter 1), the witnesses found that Jesus meant it: "if two of you here on earth are of one mind about anything they ask for, it will be done for them by My Father in heaven" (Matthew 18:19).

**We pray God's own words.** Jesus prayed the psalms on the cross. Benedict's monks prayed them every week, and Luther wove the Lord's Prayer into a garland (Volume 5, chapter 1; Volume 6, chapter 2). We will follow this thread in the next chapter.

**Prayer and obedience go together.** "The Lord would not have heard me if I had kept wrongdoing in my heart" (Psalm 66:18). Jesus told us to forgive before we pray (Mark 11:25). John Sung called on believers to name their sins and turn from them (Volume 9, chapter 12). And Volume 8 told honestly how the revival prayers of 1858 opened many hearts, while many who prayed in the North still accepted a nation divided by the great evil of slavery.

**We may come honestly.** Hannah poured out her pain (Volume 1, chapter 5), the psalms of lament ask "How long?" (Volume 1, chapter 16), and John Bunyan taught that the heart matters more than fine words (Volume 6, chapter 15).

### Where the witnesses differ

It would not be honest to pretend that the witnesses agree on everything. They do not.

Some prayed with set words, like Thomas Cranmer's prayer book (Volume 6, chapter 4). Others, like Bunyan, refused to use that book when the law forced it on everyone, and prayed in their own words. Some loved silence, like the monks of Mount Athos (Volume 5, chapter 12) and the brothers of Taizé (Volume 10, chapter 4). Others prayed aloud all together, as in Korea (Volume 9, chapter 4). Orthodox and Catholic believers ask the saints in heaven to pray for them; most Protestants do not. Churches still differ about gifts such as praying in tongues, which grew widely known after Azusa Street (Volume 9, chapter 3). They kneel, stand, bow to the ground or lift their hands.

These differences are real, and Christians should talk about them with patience and respect. But look at what lies underneath them. Every one of these witnesses believed that God exists, that He hears, that He is a Father, that we come to Him through Jesus, and that He rewards those who search for Him. The ways differ. The God does not.

### Three key insights

1. **Every true prayer begins where Hebrews 11:6 begins: God is real, and He rewards those who search for Him.** Faith is not a feeling we must work up before we pray. It is trust in the God who is there and who is good, and every witness in this series stood on it.
2. **The Great God is a Father, and we come to Him through Jesus, not through a long road of our own making.** Agbekun traveled far and never met the god he sought. The witnesses tell us that the true God comes near, and that in Christ even a little child may ask Him for what she needs.
3. **Believers who differ in many ways of praying can still agree on the God to whom they pray.** Set prayers or free prayers, silence or loud cries, the witnesses share one Father, one Lord and one Spirit. We can learn from those whose ways are not our own.

### For us today

Many of us grew up in one kind of church and learned only one way to pray. That is a gift. But this series has shown us brothers and sisters from every century and tradition, and they can teach us. A Pentecostal believer in Brazil can learn stillness from the Orthodox. A Lutheran in Germany can learn bold, loud prayer from Korea. An Anglican in Nigeria can learn from the house churches of China, and they from him.

And if you are a seeker, like Agbekun, the witnesses have a word for you too. You do not need to understand everything before you pray. You only need to believe that God is there and that He hears those who search for Him. Then come to Him as a child, in the name of Jesus.

### Try this

This week, pray on the ground all the witnesses share.

- **Begin with faith.** Before you ask for anything, say to God in your own words, "I believe that You are here, and that You hear me."
- **Say "Father."** Pray the Lord's Prayer slowly each day, and stop for a moment at the first word.
- **Learn from a different tradition.** Try one way of praying that is new to you: a time of silence, a written prayer, or praying aloud with a friend.
- **Check your heart.** If you are holding something against someone, forgive them before you pray, as Jesus taught.

### A closing prayer

Father in heaven, I believe that You are here, and that You reward those who search for You. Thank You that I do not need a priest's long road or a costly sacrifice to reach You, because Jesus has opened the way. Thank You for the witnesses of every age and every church, who taught me to come to You as a child. Where they differ, give us patience with one another. Where they agree, make me faithful. Teach me to pray with faith, to keep on asking, to pray with others, to pray Your word, and to obey what I pray. In Jesus' name. Amen.

- How would you answer Agbekun's question, "How do I pray to the Great God?"
- Which of the things the witnesses agree on do you find hardest to practice?
- What could you learn about prayer from Christians whose tradition is different from your own?

## Prayer and the Word

> "Take the veil from my eyes, so that I may see the wonders in Your instruction." (Psalm 119:18)

Picture an early summer morning in the 1840s. A German-born pastor walks out of the city of Bristol, in England, into the fields. He carries two books: his Bible, and a New Testament printed in large type, easy to read in the open air. When he grows tired of walking, he sits for a while on a stile, the wooden steps that let people climb over a farm fence. He reads slowly, one verse at a time. (He describes these walks himself, as part of a habit he began in the spring of 1841.)

His name was George Müller. In Volume 8 we met him as the man who cared for thousands of orphans by prayer alone. But in these hours he was not asking for money. He was learning something new about how to begin the day with God.

For about ten years, he wrote, his habit had been to give himself to prayer as soon as he was dressed in the morning. It was a good habit. But often it went badly. He would kneel for a quarter of an hour, half an hour, even an hour, and his mind would wander. Only after a long struggle would he really begin to pray.

Now he tried a different order. First he asked, in a few words, for the Lord's blessing on His word. Then he began to read the New Testament from the beginning, thinking over each verse, not to prepare a sermon, but to feed his own soul. And something happened that surprised him. After a few minutes, the verse in front of him led him to confess a sin, or to give thanks, or to pray for someone, or to ask for help. He had sat down to read, and he found himself praying.

He wrote that no book and no preacher had taught him this. More than fourteen years later he was still keeping the habit and still thankful for it. His heart, he said, was nourished by the truth before he spoke, and he seldom had to fight his wandering mind any more.

What Müller found was not new. It is as old as the psalms. His few words before reading were Psalm 119:18 in his own words: Lord, open my eyes. And what followed was what God's people have done for three thousand years. They have taken God's words and spoken them back to Him.

### Praying with borrowed words

Jesus prayed the Scriptures. He grew up praying the psalms (Volume 1, Introduction), and on the cross the words that came to His lips were psalms: "My God, My God, why have You abandoned Me?" (Psalm 22:1) and "Father, I place My spirit in Your hands" (Luke 23:46), from Psalm 31:5 (Volume 2, chapter 19).

The first church did the same. When Peter and John were threatened, the believers prayed Psalm 2 back to God and asked Him for courage (Acts 4:24-26; Volume 3, chapter 4).

The thread runs through every century. Macrina learned the psalms as a girl and kept them with her all day (Volume 4, chapter 12). Benedict's monks prayed all one hundred and fifty psalms every week (Volume 5, chapter 1), and Ethiopian children learned the Psalter, called *Dawit*, by heart (Volume 5, chapter 2). The Lollards in England, gathered at night around a handwritten Gospel, prayed the Lord's Prayer in their own tongue (Volume 5, chapter 15). Luther taught his barber to weave a "garland" from each line of the Lord's Prayer: what does it teach, what can I thank God for, what must I confess, what shall I ask? (Volume 6, chapter 2). Lancelot Andrewes built his private prayers largely from verses of Scripture and the ancient prayers of the church (Volume 6, chapter 11). Robert Murray M'Cheyne gave his church a calendar for reading the whole Bible, so that, feeding on the same passages, they would agree in what they asked of God (Volume 8, chapter 4). Dietrich Bonhoeffer called the psalms the prayer book of the Bible, and read them every day in his prison cell (Volume 9, chapter 14). And in our own time Tim Keller learned from Luther's letter to move from Scripture into meditation, and from meditation into prayer (Volume 10, chapter 20).

Cyprian of Carthage said that it pleases God when we pray with His own words. Müller would have agreed. Here is how he described what happened after he began reading first:

> "For my heart being nourished by the truth, being brought into experimental fellowship with God, I speak to my Father and to my Friend (vile though I am, and unworthy of it) about the things that he has brought before me in his precious word."
> George Müller, *The Life of Trust*

*Experimental* here means "known by experience." Müller did not only learn about God. He met Him.

### Speaking upward

Psalm 119 is the longest chapter in the Bible. Almost every one of its 176 verses speaks of God's word. In Volume 5 we read our key verse with John Wycliffe, and saw that we need God to take the veil from our eyes before we can understand His word.

But look at the psalm from another side. It is not a lesson about the Bible. It is a prayer. Nearly every verse is spoken to God: "Deep in my heart I have kept Your word safe" (Psalm 119:11), "How I love Your instruction!" (Psalm 119:97). Andrew Murray noticed this:

> "All the Psalmist has to say about the Word of God, whether with regard to his own attachment to it, or his need for God's teaching and quickening, is spoken upwards into the face of God."
> Andrew Murray, *The Inner Chamber*

*Quickening* means being made alive. The writer of Psalm 119 does not read God's word and then go away to think about it alone. He turns his face up and talks to God about what he has read. Our key verse is one of those prayers. It is a prayer before reading: open my eyes. And it is full of expectation. The psalmist believes there are "wonders" in God's instruction, waiting to be seen.

That is the pattern the witnesses teach. Pray before you read: "Take the veil from my eyes." Read slowly, as Müller did. Then let each verse turn into thanks, confession or a request. Jesus promised, "If you remain in Me and My words remain in you, ask for whatever you want, and it will be done for you" (John 15:7). When His words fill our prayers, our requests begin to match His will.

One honest word. Some psalms cry out for God to punish enemies, and believers have prayed them in different ways. Some teach that we pray them with Christ, handing our anger to the Judge of all the earth. Others leave them unspoken. Either way, these psalms show us that anger can be given to God instead of being taken into our own hands.

### Three key insights

1. **God's word gives us not only something to read but words to speak back to Him.** From Jesus on the cross to Benedict's monks and Luther's barber, the witnesses prayed God's own words. When we do not know what to say, the Bible gives us a voice.
2. **Reading that turns into prayer feeds our own soul before it feeds anyone else.** Müller stopped forcing his prayers and began to let Scripture lead him into them. His heart was warmed first, and the work for others flowed out of that.
3. **When God's words fill our prayers, our requests begin to match His will.** Praying Scripture keeps us from praying only our own wishes. It teaches us to want what God has promised.

### For us today

Many believers today own a Bible but rarely pray it. Others long to read but cannot, or have no Bible of their own, or live where Bibles are hard to find. The witnesses speak to all of us.

If you have a Bible, try reading less and praying more of what you read. One verse, prayed slowly, may feed you more than a whole chapter read in a hurry. If you cannot read, you can still pray Scripture. Learn a psalm by heart from someone who reads it to you, or listen to the Bible on a phone or radio, as many do in Africa and Asia. The Lollards had only a few handwritten pages, and the hidden believers of many lands have had only what they remembered. A memorized psalm cannot be taken from you.

### Try this

This week, let God's word lead you into prayer.

- **Pray before you read.** Each day, begin with Psalm 119:18: "Take the veil from my eyes, so that I may see the wonders in Your instruction."
- **Read one psalm a day.** Read it slowly, and after each verse turn it into a prayer of thanks, confession or request.
- **Weave a garland.** Take one line of the Lord's Prayer, and ask Luther's four questions: What does it teach? What can I thank God for? What must I confess? What shall I ask?
- **Carry one verse.** Choose one short verse in the morning, repeat it during the day, and pray it for someone you meet.

### A closing prayer

Lord, take the veil from my eyes, so that I may see the wonders in Your instruction. Thank You for giving me Your words, and for the witnesses who prayed them before me: Jesus on the cross, the first church under threat, the monks and the Lollards, Luther and Müller and all the rest. When I do not know what to say, give me Your words. When I read, speak to me. When I have heard You, teach me to answer You, with thanks, with confession and with trust. Let Your word remain in me, and let my prayers be shaped by it. Amen.

- Have you ever found, as Müller did, that reading Scripture slowly led you into prayer? What happened?
- Which psalm or verse would you most like to learn by heart, so that you can pray it anywhere?
- How might praying God's own words change the things you ask Him for?

## Prayer and Fasting

> "But when you fast, put oil on your head and wash your face, so that no one but your Father, who is there in secret, will know that you are fasting. Your Father sees what is hidden, and He will repay you." (Matthew 6:17-18)

In the early 1730s, at the University of Oxford in England, a small group of young men went without food every Wednesday and Friday. They were not poor, and they were not ill. They were keeping a rule.

The leaders were two brothers, John and Charles Wesley, whom we met in Volume 7. With a few friends they met to pray, to study the Bible, and to take Communion often. They visited the sick and the prisoners. And they fasted twice a week. Other students laughed and called them "the Holy Club" and "Methodists," people who lived by method and rule.

Why Wednesday and Friday? Because the early church had fasted on those days, and the young men honored the early church deeply. Years later John Wesley explained the rule. Every Methodist at Oxford, he said, fasted every Wednesday and Friday of the year, "unless in case of sickness," in imitation of the first Christians.

One poor student joined them. His name was George Whitefield, and he paid for his studies by serving richer students. Wesley later remembered that Whitefield "joined with them in fasting on Wednesdays and Fridays; in visiting the sick and the prisoners." But Whitefield, anxious to make himself right with God, pushed his fasting and his rules so hard that he became seriously ill (Volume 7, chapter 8). John Wesley, for his part, fasted for years and still had no peace, until an evening in May 1738 when he understood that he was saved by grace through faith in Christ (Volume 7, chapter 7).

Neither man stopped fasting after that. But both had learned that fasting cannot buy God's favor. It can only make room to seek Him. And near the end of his life, as we will see, Wesley told honestly what had become of the Methodists' fasting since those Oxford days.

### A thread through the centuries

The Wesleys were following a very old path. The *Didache*, one of the earliest Christian handbooks, told Christians to fast on Wednesdays and Fridays, and to fast for those who persecute them (Volume 4, chapter 1). The monks of the Egyptian desert fasted hard. Yet when some brothers asked Abba Agathon which good work takes the greatest effort, fasting or night watches or giving everything away, he answered that no labor is as great as prayer (Volume 4, chapter 10). The Ethiopian church has kept its fast days together for sixteen centuries, about one hundred and eighty days a year for ordinary believers (Volume 5, chapter 2).

The Reformers did not throw fasting away. John Calvin, whom we met in Volume 6, wrote that pastors should call the church to fasting and prayer in times of danger or great decision. He gave three reasons for a fast:

> "A holy and lawful fast has three ends in view. We use it either to mortify and subdue the flesh, that it may not wanton, or to prepare the better for prayer and holy meditation; or to give evidence of humbling ourselves before God, when we would confess our guilt before him."
> John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*

To "mortify the flesh" means to put our selfish desires to death, so that they do not run wild. Puritans in England, Scotland and New England called days of fasting and humiliation in times of war, plague or failed harvests, and David Brainerd set apart a day to fast and pray for souls "in many distant places" (Volume 7, chapter 10).

The thread runs on. Pastor Hsi in China called his household to fast and pray for three days for his troubled wife (Volume 8, chapter 12). The little group in Los Angeles that saw the beginning of Azusa Street had agreed to pray and fast for ten days (Volume 9, chapter 3). Korean believers have climbed prayer mountains to fast and pray (Volume 10, chapter 6), and Derek Prince and Bill Bright called a generation back to fasting (Volume 10, chapter 10). In many churches of Africa, Asia and Latin America, fasting is simply part of Christian life.

### Oil on your head

In Jesus' day, some people who fasted made sure everyone saw it, with gloomy faces and unwashed hair. Jesus said that such people "have been paid in full" (Matthew 6:16). They wanted to be seen, and they were seen. That was all the reward they got.

So He told His followers to do the opposite: "put oil on your head and wash your face." In that land, oil on the head was part of getting ready for an ordinary day, or even for a feast. Jesus is saying: on the day you fast, look normal. Look cheerful. Let it be a secret between you and your Father.

Notice that He says "when you fast," not "if." He expected His people to fast. When He was asked why His disciples did not fast, He answered that wedding guests do not mourn while the bridegroom is with them, but "a time will come when the bridegroom will be taken from them, and then they will fast" (Matthew 9:15). We live in that time. Christian fasting is a kind of homesickness for Jesus. Our hunger says, "Lord, You are more than bread to me. Come and fill us. Come back soon."

And notice the promise: "Your Father sees what is hidden, and He will repay you." The reward is in the Father's hands. It is not a payment we have earned. It is a Father's delight in a child who wanted Him more than food.

### Fasting wisely and safely

Here are Wesley's words, written nearly sixty years after Oxford, about the Wednesday and Friday fast:

> "So they were by the Methodists for several years; by them all, without any exception; but afterwards, some in London carried this to excess, and fasted so as to impair their health. It was not long before others made this a pretence for not fasting at all."
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

A *pretence* is an excuse. There is a ditch on each side of the road: fasting so hard that we harm the bodies God gave us, and giving up fasting altogether. Calvin noticed the same thing, and wrote that "all have not the same bodily constitution, nor the same state of health."

So be wise. Some people should not go without food at all, or only with a doctor's advice: pregnant or nursing mothers, children, the very old and frail, people with diabetes, people with heart or kidney disease, and people who take medicine that must be taken with food. If you have ever struggled with an eating disorder, such as starving yourself or making yourself sick after eating, fasting from food can be dangerous for you and can feed the illness. Choose a different fast, and talk with someone you trust. For everyone else: begin small, keep drinking water, stop if you feel faint or ill, and end a fast gently with a small meal.

You can also fast in other ways. Daniel once ate "no fine food" for three weeks (Daniel 10:3). You can give up one kind of food, or your phone, television or social media for a day, and give that time to prayer.

And a word to those who already go hungry. Many believers in the world do not have enough to eat, not by choice. God does not ask you to add hunger to hunger. Jesus once looked at a hungry crowd and said, "I feel deep pity for the crowd" (Matthew 15:32), and He fed them. Your Father sees your need. You may offer Him another gift: an hour of prayer, or a song in the night. And for those of us who have enough, the fast God chooses includes sharing our bread with the hungry (Isaiah 58:7).

### Three key insights

1. **In every century the church has fasted, and fasting has always served prayer, never replaced it.** From the *Didache* to the prayer mountains of Korea, believers have gone without food to make room for God. Agathon and Calvin agree that the purpose is prayer.
2. **A fast kept for the Father's eyes alone is a secret He delights to reward.** Jesus told us to wash our faces and look ordinary. Our hidden hunger tells Him that we want Him more than bread, and He sees it.
3. **The road of fasting has a ditch on each side: harming the body, and giving up altogether.** Wesley saw Methodists fall into both. A wise fast fits our health and our circumstances, and it never forgets the hungry.

### For us today

If your church fasts often, ask whether your fasting is still hidden, joyful and aimed at prayer. If you have never fasted, ask whether Jesus' words "when you fast" are an invitation to you. A student in Accra, a mother in Manila and a worker in São Paulo may fast in different ways, but the Father who sees in secret is the same.

### Try this

This week, try one small, hidden fast.

- **Check your health first.** If anything in the list above applies to you, choose a fast from something other than food, or ask a doctor or health worker.
- **Choose one meal or one day.** Many Christians, from the *Didache* to Wesley, fasted on Wednesday or Friday. You might skip one meal on one of those days.
- **Turn hunger into prayer.** Each time you feel hungry, pray one short prayer, such as "Lord, You are more than bread to me."
- **Keep it secret and share.** Wash your face, tell no one, and give what you saved to someone in need.

### A closing prayer

Father, You see what is hidden. Thank You for the believers of every century who fasted and prayed: for the first Christians, for the monks of the desert and the church of Ethiopia, for Calvin and Wesley, for Pastor Hsi and the believers on Korea's prayer mountains. Teach me to fast with joy and in secret, not to be seen, and not to earn Your love, but because I long for You. Keep me from harming the body You gave me, and from giving up what is good. Remember all who are hungry today, and make me generous. Lord Jesus, You are more than bread to me. Come soon. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Jesus wanted fasting to be a secret between us and our Father?
- Which ditch are you more likely to fall into: fasting in a harmful way, or not fasting at all?
- If fasting from food is not wise for you, what other fast might help you make room for prayer?

## Alone and Together

> "When he had grasped this, he made his way to the home of Mary, mother of John whose other name was Mark. A good number of people had come together there and were praying." (Acts 12:12)

In Volume 7 we visited a crowded house in the flat, wet country of eastern England. It was the rectory at Epworth, and it was never quiet. Babies cried, children recited their lessons, and servants came and went. In the middle of it all was Susanna Wesley, the rector's wife and the mother of a very large family.

Her family remembered a picture of her that has been told ever since. When she needed to pray and there was no quiet room to go to, she sat down and threw her long apron up over her head. Under that cloth she was alone with God. The children learned that when their mother's head was covered, they must leave her in peace. The story comes from family memory, not from her own letters, so we cannot be sure of every detail. But her letters show that she kept set hours with God.

Now see her on a winter Sunday evening, when her husband was away in London. The parish church held no afternoon service, so Susanna began to read a sermon and pray with her own household in the rectory kitchen. A servant boy told his parents, and they asked if they could come too. Then their neighbors came. Soon, she wrote, more than two hundred people were coming, and many were turned away for lack of room.

So the same woman who hid under her apron to be alone with God also opened her kitchen to pray with her neighbors. She did not think she had to choose. The hidden place and the crowded room both belonged to her life with God (Volume 7, chapter 6).

### Peter knew where to go

Our key verse comes at a turning point in the story of Peter's escape from prison. In Volume 3 we stood with the church that prayed for him "without letup" (Acts 12:5; Volume 3, chapter 8). Here we follow Peter himself.

An angel woke him, and the iron gate opened by itself. At first Peter "took it all for a vision" (Acts 12:9). Then, standing alone in a dark street, he understood: "Now I know for certain," he said, "that the Lord sent His angel and snatched me out of Herod's grasp" (Acts 12:11). What did he do next? He did not hide alone. "When he had grasped this, he made his way to the home of Mary." He knew which door had been opened for prayer, even at that hour of the night.

Notice too that Peter was no stranger to praying alone. A few years earlier, in the town of Joppa, he "went up on the roof to pray. It was about noon" (Acts 10:9). There, alone, God spoke to him and sent him to Cornelius. Peter's life had both a roof to pray on alone and a house where believers prayed together.

Jesus lived the same way. He told us to "go into an inner room, shut the door, and pray to your Father who is there in secret" (Matthew 6:6), and He Himself went out "long before daylight" to "a lonely place" to pray (Mark 1:35). But He also "went up the mountain to pray, taking Peter, John and James along" (Luke 9:28). And on His last night, in Gethsemane, He asked His friends, "Stay here and keep awake with Me" (Matthew 26:38). (See Volume 2, chapters 1, 6 and 18.)

### The thread through the centuries

The witnesses in this series kept both places.

Some are remembered mostly for praying alone. Antony went into the Egyptian desert to be alone with God. Yet after nearly twenty years in an old fort, Athanasius tells us, he came out, and many people came to him for help, and he prayed for them (Volume 4, chapter 9). Lancelot Andrewes, a bishop at the court of King James, prayed for hours each day from a little notebook of prayers he had written for himself and never meant to publish (Volume 6, chapter 11).

Others are remembered for praying together. The first disciples waited in the upper room, "devoted to prayer and of one mind in it" (Acts 1:14; Volume 3, chapter 1). At Herrnhut the Moravian Brethren divided the day and night into hours, so that someone was always praying (Volume 7, chapter 4). At Kells in Ireland four young men met every Friday in a schoolhouse to pray (Volume 8, chapter 9). In Pyongyang in 1907 a whole church prayed aloud at once (Volume 9, chapter 4). In the 1960s Evelyn Christenson taught women in small groups to pray short, simple prayers one subject at a time (Volume 10, chapter 8).

But look closely, and the two belong together. At Fulton Street in New York, Jeremiah Lanphier opened the door of the prayer room at noon, and for half an hour he prayed alone. Then he heard a step on the stairs (Volume 8, chapter 8). Dietrich Bonhoeffer, in his little book *Life Together*, wrote about the day with others and the day alone, and taught, in effect, that each one needs the other (Volume 9, chapter 14).

Charles Finney, the American revival preacher, saw that a prayer meeting can only be as warm as the private prayers of those who come:

> "Neglect of secret prayer. Christians who do not pray in secret, cannot unite with power in a prayer meeting, and cannot have the spirit of prayer."
> Charles G. Finney, *Lectures on Revivals of Religion*

George Müller of Bristol kept both habits through a long and busy life. In 1859 he described his week:

> "Every Wednesday evening I meet with my helpers for united prayer; and day by day I have stated seasons, when I seek to bring the work with its great variety of spiritual and temporal necessities, before the Lord in prayer, having perhaps each day 50 or more matters to bring before Him, and thus I obtain the blessing."
> George Müller, *Answers to Prayer*

Each place protects the other. Alone, we can tell God things we could never say in a meeting, but such prayer can shrink until it is only about me. Together, we hear needs we would never have known, but a meeting can turn into a show, which Jesus warned against: some "love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners where others can see them" (Matthew 6:5). The secret room keeps the meeting honest, and the meeting keeps the secret room wide.

### Three key insights

1. **Praying alone teaches us to hear God, and praying together teaches us to carry one another.** In the inner room we listen to the Father without distraction. In the meeting we hear our brothers' and sisters' burdens, and we learn to bear them before God as if they were our own.
2. **A home that is known as a place of prayer becomes a refuge for those in trouble.** Peter knew where to go in the middle of the night, because Mary's house was open for prayer. Every home that prays can become such a door for someone in danger, grief or need.
3. **The habit of secret prayer and the habit of shared prayer each protect the other from its own dangers.** Prayer alone can shrink until it is only about ourselves, and prayer together can become a show. Kept side by side, each keeps the other honest and wide.

### For us today

Some of us pray easily alone but are shy of praying aloud with others. Some pray freely in a group but hardly ever when no one is listening. The witnesses call us to both.

Many believers in the world have little or no privacy. A family of eight shares two rooms in Nairobi or Dhaka. A young worker sleeps in a crowded factory dormitory. For them, Susanna's apron is good news: a scarf over the head, a walk to fetch water, or a few minutes on a bus can become an inner room.

Others are very much alone. Some live in places where Christians cannot meet openly. Some are old or sick and cannot leave the house. For them, praying together may mean one other person on the telephone, or two friends who agree to pray for the same need at the same hour, as Susanna once did with her son Samuel when he was far away at school. Jesus promised, "For whenever two or three come together in My name, I am there among them" (Matthew 18:20).

### Try this

This week, keep both places.

- **Make an apron.** Choose one simple sign that tells the people around you that you are praying: a scarf, a corner, a chair, a walk. Use it for ten minutes each day, even if the house is noisy.
- **Find one partner.** Ask one believer to pray with you once this week, in person or by telephone. Agree on one need and pray about it together.
- **Pray at the same hour.** If you cannot meet, agree with a friend to pray for the same thing at the same time each day, wherever you both are.
- **Open your door.** If you can, invite two or three people to your home once to pray. A cup of tea and a few short prayers are enough.

### A closing prayer

Father, You see me in secret, and You meet with Your people when they gather in the name of Jesus. Thank You for Susanna Wesley under her apron and in her crowded kitchen, for Peter on the roof and at Mary's door, and for all who have prayed alone and together before me. Give me an inner room, even in a noisy house. Give me brothers and sisters to pray with, even if they are far away. Keep my secret prayers from growing small, and keep my shared prayers from becoming a show. Make my home a place where people in trouble know they will find prayer. In the name of Jesus. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Do you find it easier to pray alone or with others? Why do you think that is?
- Peter knew exactly where to go when he was set free. Is there a home or a group where people know they will find prayer? Could yours become one?
- What is one danger of praying only alone, and one danger of praying only in a group? How could you guard against both?

## Waiting on the Lord

> "But those who wait for the LORD will take on fresh strength. They will climb high on outspread wings like eagles. They will run and not wear out; they will walk and not tire." (Isaiah 40:31)

In Volume 9 we went far out into the Atlantic Ocean, to the Isle of Lewis off the north-west coast of Scotland. It is a land of wind, rain, peat bogs and low stone houses. In 1949, in the village of Barvas, two elderly sisters lived in a small cottage. Their names were Peggy and Christine Smith. As the story is usually told, Peggy was blind and Christine was bent with arthritis, and both were in their eighties. They were too frail to go to church. But they could pray.

Their parish grieved them. The island had a long and serious faith, but many young people were drifting away from God. So the sisters prayed for revival. The story says that twice a week they prayed through the night, from late evening until the early hours. They held on to one promise from God's word: "I will pour out water on parched ground and send streams across the dry land; I will pour out My Spirit on your children" (Isaiah 44:3).

Then, as the evangelist Duncan Campbell later told it, Peggy sent for her minister, James Murray MacKay, and urged him to gather the leaders of the church to pray. Some men began to meet in a barn two or three nights a week. For weeks nothing seemed to change. Then, as the story is told, one night a young deacon read from Psalm 24 about clean hands and a pure heart, and the men humbled themselves before God. In December 1949 Campbell himself came to the island, and within days, by his account, the meetings were crowded late into the night. Over the next two years many people on Lewis and Harris came to faith.

As we said in Volume 9, most of the famous details come from Campbell's later talks, and historians have rightly asked careful questions about them. Others were praying too, and the sisters' ages and part are told differently in different places. But this much is plain. There was a real revival on Lewis, and before it came, believers old and young had waited on God for a long time, with nothing to show for it but a promise (Volume 9, chapter 18).

### A promise for tired people

Isaiah first spoke our key verse to people who were worn out. God's people had lost their land and their temple. They said, "My path is hidden from the LORD, and my God lets my case slip by" (Isaiah 40:27). God answered them by pointing to Himself: He "does not tire or wear out," and "He gives strength to the exhausted" (Isaiah 40:28-29). Then He made His promise to "those who wait for the LORD."

Notice the order. First the eagle's flight, then running, and last of all walking. We might expect the list to grow more exciting. Instead it grows more ordinary. Charles Spurgeon, the London preacher, saw this in his own long life:

> "When we begin our Christian profession we mount up with wings as eagles, further on we run without weariness; but in our best and truest days we walk without fainting. Our pace seems slower, but it is more serviceable and better sustained."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *All of Grace*

Most persistent prayer is walking: the same name, the same need, brought to God again tomorrow morning.

### The thread through the centuries

Jacob is the first of the long waiters. He wrestled all night by the river Jabbok and would not let go: "I will not let you go unless you bless me" (Genesis 32:26). He was blessed, and he walked away at sunrise "limping on his injured hip" (Genesis 32:31; Volume 1, chapter 2). It is no accident that Isaiah calls the tired people of his day "Jacob."

Jesus told a parable "about their need to pray at all times and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1), the story of a widow who kept coming to an unjust judge (Volume 2, chapter 8). Monica prayed for her son Augustine for many years with tears, and for a long time nothing seemed to change (Volume 4, chapter 15). R. A. Torrey reported that George Müller prayed every day for two men for more than sixty years, and that the second came to faith within a year after Müller's death (Volume 2, chapter 8). Hudson Taylor carried a burden for inland China for months before he surrendered on the beach at Brighton and asked God for twenty-four workers (Volume 8, chapter 11). Evan Roberts had prayed for revival in Wales for years before anyone knew his name (Volume 9, chapter 1). Evelyn Christenson's mother, as Evelyn told it, prayed twenty-five years for her husband and thirty years for her son before each of them turned to Christ (Volume 10, chapter 8).

### Persistence or nagging?

Here we must be honest. Is there a kind of praying again and again that God does not want? Yes. Jesus warned, "When you pray, do not keep piling up empty words as the pagans do. They think they will be heard because they say so much." Then He gave the reason: "Your Father already knows what you need before you ask Him" (Matthew 6:7-8).

So what is the difference? Nagging treats God like the unjust judge, as if He were unwilling and must be worn down. Persistence remembers that the parable is a contrast: "Then surely God will give justice to the people He has chosen, when they call out to Him day and night" (Luke 18:7). Nagging demands a certain thing by a certain day. Persistence leaves the time to God. Nagging stops listening. Persistence keeps listening, and is ready to be corrected, as we will see with Paul in the next chapter. And nagging is only interested in the gift, while persistence keeps coming back to the Giver.

Andrew Murray, who wrote a whole book of short readings on waiting for God, saw that our waiting shows us our own hearts:

> "If we truly set ourselves to wait upon God, we shall find that it is with Him we are impatient, because He does not at once, or as soon as we could wish, do our bidding."
> Andrew Murray, *Waiting on God*

A little later in the same reading he gives the cure: "Seek not only the help, the gift, you need; seek Himself; wait for Him." The sisters on Lewis did this. They did not only ask for a crowded church. They held on to a promise of God's Spirit, and they were ready, with the men in the barn, to let God search their own hearts first.

### Three key insights

1. **The strength God gives to those who wait is usually strength to keep walking, not to fly away from the road.** Isaiah's promise ends with walking, and so does most of the life of prayer. To bring the same need to God again tomorrow, without fainting, is itself an answer to prayer.
2. **Persistence keeps coming back to God Himself, while nagging only keeps coming back to the thing we want.** Jesus does not ask us to wear God down with many words. He asks us to keep trusting a Father who already knows, and to seek Him even more than His gifts.
3. **A long prayer is easier to keep when it is held by a promise from God's word.** The sisters on Lewis held on to Isaiah 44:3. A promise does not tell us the day or the hour, but it gives our waiting a place to stand.

### For us today

What are you waiting for? A son or daughter far from God. A husband or wife who does not yet believe. A church that has grown cold. A land torn by war or corruption. A healing, a job, a way through. Some of you have been praying for so long that you have almost stopped.

The witnesses do not promise that your waiting will be short. Christenson's mother waited thirty years. Müller waited all his life, and one answer came after he had died. But they all say the same thing: keep walking. The God you are waiting for "does not tire or wear out."

Persistence is also for the persecuted church. Believers in prison, and families who do not know where their loved ones are held, are told to "call out to Him day and night" (Luke 18:7). Those of us who are free can wait with them.

### Try this

This week, practice walking prayer.

- **Write down one long prayer.** Choose one need you have prayed about for a long time. Write it down with today's date, as Hudson Taylor wrote his request in his Bible.
- **Find a promise.** Look for one verse that fits that need, and pray it back to God each day. If you have no Bible of your own, ask someone to help you learn Isaiah 40:31 by heart.
- **Seek the Giver.** Before you ask for the thing you want, spend one minute simply thanking God for who He is.
- **Wait with someone.** Tell one trusted friend about your long prayer, and ask them to pray with you for it once a week.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You do not tire or wear out, and You give strength to the exhausted. I am tired of waiting, and You know how long I have prayed. Thank You for Jacob, who would not let go, for Monica and her tears, for Peggy and Christine on their knees through the night, and for all who waited on You before me. Keep me from nagging You as if You did not care. Teach me to seek You more than Your gifts. Give me strength, not always to fly, but to keep walking and not faint, until I see Your goodness. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What is the longest you have ever prayed for one thing? What has kept you going, or what made you stop?
- How would you explain the difference between persistence and nagging to a new believer?
- Is there a promise in God's word that could hold your long prayer? What is it?

## When Heaven Seems Silent

> "I begged the Lord three times about this, asking that it would leave me. But He answered me, 'My grace is enough for you, for power comes to its fullness in weakness.'" (2 Corinthians 12:8-9)

In Volume 2 we followed Jesus to the olive garden called Gethsemane. Let us go there once more and watch only His prayer.

It is night. He has asked Peter, James and John to stay awake with Him, and He has gone a little farther on alone. He throws Himself face down on the ground. Luke, the doctor, tells us that "in His anguish He prayed all the more intensely, and His sweat became like thick drops of blood dripping down onto the ground" (Luke 22:44).

His request is plain: "My Father, if there is any way, let this cup pass by Me." The cup is the suffering of the cross. He does not pretend that He wants it. Then He adds, "But not what I want; what You want" (Matthew 26:39).

He comes back and finds His friends asleep. He goes away and prays again. He comes back, and they are asleep again. "So He left them, went away once more, and prayed a third time, saying the same words" (Matthew 26:44).

Three times the Son of God asked His Father for one thing. And the cup did not pass. Within hours He was arrested, beaten and nailed to a cross, where He cried out, "My God, My God, why did You abandon Me?" (Mark 15:34).

Was His prayer unheard? The letter to the Hebrews gives a surprising answer. "In the days of His life in the flesh, He offered up prayers and pleas, crying aloud and weeping, to the one who was able to save Him out of death, and He was heard for His godly fear" (Hebrews 5:7). He was heard. The Father did not take away the cup, but He was not silent either. Luke says an angel came "and gave Him strength" (Luke 22:43). And on the third day the Father raised Him from death, which was a greater answer than escape from it (Volume 2, chapter 18).

### Paul's thorn

Our key verse comes from a man who knew that night by heart. Paul tells the church in Corinth about "a thorn in the flesh." No one knows what it was: perhaps an illness, perhaps enemies. He calls it "a messenger of Satan, sent to beat me down" (2 Corinthians 12:7). Like his Lord in the garden, he asked three times for it to go.

It did not go. Instead the Lord answered with a word: "My grace is enough for you." Paul did not receive what he asked for. He received the Lord's own word, and a strength that worked best when he was weak. So he chose to boast about his weakness, "for then the power of Christ may spread its tent over me" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Notice that he did not stop because he had given up on God. He stopped because God had spoken.

### The thread through the centuries

The witnesses in this series knew this kind of silence.

Some refusals turned out, years later, to be a deeper answer. Monica prayed all night at Carthage that her son would not sail for Rome, and in the morning the ship was gone. Yet in Italy Augustine came to Christ (Volume 4, chapter 15). Arthur T. Pierson, telling her story in his life of George Müller, wrote:

> "God's real answers to prayer are often seeming denials. Beneath the outward request He hears the voice of the inward desire, and He responds to the mind of the Spirit rather than to the imperfect and perhaps mistaken words in which the yearning seeks expression."
> Arthur T. Pierson, *George Müller of Bristol*

But we must be careful here, because not every story has a happy ending in this life. When Herod killed the apostle James and Peter walked free, Scripture gives no reason (Volume 3, chapter 8). David Brainerd once wrestled in prayer for "multitudes of poor souls" until he was wet with sweat, and afterward felt that he "had done nothing." He lived to see a harvest among the Lenape, but tuberculosis took him at twenty-nine (Volume 7, chapter 10). Samuel Ajayi Crowther, the first African bishop of his church, asked God that every mission station might have a good, solid church before he was laid aside. Instead, in his old age, his authority was broken by younger missionaries, and he died in Lagos in 1891 (Volume 8, chapter 16). From Easter 1896 until her death the next year, Thérèse of Lisieux often lost all joy in the thought of heaven, and kept praying in the dark (Volume 8, chapter 19). Dietrich Bonhoeffer prayed in his cell for his family and his fiancée, but he never came home to them. He was hanged two weeks before American soldiers reached the camp (Volume 9, chapter 14). Mother Teresa, her letters show, felt no sense of God's presence for nearly fifty years (Volume 10, chapter 5).

And in Ravensbrück, the Nazi camp for women, Betsie ten Boom gave thanks in a barracks full of fleas. Every prisoner there longed to go home. Betsie did not go home. She died in the camp on 16 December 1944, and her sister Corrie was released at the end of that month. Before she died, as Corrie told it, Betsie said that there is no pit so deep that God's love is not deeper still (Volume 9, chapter 15).

None of them stopped praying. The South African pastor Andrew Murray wrote:

> "There are times when waiting appears to be just losing time, but it is not so. Waiting, even in darkness, is unconscious advance, because it is God you have to do with, and He is working in you."
> Andrew Murray, *Waiting on God*

### If you are grieving

Perhaps you are reading this because you prayed for someone's healing, and they died. Perhaps you prayed for a child, a marriage, a job or a release from prison, and the door stayed shut. This chapter is for you, and it will not give you a neat explanation.

First, your prayer was not wasted, and it was not too weak. Jesus Himself prayed for something that did not happen. So did Paul. So did Monica, and Betsie, and countless faithful believers whose names no one remembers. Hebrews says of the heroes of faith, "All these died still holding to faith. They did not receive what was promised, but they saw it from far off and welcomed it" (Hebrews 11:13).

Second, God is not distant from your grief. At the grave of His friend Lazarus, "Jesus wept" (John 11:35), even though He knew what He was about to do. The psalmist says to God, "Store my tears in Your flask" (Psalm 56:8). Not one of them is lost.

Third, some answers wait for the last day. "Every tear He will wipe from their eyes. Death will be no more, and no more mourning, no crying, no pain" (Revelation 21:4).

### Three key insights

1. **Even the Son of God prayed a prayer that the Father did not grant, and He was still heard.** The cup did not pass from Jesus, yet Hebrews says He was heard. A "no" from God is not proof that He was not listening.
2. **Some of our prayers will be answered only on the day when God wipes away every tear.** Many faithful people died without seeing what they asked for. Their prayers are not lost; they are waiting for the morning of the resurrection.
3. **Those who sit with grieving people should offer their presence before they offer explanations.** When Job's friends first came, "they sat with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights. No one said a word to him" (Job 2:13). Their trouble began only when they started to explain.

### For us today

In many parts of the world, believers are told that if they pray with enough faith, every sickness will be healed and every problem solved. When it does not happen, they are told that their faith was too small. That teaching has broken many hearts. The witnesses teach us better. Faith is not a tool for making God do what we want. It is trust in a Father whose love is deeper than our pit.

So if someone in your church has lost a child, a husband or their health, do not rush to explain. Paul tells us to share "the tears of those who weep" (Romans 12:15). Sit with them, pray with them, bring food. And if you are the one in the dark, keep talking to God, even if your only prayer is the cry of Jesus on the cross: "My God, My God, why did You abandon Me?"

### Try this

This week, bring God your unanswered prayer.

- **Tell Him the truth.** Find a quiet moment and tell God plainly what you asked for and what happened. Use the words of Psalm 13 if you have none of your own: "How long, LORD?"
- **Pray the garden prayer.** Pray, as Jesus did, "Father, this is what I want. But not what I want; what You want." Say it slowly, even if you can only half mean it.
- **Name your tears.** Write down or say aloud the names of the people you prayed for who were not healed, and give them back to God.
- **Sit with someone.** Visit or call one person who is grieving. Do not explain. Listen, and pray a short prayer with them if they wish.

### A closing prayer

Father, You heard Your Son in the garden, though the cup did not pass. You heard Paul, though the thorn stayed. You hear me, even when heaven seems silent. I bring You the prayers I prayed that were not answered as I asked, and the people I love who were not healed. I do not understand. Keep my tears in Your flask. Let Your grace be enough for me today, and let Your power rest on my weakness. Teach me to sit gently with others who grieve. And bring us all to the day when You will wipe every tear from our eyes, through Jesus Christ, who prayed in the dark for me. Amen.

- Is there an unanswered prayer that still hurts? What would it look like to bring it honestly to God this week?
- How can you comfort someone who is grieving without trying to explain their loss?

## For All People

> "This is good, and it pleases God our Savior, who wants everyone to be saved and to come to know the truth." (1 Timothy 2:3-4)

In Volume 8 we walked through the green hills of western Massachusetts, in the young United States, to a small school called Williams College. In the summer of 1806 some of its students walked out on Saturday afternoons to a grove of maple trees in a meadow to pray.

One hot afternoon in August only five came: Samuel J. Mills, James Richards, Francis Robbins, Harvey Loomis and Byram Green. Dark clouds rose in the west. The rain came down, and the young men ran for the nearest shelter, a large haystack in the field, and crouched beside it.

While they waited, they talked about what some of them had been studying in class: the lands of Asia, and the millions there who had never heard of Jesus Christ. Mills, a freshman of twenty-three, grew more and more earnest. Why not send the gospel to Asia? As Byram Green remembered it, he said simply, "We can do it if we will." Loomis disagreed. The time was not ripe, he said; missionaries would only be killed.

Then Mills said, "Come, let us make it a subject of prayer under the haystack, while the dark clouds are going and the clear sky is coming." So, in the wet field, they prayed one by one for the world. Loomis kept silent. Mills prayed last, and asked God boldly to protect those who would carry the message of the cross.

We know the story because Green wrote it down nearly fifty years later, so we hold the details gently. But we know what followed. Four years later Mills and his friends asked the ministers of Massachusetts whether the churches would send them, and the first American society for overseas missions was formed. In 1812 Adoniram and Ann Judson sailed for Asia, and later they gave their lives to Burma, today called Myanmar. James Richards, who prayed by the haystack, served in Ceylon, today Sri Lanka, and died there (Volume 8, chapter 1).

Five students, one storm and a prayer for a continent they had never seen.

### Why we pray for everyone

Paul wrote our key verse to Timothy, a young pastor in the city of Ephesus. Just before it, he urged that "requests, prayers, intercessions and thanks be offered for everyone" (1 Timothy 2:1). In Volume 3 we looked at his command to pray for kings and rulers. Here we look at the reason he gives.

The reason is God Himself. He is "God our Savior, who wants everyone to be saved." Peter says the same: "He does not want anyone to be destroyed, but wants all people to turn back in repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). When we pray for the whole world, we are not asking God to care about people He has forgotten. We are joining what He already wants.

Then Paul gives a second reason: "For there is one God, and one mediator between God and humanity, Christ Jesus, Himself human, who gave Himself as a ransom for all" (1 Timothy 2:5-6). A mediator is someone who stands between two sides to bring them together. Because there is only one God, every nation belongs to Him. Because there is only one Mediator, and He gave Himself "for all," there is no people on earth for whom Christ did not die. Our prayers for the nations rest on His work, not on our own strength.

### The thread through the centuries

The witnesses have prayed this way from the beginning. Abraham pleaded with God for the city of Sodom, a city that was not his own: "Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?" (Genesis 18:25; Volume 1, chapter 1). Moses offered his own place in God's book for the sake of a sinning people (Exodus 32:32; Volume 1, chapter 3). Paul wrote of his own people, "What my heart longs for, and what I ask God for them, is that they may be saved" (Romans 10:1).

Around AD 96 the church in Rome, which had buried its martyrs, prayed for the rulers of the empire that had killed them (Volume 4, chapter 2). In 1732 Count Zinzendorf knelt by a roadside to pray with two young Moravians, a potter and a carpenter, who were setting out to tell slaves in the Caribbean about Jesus (Volume 7, chapter 5). In 1784 a few poor Baptist churches in England agreed to pray together each month, asking that the gospel would spread "to the most distant parts of the habitable globe." Within ten years one of their pastors, William Carey, was in India (Volume 7, chapters 16 and 17). Samuel Ajayi Crowther prayed for the whole Niger country as Caleb once asked for his mountain (Volume 8, chapter 16). Thérèse of Lisieux never left her convent, yet she prayed for two young missionaries, one of them bound for China (Volume 8, chapter 19). In October 1993, by the organizers' count, millions of believers prayed through the 10/40 Window, two countries each day (Volume 10, chapter 13).

Charles Spurgeon, the London preacher, saw that God has chosen to work in the world through the prayers of His people:

> "God will bless Elijah and send rain on Israel, but Elijah must pray for it. If the chosen nation is to prosper, Samuel must plead for it. If the Jews are to be delivered, Daniel must intercede. God will bless Paul and the nations shall be converted through him, but Paul must pray!"
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Mighty Power in Prayer*

### A letter on the table

E. M. Bounds tells a story about Hudson Taylor and the China Inland Mission. At one mission station in China, many more people were coming to faith than at others, though the missionaries elsewhere were just as faithful. No one knew why. Then, on a visit to England, Taylor met a man after one of his talks who knew that station surprisingly well. The man explained that he and the missionary there had been friends at college. For years the missionary had written to him with the names of people who were asking about Christ and of new believers, and every day the man at home had taken those names to God in prayer. Bounds adds:

> "A praying man at home, praying definitely, praying daily, for specific cases among the heathen. That is the real intercessory missionary."
> E. M. Bounds, *Purpose in Prayer*

("Heathen" was the old word for people who did not know the true God.) We do not know the man's name, and Bounds gives us the story second-hand. But the lesson is simple. A letter on a kitchen table became a prayer list, and a friend at home shared in the harvest.

### Three key insights

1. **Intercession for the world rests on the one Mediator who gave Himself for all.** We do not pray for the nations because we are strong, but because Christ Jesus stands between God and every people, and gave Himself as a ransom for all.
2. **Every piece of news from a distant land can become a call to prayer.** A map, a calendar, a news report or a missionary's letter brings the world to our door. What we do with it next is up to us.
3. **A believer who prays faithfully for one far-off worker shares in the harvest that worker gathers.** The man at home stayed in England, yet the names in his friend's letters became his daily prayer, and God answered.

### For us today

The haystack students lived in a land that sent missionaries. Today the map has changed. Churches in Nigeria, Korea and Brazil now send missionaries of their own around the world. A grandmother in Malawi can pray for Pakistan, and a factory worker in Dhaka for Peru. The call to pray for all people belongs to every believer in every land.

You do not need money, travel or education to pray for the world. You need something to remind you. It may be a map on the wall, or a calendar that gives one country for each day. It may be the evening news: a flood, a war, an election, a hungry city. Turn the report into a prayer for the people in it and for the believers among them. It may be a letter, or a message on a phone, from a missionary or a believer far away.

And remember the persecuted church. Helen Berhane sang and prayed for her guards in a metal shipping container in Eritrea, while Christians around the world prayed for her release for two years (Volume 10, chapter 18). Believers in Iran have prayed in secret at great cost (Volume 10, chapter 19). "Keep in mind those in prison, as if you were in chains alongside them" (Hebrews 13:3). Pray for them by country, even when you cannot know their names.

### Try this

This week, let the world come to your prayers.

- **Use a map or a calendar.** Choose one country for each day of the week. Pray for its people, its leaders and its church.
- **Pray the news.** When you hear a news report from another land, stop and pray one sentence for the people in it.
- **Adopt one worker.** Find one missionary or pastor in another country. Ask for their news, and pray for the names they send you, as the man in England did.
- **Pray for the persecuted.** Each day, pray for believers in one country where following Jesus is dangerous.

### A closing prayer

God our Savior, You want everyone to be saved and to come to know the truth. Thank You for Jesus, the one Mediator, who gave Himself as a ransom for all. Thank You for Abraham and Moses, for Paul and Clement, for the Moravians and the haystack students, and for every hidden believer who has prayed for the nations. Widen my heart. Let every map, every news report and every letter turn me to prayer. Send workers into Your harvest from every nation to every nation. Strengthen Your children who suffer for Your name. And let me live to see more and more people from every land come to know You. Amen.

- Is there a missionary or a believer far away whose news you could receive and pray over regularly?

## Restore Us, O God

> "LORD God of Armies, bring us back! Turn the light of Your face on us, and we will be saved." (Psalm 80:19)

In Volume 8 (chapter 9) we walked with four young men along the country lanes of County Antrim, in the north of Ireland. It was the autumn of 1857. Every Friday evening they met in an old schoolhouse near the village of Kells, because it stood halfway between their homes. They were young laymen, not ministers. Their leader, James McQuilkin, had come to faith in Christ only the year before.

What set McQuilkin praying was George Müller's own story of how God had supplied an orphan house in Bristol, England, in answer to prayer alone. Then news came of great prayer meetings in America. A question would not leave him. If God had done such things for Mr. Müller, simply in answer to prayer, why should He not do them in Antrim?

So the four friends read the Bible and prayed for their parish. Week after week, through the winter, nothing happened that anyone could see. Then, on the first day of 1858, a young farm servant came to faith in Christ and joined them. Now there were five. Soon there were six. All through that year the work grew quietly, with no crowds and no excitement. A minister from the nearby town of Ballymena called it "a calm, quiet, gradual" work.

Then the fire spread. By the summer of 1859, farmers and weavers were leading prayer meetings all over the countryside, and tens of thousands gathered for prayer in the open air in Belfast. A Belfast professor who visited Connor, William Gibson, called it *The Year of Grace*.

Kells was not the only root of that revival: others in the parish had prayed for years. But it shows clearly what God can do through a few ordinary believers who keep on asking Him to bring His people back to life.

### What the revivals teach about prayer

Volumes 7 to 10 were full of revivals: seasons when God woke His people, and many who had been far from Him came to faith. They were not all alike. But when we lay them side by side, five lessons about prayer stand out.

**They began in prayer.** Before the crowds came to Cambuslang in Scotland in 1742, small praying societies had met in kitchens and barns for years (Volume 7, chapter 11). Evan Roberts prayed for revival in Wales long before anyone knew his name (Volume 9, chapter 1). Two old sisters on the Isle of Lewis prayed through the night, twice a week, in their cottage (Volume 9, chapter 18). In New York in 1857, one man opened a room for prayer at noon, prayed alone for half an hour, and saw only six people come on the first day (Volume 8, chapter 8).

**They came with confession.** In Wales, Roberts's first point was to confess to God every sin not yet confessed. In Pyongyang in 1907, a church elder named Kim stood up and confessed that he had hated another leader and a missionary, and asked them to forgive him (Volume 9, chapter 4). In East Africa, believers learned to "walk in the light," putting wrongs right and even returning stolen goods (Volume 9, chapter 17).

**They came with unity.** At Herrnhut in 1727, quarreling refugees made peace with each other on the way to the Lord's Supper, and later said simply, "We learned to love" (Volume 7, chapter 4). In Pyongyang, Presbyterian and Methodist missionaries prayed together for months before the revival. In East Africa, African and European believers confessed their faults to one another, which was rare in a colonial land.

**They could not be manufactured.** No one could plan a revival or switch it on. Two old women in a cottage could pray for one, but only God could send it. In our own time, the story of the 24-7 prayer rooms reminded us that excitement can be imitated, but a painted fire gives no heat (Volume 10, chapter 15). Andrew Murray put it plainly:

> "Regeneration, the giving of Divine life, we all acknowledge to be God's act, a miracle of His power. The restoring or reviving of the Divine life, in a soul or a Church, is as much a supernatural work."
> Andrew Murray, *The Ministry of Intercession*

**They had their excesses, and their fruit.** In Ulster some people trembled and fell to the ground, and a Belfast minister answered the story of 1859 with a book called *The Year of Delusion* (Volume 8, chapter 9). By 1906 Evan Roberts was worn out in body and mind, and he never returned to public life. The great prayer meetings of 1858 did not heal America of the sin of slavery (Volume 8, chapter 8). In Philadelphia, in an age of awakening, church trustees pulled Black believers from their knees in the middle of prayer (Volume 7, chapter 14). Yet the fruit was real. In Connor, the parish where Kells lies, arrests for drunkenness fell from thirty-seven in 1857 to four in 1859. In Wales, people paid old debts and made peace with their neighbors. Charles Finney, who saw many revivals, gave a test that still helps:

> "Those religious excitements which do not consist in the spirit of love, are not revivals of religion. Perhaps the church may be much excited, and bustle about with a great show of zeal, and boisterous noise, but no tenderness of spirit."
> Charles G. Finney, *Lectures to Professing Christians*

### Bring us back

Psalm 80 was prayed in a dark time. It names Ephraim, Benjamin and Manasseh, the tribes descended from Rachel, and many think it comes from the years when enemies were destroying the northern kingdom of Israel. It pictures God's people as a vine that God brought out of Egypt and planted (Psalm 80:8). Now its walls are broken, and "everyone who walks along the road picks its grapes" (Psalm 80:12).

Three times the psalm cries out with the same prayer, and each time God's name grows longer. First: "God, bring us back!" (Psalm 80:3). Then: "God of Armies, bring us back!" (Psalm 80:7). And at the end: "LORD God of Armies, bring us back!" (Psalm 80:19). As the trouble grows, the people remember more of who God is. Many English Bibles translate this cry, "Restore us." The Hebrew word means to turn back, or to return. The people are asking God to turn them, because they cannot turn themselves.

And they ask for one thing above all: "Turn the light of Your face on us." This recalls the old blessing of the priests, "May the LORD make His face shine toward you and show you favor" (Numbers 6:25). Revival, at its heart, is not a large meeting. It is God's face turned toward His people again.

Notice too what the people promise: "Bring us back to life, and we will call on You by name" (Psalm 80:18). Revival begins in prayer, and it ends in more prayer. A church that God has brought back to life does not stop praying when the crowds come. It calls on His name more than ever.

### Three key insights

1. **Revival cannot be manufactured, but God has always loved to send it to people who pray.** No plan or method can switch it on. Yet from Cambuslang to Kells to Lewis, the fire fell where ordinary believers had been asking for it, often for a long time.
2. **Revival comes first to the church, as God's people confess their sins and are made one.** In Wales, Pyongyang, Herrnhut and East Africa, believers put things right with God and with one another before the wider world was touched. We pray for others best when we let God search us first.
3. **When God brings His people back to life, they call on His name, so revival begins and ends in prayer.** Psalm 80 promises that a revived people will pray. The test of a true revival is not the crowd it draws but the praying, loving church it leaves behind.

### For us today

Many believers long to see God move in their land: in a city in Europe where the churches are nearly empty, in a village in Asia where Christians are few, in an African or Latin American church where the young are drifting away. Psalm 80 gives you words for that longing. You may pray it with tears.

But do not wait for a famous preacher or a perfect plan. Kells began with four young men in a cold schoolhouse. And do not call every loud meeting a revival, or despise every quiet one. The fruit will show what God has done.

Revival also costs something: confessing a hidden sin, making peace with a believer from another church or tribe, paying back what you owe. And where believers must meet quietly, with no crowds at all, God has often worked most deeply.

### Try this

This week, begin to pray for God to bring His people back to life.

- **Pray the refrain.** Three times a day, pray Psalm 80:19 slowly, adding the name of your church, your town or your nation. If you have no Bible, learn the verse by heart today.
- **Let God search you first.** Before you pray for others, ask, "Lord, is there anything in me You want to put right?" If He shows you a wrong, put it right this week.
- **Find one or two others.** Agree with a friend, a neighbor or a family member to pray for revival at a set time each week, together in one place or by phone.
- **Remember honestly.** Tell someone a revival story from this series, faults and all, and thank God for what He truly did.

### A closing prayer

LORD God of Armies, bring us back! Turn the light of Your face on us, and we will be saved. Thank You for the young men of Kells, the praying societies of Cambuslang, and the believers of Wales, Pyongyang and East Africa who confessed their sins and were made one. I cannot make revival happen, and I do not want a painted fire. Search my heart first. Put right what is wrong in me and in Your church. Make us one in love. Bring us back to life, Lord, and we will call on Your name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think every revival in this series began with people who prayed, often for a long time?
- Psalm 80 asks God to "bring us back." What would it mean for your own life, or your church, to be brought back to God?
- How can we thank God for past revivals and still be honest about their faults?

## The Family Altar

> "Keep these words that I am commanding you today close to your heart. Teach them carefully to your children, and speak of them as you sit in your house and as you go on your way, as you lie down and as you rise." (Deuteronomy 6:6-7)

In Volume 9 (chapter 15) we visited a narrow old house in Haarlem, in the Netherlands. The family lived upstairs, above a small watch shop, and they called their home "the Beje." Casper ten Boom was the watchmaker. His daughter Corrie, the youngest of his children, became the first woman in the Netherlands licensed as a watchmaker.

Picture the table after breakfast. Around it sit Casper and his family, the workers from the shop, and whoever else is staying, for the house is always full of guests, foster children and people in need. Casper opens the Bible and reads aloud. Then he prays. In the evening, when the shop is closed, they gather again, and he reads and prays once more. It is not a long service. It is simply what the family does, every morning and every evening, year after year.

Then came the war. When the German army occupied the Netherlands, the ten Booms hid Jews and others in danger in their home. In February 1944 the house was raided and the family was arrested. Casper, eighty-four years old, died in prison about ten days later. Corrie and her sister Betsie were sent at last to Ravensbrück, a terrible concentration camp in Germany.

They had lost their home, their father and their freedom, but not the habit of that table. Corrie had smuggled in a small Bible. As she tells it in *The Hiding Place*, written many years later, the sisters began to hold Bible readings in their crowded, flea-filled barracks, every day and sometimes twice. Women from many nations gathered round. Corrie read aloud, others passed the words on in their own languages, and Betsie prayed.

The family altar had been carried out of Haarlem, through prisons and camps, into one of the darkest places on earth. What a father did faithfully at his table became a light for many women who had never sat at it.

### Prayer at home through the ages

Hannah poured out her soul to God for a child, and when Samuel was born she gave him back to the LORD (Volume 1, chapter 5). Centuries later, Paul remembered the "sincere faith" of young Timothy, "which first lived in your grandmother Lois and in your mother Eunice" (2 Timothy 1:5). The grandmother taught the mother, and the mother taught the son (Volume 3, chapter 18). The Roman officer Cornelius "feared God, as did his whole household," and "prayed to God at all times" (Acts 10:2).

In the fourth century, Macrina turned her family's country house in Pontus into a home of prayer. From girlhood, her brother wrote, the Psalms went with her through every part of her day (Volume 4, chapter 12). In North Africa, Monica lived with a husband who did not believe and had a hot temper. She prayed for him, and for her wandering son Augustine, for years, and before he died her husband came to Christ (Volume 4, chapter 15).

In the 1640s and 1650s, Richard Baxter gave a small book to every family in Kidderminster, and a Bible to the poor families who had none. In time, he wrote, on Sundays you could hear families singing psalms as you walked through the streets (Volume 6, chapter 14). In Japan, where Christians had to hide for more than two hundred years, parents taught the prayers to their children by mouth, softly, so the neighbors would not hear (Volume 6, chapter 10). In a crowded rectory in England, Susanna Wesley gave each child one evening a week alone with her to talk about their souls (Volume 7, chapter 6). And in our own day the short songs of Taizé, each a single line of Scripture or prayer, are easy to carry home and simple enough for anyone to learn, children included (Volume 10, chapter 4).

### At home, on the road, at night and in the morning

When Moses spoke the words of our verse, Israel was about to enter a new land. He had just given them the great command: "Hear, Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one. You are to love the LORD your God with your whole heart, with your whole being, and with all your strength" (Deuteronomy 6:4-5). Jesus called this the first of all the commandments (Mark 12:29-30). Jewish families have said these words morning and evening for many centuries.

Notice the order of the verses. First, "keep these words ... close to your heart." Only then, "teach them carefully to your children." We cannot pass on what we do not hold. Children learn more from what they see in us than from what we say.

Then notice the four times Moses names: sitting at home, walking on the road, lying down and getting up. These are the ordinary moments of every day. Prayer in the home is not mainly a long meeting. It is God's word woven into meals, journeys, bedtimes and mornings.

George Whitefield, preaching on family religion in the 1700s, gave a simple reason why a household should pray together, and not only one by one:

> "Besides, there are no families but what have some common blessings, of which they have been all partakers, to give thanks for; some common crosses and afflictions, which they are to pray against; some common sins, which they are all to lament and bewail: but how this can be done, without joining together in one common act of humiliation, supplication, and thanksgiving, is difficult to devise."
> George Whitefield, *Selected Sermons of George Whitefield*

A "cross" here means a shared trouble. Every home has blessings, troubles and sins that belong to all who live in it. So every home has something to pray about together.

What is learned at home also lasts. Charles Spurgeon noticed it among the old men of his church:

> "At any rate, I have shaken hands with greyheaded men who have forgotten nearly all the events which have intervened between their old age and the time of their childhood, but little matters which transpired at home, hymns learned at their mother's knee, and words spoken by their father or sister, have lingered with them. The voices of childhood echo throughout life. The first learned is generally the last forgotten."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Come Ye Children*

### Three key insights

1. **God's words must live in our own hearts before we can pass them on at home.** Moses put the heart before the teaching. A parent, grandparent or older friend who loves God's word will pass it on, often without knowing it.
2. **Prayer at home is woven into ordinary moments: at the table, on the road, at bedtime and on waking.** Deuteronomy names the plain times of every day. A few words of Scripture and prayer at those times can shape a whole household.
3. **Every believer has a household to pray for, whether married or single, with children or without.** Macrina, Amy Carmichael and the ten Boom sisters never married, yet each became the heart of a praying family. God "gives the lonely a home to live in" (Psalm 68:6).

### For us today

Homes are very different: three generations in two rooms in Manila or Nairobi, a widow alone in a flat in Seoul, a home where only one person follows Jesus, parents who work long hours or cannot read at all.

The witnesses speak to each of these homes. If you live alone, remember that Amy Carmichael never married, yet at Dohnavur in India the rescued children called her *Amma*, "mother" (Volume 9, chapter 8). Your household may be the neighbors, friends and church family God has given you. If you long for children and have none, Hannah's tears were heard, and so are yours. If your husband or wife does not believe, take heart from Monica, and from Peter's promise that "your way of life will win them over even without your saying a word" (1 Peter 3:1). If your home is crowded, remember Susanna's apron. If you cannot read, remember the hidden Christians of Japan, who passed on their prayers by heart for about two hundred and fifty years.

No home is too poor, too busy or too broken to become a place of prayer.

### Try this

This week, make your home a place of prayer, in small and simple ways.

- **Pray at one meal.** Before one meal each day, give thanks together and say one verse aloud. Let a child choose the verse.
- **Use the four times.** Choose one short verse, such as Deuteronomy 6:5, and say it as you sit at home, as you go out, as you lie down and as you rise.
- **Ask two questions at night.** Before sleep, ask your household, "What can we thank God for today? What shall we ask Him for?" Then pray in a few words. If you live alone, ask yourself, and pray for a friend by name.
- **Sing one line.** Learn a one-line song of prayer, such as "Jesus, remember me when You come in Your kingdom" (Luke 23:42), and sing it together until even the youngest knows it by heart.

### A closing prayer

Lord our God, You are one, and I want to love You with all my heart, my soul and my strength. Thank You for Hannah, Lois and Eunice, Macrina and Monica, Susanna Wesley and the ten Boom family, and for every parent and grandparent who made a home into a place of prayer. Keep Your words close to my heart, so that I may speak of them at home and on the road, when I lie down and when I rise. Bless my household, whoever is in it. Give the lonely a home, give the childless a family in Your church, and win to Yourself those in our homes who do not yet believe. Amen.

- Which of the four times in Deuteronomy 6:7, sitting at home, walking, lying down or getting up, could you most easily fill with a word of prayer?
- Who belongs to your "household" of prayer, and how could you pray with them or for them this week?

## A Rule of Prayer

> "It is a good thing to thank the LORD, to sing and play in praise of Your name, Most High, to tell of Your loyal love at sunrise and of Your faithfulness through the nights..." (Psalm 92:1-2)

In Volume 1 (chapter 14) we met Daniel in Babylon as an old man. He had been carried there as a captive in his youth, and he had never gone home. For more than sixty years he had served foreign kings. Now he was one of the highest officials in the Persian kingdom.

Jealous men found a way to trap him. They persuaded King Darius to sign a law: for thirty days, anyone who prayed to any god or person except the king would be thrown to the lions. They knew Daniel would not obey it.

Look at what Daniel did when he heard the news. He did not call a meeting or write a protest. He did not hide. He climbed the stairs to the upper room of his house, where the windows opened toward Jerusalem, far away across the desert. There he knelt down. "Three times a day he got down on his knees and prayed, giving thanks to his God, just as he had always done" (Daniel 6:10).

The most important words are the last ones: *just as he had always done*. Daniel had no new plan for the day of danger. He had an old habit. Morning, noon and evening, for year after year, in a city full of idols, he had stopped his work, turned his face toward the city of God's promise and given thanks. The habit had been built on ordinary days, when no one was watching and nothing was at stake. When the hard day came, the habit was already there to hold him.

The Bible tells us that God shut the lions' mouths. But the story does not begin in the lions' den. It begins at an open window, three times a day, for a lifetime.

### A rhythm through the centuries

Christians have often called such a pattern a *rule* of prayer. In this sense a rule is not a law that condemns us. It is more like a measuring line, or the frame that holds up a young vine: something simple that helps our prayer grow straight. The cloud of witnesses has much to teach us here.

**Three times a day.** David prayed "at evening, at morning and at noon" (Psalm 55:17). Peter and John went up to the temple "for the afternoon prayer at three o'clock" (Acts 3:1; Volume 3, chapter 3). Around AD 100 the *Didache*, a handbook for new believers, wrote out the Lord's Prayer and then said, in an old English translation, "Thrice in the day thus pray" (Volume 4, chapter 1). Brother Roger of Taizé kept prayer three times a day, alone at first and later with his brothers (Volume 10, chapter 4).

**The whole day turned to God.** Benedict built his monks' day around seven times of prayer, from Psalm 119:164, yet he called his Rule a little rule for beginners (Volume 5, chapter 1). Columba of Iona blessed a pail of new milk, a knife, a barn and an old work horse, and Celtic believers learned to bring ordinary tasks to God in short blessings (Volume 5, chapter 4). The Russian pilgrim learned to carry the Jesus Prayer, "Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me," into every hour (Volume 8, chapter 6).

**Morning and evening.** Luther told his barber to make prayer the first business of the morning and the last of the night, and warned him that if we put prayer off until this job or that one is finished, the hour never comes (Volume 6, chapter 2). Ignatius of Loyola taught a short look back over each day, beginning with thanks (Volume 6, chapter 8). In Korea, Kil Sun-ju opened the dawn to the whole church, and Korean churches around the world still meet to pray before sunrise (Volume 9, chapter 5).

**A plan to share.** Robert Murray M'Cheyne gave his church in Dundee a calendar for reading the Bible, so that they could feed "in the same portion of the green pasture at the same time" (Volume 8, chapter 4). Yet he warned them, "Let the calendar perish rather than this rust eat up your souls." The plan was there to serve their thirst for God, not to replace it.

### Love at sunrise, faithfulness at night

The heading of Psalm 92 calls it a song for the Sabbath day. It begins by saying that praise is "a good thing," and then it gives each end of the day its own song. In the morning we tell of God's "loyal love." In the night we tell of His "faithfulness."

Think about why. In the morning, the day lies ahead of us, and we need to remember that His love goes before us into it. At night, the day lies behind us, and we look back and see that He was faithful in it, even when we were not. Every day begins and ends in God.

Andrew Murray, who wrote much about the morning hour of prayer, warned that the time alone with God is not the goal in itself:

> "The morning watch must not be regarded as an end in itself. It is not sufficient that it gives us a blessed time for prayer and Bible study, and so brings us a certain measure of refreshment and help. It is to serve as a means to an end. And that end is to secure the presence of Christ for the whole day."
> Andrew Murray, *The Inner Chamber*

That is the purpose of any rule of prayer. Morning, midday and evening are like stones laid across a stream. They are not the destination. They help us walk with Christ through the whole day.

### When we fail

Every honest believer who has tried to keep a rule of prayer has failed to keep it. We oversleep. A child is sick. Work runs late. Days, even weeks, slip by. Then we are tempted to give up, or to punish ourselves with guilt.

Francis de Sales, whom we met in Volume 6 (chapter 9), wrote for busy people who kept falling short. In a chapter on being gentle with ourselves, he imagined how we might speak to our own heart after a fall:

> "Poor heart! so soon fallen again into the snare! Well now, rise up again bravely and fall no more. Seek God's Mercy, hope in Him, ask Him to keep you from falling again, and begin to tread the pathway of humility afresh."
> Francis de Sales, *Introduction to the Devout Life*

God's mercies, Jeremiah said, "are new each morning" (Lamentations 3:23). So is the chance to begin again.

### Three key insights

1. **A rule of prayer is a help for weak people, not a prize for strong ones.** Benedict called his rule a little rule for beginners. A simple pattern carries us on the days when feeling and strength fail.
2. **Morning and evening each have their own song: God's love at sunrise and His faithfulness through the night.** Psalm 92 teaches us to greet the day by trusting His love and to close it by thanking Him for His faithfulness.
3. **When we fail to keep our rhythm, we begin again gently, because God's mercy is new every morning.** A missed hour is not the end of a life of prayer. The way back is not anger at ourselves but a quiet return to God.

### For us today

Your rule of prayer will not look like a monk's, or like your pastor's. A mother with a baby, a farmer who rises before dawn, a factory worker on night shifts, a student in a shared room and a prisoner in a cell will each pray at different times. That is good. The witnesses kept many different patterns. What they shared was faithfulness, not sameness.

Begin small. Five minutes kept every day is better than an hour kept once a month. Choose times that already exist in your day: waking, a meal, the walk to work, the moment the lights go out. If you can, share part of your rhythm with others: a family at the table, friends who agree to pray at noon, a church that meets before dawn. And if you have no Bible of your own, you can still keep a rule with the words you know by heart.

### Try this

This week, write down a simple rule of prayer, small enough to keep. Here is one pattern to begin with.

- **Morning: love.** Before you look at your phone or begin your work, thank God for His loyal love, and pray the Lord's Prayer slowly.
- **Midday: a short cry.** At noon or at your midday meal, stop for one breath and pray, "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me," or another short prayer you love.
- **Evening: faithfulness.** Before sleep, look back over the day with God. Thank Him for one thing, tell Him one thing you regret, and give Him tomorrow.
- **Once a week: together.** Choose one time each week to pray with at least one other person, in person or by phone.

### A closing prayer

Most High God, it is a good thing to thank You, to tell of Your loyal love in the morning and of Your faithfulness through the night. Thank You for Daniel at his open window, for the first believers who prayed three times a day, for Benedict and the monks of every land, for Luther, Ignatius, Kil Sun-ju and M'Cheyne, and for all who have shaped their days around You. Help me to make a small rule and to keep it. When I fail, do not let me give up or hide from You. Lift up my heart, forgive me, and let me begin again, so that my whole day belongs to You, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Daniel prayed "just as he had always done." What habits of prayer, if any, already shape your days?
- Which times in your day could become times of prayer: waking, meals, travel, work breaks or bedtime?
- How do you usually respond when you fail to pray as you planned? What would it look like to begin again gently?

## "Come, Lord Jesus"

> "The One who testifies to all this says, 'Yes, I am coming soon!' Amen! Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20)

In the autumn of 1747, in the house of Jonathan Edwards at Northampton, Massachusetts, a young man lay dying. In Volume 7 (chapter 10) we followed David Brainerd through the snowy forests of New York, New Jersey and Pennsylvania, praying for the Native peoples he had come to serve. Now he was twenty-nine years old, and tuberculosis had worn his body out. Edwards's daughter Jerusha, a girl of seventeen, nursed him day after day.

He was waiting for his brother John to come from New Jersey, and John was late. He could no longer work. Yet his mind was clear, and he still prayed for the people he loved. On 2 October he wrote in his diary for the last time:

> "I felt sweetly disposed to commit all to him, even my dearest friends, my dearest flock, my absent brother, and all my concerns for time and eternity. O that his kingdom might come in the world; that they might all love and glorify him for what he is in himself; and that the blessed Redeemer might 'see of the travail of his soul, and be satisfied!' O come, Lord Jesus, come quickly! Amen."
> David Brainerd, *The Life and Diary of David Brainerd*

Edwards, who later published the diary, added a note: here it ends. These were the last words written in it.

Two days later, someone came into the room with a Bible in her hand. Brainerd spoke of that "dear book," and said, "I shall soon see it opened!" On 9 October 1747 he died.

Notice what his last prayer held together. He longed to be with Christ. But he did not forget the world. In the same breath he prayed for his friends, for his little church among the Native people, and for God's kingdom to come over all the earth. Then he ended where the whole Bible ends: "Come, Lord Jesus."

### Golden bowls

This series takes its name from a vision. In Volume 3 (chapter 20) we stood with the apostle John, an old prisoner on the island of Patmos, as heaven's door opened before him. He saw the Lamb who had been slaughtered, and around Him the elders bowing low. "Every one of them had a harp, and they held gold bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of God's holy people" (Revelation 5:8). Later he saw an angel at the golden altar, and "the smoke of the incense rose with the prayers of God's holy people from the angel's hand before God" (Revelation 8:4).

Think of a bowl. A bowl is made to hold things and keep them. It is filled little by little. And when John looked, the bowls were already full.

Whose prayers were in them? Through these eleven volumes we have tried to find out. Abraham's pleading for Sodom. Hannah's silent tears at Shiloh. Daniel's thanks at his open window. The prayers of Jesus on the mountain at night. Polycarp's long prayer before he died. Monica's tears for her son. The whispered prayers of Japan's hidden Christians, the Moravians' watch, the dawn prayers of Korea, the songs of a woman in a shipping container in Eritrea. And millions of prayers no book records: a grandmother's in a village in Ghana, a prisoner's in a cell, a child's at bedtime.

Not one of them was spilled. Many of these believers died without seeing all they asked for. Their prayers wait in the same bowls as ours, and on the day Christ comes, God will answer them in full.

### The church's last prayer

That is why the church has always ended its prayers by looking for Him. Paul closed his first letter to Corinth with words in Aramaic, the language of the first believers: *Marana tha*, "Our Lord, come!" (1 Corinthians 16:22). The *Didache*, which we met in Volume 4 (chapter 1), gave the church a prayer of thanks after the Lord's Supper. It asks God to gather His church "from the four winds" into His kingdom, and then it cries, in an old English translation, "Let grace come, and let this world pass away," and ends, "Maran atha. Amen."

Others waited with the same longing. Old Simeon and the widow Anna waited in the temple for the first coming of Jesus (Volume 1, Conclusion). The martyrs under the altar cried, "How long?" (Revelation 6:10). In Korea, during long years of foreign rule, Kil Sun-ju preached more and more about the return of Christ, the final dawn when every wrong will be put right (Volume 9, chapter 5).

To pray "Come, Lord Jesus" is not to run away from the world. Jesus said that the gospel "will be announced throughout the world, so that every nation hears the witness, and only then will the end come" (Matthew 24:14). Peter wrote that the Lord is patient, because He "wants all people to turn back in repentance" (2 Peter 3:9). So the prayer for His coming always carries a second prayer inside it, as it did for Brainerd: "O that his kingdom might come in the world." Those who long for the King pray and work for His people while they wait.

Waiting is not easy. Charles Spurgeon knew how weary believers grow, and how the promise of Christ's coming gives strength to keep going:

> "Are we tried? Then the nearness of our joy whispers patience. Are we growing weary because we do not see the harvest of our seed-sowing? Again this glorious truth cries to us, 'Be patient.'"
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith*

### Until He comes

This series began with the "cloud of witnesses all around us" (Hebrews 12:1). We have met them one by one: patriarchs and prophets, apostles and martyrs, monks and mothers, reformers and revival preachers, missionaries, prisoners and ordinary believers from every continent. They were not perfect people. Many failed, and some failed badly. But they prayed, and their prayers rose before God like incense.

In the first chapter of this book we pictured the praying church as one prayer meeting, two thousand years long. Now we can see how it ends. The witnesses have finished their race. But there is a place in that meeting with your name on it. No one else can pray your prayers: for your family, your street, your church, your nation. They will go into the same golden bowls as theirs.

Each generation kept its place by teaching the next. Hannah gave Samuel to the LORD, Lois taught Eunice, and Paul told Timothy to pass on what he had heard "to trustworthy people who will be able to teach others as well" (2 Timothy 2:2). Someone taught you to pray. Now it is your turn to teach someone else, until the day the door opens and the One we have prayed to comes. Then the prayer meeting will become a feast, and the bowls of prayer will give way to songs of praise.

### Three key insights

1. **The hope of Christ's coming gives us patience to keep praying when we do not yet see the harvest.** The bowls in heaven fill slowly, and the witnesses before us did not receive all they asked for. But the Lord is near, and His nearness whispers patience to weary believers.
2. **Longing for Christ to come does not take us out of the world, but sends us to pray and work for it.** Brainerd's last prayer joined "Come, Lord Jesus" with a prayer for God's kingdom to come over all the earth. Those who love His coming love the people He came to save.
3. **Until the Lord comes, each generation takes its place in the prayer meeting and teaches the next one to pray.** The cloud of witnesses has run its race. Now it is our turn to pray, in our own languages and our own places, and to hand on what we have learned.

### For us today

Believers pray "Come, Lord Jesus" in very different places. For some, life is comfortable, and the prayer can feel distant. For others it is the cry of every day: a family in a refugee camp, a pastor in prison, a mother who has buried a child, a church whose building has been burned. Both need this prayer. It wakes the comfortable to long for more than this world can give. And it promises the suffering that their tears are seen and that their story does not end in the dark.

It is also a prayer the whole church can pray together. Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant, house church and cathedral, all say "Amen" to the last words of the Bible. Whatever divides us, we wait for the same Lord.

### Try this

This week, take your place in the great prayer meeting.

- **End each day with the last prayer.** Before you sleep, say Revelation 22:20, and then simply, "Come, Lord Jesus."
- **Pray with the witnesses.** Choose one person from this series each day, thank God for their life, and ask Him to give you their faith.
- **Pray for the waiting church.** Name one country where believers suffer for Christ, and pray for them by name if you can, until you hear of the next.
- **Pass on the torch.** Teach one younger believer, a child, a friend or a new Christian, a simple way to pray, as others taught you (2 Timothy 2:2).

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You are coming soon. Thank You for the great cloud of witnesses who prayed before me: for Abraham and Hannah and Daniel, for Your apostles and martyrs, for the monks and mothers, reformers and missionaries, and for every hidden believer whose prayers fill the golden bowls before Your throne. Thank You that You always live to pray for us. Give me a seat in Your prayer meeting, and keep me faithful in it. Let Your kingdom come in my home, my church and my land, and among every nation. Comfort Your suffering people while they wait. And on the day You come, gather us all, from every tribe and tongue, to see Your face. Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!

- Where is your place in the church's great prayer meeting? Who will you pray for, and with, in the years ahead?

## Conclusion: The Torch Is Yours

> "The smoke of the incense rose with the prayers of God's holy people from the angel's hand before God." (Revelation 8:4)

We began this book in the throne room of heaven, where the elders hold bowls of gold full of incense, "the prayers of God's holy people." In the eighth chapter of Revelation, John sees those prayers again. An angel stands at the golden altar before the throne, the incense is added to the prayers of all God's people, and the smoke rises before God.

Then something happens. The angel fills the censer with fire from the altar and throws it down to the earth, and there are thunder, voices and lightning (Revelation 8:5). The prayers that went up come back down as God's action in the world. Not one of them was wasted.

That is the hope of this whole series. For eleven volumes we have watched the prayers of God's people rise: from Abraham under the stars to believers praying tonight in a prison cell or a crowded city. We have seen many of them answered, some at once and some long after the one who prayed had died. And we have learned that the rest are kept, in bowls of gold, before the throne of God.

### What the witnesses taught us

Here, in a few lines, is some of what the whole cloud of witnesses taught us about our own life of prayer:

- Whenever we pray, we join a meeting that began long before us and has never closed its doors (chapter 1).
- Every true prayer begins with faith that God is real and rewards those who search for Him (chapter 2).
- God's word gives us not only something to read but words to speak back to Him (chapter 3).
- In every century the church has fasted, and fasting has always served prayer, never replaced it (chapter 4).
- Praying alone teaches us to hear God, and praying together teaches us to carry one another (chapter 5).
- Persistence keeps coming back to God Himself, while nagging only keeps coming back to the thing we want (chapter 6).
- Even the Son of God prayed a prayer that the Father did not grant, and He was still heard (chapter 7).
- Every piece of news from a distant land can become a call to prayer (chapter 8).
- Revival cannot be manufactured, but God has always loved to send it to people who pray (chapter 9).
- Prayer at home is woven into ordinary moments: at the table, on the road, at bedtime and on waking (chapter 10).
- A rule of prayer is a help for weak people, not a prize for strong ones (chapter 11).
- Until the Lord comes, each generation takes its place in the prayer meeting and teaches the next one to pray (chapter 12).

You will find every key insight from all eleven volumes gathered by theme in Appendix A, every Scripture in the series in Bible order in Appendix B, every witness in the order they lived in Appendix C, simple ways to pray in Appendix D, and the books on Ochorus where you can meet the witnesses for yourself in Appendix E.

### What they all knew

If you could gather the whole cloud of witnesses into one room and ask them what they had learned, they would not agree on everything. They lived in different centuries, spoke different languages and belonged to different churches. But on a few things, they would speak with one voice.

They would tell you that God is real, and that He hears. They would tell you that He is a Father, and that the way to Him is open through Jesus. They would tell you that prayer is not first a technique but a friendship, and that it grows the way any friendship grows: by spending time together, day after day. They would tell you to pray with the Scriptures open, to pray alone and with others, to keep going when the answer is slow, and to trust God when it does not come. And they would tell you that they were ordinary people. Abraham bargained, Hannah wept, Elijah asked God to let him die, Peter fell asleep, and Bunyan prayed in a jail while his family struggled without him. God heard them anyway.

### The torch is yours

At the end of Hebrews 11, after its long roll of the faithful, the writer says that "all these died still holding to faith. They did not receive what was promised, but they saw it from far off and welcomed it" (Hebrews 11:13). Then he turns to us: "since we too have so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us," let us run (Hebrews 12:1).

Every volume of this series has ended by passing the torch to the next. This is the last volume, and there is no next one to pass it to. The torch is yours.

You will not carry it alone. The witnesses go before you. Your brothers and sisters around the world carry it beside you. And Jesus Himself, "the pioneer who leads the way in faith and brings it to perfection" (Hebrews 12:2), holds it with you, and prays for you still.

So take your seat in the meeting. Open the Scriptures and pray them back to God. Keep your hour. Fast, when He calls you to it. Pray in secret, and pray with others. Wait, and keep waiting. Trust Him in the silence. Pray for the nations, for your city and for your home. Ask Him to revive His church, and to begin with you. And every day, until He comes, join the oldest prayer of the church: "Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20).

One day the meeting will end. The crowd that no one could number, "from every nation and from all tribes and peoples and languages" (Revelation 7:9), will stand together before the throne. Abraham will be there, and Hannah, and the apostles, the martyrs and the monks, the reformers and the missionaries, the praying grandmothers and the prisoners who sang. And if you have trusted in Jesus, you will be there too. On that day, prayer will become praise, and we will see the One we have prayed to face to face.

Until then, there is still room in the bowls for your prayers.

### A closing prayer

Our Father in heaven, thank You for the great cloud of witnesses: for the patriarchs and prophets, for Jesus our Lord, who prayed for us and prays for us still, for the apostles and martyrs, the monks and mystics, the reformers and the Puritans, the missionaries and the revivalists, and for Your praying church in every nation today. Thank You that not one of their prayers was lost. Teach me to pray as they prayed. Give me a place in the meeting, and keep me faithful in it all my days. Let my prayers rise before You like incense, with all the prayers of Your holy people. And bring us all, from every tribe and tongue, into Your presence, where faith will become sight and prayer will become praise.

"Now to the one who is able to guard you from stumbling, and to bring you into the presence of His glory without blemish and rejoicing greatly, to God alone, our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory, majesty, might, and authority, before time began, now, and for all the ages to come. Amen." (Jude 1:24-25)

Come, Lord Jesus. Amen.
