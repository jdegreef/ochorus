## Introduction: Where We Are in the Story

> "Everything that was written in earlier times was written to teach us. It was written so that we might have hope, as we endure and as the Scriptures encourage us." (Romans 15:4)

When Jesus was twelve years old, His family made the long walk from Nazareth to Jerusalem for the feast of Passover, as they did every year (Luke 2:41-42). Picture the road: whole villages walking together, families singing as they climbed toward the city. Many of the songs they sang on that road are in our Bibles. Psalm 122 begins, "I was full of joy when they said to me, 'Come, let us set out for the house of the LORD!'" (Psalm 122:1).

Jesus grew up inside prayers like that. Every Sabbath in the synagogue at Nazareth He heard the Scriptures read aloud. Luke tells us that, as a grown man, "On the Sabbath He went to the synagogue, as He always did, and stood up to read the Scriptures" (Luke 4:16). Morning and evening, faithful Jewish families said the *Shema*: "Hear, Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). He learned the Psalms by heart. When He prayed on the cross, the words that came to His lips were the words of the Psalms: "My God, My God, why have You abandoned Me?" (Psalm 22:1) and "I put my spirit into Your hand for safekeeping" (Psalm 31:5).

This book is about the prayers Jesus learned.

### The series this book begins

This is the first volume of a series called *A Cloud of Praying Witnesses*. It follows the people of God at prayer through the whole story of the church, from the earliest pages of the Bible to believers praying around the world today. The name comes from the letter to the Hebrews, which pictures all the faithful men and women who went before us as a great crowd of witnesses gathered around us:

> "So then, since we too have so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us, let us strip off every burden, and the sin that wraps itself around us so readily, and let us run the race laid out ahead of us, and run it with endurance." (Hebrews 12:1)

Hebrews says that just after it has told the story of the Old Testament's heroes of faith: Abraham, Jacob, Moses, Samuel, David and the prophets. They are the first witnesses in the cloud, and they are the people we meet in this book.

In the last book of the Bible, John sees the prayers of God's people held before His throne in golden bowls of incense. David prayed long before, "Let my prayer stand before You like incense" (Psalm 141:2). Not one sincere prayer is lost. When you pray, you join a prayer meeting that began thousands of years ago.

### The world of these prayers

The prayers in this book span more than fifteen hundred years. They begin with Abraham, a herdsman who left his home in Mesopotamia and lived in tents in the land of Canaan. They pass through Moses, who led Israel out of slavery in Egypt, and the judges, the kings and the prophets of Israel and Judah. They end with God's people scattered in exile in Babylon and Persia, and a small group rebuilding the walls of Jerusalem, waiting for God to keep His promises.

These were not perfect people. Jacob was a cheat. Moses lost his temper. David committed adultery and murder. Jonah ran away from God. Elijah wanted to die. Their prayers are not the prayers of the strong, but of the weak who had learned that God hears. That is good news for us.

### What we will see

This volume has seventeen chapters, in four movements:

- **The fathers of faith** (chapters 1 and 2). Abraham pleads with God for a city, and Jacob wrestles through the night and will not let go.
- **Moses** (chapters 3 and 4). He stands in the gap for a sinful people, and asks to see God's glory.
- **The judges and the kings** (chapters 5 to 9). Hannah pours out her soul, Samuel refuses to stop praying for his people, David sings his longing for God and his repentance, and Solomon dedicates the temple as a house of prayer.
- **The prophets, the exile and the return** (chapters 10 to 17). Elijah prays down fire and rain, kings in danger turn to God, Jonah prays from the belly of a fish, Daniel kneels at his open window, Nehemiah prays in a single breath before a king, the psalms teach us to pray honestly in pain, and Habakkuk learns to rejoice when the fields are bare.

At the end of the volume, two old people are still praying in the temple courts, waiting for God to come. One day a young couple carries a baby through the gate. That is where this volume ends, and where Volume 2, *Jesus, Teacher of Prayer*, takes up the story.

### How each chapter works

Every chapter in this series has the same shape, so you will soon know your way around.

- **A key verse** opens the chapter. It is the Scripture the chapter rests on.
- **A story** sets the scene.
- **The teaching** looks at what the passage shows us about prayer.
- **Voices from the past** quote men and women of prayer from church history, word for word from their own books in the Ochorus library.
- **Three key insights** sum up what the chapter teaches.
- **For us today** brings the lesson into ordinary life, wherever in the world you live.
- **Try this** gives one simple practice for the coming week.
- **A closing prayer** and **Questions for reflection** help you respond, alone or with others.
- **Read more on Ochorus** points you to classic books where you can go deeper.

At the end of the book you will find two appendices. The first gathers all the key insights by theme. The second lists every Scripture quoted in the book, in Bible order, with the chapters where it appears.

### How to read this book

You can read it straight through, but it is best taken slowly. One chapter a day will carry you through the book in seventeen days. If you are reading with a small group, one or two chapters a week works well, and the questions at the end of each chapter will get your conversation started.

Read with your Bible open. Every chapter tells a story from the Old Testament, and the story is always better in the Bible than in any retelling. Paul wrote that "everything that was written in earlier times was written to teach us" (Romans 15:4). These ancient prayers were written down so that we would learn to pray too. As you read, try praying their words yourself. Jesus did.

## The Friend Who Pleaded for a City

> "It is unthinkable that You would do such a thing, putting the innocent to death with the guilty and treating them alike. Surely not! Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?" (Genesis 18:25)

It was the hottest part of the day. Abraham, an old man by now, was sitting in the doorway of his tent by the great trees of Mamre, in the hill country of Canaan. He looked up and saw three men standing a little way off.

In that part of the world, then and now, a guest is treated with great honor. Abraham ran to meet them, bowed to the ground, and begged them to stay. Sarah baked bread, a servant prepared a young calf, and before the meal was over the visitors had repeated God's promise: within a year, Sarah would have a son.

Then the men got up and looked down toward Sodom, a city in the valley below. Abraham walked with them for a while, as a good host does. And the LORD spoke:

> "Then the LORD said, 'Should I keep from Abraham what I am about to do?'" (Genesis 18:17)

The LORD told Abraham that a great cry had gone up against Sodom and Gomorrah. Their sin was very heavy. He was going down to see it for Himself. Two of the visitors went on toward the city. But Abraham "stayed standing in the presence of the LORD" (Genesis 18:22).

Abraham's nephew Lot lived in Sodom with his family. Years before, Lot had chosen the rich, green valley, even though "the people of Sodom, however, were evil and grievous sinners against the LORD" (Genesis 13:13).

So Abraham stepped forward and began to speak. "Will You really destroy the innocent along with the guilty? Suppose there are fifty innocent people in the city" (Genesis 18:23-24). Then came the words at the top of this chapter: "Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?"

The LORD answered: for fifty, He would spare the whole city.

Abraham did not stop. "I have dared to speak to the Lord, I who am only dust and ashes" (Genesis 18:27). What about forty-five? Then forty. Then thirty. Then twenty. Each time he asked, he grew more humble, and each time the LORD said yes. At last: "Do not be angry, Lord, and I will speak only this one time more. Suppose there are ten" (Genesis 18:32). And the LORD said, "Because of the ten I will not destroy it."

Then the LORD went on His way, "and Abraham went back to his own place" (Genesis 18:33).

### Who he was

Abraham lived about four thousand years ago, perhaps around 2000 BC, though no one can be exact. God called him from the city of Ur, which most think lay in what is now southern Iraq, to a land he had never seen. He lived in tents, moving with his flocks, and wherever he stopped he built an altar and called on the name of the LORD.

God had promised him that "all the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (Genesis 12:3). From the very beginning, God's plan was for the nations. So it is fitting that the first long prayer in the Bible is a man praying for a city that was not his own.

God Himself calls Abraham "My friend" (Isaiah 41:8), and James repeats it: "he was called God's friend" (James 2:23). This chapter shows what that friendship looked like. God does not hide His plans from His friend. And His friend does not keep quiet. He speaks up for others.

### How he prayed

Look closely at how Abraham prayed. He did not begin with his nephew. He began with God's character. "Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?" He was holding God to who He already is.

Charles Spurgeon, the great London preacher, saw that Abraham took hold of God's justice first, and then, as the numbers dropped, held on to God's mercy:

> "It was a powerful argument by which the patriarch grasped the Lord's left hand, and arrested it just when the thunderbolt was about to fall."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Mighty Power in Prayer*

Abraham was bold and humble at the same time. He asked six times in all. Yet he called himself "only dust and ashes," and twice he said, "Do not be angry, Lord." He knew who he was, and he knew who God was. Boldness without humility is pride. Humility without boldness is silence. Abraham had both.

Why did he stop at ten? The Bible does not say. E. M. Bounds, the American Methodist pastor who wrote so much about prayer, wondered whether God might have spared the city for fewer, if Abraham had asked once more. Andrew Murray, the South African pastor, thought Abraham stopped because he had come to know God's heart:

> "He does not cease until he has learnt to know God's condescension in each time consenting to his petition, until he has learnt how far he can go, has entered into God's mind, and now rests in God's will. And for his sake Lot was saved."
> Andrew Murray, *The Ministry of Intercession*

In the end, not even ten innocent people were found in Sodom, and the cities of the plain were destroyed. But the prayer was not wasted. The Bible tells us plainly: "when God destroyed the cities of the plain, He remembered Abraham and brought Lot out of the disaster" (Genesis 19:29). Abraham prayed, and God remembered.

The next morning Abraham "went back to the place where he had stood in the presence of the LORD" (Genesis 19:27). He looked down and saw the smoke rising. Whatever he feared, he went back to the place of prayer.

### Where Jesus picks up the prayer

Many centuries later Jesus too came near a city that was heading for ruin. "When He came nearer and saw the city, He wept over it" (Luke 19:41). Like Abraham, He did not look down on Jerusalem from a distance and pass judgment. He grieved for it.

And Jesus answered Abraham's great question in a way Abraham could never have imagined. "Will You really destroy the innocent along with the guilty?" On the cross, the one truly innocent Man stood with the guilty, and died in their place, so that the guilty could be spared. As they nailed Him there, He prayed for them: "Father, forgive them. They do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34). The Judge of all the earth did what was right, and He showed mercy too.

And Jesus spoke to His disciples as God once spoke about Abraham: "I have called you friends instead, because I have made known to you everything I heard from My Father" (John 15:15). In Christ, every believer is invited into the same friendship, and into the same work of praying for others.

### Three key insights

1. **Friends of God pray for others, not only for themselves.** Abraham's first long prayer in the Bible was for a wicked city and a nephew who had made poor choices. God shares His heart with His friends so that they will care about what He cares about.
2. **We can pray boldly because we pray to a God who is just and good.** Abraham argued from God's character, not his own goodness. The more we know who God is, the more freely we can ask.
3. **Humble persistence is welcome in God's presence.** Abraham asked six times, and each time God said yes. God is not annoyed by a humble heart that keeps asking.

### For us today

Most of us live near some "Sodom": a city full of violence, a neighborhood ruled by gangs, a corrupt government, or a family member whose life is falling apart. Our first feeling may be anger, or fear, or the wish to stay far away. Abraham stood before the LORD and pleaded for the place.

You do not need to be a pastor or a leader to do this. Abraham was a herdsman living in a tent. In a crowded city like Lagos, Manila or São Paulo, you can pray for your street as you walk down it. In a village, you can pray for the town where your children have gone to find work. In the persecuted church, many believers pray for the very officials and neighbors who make life hard for them.

Abraham did not see the answer he hoped for. The city fell. But God remembered him and rescued Lot. No prayer offered to the Judge of all the earth is ever forgotten.

### Try this

This week, choose one place to stand before the LORD for, as Abraham did.

- **Name your city.** Write down the name of one town, village or neighborhood. Pray for it by name every day this week.
- **Begin with who God is.** Before you ask anything, say what you know of God's character: "You are just. You are merciful. You love this place."
- **Ask, then ask again.** Bring one specific request for that place each day, humbly and boldly, as Abraham did.
- **Pray for one "Lot."** Think of one person who has made poor choices and may not be praying for themselves. Pray for them by name.

### A closing prayer

LORD, Judge of all the earth, You always do what is right, and Your mercy is greater than we know. Thank You that You call people like us Your friends. Give me Abraham's heart, that does not walk away from a city in trouble but pleads for it. Teach me to be bold because You are good, and humble because I am only dust. Remember my city and the people in it who do not pray for themselves, as You remembered Abraham and rescued Lot. In the name of Jesus, who wept over a city and died for the guilty, I pray. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Abraham argued from God's character, not from his own goodness. What do you know about God's character that gives you courage to pray?
- Who is the "Lot" in your life, someone who needs prayer but may not be praying for themselves?
- Abraham went back to the place of prayer the next morning, even before he knew the answer. What helps you keep praying when you cannot see what God is doing?

## "I Will Not Let You Go"

> "'Let me go,' said the man, 'for the dawn is coming up.' Jacob said, 'I will not let you go unless you bless me.'" (Genesis 32:26)

Jacob had been running for twenty years.

As a young man he had cheated his twin brother Esau twice: first out of his birthright, then out of their aging father's blessing. Esau was so angry that he planned to kill him, so Jacob fled far away to his uncle Laban in Haran. There he married, raised a large family and grew rich in flocks.

Then God spoke: "Return to your fathers' land and to your own people. I will be with you" (Genesis 31:3). So Jacob set out for home, with his wives, his children, his servants and his animals. As he came near, he sent messengers ahead to Esau, hoping for peace. They came back with frightening news: "He is already coming out to meet you, with 400 men at his side" (Genesis 32:6).

Four hundred men. "Jacob was badly frightened and anxious" (Genesis 32:7). He divided his people into two camps, so that if Esau attacked one, the other might escape. Then he prayed. It is one of the most honest prayers in the Bible:

> "I am far too small for all the steadfast love and faithfulness You have shown Your servant. I had only a staff in my hand when I crossed this Jordan, and now I have become two camps. Rescue me from my brother, from Esau's hand." (Genesis 32:10-11)

He sent large gifts of animals ahead to his brother. That night he took his family across the ford of the Jabbok, a stream east of the Jordan River, and sent over everything he owned.

"Jacob stayed behind by himself. And a man wrestled with him there until dawn began to break" (Genesis 32:24).

We are not told how it began, only that it lasted all night. Jacob fought with all his strength. When the man saw that he could not win, he touched Jacob's hip, and it was wrenched out of joint. Now Jacob could not fight. He could only hold on.

"Let me go," said the man, "for the dawn is coming up."

And Jacob, in pain and unable to stand properly, clung to him and said, "I will not let you go unless you bless me."

The man asked his name. "Jacob," he said. That name was linked with grabbing and cheating, and Esau had once said bitterly, "No wonder he is called Jacob!" (Genesis 27:36). Now, saying his name was a kind of confession. And the man answered:

> "You will be called Jacob no longer. Your name is Israel, for you have contended with God and with people and have come through victorious." (Genesis 32:28)

There he blessed Jacob. "The sun was rising on him as he went past Penuel, and he went limping on his injured hip" (Genesis 32:31).

### Who he was

Jacob was the grandson of Abraham and the son of Isaac. He probably lived some time after 1900 BC, though no one can give exact dates. His new name, Israel, became the name of a whole nation. The twelve tribes of Israel came from his twelve sons.

He was not a natural saint. He was clever, ambitious and often dishonest. That is part of why his story gives hope.

Who was the "man" at the Jabbok? Genesis simply calls him a man. The prophet Hosea calls him an angel and says of Jacob, "he wept and begged for his favor" (Hosea 12:4). Jacob himself said, "I have seen God face to face" (Genesis 32:30), and he named the place Peniel, which means "face of God." Many Christians have believed this was the Son of God, appearing before He was born at Bethlehem. The Bible does not say plainly, and we do not need to be certain. What is clear is that Jacob met God that night.

### How he prayed

Jacob's night has two parts, and both teach us about prayer.

First, the prayer before the wrestling. Jacob did not pretend to be brave or good. He admitted his fear and his unworthiness. But he also reminded God of what God had said. Twice in that short prayer he quoted God's own words back to Him: "it was You who said to me," and "Yet You Yourself said" (Genesis 32:9-12). Charles Spurgeon loved this:

> "When Jacob was on the other side of the brook Jabbok, and his brother Esau was coming with armed men, he pleaded with God not to suffer Esau to destroy the mother and the children, and as a master reason he pleaded, 'And thou saidst, surely I will do thee good.' Oh the force of that plea! He was holding God to his word: 'Thou saidst.'"
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Mighty Power in Prayer*

Second, the wrestling itself. Notice the turning point. While Jacob was strong, he fought. When he was wounded, he clung. His victory did not come from his strength but from his weakness. The prayer that won the blessing was not a clever plan. It was a broken man holding on and saying, "I will not let you go."

Andrew Murray saw how much God valued that kind of holding on:

> "And that boldness that said, 'I will not,' and forced from the reluctant angel the blessing, was so pleasing in God's sight, that a new name was there given to him: 'Israel, he who striveth with God, for thou hast striven with God and with men, and hast prevailed.'"
> Andrew Murray, *The Ministry of Intercession*

And the next morning? Jacob limped toward his brother, bowing to the ground seven times. "But Esau ran to meet him and hugged him; he held him close and kissed him, and they wept together" (Genesis 33:4). God had been working on Esau's heart that night too.

### Where Jesus picks up the prayer

Jesus also knew a night of struggle in prayer. In the garden of Gethsemane, "in His anguish He prayed all the more intensely, and His sweat became like thick drops of blood dripping down onto the ground" (Luke 22:44). The letter to the Hebrews says that He "offered up prayers and pleas, crying aloud and weeping," and that "He was heard" (Hebrews 5:7). Those words sound very much like Hosea's description of Jacob, who "wept and begged."

But there is a difference. Jacob held on to God to get a blessing for himself. Jesus held on to His Father's will, even when it meant the cross: "Yet let Your will be done, not mine" (Luke 22:42). Because He did, the blessing Jacob longed for is now open to everyone who comes to God through Him. And in Volume 2 we will hear Jesus teach His followers to pray with Jacob's kind of persistence: to ask, seek and knock, and never give up.

### Three key insights

1. **God meets us in our fear, not after we have fixed ourselves.** Jacob came to that night as a frightened man with a guilty past. God did not wait for him to become good. He met him in the dark, just as he was.
2. **We may hold God to His own promises.** Jacob prayed, "You Yourself said." The strongest prayers rest on what God has already spoken. Learning God's promises gives us words to pray with.
3. **Sometimes we prevail in prayer through weakness, not strength.** Jacob's hip was wounded, and he could only cling. Often God brings us to the end of our own strength so that we will hold on to Him alone.

### For us today

Many of us carry an "Esau" in our hearts: a broken relationship, an old wrong we did, a fear of meeting someone again. In many cultures, family conflict runs deep and can last for generations. Jacob's story says: bring it to God before you face it. Tell Him the truth. Remind Him of His promises. Hold on to Him through the night.

Some believers, in parts of the Middle East, Asia and Africa, face real threats, like Jacob's four hundred men. For them, Jacob's honest prayer is a gift: "I fear that he will come and strike us all down" (Genesis 32:11). God is not offended by our fear. He wants us to bring it to Him.

And notice Jacob's limp. He walked away blessed, but he walked away wounded. Some of God's deepest work in us leaves a mark. A believer who has wrestled with God through sickness, loss or prison often walks differently afterward, more slowly perhaps, but closer to God.

### Try this

This week, take one night, or one long evening, to hold on to God for something that matters deeply to you.

- **Tell the truth.** Begin as Jacob did: admit your fear, and admit any wrong you have done. Nothing is hidden from God anyway.
- **Find a promise.** Look for one promise of God in Scripture that speaks to your need. Write it down. Pray it back to Him: "Lord, You Yourself said..."
- **Hold on.** Stay longer than usual. When your words run out, simply say, "I will not let You go unless You bless me."
- **Take one step.** If your "Esau" is a person, ask God for courage to take one step toward peace with them this week.

### A closing prayer

Lord God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, I am far too small for all the love and faithfulness You have shown me. You know my past and You know my fears. I bring them to You now. Meet me as You met Jacob in the dark. When I am weak, teach me to cling to You. When I am afraid, teach me to remember what You have said. Change my heart, and give me a new name, as You did for Jacob. I will not let You go unless You bless me. In the name of Jesus, who prayed through the night for me, Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Jacob's prayer was honest about his fear and his unworthiness. What fear or failure do you find hardest to bring to God?
- Which promise of God would you most like to "hold God to" in your life right now?
- Jacob walked away blessed but limping. Have you known a time when God blessed you through a painful experience?

## Standing in the Gap

> "But Moses sought the favor of the LORD his God. 'LORD,' he said, 'why should Your anger blaze against Your own people? You brought them out of the land of Egypt by great power and a strong hand.'" (Exodus 32:11)

Only a few weeks earlier, the whole people of Israel had stood at the foot of Mount Sinai and heard God speak. The mountain had shaken. Fire and smoke had covered it. They had promised, with one voice, to do everything the LORD said.

Then Moses went up the mountain to receive God's law, and he did not come back. Days passed. Then weeks. He was there forty days and forty nights.

The people grew restless. They crowded around his brother Aaron and said, "Get up and make us gods to lead the way for us. This Moses, the man who brought us up from the land of Egypt, we have no idea what has become of him" (Exodus 32:1). Aaron collected their gold earrings and shaped a calf of cast metal, perhaps like the bull idols they had seen in Egypt. The people bowed down to it and shouted, "Israel, these are your gods, who brought you up from the land of Egypt!" (Exodus 32:4). The next day they held a feast, with eating, drinking and wild celebration.

On the mountain, God told Moses what was happening. Then He said something astonishing:

> "So now let Me alone, so that My wrath can burn hot against them and I can put an end to them. Then I will make you into a great nation." (Exodus 32:10)

Moses could become a new Abraham, and these people had caused him nothing but trouble since they left Egypt.

Moses said no. He did not let God alone. Instead, he "sought the favor of the LORD his God," and he prayed for the very people who had forgotten him:

> "Turn from Your fierce anger, and relent from the harm You mean to bring on Your people. Remember Abraham, Isaac and Israel, who served You." (Exodus 32:12-13)

And the Bible says, "the LORD relented from the harm He had said He would do to His people" (Exodus 32:14).

Moses went down the mountain carrying the two stone tablets. When he saw the calf and the dancing, he threw the tablets down and smashed them, and he burned the idol and ground it to powder. The sin was real, and he did not pretend otherwise.

But the next day, he went back up to pray again. "What you have done is a terrible sin," he told the people. "But I will go up again to the LORD. It may be that I can make atonement for your sin" (Exodus 32:30). And on the mountain he prayed one of the most selfless prayers in the whole Bible:

> "But now, if only You would forgive their sin! But if not, then blot me out of Your book that You have written." (Exodus 32:32)

### Who he was

Moses was born in Egypt to Hebrew slaves, was raised in Pharaoh's palace, and spent forty years as a shepherd in the desert before God called him from a burning bush. He led Israel out of slavery and through the wilderness for forty more years. The dates are debated: some place the Exodus in the 1400s BC, others in the 1200s BC.

Moses had already prayed many times for Israel, and even for Pharaoh, who begged him again and again to pray for an end to the plagues. But this was the hour when his prayer mattered most. Centuries later a psalm looked back on that day:

> "So He said He would wipe them out; but Moses, His chosen one, stood in the gap before Him to turn His anger away so that He would not destroy them." (Psalm 106:23)

"Stood in the gap." In the ancient world, a city was protected by its walls. If an enemy broke a hole in the wall, someone had to stand in that broken place and defend it with his own body. Moses stood in the broken place between a holy God and a sinful people.

### How he prayed

Look at the reasons Moses gave. He made no excuses for the people. Instead he gave God three reasons that came from God Himself.

First, **they are Your people**. God had called them "your people" when He spoke to Moses (Exodus 32:7). Moses gave them back: "Your own people. You brought them out."

Second, **the nations are watching**. "Why should the Egyptians say, 'He brought them out to do them harm...'?" (Exodus 32:12). Moses cared about what the nations would think of God.

Third, **You made a promise**. "Remember Abraham, Isaac and Israel." Like Jacob before him, Moses held God to His word.

E. M. Bounds was amazed at the power of such a prayer:

> "The presence of such an influence over God fills us with astonishment, awe and fear. How lofty, bold and devoted must be such a pleader!"
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

How can a man's prayer change what God does? The Bible does not answer every question here. But God told Moses His plan, and then said, "Let Me alone," almost as if He was waiting to see whether Moses would hold on. The prophet Ezekiel later heard God say, "I looked for someone among them who would repair the wall and stand before Me in the gap on behalf of the land, so that I would not destroy it, but I found no one" (Ezekiel 22:30). God is looking for people who will stand in the gap. He has chosen to work through their prayers.

And Moses did not pray only once. Years later, he told the next generation that he had lain face down before the LORD "for forty days and forty nights, without eating food or drinking water, because of all the sin you had committed" (Deuteronomy 9:18). Andrew Murray summed up the heart of the man:

> "A man, too, absolutely given up to his people, ready to sacrifice himself, if they may be saved."
> Andrew Murray, *The Inner Chamber*

### Where Jesus picks up the prayer

God did not accept Moses' offer. "It is the one who has sinned against Me whom I will blot out of My book," He said (Exodus 32:33). Moses could not take the people's place. He was a sinner too.

But what Moses offered, Jesus did. He stood in the gap for us, not only in prayer but with His own life. On the cross He prayed for the people who were killing Him: "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). And He has not stopped. Today, the Bible says, Christ "is at the right hand of God, where He pleads on our behalf" (Romans 8:34). Andrew Murray saw Moses as a picture of what Christ does for us:

> "He proves that the man who truly lives near to God, and with whom God speaks face to face, becomes partaker of that same power of intercession which there is in Him who is at God's right hand and ever lives to pray."
> Andrew Murray, *The Ministry of Intercession*

### Three key insights

1. **An intercessor stands between God and people who cannot stand for themselves.** Moses did not pray for himself. He stood in the broken place for a nation that had forgotten him, and God spared them because he did.
2. **The strongest prayers appeal to God's own character, name and promises.** Moses reminded God that they were His people, that the nations were watching what He would do, and that He had made a promise.
3. **True intercession costs the one who prays.** Moses turned down a great future for himself and even offered his own life. What he offered, Jesus gave, and He still pleads for us.

### For us today

Every family, church and nation has broken places in its wall: sin, division, corruption, idolatry old or new. It is easy to become a critic, to stand at a distance and say, "They deserve whatever comes." Moses was angry at the sin, and he dealt with it. But then he went back up the mountain to pray.

Intercession is a work that every believer can do, rich or poor, young or old, free or in prison. You do not need a title. Some of the strongest intercessors in the world today are unknown grandmothers in village churches in Africa, house church members in Asia, and widows in Latin America who pray every morning for their nation. God sees them.

Intercession often means praying for people who have let us down. Moses prayed for the very people who had complained against him and forgotten him. That is hard. It is also very close to the heart of Jesus.

### Try this

This week, choose one "broken place in the wall" and stand in it before God.

- **Choose a gap.** Name one group: your church, your town, your nation, or a people far away who do not know Christ.
- **Use Moses' three reasons.** Pray: "They are Yours. The nations are watching. You have made promises." Find one promise of God to pray for them.
- **Pray for someone who has let you down.** Like Moses, bring before God one person who has disappointed or hurt you, and ask God to bless and forgive them.
- **Give something up.** If you are able, skip one meal this week and use that time to pray for your chosen gap.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You stood in the gap for me. You took the place I deserved, and even now You plead for me at the Father's right hand. Thank You. Make me an intercessor like Moses. When I see sin around me, keep me from standing at a distance, and give me a heart for my family, my church and my nation. Teach me to pray from Your character, Your name and Your promises. And let me find my joy, as Moses did, in seeing Your mercy win. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Moses turned down the offer to become a great nation himself. Why do you think he refused?
- What is one "broken place in the wall" in your family, church or nation that you could stand in through prayer?
- Is there someone who has let you down whom God might be asking you to pray for?

## "Let Me See Your Glory"

> "Then Moses said, 'Please, let me see Your glory.'" (Exodus 33:18)

The golden calf had been ground to dust, and the nation had been spared. But something was still terribly wrong.

God told Moses to lead the people on to the promised land, "a land flowing with milk and honey." Then He added a hard sentence: "But I will not go up among you Myself, since you are a stiff-necked people" (Exodus 33:3). They could have the land. They could have an angel to guide them. But they would not have God.

When the people heard this, they mourned. No one put on jewelry. They had begun to understand that a promised land without the presence of God would be an empty place.

In those days Moses used to set up a tent outside the camp, which he called the tent of meeting. Whenever he walked out to it, the whole people stood at the doors of their own tents and watched him go. When he went inside, a pillar of cloud came down and stood at the entrance. And then the Bible says something remarkable:

> "The LORD would speak with Moses face to face, the way a person talks with a friend." (Exodus 33:11)

Then, as a friend talks with a friend, Moses prayed three prayers, each one going deeper than the last.

First he asked to know God: "Please show me Your ways. Then I will know You" (Exodus 33:13).

God answered, "My own presence will go along, and I will bring you to rest" (Exodus 33:14).

Then Moses asked for God's presence, not only for himself but for all the people: "Unless Your presence goes with us, do not make us leave this place" (Exodus 33:15). God said yes to this too.

Moses had what he needed now. The nation had been spared, and God would go with them. But Moses was not finished. He asked for something more, something no one had ever dared to ask:

"Please, let me see Your glory."

God's answer was full of tenderness. "I will make all My goodness go past you, and I will announce the name 'The LORD' before you" (Exodus 33:19). But Moses could not see God's face: "No human being can see Me and stay alive" (Exodus 33:20). So God would place him in a cleft of the rock, cover him with His hand while His glory passed, and then let him see His back.

Early one morning Moses climbed Mount Sinai again, alone, carrying two new stone tablets. The LORD came down in the cloud and passed in front of him, and He spoke His own name:

> "The LORD, the LORD, a God of tender compassion and grace, slow to grow angry, rich in faithful love and in truth, who keeps faithful love for thousands and forgives wrongdoing, rebellion and sin." (Exodus 34:6-7)

"Moses quickly sank down with his face to the ground in worship" (Exodus 34:8).

### What he asked for

In the last chapter, Moses prayed for the people. In this one, he prays for God Himself.

That is a different kind of prayer. Most of our prayers are for things: health, food, work, safety, the people we love. Those prayers are good, and God invites them. But Moses had seen enough of God to know that God is better than anything God gives. The land was a gift. The angel was a gift. Moses wanted the Giver.

Notice too that the more Moses knew God, the more he wanted to know Him. He already spoke with God "face to face" in the tent. Nobody alive was closer to God than Moses. And yet it was Moses, not the people at a distance, who cried, "Let me see Your glory." Closeness to God does not make us satisfied with what we have. It makes us hungry for more.

E. M. Bounds saw this in Moses:

> "Those who know God the best are the richest and most powerful in prayer. Little acquaintance with God, and strangeness and coldness to Him, make prayer a rare and feeble thing."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

### What he saw

God's answer is surprising. Moses asked to see God's glory, and God said, "I will make all My goodness go past you." God's glory is His goodness. When God showed Moses who He really is, He did not show a blaze of power. He spoke words of mercy: compassion, grace, patience, faithful love, forgiveness. This description of God's name became one of the most quoted passages in the Old Testament, repeated in the Psalms and the prophets.

God also told Moses the truth. No sinful human could see His face and live. God protected Moses in the rock and covered him with His hand. There is a holy distance between us and God, and only God can bridge it.

After forty more days on the mountain, Moses came down, and "he was unaware that the skin of his face shone with rays of light, because he had been speaking with God" (Exodus 34:29). He did not know. The people saw it and were afraid to come near him. Andrew Murray drew a lesson from this:

> "Close and continued fellowship with God will in due time leave its mark and make itself manifest before men."
> Andrew Murray, *The Inner Chamber*

### Where Jesus picks up the prayer

Moses' prayer was answered more fully many centuries later, on another mountain. Jesus took Peter, James and John up a mountain to pray, and "while He prayed, the look of His face was changed, and His clothes turned white and flashed like lightning. Suddenly two men were talking with Him: Moses and Elijah" (Luke 9:29-30). The man who once hid in a rock while God's glory passed by now stood and saw the glory of God in the face of Jesus.

John, who was on that mountain, later wrote, "We have seen His glory, the glory of the one and only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth" (John 1:14). Those last words echo what God proclaimed to Moses: "rich in faithful love and in truth." And John adds, "No one has ever seen God. The one and only Son... has made Him known" (John 1:18). When Philip asked, "Show us the Father," Jesus answered, "To have seen Me is to have seen the Father" (John 14:9).

And on the night before He died, Jesus prayed for us: "it is My will that they too may be with Me where I am, to see My glory" (John 17:24). Moses' longing is Jesus' own prayer for His people.

### Three key insights

1. **The best prayer asks for God Himself, not only for His gifts.** The nation had been spared, the land was promised, and God would go with them. Still Moses asked for more: to know God and see His glory. God's presence is worth more than anything He gives.
2. **The more we know God, the more we long to know Him.** Moses already spoke with God face to face, yet he was the one who asked to see His glory. Closeness to God does not end our hunger. It makes it grow.
3. **God's glory is His goodness, and we see it most clearly in Jesus.** When God showed Moses His glory, He proclaimed His mercy, grace and faithful love. Today we see that glory "in the face of Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6).

### For us today

Many believers pray mostly when they need something. That is natural, especially for those who live with poverty, sickness or danger every day. God welcomes those prayers. But Moses invites us to add another kind: prayer that simply wants God.

This kind of prayer does not need a quiet house or a long holiday. A farmer in the fields, a mother cooking for a large family, a believer in a crowded prison cell can all say, "Lord, show me Your ways. Let me know You." Some of the deepest knowledge of God in the world today is found among believers who have very little else.

Moses also teaches us that the people of God are known by God's presence. "Is it not by Your going with us?" he asked (Exodus 33:16). A church may have a fine building or a poor one, many members or few. What sets it apart is whether God is there.

And remember Moses' shining face. We do not need to try to look holy. If we spend time with God, others will notice, even when we do not.

### Try this

This week, spend at least one time of prayer asking for nothing but God Himself.

- **Pray Moses' three prayers.** Each day, pray slowly: "Show me Your ways, so that I may know You. Go with me. Show me Your glory."
- **Read God's name.** Read Exodus 34:6-7 aloud each morning. Thank God for each word: compassion, grace, patience, faithful love, forgiveness.
- **Look at Jesus.** Read one story from the Gospels each day and ask, "What does this show me about who God is?"
- **Notice your hunger.** At the end of the week, ask yourself whether you want God more than you did at the start.

### A closing prayer

LORD, the LORD, God of tender compassion and grace, slow to grow angry, rich in faithful love and in truth: I worship You. Thank You for all Your gifts, but I do not want only Your gifts. I want You. Show me Your ways, so that I may know You. Go with me wherever I go. And let me see Your glory, the glory You have shown in the face of Jesus Christ. Hide me in the Rock, and cover me with Your hand. Let my life be marked by time spent with You. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Look back over your prayers this past week. How many asked for gifts, and how many asked for God Himself?
- Why do you think Moses, who was already so close to God, wanted to know Him even more?
- God showed His glory by proclaiming His goodness and mercy. How does that change the way you picture God?

## Pouring Out the Soul

> "But Hannah answered, 'No, my lord, I am a woman deeply troubled in spirit. I have not been drinking wine or beer; I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD.'" (1 Samuel 1:15)

Every year it was the same.

Elkanah, a man from the hill country of Ephraim, took his family to Shiloh to worship the LORD and offer a sacrifice. Shiloh was where the tabernacle stood, the tent of worship that Israel had carried since the days of Moses. For one woman in the family, it was the hardest time of the year.

Elkanah had two wives, Hannah and Peninnah. In that time some men took more than one wife, and the Bible is honest about the pain it caused. Peninnah had children. Hannah had none.

At the feast Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion, because he loved her. But his love could not stop the cruelty of the other wife:

> "Her rival kept taunting her bitterly to upset her, because the LORD had closed her womb. So it went on year after year." (1 Samuel 1:6-7)

Year after year. Hannah wept and would not eat. Her husband tried to comfort her: "Hannah, why are you crying? Why do you not eat? Why are you so downcast? Do I not mean more to you than ten sons?" (1 Samuel 1:8). He meant well. But he did not understand.

One year, when the meal was over, Hannah got up and went to the house of the LORD. The old priest Eli was sitting on his seat by the doorpost. "She was in deep anguish, and she prayed to the LORD, weeping without restraint" (1 Samuel 1:10). And she made a vow:

> "The LORD of Armies, if You will only look on the misery of Your servant and remember me, if You will not forget Your servant but will give Your servant a son, then I will give him to the LORD for his whole life, and no razor will ever touch his head." (1 Samuel 1:11)

She went on praying for a long time. Many think people then usually prayed aloud. But Hannah prayed silently. "Only her lips moved; no sound of her voice could be heard" (1 Samuel 1:13). Eli watched her mouth moving, and he decided she was drunk. "How long will you go on being drunk?" he said. "Get rid of your wine!"

She had come to God's house with a broken heart, and God's priest accused her of being drunk. But Hannah answered him honestly, in the words at the top of this chapter: "I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD." She added, "Do not take your servant for a worthless woman. All this time I have been speaking out of my great anguish and distress" (1 Samuel 1:16).

Eli understood. "Go in peace," he said. "May the God of Israel grant what you have asked of Him" (1 Samuel 1:17).

"Then the woman went her way and ate, and her face was no longer downcast" (1 Samuel 1:18).

### Who she was

Hannah lived about three thousand years ago, perhaps around 1100 BC, near the end of the time of the judges. It was a dark time: the book of Judges ends with the words, "everyone did what they thought was right" (Judges 21:25). Even at Shiloh things were wrong. Eli's two sons served as priests there, but "Eli's sons were worthless men; they did not know the LORD" (1 Samuel 2:12). Hannah begged not to be taken for "a worthless woman," while the truly worthless men served at the altar.

In Hannah's world, as in many parts of the world today, a woman who could not have children often lived with shame. Hannah carried that pain for years, with a rival in her own home reminding her of it.

And yet God was writing a bigger story. Israel needed a leader who would call the nation back to God. Hannah wanted a son. God wanted to give Israel a prophet. As Thomas Watson, the English Puritan, put it:

> "Hannah asked a son, and God not only gave her a son, but a prophet."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

### How she prayed

"Pouring out my soul." Think of turning a jar upside down until every last drop runs out. That is what Hannah did. She did not tidy up her feelings before she came to God. She did not hide her tears or use fine religious words. She emptied her heart before Him. The psalms invite us to do the same: "empty your hearts out before Him. God is our shelter" (Psalm 62:8).

E. M. Bounds held up Hannah's words as a measure for all our praying:

> "Let all the present day praying be measured by these standards, 'Pouring out the soul before God,' and 'Seeking with all the heart,' and how much of it will be found to be mere form, waste, worthless."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

Notice three more things.

It was **specific**. She did not ask vaguely for God to bless her. She asked for a son.

It was **surrendered**. She promised to give the son back to God, so that her child would belong to the LORD.

It **changed her before it changed her situation**. When she left the house of the LORD, she was not yet pregnant. Nothing outside had changed. But she ate, and her face was no longer sad. She had left her burden with God.

In time, "the LORD remembered her" (1 Samuel 1:19). She had a son and named him Samuel, "for she said, 'I asked the LORD for him'" (1 Samuel 1:20). When he was old enough, she kept her vow. She brought him to Shiloh and said to Eli, "I prayed for this boy, and the LORD has granted me what I asked of Him" (1 Samuel 1:27).

And then Hannah prayed again. This time it was not tears but a song:

> "My heart is glad in the LORD; in the LORD my horn is raised high." (1 Samuel 2:1)

She sang of a God who turns the world upside down: "He lifts the poor up out of the dust and raises those in need from the ash heap, to seat them with nobles" (1 Samuel 2:8).

### Where Jesus picks up the prayer

About a thousand years later, another young woman sang a song very much like Hannah's. Mary, the mother of Jesus, praised God that "He has pulled the mighty down from their thrones and raised up the lowly. The hungry He has filled with good things, and the rich He has sent away with nothing" (Luke 1:52-53). Many think Mary knew Hannah's song well, and Jesus grew up hearing the Scriptures that held it.

Hannah's song ends with the LORD raising "high the horn of His anointed" (1 Samuel 2:10). The word "anointed" in Hebrew is the word from which we get "Messiah." Hannah sang about a coming king before Israel even had one. Jesus is that King. When He began to teach, He announced the same good news Hannah sang about: "Blessed are you poor... Blessed are you who are hungry now, for you will eat your fill" (Luke 6:20-21).

### Three key insights

1. **We can bring our deepest pain to God exactly as it is.** Hannah did not tidy up her feelings first. God is not offended by our tears. He invites us to empty our hearts before Him.
2. **Prayer can give us peace before it changes our circumstances.** Hannah left the house of the LORD with nothing changed, yet she ate and her face was no longer sad. When we leave our burden with God, we can rest even while we wait.
3. **God often answers in a way that serves His bigger purposes.** Hannah asked for a son. God gave her a son and gave Israel a prophet. When we offer our requests back to God, He can use them to bless many.

### For us today

Some readers know Hannah's pain very closely. In many cultures in Africa, Asia and elsewhere, a woman without children is still blamed or pushed aside, sometimes by her own family. Others carry a grief no one seems to understand: a long sickness, a lost job, a son in prison, a husband who has left. Like Hannah, you may have heard careless words from people who should have comforted you.

Hannah's story says: bring it all to God, not just the polite version. You do not need loud words, or even words at all. The Holy Spirit "pleads on our behalf, with groans that no words carry" (Romans 8:26).

Hannah also reminds church leaders to be gentle. Eli judged too quickly. Many people sitting quietly in church carry a burden we cannot see.

And when the answer comes, do not forget Hannah's second prayer. She came back to sing.

### Try this

This week, practice pouring out your soul before God.

- **Find a private place.** If your home is crowded, pray silently, as Hannah did. Only your lips need to move.
- **Empty the jar.** Tell God everything about one burden you carry: what happened, how it feels, what you fear. Hold nothing back.
- **Offer it back.** Tell God how you would give the answer back to Him.
- **Write a song or psalm of thanks.** Think of one prayer God has answered in your life, and write a few lines of praise, as Hannah did.

### A closing prayer

LORD of Armies, You see the misery of Your servants and You do not forget us. You know the burdens I carry, even the ones I have told no one. I pour out my soul before You now. Give me Hannah's peace, that can rest before the answer comes. Whatever You give me, I give back to You. You lift the poor from the dust and fill the hungry with good things. Thank You for Jesus, the Anointed King Hannah sang about. In His name I pray. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Hannah's prayer was honest and full of tears. What makes it hard for you to pour out your soul before God?
- Hannah's face changed before her circumstances did. Have you ever known God's peace while you were still waiting for an answer?
- Hannah gave her answered prayer back to God. What has God given you that you could offer back to Him?

## The Sin of Not Praying

> "As for me, far be it from me to sin against the LORD by ceasing to pray for you. I will go on teaching you the way that is good and right." (1 Samuel 12:23)

The old man stood before the whole nation. His hair was gray. Now they had a king, a tall young man named Saul, and the people had just gathered at Gilgal, near the Jordan River, to make him king before the LORD (1 Samuel 11:14-15). Most readers think Samuel gave his farewell speech at that same gathering.

It was not an easy day for Samuel. The people had asked for a king "so that we too may be like all the nations" (1 Samuel 8:20). Part of their reason was painful for him to hear: his own sons, whom he had made judges, "took bribes and bent justice" (1 Samuel 8:3). In a sense, the nation had said to him, "We do not want your way any longer."

Samuel did not hide from the people. He asked them to judge his life in public: "Whose ox did I take? Whose donkey? Whom did I cheat?" (1 Samuel 12:3). They answered that he had never cheated anyone. Then he reminded them how often God had rescued their ancestors when they cried out to Him. God Himself had been their king.

Then Samuel did something strange. It was the time of the wheat harvest. In the land of Israel the wheat is cut in early summer, in the dry season, when rain almost never falls. Samuel said he would call on the LORD, and the LORD would send thunder and rain.

> "So Samuel called on the LORD, and that same day the LORD sent thunder and rain. All the people were filled with fear of the LORD and of Samuel." (1 Samuel 12:18)

Picture the farmers looking at their ripe fields under a dark sky. Picture the thunder rolling over the hills. The people suddenly understood what they had done. And in their fear they ran to the very man they had pushed aside: "Pray for your servants to the LORD your God, or we will die!" (1 Samuel 12:19).

Samuel could have answered, "Now you want my prayers? Ask your king." Instead he told them not to be afraid, and not to turn away from the LORD. Then he spoke the words at the head of this chapter.

### Who he was

In the last chapter we met Hannah, who poured out her soul at Shiloh for a son. Samuel was that son, the answer to her prayer. E. M. Bounds put it simply:

> "Praying Samuels come from praying Hannahs."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

Samuel grew up at the house of God in Shiloh, where Eli was priest. As a boy he heard God call his name in the night, and he answered, "Speak, for your servant is listening" (1 Samuel 3:10). He became a prophet, a judge and a leader. Every year he traveled a circuit to Bethel, Gilgal and Mizpah, and then went home to Ramah, where "he built an altar there to the LORD" (1 Samuel 7:17).

Above all, he was a man who prayed for others. Years before the day at Gilgal, when the Philistines were crushing Israel, Samuel called the people together at Mizpah and said, "I will pray to the LORD for you" (1 Samuel 7:5). When the enemy army came near, the people begged him:

> "The Israelites said to Samuel, 'Do not stop crying out for us to the LORD our God, so that He will save us from the power of the Philistines.'" (1 Samuel 7:8)

He cried out, and the LORD answered. Israel never forgot it. Centuries later a psalm remembered him: "among those who called on His name was Samuel; they called out to the LORD, and He answered them" (Psalm 99:6). And when God spoke to Jeremiah about the greatest pleaders in Israel's history, He named two: "Even if Moses and Samuel stood before Me to plead for them" (Jeremiah 15:1).

### How he prayed

Look at how Samuel prayed when he was hurt. When the elders first demanded a king, the Bible says, "Samuel was displeased ... and he prayed to the LORD" (1 Samuel 8:6). He took his pain to God. And God comforted him: "It is not you they have rejected; it is Me they have rejected as their king" (1 Samuel 8:7).

Look at how he prayed when he grieved. Later, when King Saul turned away from God, "Samuel was angry, and he cried out to the LORD all night long" (1 Samuel 15:11). He had not stopped caring about Saul.

And look at what Samuel joined to his prayer: "I will go on teaching you the way that is good and right." He did not pray instead of teaching, or teach instead of praying. He did both. Prayer was the hidden half of his work for the people.

Andrew Murray met with many ministers at church conventions, and everywhere he heard the same sad confession: "We pray too little!" He took Samuel's words seriously:

> "Samuel once said, 'As for me, God forbid that I should sin against the Lord in ceasing to pray for you.' Ceasing from prayer is sin against God."
> Andrew Murray, *The Ministry of Intercession*

That may sound hard. Murray did not mean that God keeps a list of our missed prayers to punish us. He meant that prayer for others is not a hobby for a few very spiritual people. It is a trust God has given to all His people. If God has put people into our lives, He has put them into our prayers too.

Samuel's promise points forward to Someone greater. Jesus prayed for His disciples, and for us: "My prayer is also for those who are going to believe in Me through their message" (John 17:20). He prayed for Peter before Peter fell (Luke 22:32). He prayed for those who were nailing Him to the cross. Samuel said he would not stop praying for the people. Jesus never stops:

> "For this reason He is able to save completely those who approach God through Him, since He is always alive to plead on their behalf." (Hebrews 7:25)

### Three key insights

1. **Praying for others is a duty of love, not an extra.** Samuel called it a sin to stop praying for the people God had given him. The people in our lives are a trust from God, and prayer is one of the main ways we care for them.
2. **We can keep praying for people who have hurt or rejected us.** Israel pushed Samuel aside, yet when they asked for his prayers he did not hesitate. Praying for those who wounded us is one of the surest ways to keep bitterness out of our hearts.
3. **Jesus never stops praying for us, so we need never stop praying for others.** Samuel's promise was faithful but human, and he died. Jesus lives forever to plead for His people. When we pray for others, we join the prayer He is already praying.

### For us today

Who are your "people"? For a mother, they are her children. For a pastor, his congregation. For a factory worker, the men and women on his shift. Each of us has a circle of people God has placed around us, and Samuel's words apply to all of us: far be it from me to stop praying for them.

Perhaps someone in your circle has hurt you. A son who left home in anger. A church that chose another leader. A neighbor who spoke against you. Samuel shows another way. He told the people the truth about their sin, and he went on praying for them. We do not pretend the hurt did not happen, but we refuse to let it close our mouths before God.

Think also of nations. Samuel prayed for a nation that had made a poor choice of government. In many lands today Christians live under leaders they did not choose and cannot change. Some live under governments that persecute them. The apostle Paul told believers in the Roman Empire to pray "for kings and for all who hold high office" (1 Timothy 2:2), and he wrote this under emperors who did not love Christ. Bounds wrote of Samuel, "National affairs need to be prayed over." That is as true in Nairobi or Manila as it is in London or Lima.

Your people may never know you are praying for them. God knows, and the work is real.

### Try this

This week, take Samuel's promise as your own.

- **Write your list.** Write down the names of the people God has given you: family, church, work, neighbors. Keep the list in your Bible or on your phone.
- **Pray through it daily.** Each day, pray for a few names. You do not need many words. "Father, bless my sister today and keep her close to You" is a real prayer.
- **Add one hard name.** Choose one person who has hurt you or rejected you. Pray for that person's good every day this week, even if your feelings do not change at first.
- **Pray for your leaders.** Pray by name for the leaders of your town and nation, including those you did not choose.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You gave Samuel to Israel as a man who prayed, and You have given me people to love and to carry before You. Forgive me for the times I have stopped praying for them. Forgive me when I have let hurt or tiredness close my mouth. Give me Samuel's faithfulness, to tell the truth and to keep on praying. Thank You for Jesus, who always lives to plead for me. Let me join His prayers for the people around me, until the day I see Him face to face. In His name I pray. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Who are the people God has placed in your circle? How often do you pray for them by name?
- Samuel prayed for people who had rejected him. Is there someone you find it hard to pray for? Why?
- What difference does it make to know that Jesus is praying for you right now?

## One Thing I Ask

> "I have asked the LORD for one thing, and it is what I am looking for: to live in the house of the LORD as long as I live, to look on the loveliness of the LORD and to seek Him in His temple." (Psalm 27:4)

Long before he was a king, David was a shepherd boy in the hills around Bethlehem. He was the youngest of Jesse's eight sons, and his place was out in the fields with the sheep. It was lonely work, and dangerous. While he was still young, he told King Saul, "any time a lion or a bear would come and carry off a sheep from the flock, I went after it, struck it, and rescued the sheep from its mouth" (1 Samuel 17:34-35).

Perhaps it was in those long days and nights in the open that David learned to play the lyre, a small harp with strings. He became so skilled that he was brought to the palace to play for Saul when the king was troubled in spirit (1 Samuel 16:23). At the end of his life the Bible calls him "the sweet singer of Israel's songs" (2 Samuel 23:1).

His life was not quiet for long. After he killed the giant Goliath, Saul grew jealous and tried to kill him. For years David lived as a hunted man, hiding in caves and in the desert. Even after he became king, there were wars, rebellions and enemies who told lies about him.

We do not know exactly when David wrote Psalm 27. But the psalm itself tells us what his life was like when he wrote it:

> "If an army sets up camp against me, my heart will not be afraid; if war rises up against me, I will still hold on to my trust." (Psalm 27:3)

Picture a man looking down from a hill at the tents of an enemy army. He can see the campfires. He knows that people want him dead. What would you ask God for at a moment like that? Victory? Safety? A way of escape? All of those would be good prayers, and in other psalms David prays them.

But here David says something surprising. Out of all the things he could ask for, he gathers his whole heart into one request. "I have asked the LORD for one thing." He does not ask first for the enemies to disappear. He asks to be near God. He wants to live in God's house, to look at God's beauty, and to seek Him.

In the next verse he explains why this is not running away from his troubles. It is the safest place there is: "When the day of trouble comes, He will tuck me away in His shelter, keep me hidden deep inside His tent, and put me up on a rock out of reach" (Psalm 27:5).

### What David asked for

Notice the three parts of David's one request.

First, **to live in the house of the LORD**. In David's day there was no stone temple in Jerusalem yet. When David brought the ark of the covenant to Jerusalem, he "set it in its place within the tent David had pitched for it" (2 Samuel 6:17). The word "temple" here simply means God's house, the place where He had promised to meet His people. David is not asking to move into a tent. He is asking to live his whole life close to God.

Second, **to look on the loveliness of the LORD**. David wants to see how beautiful God is. Like Moses in chapter 4, David is asking for God Himself. It is the prayer of a friend, not only of a person in need.

Third, **to seek Him**. To seek here means to ask, to inquire, to come close and listen. David did not think he had already arrived. Even a man who knew God well wanted to know Him more.

A few verses later David tells us how this longing began. It did not start with him:

> "My heart says to You what You have said: 'Seek My face.' So I will seek Your face, LORD." (Psalm 27:8)

God spoke first. God said, "Seek My face," and David's heart answered. Prayer is often like that. The desire to pray is itself a gift, an invitation from God.

E. M. Bounds noticed how focused David's desire was:

> "David did not yearn for everything; nor did he allow his desires to spread out everywhere and hit nothing."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Necessity of Prayer*

### The singer who taught Israel to pray

David did not keep his prayers to himself. He turned them into songs. Nearly half of the psalms carry his name in their headings. When God's people needed words to pray, in fear, in joy, in sorrow, in sin, they often found them in David's songs. A. W. Tozer, the American pastor, wrote:

> "David's life was a torrent of spiritual desire, and his psalms ring with the cry of the seeker and the glad shout of the finder."
> A. W. Tozer, *The Pursuit of God*

These songs became the prayer book of Israel, and so they became the prayer book of Jesus. In Nazareth, where He grew up, He went to the synagogue on the Sabbath, "as He always did" (Luke 4:16), and there the psalms were read and sung. On the night before He died, after the Passover meal, "they sang a hymn and went out to the Mount of Olives" (Matthew 26:30). Many think, from Jewish custom, that this hymn came from the psalms sung at Passover. And on the cross He prayed in the words of the psalms, as we will see in chapter 16.

Jesus shared David's one desire too. When He was twelve, His worried parents found Him in the temple. He said, "Did you not know that I must be in My Father's house?" (Luke 2:49). Later, when Martha was busy and anxious, He said of her sister Mary, who sat at His feet and listened: "there is need of few things, or only of one. Mary has picked the good share" (Luke 10:42).

And there is more. David longed "to look on the loveliness of the LORD." The letter to the Hebrews says that the Son "is the radiance of God's glory" (Hebrews 1:3). In Jesus, the beauty David longed to see has come close to us.

### Three key insights

1. **The best thing we can ask of God is God Himself.** David could have asked for many things, but his one request was to be near God and to see His beauty. Every other gift is good, but nothing is as beautiful as God Himself.
2. **A single desire gives our prayers focus and strength.** David gathered his scattered wishes into one. When we know what we want most, our prayers become clearer, and the smaller worries take their right place.
3. **God's presence is our shelter in the day of trouble.** David prayed this with enemies all around him. Seeking God is not running from our problems. It is finding the one safe place in the middle of them.

### For us today

You do not need a temple to pray Psalm 27:4. Because of Jesus, God's house is wherever His people meet Him. A cleaner on a night shift in a big city can seek God's face with a mop in her hand. A farmer in a dry field can look up and pray, "One thing I ask." A prisoner for Christ in a crowded cell can say, "He will tuck me away in His shelter."

Brother Lawrence, a humble cook in a monastery kitchen in France, wrote to a friend about a young soldier they both knew:

> "A little lifting up of the heart suffices. A little remembrance of God, one act of inward worship, though upon a march, and a sword in hand, are prayers, which, however short, are nevertheless very acceptable to God"
> Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*

David was a soldier too, and he knew this. You can seek God's face on a march, at a market stall, or on a crowded bus.

Singing can help. In many churches in Africa, Asia and Latin America, prayer and song flow into each other, and a simple chorus sung again and again becomes a prayer. You do not need a fine voice. David sang to God in the fields, where only the sheep could hear him.

Finally, ask yourself what your "one thing" really is. Many of us, if we are honest, would say money, safety, success or a good marriage. Those are not bad things. But Jesus said, "Above all, seek the kingdom and His righteousness, and you will be given all these things as well" (Matthew 6:33).

### Try this

This week, let Psalm 27 shape your prayers.

- **Read the whole psalm each day.** It takes only a few minutes. Read it aloud if you can, and slowly.
- **Answer God's invitation.** Each morning, hear God say to you, "Seek My face." Answer with David's words: "So I will seek Your face, LORD."
- **Write your "one thing."** Write one sentence that begins, "I have asked the LORD for one thing." Be honest. Then ask God to make His presence your first desire.
- **Sing a prayer.** Sing a psalm or a simple song of worship, alone or with your family, once each day.

### A closing prayer

LORD, You are my light and the One who saves me. You have said to me, "Seek My face," and today my heart answers, "I will seek Your face." Forgive me for wanting Your gifts more than I want You. Gather my scattered desires into one. Let me live close to You all the days of my life, and show me Your beauty in the face of Jesus. When trouble comes, hide me in Your shelter, and teach me to sing to You even there. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- If you could ask God for only one thing, what would it be? What does your answer tell you about your heart?
- What helps you to "look on the loveliness of the LORD" in an ordinary day?
- David prayed this psalm with enemies around him. How can seeking God help you face your own fears?

- Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*, especially "Third Letter"

## A Pure Heart

> "God, create a pure heart for me, and make my spirit new and steady inside me." (Psalm 51:10)

It was the time of year when kings went out to war, most likely the spring. David's army was away fighting the Ammonites, but David himself stayed at home in Jerusalem.

One evening he got up from his bed and walked on the flat roof of his palace. From there he saw a woman bathing, and she was very beautiful. He sent someone to find out who she was. The answer came back: she was Bathsheba, "the wife of Uriah the Hittite" (2 Samuel 11:3). Uriah was one of David's own soldiers, away at the war.

> "David sent messengers and had her brought. She came to him, and he slept with her." (2 Samuel 11:4)

We should be honest about what happened. David was the king. When the king's messengers came to her door, Bathsheba would have had little power to refuse. Later, when God sent a prophet to speak to David, the story the prophet told placed the guilt on David, the powerful man who took what was not his.

Then Bathsheba sent David a message: "I am pregnant" (2 Samuel 11:5). David tried to hide what he had done. He called Uriah home from the war and told him to go home to his wife, so that people would think the child was Uriah's. But Uriah was a loyal soldier. He would not go home while his fellow soldiers were sleeping in the open field. He slept at the palace door instead.

So David did something even worse. He wrote a letter to Joab, his army commander, and made Uriah carry it himself. It said: "Station Uriah in the front line where the battle is heaviest. Then fall back from him, so that he is struck down and dies" (2 Samuel 11:15). Joab obeyed, and Uriah was killed. When Bathsheba's time of mourning was over, David married her, and she gave birth to a son. "But the thing David had done was evil in the eyes of the LORD" (2 Samuel 11:27).

Months passed. Then the prophet Nathan came to the king with a story. A rich man with many flocks had a guest to feed. Instead of taking one of his own sheep, he took the only lamb of a poor neighbor, a little lamb the poor man loved "like a daughter" (2 Samuel 12:3). David burned with anger: "the man who did this deserves to die!"

Nathan looked at him and said, "You are the man!" (2 Samuel 12:7).

The king could have had the prophet killed. Instead he said simply, "I have sinned against the LORD" (2 Samuel 12:13).

### What David prayed

An ancient heading over Psalm 51, kept in many Bibles, says that David wrote it after Nathan came to him. Here is how it begins:

> "God, be gracious to me in keeping with Your loyal love; in the fullness of Your compassion, wipe away my rebellious acts. Scrub me completely clean of my guilt, and purify me from my sin." (Psalm 51:1-2)

Notice where David begins. He does not begin with excuses, such as "Kings do these things." He begins with God's character: God's "loyal love" and God's "compassion." His only hope is who God is.

Then he names what he has done, with no soft words: "I myself know my rebellious acts, and my sin is never out of my sight" (Psalm 51:3). He even says, "I have sinned against You, against You alone" (Psalm 51:4). He did not mean that Bathsheba and Uriah did not matter. He had wronged them terribly. He meant that every sin is first of all a sin against God, the One who made them and loved them.

Charles Spurgeon wrote about this psalm:

> "Learn in confession to be honest with God. Do not give fair names to foul sins; call them what you will, they will smell no sweeter."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Evening by Evening*

Then David asks for washing: "Take away my sin with hyssop, and I will be pure" (Psalm 51:7). Hyssop was a small plant. Israel's priests used a bunch of it to sprinkle water or blood in ceremonies of cleansing. David knows he cannot wash himself. God must do it.

### A new heart

Then comes the verse at the head of this chapter: "God, create a pure heart for me." The word David uses for "create" is the same word used in the first verse of the Bible, where God creates the heavens and the earth. David is not asking God to repair his old heart a little. He is asking for something only God can do: a new creation.

He also prays, "do not withdraw Your Holy Spirit from me" (Psalm 51:11). David had seen what happened to King Saul: "the Spirit of the LORD had left Saul" (1 Samuel 16:14). David feared that more than anything.

Did God answer? Nathan had already told him: "The LORD has taken away your sin. You will not die" (2 Samuel 12:13). But Nathan also said that the consequences would remain. The child would die, and trouble would never leave David's family. Forgiveness did not undo the past. The baby became ill. David fasted and spent the nights lying on the ground, pleading for the child (2 Samuel 12:16). On the seventh day the child died. Then David got up, washed, and "entered the house of the LORD and bowed in worship" (2 Samuel 12:20). Forgiven and grieving, he came back to God.

E. M. Bounds summed it up:

> "David knew where to find a sin-pardoning God, and was received back again and had the joys of salvation restored to him by earnest, sincere, penitential praying."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

In Volume 2 we will hear Jesus' story of the tax collector, who prayed with the broken heart of Psalm 51:17. Here we see where that broken heart came from: a king who had no excuses left.

Jesus Himself never prayed Psalm 51 for His own sins. He had none. The Bible says He was tested "in every way, as we are, but without sinning" (Hebrews 4:15). But He taught us to pray every day, "Forgive us our sins" (Luke 11:4). And He did something David could never do. He took the guilt David confessed: "Christ had never known sin, yet for our sake God made Him to be sin" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Matthew does not hide this story either. In the family line of Jesus he writes, "David was the father of Solomon by the wife of Uriah" (Matthew 1:6). The Savior came from a family that needed saving.

### Three key insights

1. **Real confession calls sin by its true name.** David stopped making excuses and said, "I have sinned." God cannot heal what we keep hiding, but He is quick to forgive what we honestly confess.
2. **Only God can make a heart new.** David did not ask for a little improvement. He asked God to create a pure heart in him, and that is exactly what God promises to do for all who come to Him through Jesus.
3. **No one has fallen too far to come home to God.** David was guilty of adultery and murder, yet he was forgiven and restored. If God's mercy reached David, it can reach you.

### For us today

Some readers of this book carry a secret like David's. Or everyone knows what you did, and you feel you can never lift your head again. Psalm 51 was written for you. God's "loyal love" is bigger than your sin. John wrote, "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous: He will forgive our sins and cleanse us from every kind of wrongdoing" (1 John 1:9).

In many cultures, in Africa, Asia, Latin America and beyond, a family's honor matters very much, and to admit a wrong brings shame on everyone. That makes confession very hard. But God does not ask us to protect our honor before Him. He asks us to come as we are, and He covers our shame with His mercy.

There is a hard word here too, especially for leaders. David was a king who used his power to take what he wanted. Real repentance does not only say sorry to God. It stops the wrong, tells the truth, and does what it can to protect and help those who were hurt. God's forgiveness does not mean that the wrong should be kept hidden from the people who need to know.

And there is hope here for the wounded. Bathsheba's story did not end in shame. God gave her another son, Solomon, and she is remembered in the family line of the Savior.

### Try this

This week, let Psalm 51 lead you into honest confession and real joy.

- **Read Psalm 51 slowly.** Read it once each day. Mark the words you most need to pray.
- **Name your sin to God.** Tell God plainly what you have done, with no excuses and no soft names.
- **Make it right.** If your sin hurt another person, ask God what you should do: an apology, paying back what you took, or telling the truth to someone who needs to hear it.
- **Receive forgiveness.** Read 1 John 1:9 aloud. Thank God that in Jesus your sin is taken away. Then pray Psalm 51:10 as your prayer for the day ahead.

### A closing prayer

God, be gracious to me in keeping with Your loyal love. I will not hide my sins from You or give them soft names. I have sinned against You. Wash me, and I will be clean. Create a pure heart in me, and make my spirit new and steady inside me. Do not take Your Holy Spirit from me. Give back to me the joy of Your salvation. Thank You that Jesus, who never sinned, carried my sin, so that I could come home to You. Help me to make right what I can, and to walk with You in a new way from today. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think David tried so hard to hide his sin? What do we usually do with the wrongs we have done?
- What is the difference between being sorry we were caught and being sorry we sinned against God?
- Is there someone you have wronged to whom you need to make things right?

## If My People Will Pray

> "and if My people who bear My name humble themselves and pray, seek My face and turn back from their evil ways, then I Myself will hear from heaven. I will forgive their sin and heal their land." (2 Chronicles 7:14)

For seven years the workers had been building. Now, at last, the house of the LORD stood finished on the hill in Jerusalem, shining with gold. King David had longed to build it, but God had told him that his son would build it instead. That son was Solomon, the child God gave to David and Bathsheba after their great sorrow.

In the seventh month of the year, in the autumn, the people of Israel gathered for the festival from every corner of the land. The singers and the trumpeters stood together, and as they sang, "For He is good; His loyal love lasts forever," a cloud filled the house. The priests could not even stand to do their work, "since the house of God was full of the glory of the LORD" (2 Chronicles 5:14).

Then Solomon stepped up onto a bronze platform in the middle of the courtyard, where all the people could see him. He knelt down in front of them all and spread out his hands toward heaven (2 Chronicles 6:13). The king of Israel was on his knees.

His prayer, one of the longest in the Bible, holds a question full of wonder:

> "But will God really live on earth with human beings? Heaven itself, even the highest heaven, cannot contain You. How much less this house that I have built!" (2 Chronicles 6:18)

Then Solomon asked God to hear the prayers His people would pray toward this house in every kind of trouble: when they sinned against a neighbor, when an enemy defeated them, when the sky was shut and no rain fell, when famine or disease came, even when they were carried away as prisoners to a far country. Again and again he asked God to "hear from heaven and forgive" (2 Chronicles 6:25).

When he finished, fire came down from heaven and burned up the sacrifices, and the glory of the LORD filled the house. All the people fell with their faces to the pavement and worshiped. After the festival the people went home "joyful and glad at heart" (2 Chronicles 7:10).

Then, during the night, the LORD appeared to Solomon. He gave His answer to that long prayer:

> "I have heard your prayer, and I have chosen this place for Myself as a house of sacrifice. When I shut up the sky so that no rain falls, or command locusts to eat up the land, or send a plague among My people..." (2 Chronicles 7:12-13)

And then came the words at the head of this chapter.

### Who he was

Solomon began his reign in prayer. Early on, God appeared to him and invited him to ask for anything. Solomon asked, "Now give me wisdom and knowledge, so that I may go out and come in before this people as their leader" (2 Chronicles 1:10). God gave him wisdom, and riches as well.

E. M. Bounds was struck by Solomon's prayer at the temple:

> "The prayer of Solomon at the dedication of the temple is the product of inspired wisdom and piety, and gives a lucid and powerful view of prayer in the wideness of its range, the minuteness of its details, and its abounding possibilities and its urgent necessity."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Possibilities of Prayer*

Solomon even prayed for foreigners who would come from distant lands to pray at this house. In Volume 2 we will see how Jesus, cleansing the temple a thousand years later, spoke the same hope in the words of Isaiah: "a house of prayer for all the nations" (Mark 11:17).

But the story of Solomon is also a warning. God told him that night that if he and the people turned away to other gods, this house would become a ruin (2 Chronicles 7:19-22). And sadly, "When Solomon was old, his wives turned his heart to follow other gods" (1 Kings 11:4). The king who prayed so beautifully at the beginning did not finish well. A good start in prayer must become a whole life of prayer.

### What God promised, and to whom

Many Christians love 2 Chronicles 7:14. It is printed on banners and quoted at prayer meetings around the world. It is a wonderful verse, but we should read it carefully.

First, see **to whom it was spoken**. God was speaking to Solomon about Israel, the nation in covenant with Him, at the dedication of the temple in Jerusalem. "My people" meant Israel. "Their land" meant the land God had given them. The troubles named in verse 13, drought, locusts and plague, were the very troubles God had warned Israel about in His covenant with them. And the promise was tied to "the prayer made in this place" (2 Chronicles 7:15), the temple.

So here is what we may **not** do. We may not take this verse as a guarantee that any modern nation will become rich, safe or free of trouble if enough Christians pray. No nation today is Israel under the covenant of Moses. Christians are scattered among every nation on earth, and God has not promised to heal our countries at the time we choose.

But here is what we **may** do, with confidence. The verse shows us the heart of God, and God does not change. He still hears people who humble themselves, pray, seek His face and turn from evil. James writes, "Make yourselves low before the Lord, and He will raise you high" (James 4:10). And in the New Testament, the people who bear God's name are the church of Jesus from every nation. Peter writes, "you are a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people God has claimed as His own" (1 Peter 2:9).

Notice too who must humble themselves. God did not say, "If the wicked turn." He said, "If **My** people." The call begins with God's own people, not with our neighbors or our governments. Charles Spurgeon read the verse that way:

> "Called by the name of the Lord, we are nevertheless erring men and women. What a mercy it is that our God is ready to forgive! Whenever we sin let us hasten to the mercy-seat of our God, seeking pardon."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith*

And what about "this place"? Jesus changed everything. He said, "Tear this temple down, and I will raise it up again within three days," and John explains, "the temple He meant was His own body" (John 2:19-21). Jesus is the place where God meets us now. He is "something greater than Solomon" (Matthew 12:42), and His temple is open to every nation. We no longer pray toward a building in Jerusalem, but in His name, wherever we are.

### Three key insights

1. **God's people are called to humble themselves before they point at others.** The promise begins with "My people," not with the sins of the nation. Healing begins when the church kneels, as Solomon knelt before all Israel.
2. **God always hears those who turn back to Him.** The promise was given to Israel, but it shows God's unchanging heart. When His people humble themselves, pray, seek His face and turn from evil, He hears and forgives.
3. **We pray for our land without demanding a guarantee.** We may pray boldly for the healing of our towns and nations, because God is merciful. But we leave the time and the way of the answer in His hands.

### For us today

Christians in many countries are a small minority. In parts of the Middle East, North Africa and Asia, believers cannot change their nation by voting or by marching. Yet they can still humble themselves, pray, seek God's face and turn from evil. God told the exiles in Babylon, "Seek the peace of the city where I have sent you as exiles, and pray to the LORD for it" (Jeremiah 29:7). Your prayers matter, even if you are few.

Where Christians are many and powerful, the danger is different: it is easy to turn this verse into a slogan against other people. But God's call is first to us. Before we pray about the sins of our nation, we must confess our own: pride, love of money, unkindness to the poor, division in the church.

And some readers know the troubles of verse 13 very well. Drought and locusts are not old stories in East Africa or South Asia. You may pray for rain, as Solomon did, and God often sends it in mercy. When He does not, He has still not stopped hearing you. Later in this volume we will meet Daniel, far away in Babylon, praying toward Jerusalem just as Solomon had asked, and God hearing him there.

### Try this

This week, take the four parts of God's call one at a time.

- **Humble yourself.** On the first day, kneel if you are able, as Solomon did, and tell God that you need Him.
- **Pray.** On the second day, pray for your town or city by name: its leaders, its poor, its churches.
- **Seek His face.** On the third day, pray Psalm 27:8: "So I will seek Your face, LORD." Ask for God Himself, not only for His help.
- **Turn back.** On the fourth day, ask God to show you one wrong way in your life, and turn from it. If you can, meet with other believers to pray and confess together.

### A closing prayer

LORD God, the highest heaven cannot contain You, yet You hear the prayers of Your people. I humble myself before You. Forgive my pride and my sin, and the sins of Your church in my land. Teach us to seek Your face and to turn from our evil ways. Have mercy on my town and my nation, and heal what is broken, in Your time and in Your way. Thank You for Jesus, who is greater than Solomon and greater than the temple, through whom I can come to You from anywhere on earth. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think God began with "My people" rather than with the nation as a whole?
- What is the difference between claiming 2 Chronicles 7:14 as a guarantee and praying it as an invitation?
- What would it look like for your church to "humble themselves and pray" this year?

## Fire from Heaven, Rain on the Earth

> "Answer me, the LORD, answer me, so that these people may know that You, the LORD, are God and are turning their hearts back again." (1 Kings 18:37)

For more than three years there had been no rain in Israel. The fields were dust, and the people were hungry.

Then, on Mount Carmel, a ridge of hills by the Mediterranean Sea, the nation gathered to watch a contest. On one side stood 450 prophets of Baal, the god that Queen Jezebel had brought into the land. On the other side stood one man, the prophet Elijah. "How long will you go on hobbling between two choices?" he asked the crowd (1 Kings 18:21). No one answered. So Elijah proposed a test. Each side would prepare a bull for sacrifice and lay it on wood, but light no fire, and pray. "Whichever god answers with fire, he is God" (1 Kings 18:24).

The prophets of Baal went first. From morning until noon they cried, "Baal, answer us!" At noon Elijah mocked them: perhaps their god was asleep. So they shouted louder and cut themselves until the blood flowed, on and on until the time of the evening offering. "But there was no sound, no answer, no one taking notice" (1 Kings 18:29).

Then Elijah called the people close. He repaired the LORD's altar, which had been torn down, using twelve stones, one for each tribe of Israel. He dug a trench around it and laid the wood and the bull in order. Then he had them pour water over it all, four large jars, three times, until even the trench was full.

At the time of the evening offering, Elijah stepped forward and prayed. It was a short prayer. He asked God to make it known that He was God in Israel, and that Elijah was His servant. Then he cried, "Answer me, the LORD, answer me."

> "Then fire from the LORD fell. It burned up the burnt offering, the wood, the stones and the soil, and licked up the water in the trench. When all the people saw it, they fell face down and said, 'The LORD, He is God! The LORD, He is God!'" (1 Kings 18:38-39)

But the land was still dry. Elijah told King Ahab to go and eat, because "there is the sound of a roaring downpour" (1 Kings 18:41). Yet no cloud could be seen. Elijah climbed to the top of Carmel, bowed down to the ground, and put his face between his knees. And he prayed again.

> "He said to his servant, 'Go up and look toward the sea.' The servant went up and looked, and said, 'There is nothing there.' Seven times Elijah told him, 'Go back.' The seventh time the servant said, 'Look, a cloud as small as a man's hand is rising out of the sea.'" (1 Kings 18:43-44)

In a very short time the sky grew black with clouds and wind, and a heavy rain came.

### Who he was

Elijah came from Tishbe in Gilead, the hill country east of the Jordan River. His name means "My God is the LORD." He lived in the northern kingdom of Israel, about 870 to 850 years before Christ, in the days of King Ahab. Ahab "did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, more than all who were before him" (1 Kings 16:30). He married Jezebel, a princess from Sidon on the coast to the north, and he served Baal.

Baal was worshiped as the god of storms and rain, who made the crops grow. So when Elijah stood before Ahab and said, "There will be neither dew nor rain in the years ahead unless I give the word" (1 Kings 17:1), he was striking at the very heart of Baal worship. On Carmel the question was settled in front of everyone: who is the true God?

Jesus knew this story well (Luke 4:25-26). On the mountain of the Transfiguration, Elijah appeared with Moses and talked with Jesus "of His departure, the exodus He was about to complete in Jerusalem" (Luke 9:31).

### How he prayed

**On Carmel, he prayed for God's glory.** Notice what Elijah asked for: "so that these people may know that You, the LORD, are God." He asked God to show Himself, so that the people would turn back. And notice how short his prayer was, after the prophets of Baal had shouted for hours. Jesus later warned against their kind of praying: "When you pray, do not keep piling up empty words as the pagans do. They think they will be heard because they say so much" (Matthew 6:7).

Jesus prayed with the same purpose. At the tomb of Lazarus He lifted His eyes and said, "Father, I give You thanks that You have heard Me... But I have spoken for the sake of the crowd standing around, so that they may believe that it was You who sent Me" (John 11:41-42). Like Elijah, He prayed aloud so that the people would know where the miracle came from.

Jesus also showed what Elijah's fire was not for. When a Samaritan village refused Him, James and John asked, "Lord, is it Your wish that we command fire to come down from heaven and burn them up?" (Luke 9:54). Jesus rebuked them. On Carmel the fire fell on the sacrifice, not on the crowd. Prayer is never a weapon against people.

**On the mountain top, he prayed with persistence and expectation.** God had already promised rain: "Go and appear before Ahab, and I will send rain on the land" (1 Kings 18:1). Yet Elijah bowed to the ground and prayed for what God had promised. Again and again the servant came back with nothing to report, and Elijah sent him back. E. M. Bounds saw what this meant:

> "In this instance it was expectant prayer, watchful of results, looking for the answer. Elijah had the answer in the small cloud like a man's hand. He had the inward assurance of the answer even before he had the rain."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

A cloud the size of a hand is not much. But Elijah knew what it meant.

### A man just like us

It would be easy to think of Elijah as someone far above ordinary believers. The apostle James says otherwise:

> "When a righteous person prays, that prayer is at work, and it can do a great deal. Elijah was a human being just like us. He prayed earnestly that it would not rain, and no rain fell on the land for three years and six months. Then he prayed again, and the sky gave rain and the land produced its crops." (James 5:16-18)

The very next chapter of 1 Kings proves it. When Jezebel threatened his life, Elijah ran into the wilderness, sat under a bush and "prayed that he might die" (1 Kings 19:4). God did not scold him. He fed him and spoke to him again. The power was never in Elijah. It was in the God he prayed to.

### Three key insights

1. **The best prayers ask God to make Himself known.** Elijah did not pray to win an argument, but "so that these people may know that You, the LORD, are God." When God's glory is the goal of our prayer, we pray as Jesus prayed.
2. **God's promises are not a reason to stop praying, but the ground we pray on.** God had promised rain, and Elijah prayed for rain. A promise tells us what to ask for and gives us the confidence to keep asking.
3. **Keep praying and keep looking until the small cloud appears.** Again and again the answer was "nothing," and Elijah kept praying. Persistent prayer is also watchful prayer: it expects God to act.

### For us today

Many places in the world know what drought means. Farmers in the Sahel of Africa, in the dry hills of India, or in the high plains of the Andes watch the sky and wait for the rains. Christians there are right to pray for rain. The God who sends rain is the God who hears.

But there is also a drought of the heart. Many of our families, churches and nations are like Israel on that day: "hobbling between two choices," saying nothing in reply. Elijah shows us how to pray for them. Pray that God will make Himself known. Pray on the ground of His promises. And when you see only a small sign, one person who begins to ask about God, a small group that starts to meet for prayer, do not despise it. It may be a cloud the size of a man's hand.

Perhaps you feel too ordinary to pray like this. Remember James: Elijah was a human being just like us. His God still lives, and hears you as He heard him.

### Try this

This week, pray like Elijah for one person or place that seems dry toward God.

- **Choose your Carmel.** Name one person, family, village or city that you long to see turn back to God.
- **Pray for God's glory.** Pray in the spirit of Elijah: ask God to answer so that this person or place may know that He is God.
- **Pray seven times.** Pray for this every day for seven days. Each day, "look toward the sea": watch for any small sign of God at work, and write it down.

### A closing prayer

LORD God of Elijah, You are the living God, and there is no other. You sent fire on the altar and rain on the dry land. Send Your fire on my cold heart, and Your rain on the dry places of my family, my church and my nation, so that people may know that You are God and turn back to You. When I see nothing, help me to keep praying and keep looking. Thank You that Your ear is open to me as it was to Elijah, through Jesus my Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Elijah's prayer on Carmel was very short. Why do you think it was so powerful?
- God had promised rain, yet Elijah still prayed for it. What promise of God could you pray back to Him this week?
- What is a "small cloud" you have seen recently: a sign that God may be beginning to answer?

## "Our Eyes Are Turned Toward You"

> "Our God, will You not judge them? We have no strength to face this great army that is coming against us. We have no idea what we should do, but our eyes are turned toward You." (2 Chronicles 20:12)

The news was terrible. "A great army is coming against you from beyond the Dead Sea," men told King Jehoshaphat (2 Chronicles 20:2). Three peoples, the Moabites, the Ammonites and some of the Meunites, had joined together for war. They had already crossed into Judah and were camped at En-gedi, an oasis on the western shore of the Dead Sea. They were not far from Jerusalem.

The Bible is honest about the king's first feeling: "Jehoshaphat was afraid." But it goes straight on: "and he resolved to seek the LORD. He proclaimed a fast for all Judah" (2 Chronicles 20:3).

From every town in Judah the people came. They filled the courtyard of the temple in Jerusalem. Then the king stood up in front of them all and prayed aloud. He began not with the army but with God:

> "LORD, God of our ancestors, are You not God in heaven? You rule over every kingdom of the nations. In Your hand are power and might, and no one is able to withstand You." (2 Chronicles 20:6)

He reminded God of what He had done: He had given this land to the descendants of Abraham, "Your friend." He reminded God of the promise made when the temple was built, that if disaster came, the people would stand before this house and cry out, and God would "hear and save us" (2 Chronicles 20:9). He told God plainly what the enemy was doing. And then he ended with the words of our key verse: no strength, no idea what to do, "but our eyes are turned toward You."

The chronicler adds a small detail that is easy to miss: "All Judah stood before the LORD, together with their little ones, their wives and their children" (2 Chronicles 20:13). Whole families stood there, waiting.

Then the Spirit of the LORD came on a Levite named Jahaziel, and he spoke out in the middle of the crowd:

> "Do not be afraid or lose heart because of this great army, for the battle is not yours; it belongs to God." (2 Chronicles 20:15)

The king bowed with his face to the ground, and all the people fell down and worshiped. The Levites stood up and praised the LORD "with a very loud voice" (2 Chronicles 20:19).

Early the next morning the army set out. But at the front Jehoshaphat placed not soldiers but singers. They walked toward the enemy singing, "Give the LORD thanks, for His loyal love lasts forever" (2 Chronicles 20:21). The moment they began to praise, the LORD threw the enemy armies into confusion, and they turned on each other and destroyed one another. When Judah reached the lookout point over the desert, there was no army left to fight.

### Who he was

Jehoshaphat was king of Judah, the southern kingdom, about 870 to 848 years before Christ. He lived at the same time as Elijah and King Ahab of the last chapter. He was not a perfect man. He made an unwise alliance with Ahab and nearly died in one of Ahab's wars (2 Chronicles 18). But he loved the LORD, sent teachers through the towns of Judah to teach God's law, and appointed honest judges.

His prayer stands on the foundation laid in chapter 9. When Solomon dedicated the temple, God had promised that "if My people who bear My name humble themselves and pray, seek My face and turn back from their evil ways, then I Myself will hear from heaven" (2 Chronicles 7:14). Jehoshaphat took God at His word. He brought the people to the very place where that promise had been given, and asked God to keep it.

### What he prayed

Look at the shape of the prayer. It moves in four steps.

**First, who God is.** "You rule over every kingdom of the nations." Before Jehoshaphat looked at the army, he looked at God. That changed the size of everything.

**Second, what God has done and promised.** He remembered the gift of the land and the promise made at the temple. Remembering the past gives courage for the present.

**Third, the honest problem.** He named the enemy and what they were doing. There is no pretending here.

**Fourth, an honest confession and a fixed gaze.** "We have no strength... We have no idea what we should do, but our eyes are turned toward You." That is the heart of the prayer. Jehoshaphat had no plan. He had a direction to look.

Jesus once put His disciples in the same place. A huge crowd had followed Him into the countryside, and there was nothing to eat. He asked Philip, "Where are we going to buy bread to feed these people?" John adds: "Jesus said this to test Philip; He already knew what He was going to do" (John 6:5-6). Philip had no idea what to do. Jesus did. That is the whole secret of Jehoshaphat's prayer: we do not know, but God does.

Jesus Himself prayed with His eyes turned toward His Father. Before He fed the crowds He looked up to heaven, and before His last great prayer "He raised His eyes toward heaven" (John 17:1). The letter to the Hebrews calls us to do the same: "Let us keep our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer who leads the way in faith and brings it to perfection" (Hebrews 12:2).

### Praise before the battle

The most surprising part of this story is the choir. Judah praised God before anything had changed. All they had was a promise spoken by one Levite in a crowd.

Charles Spurgeon wrote a short reading on God's word to Jehoshaphat, "The LORD will be with you" (2 Chronicles 20:17), and made it his own:

> "This was a great mercy for Jehoshaphat, for a great multitude had come out against him; and it will be a great mercy for me, for I have great need, and I have no might or wisdom. If the Lord be with me, it matters little who may desert me."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith*

And A. B. Simpson, the Canadian pastor who founded a great missionary movement, saw why God sometimes lets us run out of ideas:

> "God wants us “at our wit's end,” and then He will show His wisdom, love and power. How often we ask God to help, and then begin to count up the human probabilities!"
> A. B. Simpson, *Days of Heaven upon Earth*

"At our wit's end" means we have used up every idea we had. That is not a bad place to be, if our eyes are on God. As we saw in chapter 10, Jesus did the same at the tomb of Lazarus: He thanked His Father before Lazarus came out of the tomb (John 11:41).

### Three key insights

1. **Saying "we do not know what to do" is the beginning of real prayer, not the end of it.** Jehoshaphat's weakness did not stop his prayer; it shaped it. When we stop pretending to be strong, we are ready to look to God.
2. **When God's people seek Him together, even the children have a place.** The whole nation stood before the LORD, little ones included. Families and whole churches can wait on God side by side.
3. **Praise can go out ahead of the answer.** The singers went in front of the army before the battle was won. Thanking God for His loyal love, before we see what He will do, is one of the strongest forms of faith.

### For us today

Most of us will never face an invading army. But many believers do face things far too big for them. A church in northern Nigeria hears that armed men are coming. A family in a crowded city loses its only income. A pastor in a country where faith is forbidden is called in for questioning. A mother hears a diagnosis she cannot understand. In such moments the honest words come easily: "We have no strength. We have no idea what we should do."

Jehoshaphat teaches us what to do next. Gather the people. Fast and seek God, if you are able. Begin with who God is, not with the size of the problem. Remember what He has done before. Tell Him the problem plainly. And then turn your eyes toward Him and keep them there.

In many churches around the world, especially in Africa and Asia, believers already know how to praise God before the answer comes. They sing and dance in the middle of hardship, not because the trouble is small, but because their God is great. That is Jehoshaphat's choir, still singing.

### Try this

This week, when you face something too big for you, pray in Jehoshaphat's four steps.

- **Begin with God.** Before you mention the problem, tell God who He is. Read 2 Chronicles 20:6 aloud.
- **Remember.** Name one thing God has done for you or your family in the past.
- **Be honest.** Tell Him plainly, "I have no strength for this, and I do not know what to do."
- **Look and praise.** End with the key verse, then sing or say, "Give the LORD thanks, for His loyal love lasts forever," before you see the answer.

### A closing prayer

LORD, God of our ancestors, You are God in heaven, and You rule over every kingdom of the nations. You have been faithful to Your people in every age. Today I bring You the things that are too big for me. I have no strength to face them, and I do not know what to do. But my eyes are turned toward You. Teach me to wait for Your word, to stand with Your people, and to praise You before I see the victory, because the battle belongs to You. Through Jesus my Lord, Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What is something in your life right now where you honestly "have no idea what to do"?
- Why do you think Jehoshaphat began his prayer with who God is, and not with the army?
- What would it look like for you to praise God before you see the answer?

## Opening the Letter Before the Lord

> "Hezekiah took the letter from the messengers' hand and read it through. He went up to the house of the LORD and opened it out in the presence of the LORD. Then Hezekiah prayed before the LORD: 'LORD, God of Israel, enthroned above the cherubim: You, and no one else, are God of every kingdom on earth. It was You who made the heavens and the earth.'" (2 Kings 19:14-15)

Assyria was the great power of the world. From its capital, Nineveh, in what is now northern Iraq, its armies had swept across nation after nation. They were feared for their cruelty, and the northern kingdom of Israel had already fallen to them. Now Sennacherib, king of Assyria, had "marched against all the fortified cities of Judah and captured them" (2 Kings 18:13). Only Jerusalem was left.

Sennacherib sent his officials with a large army to the walls of the city. His chief officer stood outside and shouted up to the people on the wall, in their own language, so that everyone could understand. His message was simple: your king cannot save you, and neither can your God. "Do not let Hezekiah lead you to rely on the LORD" (2 Kings 18:30). Had the gods of any other nation saved their land? Why should the LORD be any different?

The people on the wall said nothing. King Hezekiah had ordered them, "Do not answer him" (2 Kings 18:36).

When Hezekiah heard the report, he tore his clothes as a sign of grief, put on rough sackcloth, and went into the house of the LORD. He sent his leaders to the prophet Isaiah with a request: "Lift up a prayer, then, for the remnant that is still here" (2 Kings 19:4). Isaiah sent back a word of comfort from God: do not be afraid.

But the threats did not stop. Sennacherib sent a letter. It said the same thing again, in writing this time: "Do not let your God, the one you rely on, deceive you" (2 Kings 19:10). Look at all the lands we have destroyed. Look at their gods. Do you really expect to escape?

Hezekiah read the letter through. Then he did something that believers have remembered ever since. He took the letter up to the temple, and there he opened it out before the LORD. It was as if he wanted God Himself to read every word.

And then he prayed. He began with who God is: enthroned above the cherubim, the God of every kingdom on earth, the maker of heaven and earth. He asked God to listen and to look: "Turn Your ear, LORD, and listen; open Your eyes, LORD, and look" (2 Kings 19:16). He admitted the facts: yes, the kings of Assyria had destroyed nations and burned their gods, "Those were never gods, only wood and stone shaped by human hands" (2 Kings 19:18). And he made his request:

> "So now, LORD our God, please rescue us from his power, so that every kingdom on earth will know that You, LORD, alone are God." (2 Kings 19:19)

Isaiah sent word: "I have heard your prayer" (2 Kings 19:20), says the LORD. The king of Assyria would not enter the city or shoot a single arrow into it. That night the angel of the LORD struck the Assyrian camp, and Sennacherib went home to Nineveh.

### Who he was

Hezekiah became king of Judah at the age of twenty-five and reigned twenty-nine years, about 715 to 686 years before Christ by one common reckoning (scholars differ by a few years). The Assyrian attack came about the year 701 before Christ. His father Ahaz had been one of the worst kings in Judah's history. But Hezekiah was different: "He trusted in the LORD, the God of Israel. Among all the kings of Judah there was no one like him" (2 Kings 18:5).

He cleansed the temple, broke down the places of idol worship, and even smashed the bronze snake that Moses had made, because the people had begun to burn incense to it (2 Kings 18:4). He called the whole nation to keep the Passover again. When many came who were not ritually clean, Hezekiah prayed for them: "May the LORD, who is good, pardon everyone who sets their heart on seeking God" (2 Chronicles 30:18-19). And "the LORD heard Hezekiah and healed the people" (2 Chronicles 30:20).

History outside the Bible remembers this crisis too. Sennacherib had his own account of the campaign carved on clay prisms, which can be seen in museums today. He boasts of the many towns of Judah that he captured, and says that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem like a bird in a cage. But he does not claim to have taken the city.

Hezekiah also appears in the family line of Jesus. Matthew lists him among the ancestors of the Messiah (Matthew 1:9-10). The king who prayed for Jerusalem was a forefather of the King who would weep over it.

### How he prayed

E. M. Bounds noticed what Hezekiah did first, both times the trouble came:

> "His very first impression was to turn to God by going to the “house of prayer.” God was in his thoughts, and prayer was the first thing to be done."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

Prayer was not the last thing he tried. It was the first thing he did. Notice three more things.

**He brought the real thing to God.** Hezekiah did not only tell God about the letter. He laid the letter itself before Him. His prayer was concrete, honest and specific.

**He looked at God before he looked at the enemy.** The letter said, "You yourself have heard how the kings of Assyria dealt with all the lands" (2 Kings 19:11). Hezekiah's prayer said, "You, and no one else, are God of every kingdom on earth." Big prayers come from a big view of God. Jesus began His own prayers in the same way. "Father, Lord of heaven and earth, I give You praise," He prayed (Matthew 11:25). And He taught us to begin, "Our Father in heaven, may Your name be kept holy" (Matthew 6:9).

**He prayed for God's name, not only for his own safety.** Sennacherib had insulted "the living God" (2 Kings 19:16). Hezekiah asked for rescue "so that every kingdom on earth will know that You, LORD, alone are God." Like Elijah on Carmel, he wanted the answer to make God known. That is what Jesus meant when He taught us to pray first for God's name to be honored and His kingdom to come.

### Three key insights

1. **Take the thing that frightens you and lay it before God.** Hezekiah did not hide the letter or argue with it. He opened it out before the LORD. God invites us to bring Him the actual papers, words and fears that threaten us.
2. **Big prayers begin with a big view of God.** Before Hezekiah asked for anything, he declared that God rules every kingdom and made heaven and earth. When God fills our sight, the enemy becomes the right size.
3. **Pray for the answer that will make God known.** Hezekiah asked for rescue so that all the nations would know the LORD. When our requests serve God's glory, we can ask with great boldness.

### For us today

What is your letter? For one believer it is a notice from the landlord. For another it is a hospital report, a court summons, a message from a government office, or school fees that cannot be paid. For a pastor in a land where churches are closed, it may be an official order to stop meeting. Some letters come on paper; others come as a message on a phone, or a cruel word that keeps repeating in the mind.

Hezekiah shows us what to do. Read it through; do not pretend it is not there. Then take it to God. If it is a paper, you may literally lay it out before Him as you pray. If it is a fear, name it aloud. Peter tells us to keep "throwing every worry you have onto Him, because you matter to Him" (1 Peter 5:7).

Catherine Booth, who with her husband William founded The Salvation Army, saw Hezekiah's way of praying as a model for carrying the needs of others too:

> "Do you go into your closet, and spread it before the Lord, as Hezekiah and Jeremiah and Hosea did? Do you look at it, and turn it over, and weep over it, and pray and cry, as Daniel and Paul did?"
> Catherine Booth, *Godliness*

(A "closet" in older English is a small private room.) Hezekiah prayed for his city. We can open before God not only our own letters, but the needs of our neighbors, our nation and the world.

### Try this

This week, practice Hezekiah's way of praying.

- **Find your letter.** Choose one thing that worries you: a bill, a report, a message. If it is on paper, take it with you to your place of prayer.
- **Open it before God.** Lay it out in front of you. Say, "LORD, look at this. Listen to what it says."
- **Begin with God's greatness.** Before you ask, pray the words of 2 Kings 19:15 aloud.
- **Ask for His glory.** End by asking God to act in a way that shows others that He alone is God.

### A closing prayer

LORD, God of Israel, You are enthroned above the cherubim. You alone are God of every kingdom on earth, and You made the heavens and the earth. I bring You the things that frighten me. I lay them open before You. Turn Your ear and listen; open Your eyes and look. Rescue me and those I love, not only for our sake, but so that the people around us may know that You alone are God. Thank You that Jesus, the Son of David, now carries my burdens to You. In His name, Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- When trouble comes, is prayer the first thing you do or the last thing you try? What would help it come first?
- What "letter" in your life do you need to open before God?
- How might your prayers change if you asked, "How could this answer make God known?"

- Catherine Booth, *Godliness*, especially "Love"

## A Prayer from the Depths

> "When I was in trouble I cried out to the LORD, and He gave me an answer; from deep in Sheol I called for help, and You heard my voice." (Jonah 2:2)

God's word came to Jonah son of Amittai: "Get up, go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it, because their wickedness has come up before Me" (Jonah 1:2). Nineveh was a great city of Assyria (and later its capital), the cruel empire we met in the last chapter, far to the east.

Jonah got up. But he went west. He went down to the port of Joppa, paid his fare, and boarded a ship bound for Tarshish, a distant port at the far end of the Mediterranean Sea. Many think it was in Spain. He was running "away from the presence of the LORD" (Jonah 1:3).

Then the LORD hurled a great wind onto the sea. The storm was so violent that the ship was about to break apart. The sailors were terrified. Each cried out to his own god. And Jonah? He was down in the hold, fast asleep. The captain shook him awake: "How can you sleep? Get up, call out to your god!" (Jonah 1:6).

The sailors cast lots to find out who was to blame, and the lot fell on Jonah. He told them the truth: he worshiped the LORD, "who made the sea and the dry land" (Jonah 1:9), and he was running from Him. "Pick me up and hurl me into the sea," he said, "and the sea will calm down for you" (Jonah 1:12). The sailors rowed hard for shore, but could not reach it. At last they prayed to the LORD and threw Jonah into the sea, and the sea grew calm.

Then "the LORD appointed a great fish to swallow Jonah, and Jonah was inside the fish for three days and three nights" (Jonah 1:17).

There, in the dark, in the belly of the fish, the man who had run from God began to pray. "Then Jonah prayed to the LORD his God from inside the fish" (Jonah 2:1). He described where he had been:

> "You threw me into the depths, into the heart of the seas, and the current swirled around me; all Your breaking waves and rollers have gone over my head. I said, 'I have been driven away from Your sight; still, I will look again toward Your holy temple.' The waters closed over me up to my throat; the deep was all around me; seaweed was wrapped around my head. I went down to the roots of the mountains; the earth's bars closed behind me forever. But You brought my life up from the pit, LORD my God." (Jonah 2:3-6)

And he ended his prayer with a promise and a shout of faith:

> "As my life was ebbing away, I remembered the LORD, and my prayer came to You, into Your holy temple. Those who devote themselves to useless idols abandon their loyal love. But I will sacrifice to You with a song of thanksgiving; what I have vowed I will make good. It is the LORD who saves." (Jonah 2:7-9)

"Then the LORD spoke to the fish, and it vomited Jonah out onto dry land" (Jonah 2:10).

### Who he was

Jonah was a real prophet of the northern kingdom of Israel. Another book of the Bible tells us that he came from Gath-hepher, a village in Galilee, and that he spoke God's word in the days of King Jeroboam II (2 Kings 14:25), about 780 to 750 years before Christ. Gath-hepher is usually placed only a few miles from Nazareth, where Jesus would grow up.

Jonah did not run from God because he was afraid of Nineveh. He tells us the real reason later, after God had spared the city: "I knew that You are a God of grace and tender compassion, slow to grow angry, rich in faithful love" (Jonah 4:2). He did not want his enemies to receive mercy. The book ends with God gently asking him to care for that great city as God did (Jonah 4:11).

Jesus took up Jonah's story Himself. When people asked Him for a miraculous sign, He answered:

> "An evil, unfaithful generation goes looking for a sign, but the only sign it will be given is the sign of Jonah the prophet. Jonah spent three days and three nights inside the great sea creature, and in the same way the Son of Man will spend three days and three nights deep in the earth." (Matthew 12:39-40)

Jonah came up from the depths alive. Jesus would come up from the grave. And He added, "Yet here is something greater than Jonah" (Matthew 12:41).

### How he prayed

**He prayed from the worst possible place.** Jonah prayed from "deep in Sheol," a Hebrew word for the place of the dead. He felt as good as buried. And it was his own fault. Yet his prayer reached God's "holy temple." E. M. Bounds wrote:

> "Prayer brought God to the rescue of unfaithful Jonah, despite his sin of fleeing from duty, and God could not deny his prayer. Nothing is too hard for prayer because nothing is too hard for God."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

**He prayed with words he already knew.** Read Jonah's prayer beside the Psalms and you will hear echoes everywhere. "When I was in trouble I cried out to the LORD, and He gave me an answer" is also the first line of Psalm 120. "All Your breaking waves and rollers have gone over my head" is found in Psalm 42:7. Many think Jonah had the psalms of the temple in his memory, and in the dark they became his own prayer.

Jesus prayed the psalms in His darkest hour too. On the cross He died praying Psalm 31: "Father, I place My spirit in Your hands" (Luke 23:46). In chapter 16 we will hear Him pray the first line of Psalm 22 as well.

**He gave thanks before he was free.** Jonah's prayer speaks as if the rescue has already happened: "You brought my life up from the pit." Yet he was still inside the fish. He promised to offer thanks and ended with a great cry of faith: "It is the LORD who saves." Hundreds of years later an angel told Joseph to call Mary's child Jesus, a name that means "the LORD saves," "because He is the one who will save His people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21).

J. C. Ryle, an English bishop, saw comfort for every troubled believer in Jonah's words about the waves:

> "Are we ever tempted to cry with Jonah, “The floods compass me about: all thy billows and thy waves pass over me” (Jonah 2:3)? Let us remember they are his billows. Let us wait patiently."
> J. C. Ryle, *Expository Thoughts on Matthew*

Even the waves that crash over us belong to God.

### Three key insights

1. **No place is too deep for prayer to reach God.** Jonah prayed from the belly of a fish in the depths of the sea, and his prayer came into God's holy temple. No prison, hospital bed or dark place of the heart is beyond the reach of prayer.
2. **God hears the prayers of those who have run from Him.** Jonah was in trouble because he disobeyed, yet God listened and rescued him. If you have run from God, you do not need to clean yourself up first. Call to Him from where you are.
3. **Scripture stored in the heart becomes prayer in the dark.** Jonah had no Bible in the fish, but he had the psalms in his memory. What we learn in quiet days becomes our language in hard days.

### For us today

Many believers today pray from places as dark as Jonah's. Christians in prison for their faith in North Korea, Eritrea or Iran pray in cells with no light and no Bible. A woman in a refugee camp prays with no home to return to. A man who has wasted his life prays for the first time in years, sure that God cannot want him back. Jonah's prayer says to each of them: the LORD hears from the depths.

Some of us are like Jonah in another way. We know what God wants, and we are going in the opposite direction. We may still be in church every week, while our hearts are on the ship to Tarshish. The storm in our life may be God's mercy, calling us back. It is never too late to pray, "LORD, I am in trouble, and I am calling to You."

Jonah also shows why it matters to fill our memory with Scripture. Believers in persecuted lands often learn whole psalms by heart, because a printed Bible can be taken away but a memorized one cannot.

### Try this

This week, learn to pray from the depths.

- **Memorize one psalm verse.** Choose Psalm 120:1 or Jonah 2:2. Say it every morning until you know it by heart.
- **Name your depths.** Tell God honestly where you are: in trouble, afraid, or running from Him.
- **Turn back.** If you have been running from something God asked of you, tell Him so, and take one step back toward it.
- **End with thanks.** Close each prayer this week with Jonah's words: "It is the LORD who saves."

### A closing prayer

LORD my God, You hear from the depths. When I was running from You, You did not let me go. When the waters closed over my head, You were there. I call to You now from the place where I am. Bring my life up from the pit. Fill my memory with Your words, so that in the dark I will know how to pray. Thank You for Jesus, the One greater than Jonah, who went down into death and rose again for me. I will sing to You with thanksgiving, for it is the LORD who saves. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Have you ever felt too far from God to pray? What does Jonah's prayer say to that feeling?
- Which verses of Scripture do you know by heart? Which ones would you like to learn next?
- Jonah ran because he did not want his enemies to receive mercy. Is there anyone you find it hard to want God to bless?

## Windows Open Toward Jerusalem

> "When Daniel learned that the decree had been signed, he went home to his upstairs room, where the windows opened toward Jerusalem. Three times a day he got down on his knees and prayed, giving thanks to his God, just as he had always done." (Daniel 6:10)

Daniel was an old man now.

Long ago, as a young man, he had been taken from Jerusalem to Babylon as a captive (Daniel 1:1-6). He never went home. He served one king after another for more than sixty years. If he was a teenager when he was taken, as many think, he was now past eighty.

Then Babylon fell to the Medes and Persians, and a new ruler named Darius took charge. (Scholars still discuss exactly who this Darius was.) Daniel was one of his three highest officials, and the king planned to put him over the whole kingdom.

That made the other officials jealous. They searched Daniel's work for some mistake or bribe, and found nothing. So they said, "We will never find any grounds for a charge against this Daniel unless it has to do with the law of his God" (Daniel 6:5).

They went to the king with a clever plan. For thirty days, no one in the kingdom could pray to any god or person except the king. Anyone who did would be thrown into a den of lions. Darius was flattered, and he signed it. Under the law of the Medes and Persians, once the king signed a decree, not even he could change it.

Daniel heard the news. He did not argue, and he did not hide. He went home and knelt by his open windows, as he did every day.

The men were watching. They "found Daniel praying and asking his God for help" (Daniel 6:11), and they ran to the king. Darius was deeply upset, and until sunset he tried everything to save Daniel. But the law could not be changed. Daniel was thrown to the lions, a stone was rolled over the opening, and the king sealed it with his own ring.

Darius did not eat or sleep that night. At the first light of morning he hurried to the den and called out in an anguished voice, "Daniel, servant of the living God! Has your God, whom you faithfully serve, been able to rescue you from the lions?" (Daniel 6:20).

And from the dark came the old man's voice: "My God sent His angel and shut the lions' mouths, and they have not hurt me" (Daniel 6:22).

### Why the windows faced Jerusalem

Look again at the last words of our key verse: "just as he had always done." Daniel did not begin to pray when the danger came. He simply did not stop.

Why did he face Jerusalem? About four hundred years earlier, at the opening of the temple, King Solomon had prayed for a day when God's people would be taken away as captives. If they turned back to God in that far land, Solomon asked, and prayed "facing their land, which You gave to their ancestors, the city You chose, and the house I built for Your name," then "hear in heaven, Your dwelling place, their prayer and their plea" (1 Kings 8:48-49). Daniel was praying Solomon's prayer back to God.

But think what he was facing. Jerusalem lay in ruins. The temple had been burned to the ground. Daniel knelt every day toward a heap of broken stones, because God had made a promise about that place, and Daniel believed it.

Three times a day was an old pattern too. David had written, "At evening, at morning and at noon I pour out my troubles and groan aloud, and He hears me" (Psalm 55:17). Faithful Jews still kept set hours of prayer in the time of Jesus (Acts 3:1).

E. M. Bounds saw the secret of the lions' den in Daniel's ordinary days:

> "Do not forget that this was the regular habit of this man of God."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

### A prayer of confession

In the first year of Darius, Daniel was reading the book of the prophet Jeremiah. There he found God's promise that the exile would last seventy years (Jeremiah 29:10). Daniel counted. The seventy years were almost over.

Daniel did not sit back and wait. "So I turned to the Lord God, seeking Him in prayer and pleading for mercy, with fasting, in sackcloth and ashes" (Daniel 9:3). Sackcloth was rough cloth worn in grief. God's promise did not make Daniel's prayer unnecessary. It gave him something to pray.

His prayer in Daniel 9 is one of the great confessions of the Bible. Notice the little word "we": "We have sinned and done wrong; we have been wicked and have rebelled" (Daniel 9:5). He does not point at his people. He kneels among them. And he asks for nothing on the basis of his own goodness:

> "Turn Your ear to us, my God, and hear... We do not bring our pleas to You because we are righteous, but because of Your great mercy. Lord, listen! Lord, forgive! Lord, take note and act!" (Daniel 9:18-19)

The American evangelist D. L. Moody was struck by this:

> "Daniel, as we have seen, confessed his sins and those of his people. Yet there is nothing recorded against Daniel. He was one of the best men then on the face of the earth, yet was his confession of sin one of the deepest and most humble on record."
> D. L. Moody, *Prevailing Prayer*

While Daniel was still praying, the angel Gabriel came to him "about the hour of the evening offering" (Daniel 9:21). The temple had offered nothing for many years, but Daniel still kept the hour. And Gabriel told him, "As soon as you began to plead, an answer was given, and I have come to tell it to you, for you are greatly loved" (Daniel 9:23).

### Where Jesus picks it up

Jesus grew up among people who prayed toward Jerusalem, yet He told a Samaritan woman something new: "An hour is coming when your worship of the Father will not be on this mountain, nor in Jerusalem either" (John 4:21). Jesus Himself is the new meeting place between God and His people, the true temple, as we saw in chapter 9. Today we do not need to face any city. We pray through Jesus.

Christians have also noticed how Daniel's story points ahead: a stone sealed over the den, a stone sealed over the tomb, and early in the morning the one left for dead found alive. Many think it was Daniel's book, too, that gave Jesus the name He used most often for Himself, "the Son of Man." In a vision Daniel saw "one like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven" (Daniel 7:13).

### Three key insights

1. **The habit of prayer we build in quiet days carries us through dangerous ones.** Daniel did not make a brave decision on the day of the decree. He made it years before, day after day, at his window. When the test came, he simply did "just as he had always done."
2. **We can pray toward a promise even when we are looking at ruins.** Daniel faced a city that lay broken, because God had spoken about it. Faith does not wait until things look better. It kneels toward what God has said.
3. **Honest confession comes before bold requests.** Daniel said "we," not "they," and he asked on the ground of God's mercy, not his own goodness. Heaven answered at once, and called him "greatly loved."

### For us today

Daniel lived his whole working life as a foreigner, far from home, serving a government that did not share his faith. Many readers of this book know that life. Some work abroad to support their families, or in offices where no one else prays. Daniel shows that you can serve faithfully and honestly in a foreign place without letting go of your God.

In many parts of the world today, praying is not safe. Believers in Asia, Africa and the Middle East meet in secret and can lose their jobs or their freedom for their faith, and still they pray. Pray for them, and learn from them.

And for those of us who live in freedom, the warning is quieter. No king has forbidden us to pray. We simply forget. Christians in many traditions have kept set hours of prayer, morning and evening and sometimes at midday, for exactly this reason. A fixed time is not a cage. It is a door you have agreed to open, whatever kind of day it is.

### Try this

This week, try Daniel's pattern of three short times of prayer each day.

- **Choose your three times.** Morning, midday and evening work for many people. Tie each one to something you already do, like a meal or a break.
- **Choose your "window."** Daniel turned toward the place of God's promise. Before you pray, open your Bible to one promise and keep your eyes on it as you begin.
- **Give thanks first.** Daniel prayed "giving thanks to his God," even on the day of the decree. Begin each time by thanking God for one thing.
- **Pray Daniel 9 with "we."** Once this week, confess the sins of your church, your town or your nation as your own, and ask for mercy.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You heard Daniel at his open window, and You shut the mouths of lions. Teach me to pray as he did, day after day, in easy times, so that I will not stop when hard times come. When I look at ruins, help me to look past them to Your promises. Forgive us, Lord, for we have sinned, and hear us not because we are good, but because of Your great mercy. Keep my brothers and sisters who pray in danger today. And thank You that, through Jesus, I can come to You from any place on earth. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Daniel prayed "just as he had always done." What prayer habits do you have now that would hold you steady in a crisis?
- What "ruins" are you looking at today, in your life, family or country? What promise of God can you face toward?
- Why do you think Daniel said "we have sinned" when there is nothing recorded against him?

## Arrow Prayers and Long Fasts

> "The king said to me, 'What is it you want?' So I prayed to the God of heaven, and I said to the king, 'If it pleases the king, and if your servant has found favor with you, send me to Judah, to the city where my ancestors are buried, so that I can rebuild it.'" (Nehemiah 2:4-5)

It was a spring day in the palace at Susa, the winter capital of the Persian kings, in what is now southwest Iran. The year was about 445 BC. King Artaxerxes sat at his meal, with the queen beside him. His cupbearer came forward with the wine.

The cupbearer was Nehemiah, a Jew whose family had probably stayed in the east after the exile. A cupbearer was much more than a waiter. He chose the king's wine, often tasted it first, and handed it to him. In a court full of plots, where a king could be poisoned, only a man he trusted completely could hold that cup.

But today Nehemiah could not hide his face. Four months earlier, in the month of Kislev, his brother Hanani had come from Judah with terrible news. "The wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates have been burned" (Nehemiah 1:3). Many of the exiles had gone home almost a hundred years before, but the city was still a ruin, open to any enemy.

Nehemiah writes, "I had never been sad in his presence before" (Nehemiah 2:1). It seems a servant was expected to look cheerful before the king. A sad face could look like a complaint, or even like a plot.

The king noticed at once. "Why do you look so sad? You are not sick. This can only be sadness of heart."

"Then I was terribly afraid," Nehemiah writes (Nehemiah 2:2). He answered carefully: "May the king live forever! Why should I not look sad, when the city where my ancestors are buried lies in ruins and its gates have been destroyed by fire?" (Nehemiah 2:3).

Then came the king's question, the moment Nehemiah had prayed about for four months: "What is it you want?"

There was no time to kneel. No time to leave the room. The king was waiting. And in the space between the question and the answer, Nehemiah did something no one else at that table could see. "So I prayed to the God of heaven." Then he spoke.

He asked for everything: leave to go, letters for safe travel, even timber from the king's forest for the gates and the wall. And the king said yes. Nehemiah tells us why: "the good hand of my God was on me" (Nehemiah 2:8).

### The long prayer behind the short one

That quick prayer at the king's table was not the beginning. It was the end of a long road.

When the news first came, Nehemiah says, "I sat down and wept. For some days I mourned, and I kept fasting and praying before the God of heaven" (Nehemiah 1:4). Fasting means going without food for a time in order to give yourself to prayer. The news came in Kislev, and he spoke to the king in Nisan, about four months later. His own words say he prayed "day and night" (Nehemiah 1:6).

The prayer he wrote down in chapter 1 shows how he prayed. E. M. Bounds saw a pattern in it:

> "The prayer is recorded in Nehemiah 1, and is a model after which to pattern our prayers. He begins with adoration, makes confession of the sins of his nation, pleads the promises of God, mentions former mercies, and begs for pardoning mercy."
> E. M. Bounds, *Prayer and Praying Men*

Look at the steps. He begins with who God is: "the great and awesome God, who keeps the covenant and faithful love" (Nehemiah 1:5). He confesses, like Daniel, using "we": "I and my father's family have sinned" (Nehemiah 1:6). He reminds God of His own word to Moses, that if His scattered people returned to Him, He would gather them again (Nehemiah 1:8-9). And at the very end, he makes one specific request:

> "Give Your servant success today, and grant him mercy in the sight of this man." (Nehemiah 1:11)

"This man" was the most powerful king on earth. To Nehemiah, he was simply "this man." The God of heaven was greater.

### Arrow prayers

Christians have long called a prayer like Nehemiah's at the table an "arrow prayer": a short prayer sent up to God in a moment, silently, in the middle of life. You do not need a quiet room or closed eyes. You only need to turn your heart toward God.

Nehemiah kept praying this way for the rest of his story. When enemies mocked the builders, he prayed, "Hear, our God, for we are despised!" (Nehemiah 4:4). When they plotted to attack, "we prayed to our God and posted a guard day and night" (Nehemiah 4:9). He prayed and he worked, and he saw no conflict between the two. When they tried to frighten him, he prayed in a few words: "But now, O God, strengthen my hands" (Nehemiah 6:9). Again and again he wrote, "Remember me, my God, for good" (Nehemiah 5:19). The wall was finished in fifty-two days (Nehemiah 6:15).

But notice the order. Nehemiah could shoot an arrow prayer at the king's table because he had already spent months on his knees. The short prayer flew far because the long prayer had made the bow strong.

### Where Jesus picks it up

Jesus prayed both ways too. He spent forty days fasting in the wilderness (Matthew 4:2), and He spent a whole night in prayer before choosing the twelve apostles (Luke 6:12). But He also prayed short prayers in the middle of a crowd. When His soul was troubled before the cross, He prayed in a few words, "Father, glorify Your name" (John 12:28).

And like Nehemiah, Jesus wept over Jerusalem (Luke 19:41), as we saw in chapter 1. Nehemiah wept because the city's walls were broken. Jesus wept because its people did not know "the things that lead to peace" (Luke 19:42), and He went on to die for them. Even Nehemiah's prayer, "Remember me," was prayed to Jesus by a dying thief on a cross: "Jesus, remember me when You come in Your kingdom" (Luke 23:42). And Jesus answered him at once.

### Three key insights

1. **A short prayer in the moment is real prayer, and God hears it.** Nehemiah had only seconds between the king's question and his answer. That was enough time to turn to the God of heaven, and God answered through the king's yes.
2. **Long, hidden prayer prepares us for the sudden moment.** Nehemiah's arrow prayer was the end of four months of fasting and praying day and night. The time we spend with God in quiet days is what makes us ready when the door opens.
3. **Prayer and work belong together.** Nehemiah prayed and posted a guard, prayed and picked up his tools. He did not use prayer as a reason to do nothing, and he did not work as though God were not there.

### For us today

Most of us cannot leave the room when we need God. A mother is cooking with a baby on her hip when bad news comes. A trader in a busy market faces a dishonest customer. A student sits down for an exam. A nurse walks into a room where a patient is dying. In each of those moments, you can do what Nehemiah did. You can send one silent sentence to the God of heaven before you speak.

Paul wrote, "Never stop praying" (1 Thessalonians 5:17). For people with crowded homes and long working days, arrow prayers are one of the main ways that command is kept. Many believers through history have prayed short sentences again and again through the day, in the field, at the loom, on the road. Some traditions use one short prayer, such as "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me," repeated quietly through the hours. The words matter less than the turning of the heart.

But do not let the arrows replace the bow. Nehemiah also wept, fasted and prayed for months. There are things in our families, churches and nations that need that kind of prayer: a broken city, a wall that has been down for many years. Fasting is still practiced by Christians across Africa, Asia and Latin America, and in many churches in the West. It is not a way to make God do what we want. It is a way of saying, "This matters more to me than my next meal."

### Try this

This week, practice both kinds of prayer.

- **Choose one burden.** Pick one broken "wall" that grieves you: a person, a church, a place. Write it down.
- **Pray long once.** Set aside one longer time this week, perhaps with a fast from one meal if your health allows. Pray through Nehemiah's pattern: praise God, confess, hold on to a promise, and make one clear request.
- **Pray short often.** Before each conversation that matters, send a silent arrow: "God of heaven, help me now."
- **Notice the answers.** At the end of the week, write down any moment when you saw "the good hand of my God" on you.

### A closing prayer

God of heaven, You are great and awesome, and You keep Your covenant of love. Give me a heart that weeps over what grieves You, as Nehemiah wept for Jerusalem and as Jesus wept over it. Teach me to wait before You in long and hidden prayer, and to turn to You in a moment when I have no time at all. When I stand before powerful people, remind me that You are greater. Strengthen my hands for the work You give me. Remember me, my God, for good. In Jesus' name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- When did you last pray a quick prayer in the middle of a hard moment? What happened?
- Nehemiah prayed for four months before the door opened. What are you praying for that may need that kind of patience?
- Why do you think prayer and hard work belong together, and not against each other?

- Charles H. Spurgeon, *Mighty Power in Prayer*, especially "Pray Without Ceasing"

## "How Long, LORD?" Honest Prayer

> "How long, LORD? Will You go on forgetting me for good? How long will You turn Your face away from me?" (Psalm 13:1)

We do not know what trouble David was in when he wrote Psalm 13. The psalm does not tell us. He spent years running for his life from King Saul, hiding in the hills and sleeping in caves. Later, as king, he fled from his own son Absalom. Any of those nights could have produced this prayer. Perhaps it is better that we do not know. It means the psalm can belong to anyone.

Listen to how it begins. There is no polite greeting, no "Dear Lord, thank You for this day." It starts with a cry:

"How long, LORD?"

Then he says it again. And again. And again. Four times in two verses:

> "How long, LORD? Will You go on forgetting me for good? How long will You turn Your face away from me? How long must I keep turning plans over in my mind, with grief in my heart all day long? How long will my enemy stand over me in triumph?" (Psalm 13:1-2)

Picture a man lying awake at night, turning plans over and over in his mind, finding no way out. The days have become weeks. Everyone around him seems to be winning. And the worst part is not the enemy. The worst part is that God seems to have looked away.

This is a strong thing to say to God. "Will You go on forgetting me?" David knew, as every believer knows, that God does not really forget His people. But that is how it felt, and he did not pretend otherwise.

Then the prayer turns. He stops describing his pain and starts asking:

> "Turn and look, LORD my God, and answer me! Bring the light back to my eyes, or I will fall asleep in death." (Psalm 13:3)

And then, without warning, comes a third turn. Nothing outside him has changed. The enemy is still there. No answer has come. But David says:

> "But I trust in Your loyal love; my heart will be glad because You save. I will sing to the LORD, because He has been so generous to me." (Psalm 13:5-6)

The whole psalm takes less than a minute to read. In that minute, a man walks from "How long?" to "I will sing."

### Prayers of lament

A prayer like this is called a lament. To lament means to cry out in grief. The psalms are full of them. Scholars count more laments than any other kind of psalm, perhaps a third of the whole book. That alone should tell us something. The prayer book God gave His people is not full of smiling faces. It is full of tears.

"LORD, why do You keep so far away? Why do You hide Yourself when trouble comes?" (Psalm 10:1). "Wake up, Lord! Why are You sleeping?" (Psalm 44:23). "Day and night I have fed on my tears, while all day long people ask me, 'Where is your God?'" (Psalm 42:3). One psalm, Psalm 88, never reaches the bright turn at all. It ends with the words, "My one companion is the dark" (Psalm 88:18). Even that prayer is in the Bible.

Most laments follow the path of Psalm 13. First comes the complaint: the honest telling of what hurts. Then the request: "Turn and look, LORD my God, and answer me!" Then, very often, a turn to trust: "But I trust in Your loyal love." Notice that the trust does not come first. David does not hide his pain behind a brave smile. He brings it all out, and then, with the pain still there, he chooses to lean on God's love.

E. M. Bounds wrote a whole chapter on prayer and trouble. He saw how closely the two belong together:

> "Trouble often drives men to God in prayer, while prayer is but the voice of men in trouble."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Essentials of Prayer*

### Why honest prayer is faithful prayer

Some believers feel that it is wrong to complain to God. They think faith means always sounding happy. But look again at Psalm 13. David does not complain *about* God to other people. He complains *to* God. That is the difference between grumbling and lament. Grumbling walks away from God. Lament runs toward Him, even with angry, tearful words, because it still believes He is the only one who can help.

Another psalm puts it simply: "I spill out my complaint in front of Him; I tell Him all about my distress" (Psalm 142:2). You cannot spill out what you are still hiding.

Samuel Rutherford, a Scottish pastor of the 1600s who was banished from his church for a time and wrote many letters of comfort, knew how hard faith can be in the dark:

> "I find it hard work to believe when the course of providence goeth cross-wise to our faith, and when misted souls in a dark night cannot know east by west, and our sea-compass seemeth to fail us. Every man is a believer in daylight: a fair day seemeth to be made all of faith and hope."
> Samuel Rutherford, *Letters of Samuel Rutherford*

"Every man is a believer in daylight." The laments are prayers for the night, when our compass seems to fail. They give us words when we have none.

### Where Jesus picks it up

Jesus prayed the laments of His people. In Gethsemane He told His friends, "My soul is filled with grief, grief enough to die" (Mark 14:34). Many hear in those words an echo of Psalm 42: "Why are you bent so low, my soul?" (Psalm 42:5). And on the cross, at about three in the afternoon, Jesus cried out with a loud voice the first line of Psalm 22: "My God, My God, why have You abandoned Me?" (Matthew 27:46).

The Son of God, in His darkest hour, did not invent a new prayer. He prayed a lament He had known since childhood. In those days people often named a psalm by its first line, so many believe Jesus had the whole psalm in mind. Psalm 22 begins in darkness, but it ends with praise and with "every clan of every nation" turning back to the LORD (Psalm 22:27). It ends, "He has done this!" (Psalm 22:31).

So when you pray "How long?" you are not praying alone. Your Savior has prayed in the dark before you, and so we have a high priest who can "share the feeling of our weaknesses" (Hebrews 4:15).

### Three key insights

1. **God invites us to bring Him our real feelings, even our complaints.** A great many of the psalms are laments. God put these prayers in His book so that His people would never think tears and questions are too much for Him.
2. **Lament is not unbelief; it is faith that refuses to walk away.** Grumbling complains about God to others. Lament complains to God Himself, because it still trusts that He alone can help.
3. **Jesus Himself prayed the laments, so we never pray them alone.** On the cross He cried the first words of Psalm 22. When we pray "How long?" we pray beside the Son who knows the dark from the inside.

### For us today

Many readers of this book are living in a long "How long?" right now. A mother has buried a child. A young man has looked for work for years. A family has fled from war and lives in a camp. A believer has been in prison for the name of Jesus, and his church has prayed for his release for a long time. A widow waits for justice that does not come. To all of them the psalms say: you may tell God exactly how it feels.

In some churches, we sing only happy songs. That is a gift when life is good. But in many parts of the world, especially where believers suffer, Christians have kept the practice of lament alive. They sing their grief to God, together, and find that it does not destroy their faith. It deepens it. Every church needs room for both "I will sing" and "How long?"

And if you are not in the dark yourself, someone near you is. Lament can also be a prayer we pray *with* others. Sit with a grieving friend. Pray their "How long?" for them when they cannot pray it themselves.

### Try this

This week, write your own lament, using Psalm 13 as your guide.

- **Complain honestly.** Begin with "How long, LORD?" and tell God plainly what hurts. Do not soften it.
- **Ask clearly.** Write a sentence like "Turn and look, LORD my God, and answer me!" Tell God what you need Him to do.
- **Choose trust.** End with "But I trust in Your loyal love," and name one way God has been good to you before.
- **Pray a psalm with Jesus.** Read Psalm 22 slowly from beginning to end, remembering that Jesus prayed it on the cross.

### A closing prayer

LORD, You know the things that make me cry, "How long?" Some nights I feel that You have forgotten me, and I do not understand Your ways. Thank You that I may tell You so. Turn and look at me, LORD my God, and answer me. Bring the light back to my eyes. Thank You, Lord Jesus, that You prayed in the dark before me, and that You know my weakness from the inside. Even now, I choose to trust in Your loyal love. My heart will be glad, because You save. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Have you ever felt that it was wrong to tell God how you really feel? Where did that idea come from?
- What is the difference between grumbling about God and lamenting to God?
- How does it change your own prayers in the dark to know that Jesus prayed Psalm 22 on the cross?

## Though the Fig Tree Does Not Flower

> "Though the fig tree does not flower and the vines bear no grapes, though the olive harvest fails and the fields yield nothing to eat, though the sheep vanish from the pen and the stalls hold no cattle, yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will find joy in God, my Savior." (Habakkuk 3:17-18)

Habakkuk was a prophet in Judah, probably a few years before the armies of Babylon came. We know almost nothing about his life, but his little book is unusual. Most prophets speak to the people for God. Habakkuk spends most of his book speaking to God for himself.

He begins with a complaint, and it sounds like the psalms we read in the last chapter:

> "How long, LORD, must I cry for help while You do not listen? I cry out to You, 'Violence!' but You do not save." (Habakkuk 1:2)

All around him he saw violence, quarrels and twisted justice. He had prayed, and nothing had changed.

Then God answered, and the answer was worse than the silence. "For I am raising up the Chaldeans, that fierce and reckless nation" (Habakkuk 1:6). The Chaldeans were the Babylonians. God was going to judge His people by sending an army more cruel than anyone in Judah.

Habakkuk could not understand it. He prayed again: "Your eyes are too pure to look on evil... Why then do You look on the treacherous, and keep silent while the wicked swallow up those more righteous than they?" (Habakkuk 1:13).

And then he did something wise. He did not walk away. He waited.

> "I will stand at my watchpost and take my place on the rampart. I will look out to learn what He will say to me, and how I should answer about my complaint." (Habakkuk 2:1)

A watchman stood on the city wall, looking out over the land. Habakkuk took that place in his heart, and watched for God.

God's second answer did not explain everything. But it gave him something to live by:

> "For the vision still waits for its appointed time; it hurries toward the end and will not prove false. Though it lingers, wait for it; it will certainly come and will not be late... but the righteous will live by their faithfulness." (Habakkuk 2:3-4)

God also showed him the end of the story: "For the earth will be as full of knowing the glory of the LORD as the sea is full of water" (Habakkuk 2:14).

The book ends with a prayer that was set to music and sung. He remembers what God had done long ago, and trembles. "LORD, renew Your work in our own time... in wrath, remember mercy" (Habakkuk 3:2). He knows the invasion is still coming. And then he sings the words of our key verse.

### What he gave up

To feel the force of Habakkuk's song, think about what those things meant. Figs, grapes and olives were the main crops of the land. The fields gave grain for bread. The sheep and cattle gave milk, meat, wool and leather. Together they were the whole living of a family. If all of them failed, there would be hunger.

So Habakkuk is saying, "Even if everything I depend on is taken away, I will still rejoice in God."

Notice what he does *not* say. He does not say, "I am happy that the crops have failed." He does not pretend. Only a verse before, he says, "My body trembled; my lips quivered at the sound... Yet I will wait quietly" (Habakkuk 3:16). The joy of verse 18 is not a feeling that pushes the fear away. It is a choice he makes while he is still shaking. He chooses to find his joy in God Himself, not in what God gives.

And then he adds, "The Lord GOD is my strength; He makes my feet as sure as a deer's and lets me walk on the heights" (Habakkuk 3:19). His ground has not become safer. God has made his feet sure.

### A song in the night

Charles Spurgeon wrote about the songs God gives to His people in hard times. He named Habakkuk's song among them:

> "No man can make a song in the night of himself; he may attempt it, but he will find that a song in the night must be divinely inspired."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Evening by Evening*

Samuel Rutherford, whom we met in the last chapter, saw in Habakkuk's watchpost a picture of what God's people do in dark times:

> "It is time now for the Lord's secret ones, who favour the dust of Zion, to cry, 'How long, Lord?' and to go up to their watch-tower, and to stay there, and not to come down until the vision speak; for it shall speak (Hab. ii. 3). In the mean time, the just shall live by faith. Let us wait on and not weary."
> Samuel Rutherford, *Letters of Samuel Rutherford*

"Let us wait on and not weary." That is Habakkuk's whole book in seven words.

### Where Jesus picks it up

The Old Testament ends with God's people still waiting. They came back from Babylon and rebuilt the temple and the walls, but the great promises had not yet come true. The last book of the prophets speaks of a day when "suddenly the Lord you are seeking will come to His temple" (Malachi 3:1). Then, as Jewish tradition and many Christians have said, no new prophet spoke for about four hundred years.

Through those years, the faithful kept watch. At the end of this book we will meet two of them, Simeon and Anna, still praying in the temple when the baby Jesus was carried in. They were standing at Habakkuk's watchpost.

Then, "when the time God had set was complete, God sent out His Son" (Galatians 4:4). The vision had waited for its appointed time, and it was not late. When Jesus began to preach in Galilee, Mark sums up His message in these words: "The appointed time has arrived in full" (Mark 1:15).

Habakkuk's words travel on into the New Testament. A young woman named Mary, expecting the Child, sang, "My spirit has found its delight in God my Savior" (Luke 1:47), the same joy Habakkuk had chosen. And "the righteous will live by their faithfulness" (Habakkuk 2:4) became one of the great sentences of the gospel. Paul quoted it as "The one who is righteous will live by faith" in Romans 1:17 and Galatians 3:11, and the letter to the Hebrews quoted it together with a promise: "the one who is coming will arrive and will not be slow in coming" (Hebrews 10:37).

### Three key insights

1. **When God's answer is hard to understand, we can still wait at the watchpost.** Habakkuk did not get every answer he wanted. But he did not walk away. He stayed at his post, watched and listened, and God spoke.
2. **The righteous live by faithfulness, trusting God's promise before it comes.** God told Habakkuk that the vision would come at its appointed time. Faith is not seeing the answer. It is holding on to God's word until the answer comes.
3. **We can rejoice in God Himself even when everything else is taken away.** Habakkuk's joy did not depend on figs or flocks. It rested on "God, my Savior," and that is a joy no failed harvest can steal.

### For us today

For many readers, Habakkuk 3:17 is not a picture. It is this year. Rains fail and crops wither. Prices rise until a family cannot buy food. A factory closes. A war takes the land. In such times, the question is not only "How long?" but "What do I have left?" Habakkuk answers: you still have God, and He is enough to sing about.

This is not an easy answer, and it is not a reason to stop working or asking for help. Habakkuk still cried out against injustice. But many believers who have lost everything have found, like him, that God's love was the one thing no one could take. They are often the ones who sing the loudest in church.

For those whose barns are full, the song asks a quiet question: is our joy in God, or in His gifts?

And all of us still stand at a watchpost. The vision of Habakkuk 2:14, an earth full of the knowledge of God's glory, has not yet fully come. God's people waited for the first coming of Jesus. Now we wait for Him to come again. The Bible's last prayer is a waiting prayer: "Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20).

### Try this

This week, practice Habakkuk's waiting faith.

- **Climb the watchpost.** Take one question you have brought to God for a long time. Sit quietly with it for ten minutes and say, "I will look out to learn what You will say to me."
- **Write your own "though."** Using verses 17-18 as a pattern, write: "Though ___ fails, and ___ is taken, yet I will rejoice in the LORD." Fill in the things you fear losing most.
- **Sing it.** Habakkuk's prayer was set to music. Sing a song of trust to God this week, alone or with others, even if your voice shakes.
- **Pray the last prayer.** End each day with the Bible's final prayer: "Come, Lord Jesus."

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You are from everlasting, my God, my Holy One. When I do not understand Your ways, help me not to walk away, but to stand at my watchpost and wait for You. Teach me to live by faith until Your promises come true. Though the harvest fails and the things I depend on are taken, I choose to rejoice in You, God, my Savior. Make my feet as sure as a deer's on the heights. Thank You that You sent Your Son at the right time, just as You promised. Keep me watching and waiting until He comes again. Amen.

## Conclusion: Passing the Torch

> "She never left the temple, but served God night and day with fasting and prayer." (Luke 2:37)

Some four hundred years after Malachi, the last of the Old Testament prophets, an old woman named Anna was still praying in the temple courts in Jerusalem.

Luke tells us she was eighty-four. She had been married for only seven years when her husband died, and she had been a widow ever since. For all those years "she never left the temple, but served God night and day with fasting and prayer" (Luke 2:37). She was waiting, as faithful Israelites had waited for centuries, for God to keep His promises.

An old man named Simeon was waiting too. Luke says he "longed for the day God would comfort Israel" (Luke 2:25), and the Holy Spirit had told him he would not die before he saw the Lord's Messiah.

Then one day a young couple from Galilee walked through the temple gate carrying a baby. They had come to offer the sacrifice the law required for a firstborn son. Simeon, led by the Spirit, took the child in his arms and prayed:

> "Master, now You are letting Your servant go in peace, as You promised, because my eyes have seen the salvation that comes from You, which You have prepared in full view of every people: a light of revelation for the nations, and the glory of Your people Israel." (Luke 2:29-32)

And Anna "came up just then, gave thanks to God, and went on speaking about Him to everyone who was waiting for God to set Jerusalem free" (Luke 2:38).

Two old people, praying in the temple. That is where the Old Testament's long prayer meeting ends, and where everything begins again.

### What the witnesses taught us

In this book we have listened to the prayers of the Old Testament. Here, in a few lines, is some of what they taught:

- Friends of God pray for others, boldly, because God is just and good (chapter 1, Abraham).
- God meets us in our fear, and we may hold Him to His promises (chapter 2, Jacob).
- An intercessor stands in the gap, and pleads God's own name and promises (chapter 3, Moses).
- The best prayer asks for God Himself, not only for His gifts (chapter 4, Moses).
- We can pour out our deepest pain to God exactly as it is (chapter 5, Hannah).
- Praying for others is a duty of love, even for those who reject us (chapter 6, Samuel).
- One thing is worth asking above all: to live near God (chapter 7, David).
- Real confession calls sin by its true name, and only God can make a heart new (chapter 8, David).
- God's people humble themselves first, and God hears all who turn back to Him (chapter 9, Solomon).
- Pray that God will make Himself known, and keep looking for the small cloud (chapter 10, Elijah).
- "We do not know what to do" is where real prayer begins (chapter 11, Jehoshaphat).
- Take what frightens you and lay it out before God (chapter 12, Hezekiah).
- No place is too deep for prayer to reach God (chapter 13, Jonah).
- The habit of prayer built in quiet days carries us through dangerous ones (chapter 14, Daniel).
- A one-breath prayer is real prayer, and long hidden prayer prepares us for it (chapter 15, Nehemiah).
- God invites our honest complaints; lament is faith that refuses to walk away (chapter 16, the psalms).
- We can rejoice in God Himself even when everything else is taken away (chapter 17, Habakkuk).

You will find every one of these lessons gathered by theme in Appendix 1, and every Scripture in this book listed in Bible order in Appendix 2.

### The prayers Jesus learned

Look again at the people in this book, and you will see a pattern. None of them was perfect. Abraham twice passed off his wife as his sister to save himself. Moses killed a man before God ever called him. Jonah sulked when God was kind to his enemies. And yet God heard them all.

That is the great lesson of the Old Testament's prayers: God listens to weak people who come to Him honestly. Their prayers were not powerful because they were good, but because God is good.

These are the prayers Jesus learned. He grew up singing David's psalms. He knew Hannah's song, which His mother echoed when she sang, "My soul declares the greatness of the Lord" (Luke 1:46). He prayed the words of Psalm 22 and Psalm 31 on the cross. He did not come to throw these prayers away. "I have not come to do away with them but to fulfill them," He said of the Law and the Prophets (Matthew 5:17).

And He fulfilled them in a way no one expected. Abraham pleaded for Sodom; Jesus wept over Jerusalem and died for it. Moses offered to be blotted out for his people; Jesus was cut off for us. David cried out for a clean heart; Jesus, who had no sin, carried ours. Jonah went down into the depths for three days; Jesus went down into death and rose on the third day. Every prayer in this book was waiting for Him.

### The torch is passed

The writer of Hebrews, after telling the stories of these same Old Testament believers, says something surprising:

> "Through their faith all of these were attested by God, and yet they did not receive the promise. God had in view something better for us: they were not to be made perfect apart from us." (Hebrews 11:39-40)

They prayed toward a promise they never saw. We live on the other side of it. The Messiah they longed for has come.

In **Volume 2, *Jesus, Teacher of Prayer***, we will follow that baby from the temple courts to the hills of Galilee. We will watch Him rise before dawn to pray, climb a mountain to pray all night, and teach His friends to say "Our Father." We will hear Him pray for Peter, for His church, in Gethsemane and on the cross. And we will see Him at the right hand of the Father, where He always lives to pray for us.

The prayers of the Old Testament were the prayers Jesus learned. In the next book, He teaches them to us.

### Your turn

Before you go on, take the prayers of this book and make them your own. Plead for someone the way Abraham did. Hold on to a promise the way Jacob did. Ask, with Moses, to see God's glory. Pour out your heart with Hannah. Pray Psalm 51 when you have sinned and Psalm 13 when God feels far away. Open your frightening letters before the Lord with Hezekiah, and kneel at your open window with Daniel. And when the fig tree does not flower, sing with Habakkuk.

You are not the first to pray these prayers. You join a long line of praying people that runs back to Abraham under the stars, and forward to every believer who will ever say, "Lord, teach us to pray."

### A closing prayer

LORD God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, thank You for the prayers of Your people in every age. Thank You that You heard them, weak and failing as they were, and that You hear me. Teach me to pray as they prayed: to plead for others, to hold on to Your promises, to long for You above all Your gifts, to confess my sin honestly, and to trust You when the fields are bare. Thank You for Jesus, who learned these prayers, fulfilled them, and now teaches them to me. Like Anna and Simeon, let me wait for You in faith, and rejoice when I see Your salvation. Amen.
