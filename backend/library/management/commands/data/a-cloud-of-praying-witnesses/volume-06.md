## Introduction: Where We Are in the Story

> "Father, I ask that all of them may be one. Just as You are in Me and I am in You, so may they too be in Us, so that the world may come to believe that You sent Me." (John 17:21)

On the night before He died, Jesus prayed that His followers would be one. In this book we come to the century when the church in the West broke apart.

In 1517 a German monk and teacher named Martin Luther wrote ninety-five points for debate against the sale of indulgences, papers that promised to shorten punishment for sin after death. Later tradition says he nailed them to a church door in Wittenberg; we know he sent them to his archbishop. He wanted to reform the church, not to leave it. But within a few years Europe was divided. Some followed Luther, some followed other reformers such as Zwingli and Calvin, some went further still, and many stayed with the church of Rome, which began its own renewal. Christians argued, preached, wrote and prayed against one another. Too often they also imprisoned, burned and drowned one another.

It is a painful story, and we will not pretend otherwise. Yet it is also a story of prayer. On every side of those divisions there were men and women who loved Christ, who longed for a purer and more living faith, and who learned to pray with new honesty and depth. Many of them would not have prayed beside one another. Today their books sit side by side on the shelves of believers around the world, and God has used all of them to teach His people to pray.

### Where we are in the series

This is the sixth volume of *A Cloud of Praying Witnesses*, a series that follows the people of God at prayer through the whole story of the church. The name comes from Hebrews 12:1, which pictures all the faithful who went before us as "so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us."

The first volumes listened to the prayers of the Old Testament, sat with Jesus, the Teacher of Prayer, and followed the first church, the martyrs and the desert fathers. Volume 5, *Seven Times a Day*, followed the monks, mystics and missionaries of a thousand years, and ended on the eve of the Reformation, with Wycliffe and Hus calling the church back to the word of God and Thomas à Kempis calling believers to a humble, inward life with Christ.

This volume follows what happened next, from about 1500 to the middle of the 1600s.

### The world of these witnesses

The printing press changed everything. For the first time, ordinary people could own a Bible in their own language: Luther's German New Testament appeared in 1522, William Tyndale's English one in 1526, and many others followed. Prayer books, hymn books and books on prayer spread in the same way. A farmer or a barber could now read what only scholars had read before.

It was also a violent age. Wars were fought between Catholic and Protestant rulers, and the Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648) left much of Germany in ruins. Kings decided what church their people would belong to, and those who would not conform were fined, exiled, imprisoned or killed. Ships from Spain and Portugal carried missionaries to Asia, Africa and the Americas, often on the same voyages as soldiers and traders, and the gospel came to many peoples mixed with conquest. Yet in places like Japan it took deep root, and believers there kept their faith for centuries under persecution.

The witnesses in this book come from both sides of the great divide, and from groups that both sides persecuted. We will meet Lutherans and Reformed, Anglicans and Puritans, Catholics and Anabaptists. Where they disagreed, and where churches still disagree, we will describe what they believed and practiced simply and fairly, and listen for what every believer can learn from them about prayer.

### What we will see

This volume has seventeen chapters, in four movements:

- **The reformers at prayer** (chapters 1 to 5). Martin Luther learns that the busier he is, the more he needs to pray, and teaches his barber a simple way to pray. John Calvin calls prayer the principal exercise of faith. Thomas Cranmer gives the English church a book of common prayer in its own language, and John Knox prays for his nation.
- **Renewal in the Catholic church** (chapters 6 to 9). Teresa of Ávila teaches that prayer is friendship with God. John of the Cross writes of the dark night, when God seems far away. Ignatius of Loyola teaches believers to search their hearts before God each day, and Francis de Sales teaches busy people how to pray.
- **Hidden and humble prayers** (chapters 10 to 13). The Christians of Japan keep praying in secret for two hundred and fifty years, most of them without a priest. Lancelot Andrewes spends hours each day with a little book of prayers that he wrote out by hand, George Herbert turns prayer into poetry, and Richard Sibbes comforts weak believers whose prayers feel like a bruised reed.
- **Prayer in hard places** (chapters 14 to 17). Richard Baxter, expecting to die, sets his mind on heaven. John Bunyan learns to pray in a Bedford jail. Brother Lawrence finds God among the pots and pans of a monastery kitchen. And the Anabaptist martyrs pray for their persecutors in the fire.

### How each chapter works

Every chapter in this series has the same shape:

- **A key verse** opens the chapter, the Scripture it rests on.
- **A story** sets the scene.
- **The teaching** looks at what the story shows us about prayer.
- **Voices from the past** quote men and women of prayer, often the witnesses themselves, word for word from their own books in the Ochorus library.
- **Three key insights** sum up what the chapter teaches.
- **For us today** brings the lesson into ordinary life, wherever in the world you live.
- **Try this** gives one simple practice for the coming week.
- **A closing prayer** and **Questions for reflection** help you respond, alone or with others.
- **Read more on Ochorus** points you to classic books where you can go deeper.

At the end of the book, two appendices gather all the key insights by theme and list every Scripture quoted, in Bible order, with the chapters where it appears.

### A word about these stories

Many of the people in this book wrote a great deal, and we can often let them speak for themselves. Some famous sayings, though, were put in their mouths later, and we will say so when that is the case. We will also try to be fair. Every church in this book did wrong to others in this age, and every church in this book also produced people of deep and humble prayer.

### How to read this book

One chapter a day will carry you through the book in seventeen days. With a small group, one or two chapters a week works well.

As you read, remember the prayer of Jesus at the top of this page. The believers in this book did not always live up to it. But their prayers can still draw us closer to Christ, and the closer we come to Him, the closer we come to one another.

## Too Busy Not to Pray

> "God is a shelter and a strength for us, a help always close at hand in times of distress." (Psalm 46:1)

In the autumn of 1517, in Wittenberg, a small university town in Saxony, a monk and Bible teacher named Martin Luther wrote out ninety-five statements for debate. They attacked the sale of indulgences, the papers that promised a shorter punishment for sin in exchange for money. On 31 October he sent them to the archbishop of Mainz. The story is often told that he also nailed them to the door of the Castle Church, where notices for debate were posted, though some historians doubt that part. Either way, printers soon copied his words, and within weeks they were being read across Germany. A quarrel had begun that would divide the Western church.

Thirteen years later, in the summer of 1530, Luther was living in a fortress. The emperor, Charles V, had called a great meeting of the German princes, called a diet, in the city of Augsburg. Luther had been declared an outlaw of the empire in 1521, so he could not safely go. His prince left him instead at the castle of Coburg, high on a hill, about four days' journey away. From there he wrote letters to his friend Philip Melanchthon, who was in Augsburg speaking for their churches.

These were hard months. Early in June, word came that Luther's father had died. Luther was often ill, with a buzzing and dizziness in his head that kept him from reading. In Augsburg, Melanchthon was anxious and afraid. Many feared war. On 25 June, the princes who followed Luther read out a statement of their faith before the emperor, now known as the Augsburg Confession. No one knew what would follow.

With Luther in the castle was his young secretary, Veit Dietrich. At the end of June, Dietrich wrote to Melanchthon about what he saw. Here is his letter as an old English translation gives it:

> "I cannot enough admire the extraordinary, cheerfulness, constancy, faith and hope of the man in these trying and vexatious times. He constantly feeds these gracious affections by a very diligent study of the Word of God. Then not a day passes in which he does not employ in prayer at least three of his very best hours. Once I happened to hear him at prayer. Gracious God! What spirit and what faith is there in his expressions! He petitions God with as much reverence as if he was in the divine presence, and yet with as firm a hope and confidence as he would address a father or a friend."
> Veit Dietrich, quoted in E. M. Bounds, *Purpose in Prayer*

Dietrich goes on to give some of the words he overheard. Luther was reminding God that the cause of the gospel was God's own cause, and he was pleading the promises of the Psalms "as if he were sure his petitions would be granted." Dietrich says that as he listened, his own soul seemed to catch fire.

### Who he was

Martin Luther was born in 1483 in Eisleben, in Germany. His father, Hans, worked in the copper mines and wanted his clever son to become a lawyer. But in July 1505, caught in a terrible thunderstorm, the young student cried out a vow to become a monk. Two weeks later he entered the monastery of the Augustinian friars in Erfurt.

He was a serious monk. He fasted, prayed the daily services and confessed his sins for hours. Yet he found no peace. He was sure that God was a judge whom he could never satisfy. Later his order sent him to teach the Bible in Wittenberg. There, as he studied the Psalms and Paul's letter to the Romans, he came to see that the righteousness of God is not only a standard that condemns us. It is a gift that God gives freely to all who trust in Christ. That discovery changed his life, and his prayers.

Luther went on to translate the Bible into German, write hymns and catechisms, marry a former nun named Katharina von Bora, and raise a large household. He died in 1546. He was not a gentle man. He could be coarse and harsh in argument, and some of his late writings, especially against the Jews, were cruel and have rightly grieved Christians ever since. But he was a man who prayed.

### How he prayed

Luther is remembered for saying: "I have so much to do that I shall spend the first three hours in prayer." No one has found these words in Luther's own writings. They may have grown out of Dietrich's letter, and they appear in many forms in later books; by the 1800s preachers such as Charles Spurgeon were repeating them to show that, as Spurgeon put it, "praying is a saving of time."

What we know from Dietrich is enough. In one of the busiest and most frightening seasons of his life, Luther did not give God what was left over. He gave Him "three of his very best hours," the hours of the day when his mind was freshest.

It had not always been so. Luther told his friends at table how, as a busy young monk, he would fall behind in the prayers the rules required. On Saturdays he locked himself in his cell to make up for the whole week. At last the work grew so heavy that he gave up even that. In those days prayer was a debt he owed. Later it became the place where a child spoke to his Father.

And it became his defense. In Volume 4 we heard Tertullian call prayer "the wall of faith." Luther used a picture very like it in his *Table Talk*:

> "Prayer is a strong wall, and a fort of the church; it is a godly Christian's weapon, which no man knoweth nor findeth, but only he who hath the spirit of grace and of prayer."
> Martin Luther, *Table Talk*

A few years before Coburg, he had put the same faith into a song. He took Psalm 46 and turned it into a hymn, "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott." Frederic Hedge's English version of 1853 begins: "A mighty fortress is our God, a bulwark never failing; our helper He amid the flood of mortal ills prevailing." (A bulwark is a strong defensive wall.) The psalm ends the same way: "The LORD of Armies is on our side; the God of Jacob is our high fortress" (Psalm 46:11).

### Three key insights

1. **When the danger is greatest, prayer is our safest fortress.** Luther could not go to Augsburg and could not stop the emperor. But he could go to God. The walls of Coburg did not protect the gospel; the God of Psalm 46 did.
2. **The busier the day ahead, the more it needs to begin with God.** Luther did not wait for a quiet season to pray. In his most crowded and anxious months he gave God his freshest hours first, and the rest of the work followed from there.
3. **God's promises are the firm ground we stand on when we pray.** Luther did not pray as if he hoped to change a stranger's mind. He reminded God of what God Himself had promised in the Psalms, and he trusted Him to keep His word.

### For us today

Few of us can spend three hours a day in prayer. A mother with small children in Lagos, a farmer in Bolivia at harvest time, a student in Seoul before exams, a worker in Manila who travels two hours each way to the factory: their days are already full. The lesson of Luther is not a number of hours. It is a choice about what comes first. When the day is crowded, prayer is not one more task that pushes the others out. It is the place where we hand the whole day to God.

Many believers today also know what it is to wait, like Luther in his castle, while others decide their future. Church leaders face courts. Families wait for news of loved ones in prison. Christians in some lands hear rumors of attacks. Psalm 46 was written for such days. The earth may shake and the waters roar, but God is "a help always close at hand in times of distress" (Psalm 46:1).

### Try this

This week, give God the best part of your day, not the leftovers.

- **Choose your best hour.** Notice when your mind is clearest, perhaps early morning, perhaps late at night, and give the first part of it to prayer before anything else.
- **Pray the fortress psalm.** Read Psalm 46 aloud each morning. Then tell God plainly what is troubling you today.
- **Stand on a promise.** Find one promise in the Psalms, such as Psalm 50:15, and pray it back to God: "You said this. I am trusting You to do it."
- **Sing when you are afraid.** Learn the first verse of "A Mighty Fortress" or another hymn of trust, and sing it when fear comes.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You are our shelter and our strength, a help always close at hand in times of distress. Thank You for Your servant Martin Luther, who brought his fears and his heavy work to You and found You faithful. Forgive me for giving You only the tired ends of my days. Teach me to come to You first, with reverence and with the confidence of a child. When the ground shakes beneath me, be my high fortress. Remind me of Your promises, and make me sure that You will keep them, through Jesus Christ, who fights for us. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Luther reminded God of what He had promised in the Psalms. Which promise of God could you pray back to Him this week?
- When your days are busiest, what usually happens to your prayer? What would it look like to give God your best hour instead of your last?
- What danger or worry in your life today could you bring to God, using the words of Psalm 46?

## A Simple Way to Pray

> "Instead, the instruction of the LORD is their delight, and they recite it quietly to themselves day and night." (Psalm 1:2)

In Wittenberg in the 1530s, one of the people who knew Martin Luther best was not a professor or a prince. He was a barber. His name was Peter Beskendorf, and he was a master in his trade. In those days a barber did more than cut hair and shave beards. He also pulled teeth, cleaned wounds and let blood, so he was a kind of village doctor as well. Peter had a shop in the town, and Luther counted him a good friend.

One day, Peter asked Luther a simple question: how should he pray? We do not know his exact words, or where he asked it. What we know is how Luther answered. He did not tell his friend to go and read the scholars. He sat down and wrote him a little book, which was printed in 1535. Its title, in English, is *A Simple Way to Pray*.

Luther was the most famous teacher in Germany, and the busiest. Yet in this little book he did not write as an expert. He wrote as one friend to another. He told Peter that he would simply describe what he himself did when he prayed.

He began by admitting that his own heart often grew cold. Work and worries crowded in, and he did not feel like praying at all. When that happened, he said, he would take his little book of psalms, go to his room, or to church if the people were gathered there, and begin to say quietly to himself the Ten Commandments, the Creed and the Lord's Prayer, word by word, as children do. If there was time, he added some words of Christ, or of Paul, or a psalm. Slowly his heart grew warm again.

He urged his friend to make prayer the first business of the morning and the last of the night. He warned him about a dangerous thought: that he would pray in an hour, once he had finished this job or that one. Such thoughts, Luther said, lead us away from prayer, and the hour never comes. And he used an example Peter would understand at once. A good barber must keep his eyes and his mind on the razor and the beard. If he chatters and looks around the room, he will cut his customer's nose, or even his throat. Prayer, too, needs the whole heart.

### A garland of four strands

Luther then showed Peter what to do with the words he knew by heart. Take one commandment, or one line of the Lord's Prayer or the Creed, he said, and make of it a garland, a crown woven from four strands, like a rope or a wreath of flowers.

The first strand is instruction: what is God teaching me here? The second is thanksgiving: what can I thank Him for? The third is confession: what sin does this show me? The fourth is prayer: what shall I ask?

Here is how it might sound with one line of the Lord's Prayer, "Give us today the bread we need" (Matthew 6:11). *Instruction:* God is the giver of every meal, and He wants me to trust Him for today. *Thanksgiving:* Father, thank You for the food on my table and the work of the farmers who grew it. *Confession:* forgive me for worrying about tomorrow, and for eating without thanking You. *Prayer:* give bread to my family, and to the hungry families in my town, and make me generous with what You have given me.

This is very close to what Psalm 1 describes. Those who delight in God's instruction "recite it quietly to themselves day and night" (Psalm 1:2). The Hebrew word speaks of murmuring under the breath, the way a person repeats words softly, again and again, to think about them. Luther did this with the plain words every child in his church learned. He never thought he had grown too old for them. In his *Table Talk* he describes the end of his own day:

> "And when I lay me down to rest, I pray the Lord's Prayer, and afterwards take hold on two or three sentences out of the Bible, and so betake myself to sleep, then I am well satisfied."
> Martin Luther, *Table Talk*

Luther also gave his friend a wise freedom. If, while you are praying, your heart is set on fire and good thoughts come, he said, let your list go and listen. The Holy Spirit Himself is teaching you, and one word from Him is better than a thousand of your prayers.

### A barber's prayer is not small

Peter may have felt that real prayer belonged to monks and preachers, not to a man who cut hair. Luther had already answered that fear in his *Large Catechism*. Because God commands every believer to pray, and promises to hear, a simple person may come with full confidence:

> "Therefore you should say: My prayer is as precious, holy, and pleasing to God as that of St. Paul or of the most holy saints."
> Martin Luther, *The Large Catechism*

Luther's reason is not that our prayers are good. It is that God's command and promise are good. He told his readers to come to God saying, in effect: I pray not because I am worthy, but because You told me to, and You promised to hear.

### When the barber fell

The story of this little book has a sad ending. In the spring of 1535, soon after the book appeared, Peter Beskendorf had too much to drink at a family meal, quarreled with his son-in-law, Dietrich Freyenhagen, and stabbed him to death. Luther and other friends pleaded for his life. He was spared the death penalty, but he was sent away from Wittenberg for good, and he lived out his days in exile in the town of Dessau. We are not told what passed between Peter and God in those years.

It would be easy to say: then what good was the book? But the story teaches the opposite. Peter was an ordinary sinner who needed God's mercy more than anyone guessed, and so are we. None of us knows when our own hour of temptation will come. That is exactly why we need to pray every morning and every night, "do not lead us into testing, but rescue us from the evil one" (Matthew 6:13). And Luther did not abandon his friend when he fell. He pleaded for him. And the little book lived on. For nearly five hundred years it has taught ordinary people to pray.

### Three key insights

1. **The simple words we learned as children can become a lifelong school of prayer.** Luther, the great teacher, still prayed the Lord's Prayer, the Commandments and the Creed every day. He never outgrew them, because God's word is deeper than our learning.
2. **Every truth God teaches us can become thanksgiving, confession and request.** Luther's four strands turn reading into praying. We hear what God says, thank Him for it, confess where we have failed, and ask for His help.
3. **God's command and promise, not our own worth, make our prayers welcome.** Because God commands us to pray and promises to hear, a barber's prayer, offered in Christ's name, is as precious to Him as the prayer of an apostle. Peter's fall reminds us that we all pray as sinners who live by mercy.

### For us today

Many believers around the world cannot read well, or own only one book, or none at all. Some live where Bibles are hard to find. Luther's way of prayer needs no library. The Lord's Prayer, the Ten Commandments and the Creed can be carried in the memory, in any language, through any day. A shopkeeper in Accra, a fisherman in Indonesia, a grandmother in a village in Peru can weave a garland of prayer while they work, from words they learned as children.

Luther's way also helps when prayer feels dry. You do not have to wait until you feel holy. Start with the words you know. Say them slowly. Let each one lead you to thanks, to confession and to asking. Often the heart grows warm on the way.

### Try this

This week, try Luther's simple way.

- **Begin with the words you know.** Each morning, say the Lord's Prayer slowly, one line at a time, before you pick up your phone or begin work.
- **Weave one garland a day.** Choose one line of the Lord's Prayer or one commandment. Ask: What does it teach? What can I thank God for? What must I confess? What shall I ask?
- **Murmur a verse at night.** Like Luther, end the day with the Lord's Prayer and "two or three sentences" of Scripture, such as Psalm 1:1-3.
- **Pray for someone who has fallen.** Think of a person whose sin has brought them shame, and ask God to have mercy on them.

### A closing prayer

Heavenly Father, You teach us to pray with simple words, and You hear the prayers of ordinary people. Thank You for Your servant Martin Luther, who took time to teach his barber, and for every plain word of Yours that I learned as a child. When my heart is cold, warm it with Your word. Teach me to hear what You say, to thank You, to confess my sins and to ask for Your help. Keep me in the hour of testing, and have mercy on all who have fallen. Let Your instruction be my delight, day and night, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Luther said the hour we promise to pray "later" often never comes. What usually pushes prayer out of your day?
- Which of the four strands, instruction, thanksgiving, confession or prayer, comes most easily to you, and which do you most often leave out?
- What does Peter Beskendorf's story teach you about your own need for God's mercy, and about how to treat a believer who falls?

## The Principal Exercise of Faith

> "So let us, with confidence, approach the throne of grace, to receive mercy and to find grace that brings help just when we need it." (Hebrews 4:16)

In the summer of 1536, a young Frenchman stopped for one night in the city of Geneva. His name was John Calvin. He was twenty-seven years old, a quiet scholar who had trained in law and loved books more than crowds. He was also a refugee. Because of his Protestant faith he could no longer live safely in France, and he was on his way to the city of Strasbourg, where he hoped to spend his life in peaceful study. War had closed the direct road, so he went a longer way, through Geneva.

Geneva was a city in turmoil. Only a few months earlier its people had voted to follow the Reformation, but the city was divided and unsettled. The leading preacher there was a fiery older Frenchman named William Farel. When Farel learned that the author of a new book on the Christian faith was staying in town, he went at once to find him.

Calvin told the story himself, more than twenty years later, in the preface to his commentary on the Psalms. Farel begged him to stay and help. Calvin refused: he wanted to be free for his studies. Then Farel did something startling. He called on God to curse Calvin's quiet studies if he went away when the need in Geneva was so great. Calvin wrote that he felt it was "as if God had from heaven laid his mighty hand upon me to arrest me." He was so shaken that he gave up his journey.

He stayed. Except for three years when the city sent him away, Calvin spent the rest of his life in Geneva, preaching, teaching, writing and caring for its people, until his death in 1564. The quiet scholar who wanted a private life became one of the most influential pastors in history.

And at the heart of his work was prayer. His great book, the *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, grew larger with each new edition. In its final form, one of its longest chapters is on prayer. In Calvin's own Latin title, prayer is called *praecipuum fidei exercitium*, the chief, or principal, exercise of faith. The old English edition in our library sums up the chapter like this:

> "The Twentieth Chapter is devoted to Prayer, the principal exercise of faith, and, as it were, the medium or instrument through which we daily procure blessings from God."
> John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*

### Who he was

John Calvin was born in 1509 in Noyon, in northern France. His father sent him to Paris to study for the church, and later to study law. Around the age of twenty-four he turned to the Protestant faith. He wrote that God "by a sudden conversion subdued and brought my mind to a teachable frame."

In 1534 the king of France turned fiercely against the Protestants, and many were burned. Calvin fled. In Basel, in Switzerland, he wrote the first short edition of the *Institutes*, partly to defend his persecuted friends in France.

After he was sent away from Geneva in 1538, he became pastor of a congregation of French refugees in Strasbourg. There he married Idelette de Bure, a widow with two children. When Geneva called him back in 1541, he returned. Over the following years, Geneva itself filled with refugees, thousands of them, fleeing persecution in France, Italy, England and other lands. Calvin, a refugee himself, preached to them many times each week. He had the psalms put into French verse so that ordinary people could sing them, and he called the Psalms "An Anatomy of all the Parts of the Soul," because every feeling of the human heart can be found there.

We must also be honest. Like almost everyone of his time, Calvin believed that the government should punish false teaching. In 1553 Geneva burned a man named Michael Servetus for his teaching about the Trinity, and Calvin supported his condemnation. Christians of every church today grieve such cruelty.

### How he taught us to pray

Calvin begins with a picture. God has hidden treasure for us in Christ, and the gospel shows us where it lies. But knowing where treasure is buried does not make anyone rich. You must dig. In his words, "prayer digs up those treasures which the Gospel of our Lord discovers to the eye of faith."

Then he gives four rules for right prayer. First, reverence: we must come with minds set free from careless thoughts, remembering that we are speaking with God. Second, a real sense of our need: we must truly want what we ask, and not pray only out of habit. Third, humility: we must lay aside every thought that we deserve to be heard. Fourth, confident hope: although we are humbled, we must still pray with "the sure hope of succeeding."

Calvin admits that the third and fourth rules seem to pull against each other. How can we feel unworthy and confident at the same time? The answer is Christ. Hebrews says that our high priest is able to "share the feeling of our weaknesses" (Hebrews 4:15), and so we may approach "the throne of grace" (Hebrews 4:16). Calvin explains:

> "For it is impossible to think of the dread majesty of God without being filled with alarm; and hence the sense of our own unworthiness must keep us far away, until Christ interpose, and convert a throne of dreadful glory into a throne of grace."
> John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*

Calvin also knew how weak and forgetful we are. He taught that we should pray at all times, but because our hearts are slow, he urged believers to keep five set times of prayer each day: when we rise in the morning, before we begin our daily work, when we sit down to eat, after we have eaten, and when we go to bed. Then he added a warning:

> "This, however, must not be a superstitious observance of hours, by which, as it were, performing a task to God, we think we are discharged as to other hours; it should rather be considered as a discipline by which our weakness is exercised, and ever and anon stimulated."
> John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*

("Ever and anon" means again and again.) Calvin practiced what he taught. His lectures on the prophets usually ended with a short prayer, which his students wrote down, and many of these prayers are still printed today.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer is how faith takes hold of the treasures God has promised.** Believing that God's gifts are real is only the beginning. Faith goes on to ask for them, day by day, as a child asks a generous father.
2. **Humble sorrow for our sins and bold confidence in God's mercy belong together in prayer.** Calvin would not let us choose between them. We come as sinners who deserve nothing, and as children who are sure to be heard.
3. **Because Jesus is our high priest, the throne we approach is a throne of grace.** We do not come before God on our own. Christ stands between us and the judgment we deserve, and He turns the place of fear into a place of welcome.

### For us today

Calvin was a refugee pastor in a city of refugees. Today many millions of people have been forced from their homes by war, hunger and persecution, in Sudan and Syria, Myanmar and Ukraine, Venezuela and many other places. Many of them are believers, praying in camps, in crowded rooms and in strange cities. Calvin's teaching is for them: the throne of grace is open in every land, and no border can keep a child of God away from it. It is also for churches that welcome refugees. God "protects the foreigners living in the land" (Psalm 146:9), and He often does it through His people.

Calvin's set times of prayer suit busy lives too. Waking, starting work, eating and going to bed happen every day, whether we live in a city apartment or a village hut. Each one can become a small door into God's presence.

### Try this

This week, practice Calvin's way of coming to the throne of grace.

- **Keep the five doors.** Pray briefly when you wake, before you begin work, before you eat, after you eat and before you sleep.
- **Check the four rules.** Before you pray, pause and ask: Am I remembering who God is? Do I truly want what I am asking? Am I trusting His mercy, not my worthiness? Do I expect Him to hear me?
- **Dig for one treasure.** Choose one promise of God, such as Hebrews 4:16, and ask Him each day for what it offers.
- **Pray for refugees.** Pray by name for one country where people are fleeing their homes, and for the churches that care for them.

### A closing prayer

Almighty God, You have opened the throne of grace to us through Jesus Christ, our great high priest. Thank You for Your servant John Calvin, who taught Your people to pray with reverence and with confidence. I come to You aware of my sins, and I trust only in Your mercy. Teach me to dig up the treasures You have promised, and to ask You for them every day. Keep me faithful at the beginning and end of each day, at my work and at my table. Be a shelter for every refugee, and make Your church a home for them. I ask this in the name of Jesus Christ, who shares the feeling of our weaknesses. Amen.

- Which of Calvin's four rules do you find hardest: reverence, a sense of need, humility or confident hope? Why?
- How could the ordinary moments of your day, waking, working, eating and sleeping, become times of prayer?

## Praying with the Mind Also

> "What follows, then? I will pray in the spirit, and I will pray with my mind as well. I will sing praise in the spirit, and I will sing praise with my mind as well." (1 Corinthians 14:15)

On 9 June 1549, the feast of Pentecost, something new happened in the parish churches of England. In towns and villages across the land, the people gathered as they always had. But that morning the priest opened a new book. It was called *The Book of Common Prayer*, and by law it was now to be used in every church. For the first time, the whole service, from beginning to end, was read in English.

For about a thousand years, the services of the church in England had been in Latin. Many people prayed with deep devotion during them, but most could not follow the words. The priest spoke the prayers, often quietly, and the people prayed their own private prayers while he did.

Now they heard words like these, near the start of the Communion service, in their own language: "Almighty God, unto whom all hearts be open, all desires known, and from whom no secrets are hid; Cleanse the thoughts of our hearts by the inspiration of thy Holy Spirit, that we may perfectly love thee, and worthily magnify thy holy Name; through Christ our Lord." And at the end, they could answer "Amen" and know what they were agreeing to. (Here and below we quote the prayer book as printed in 1662, which keeps Cranmer's words almost unchanged, in later spelling.)

Not everyone was glad. Some people loved the old services and grieved to lose them. In the far west of England, in Devon and Cornwall, there was an armed uprising that same summer, and the new book was one of its causes. Some of the Cornish people protested that they did not even speak English, so the new service was as strange to them as the Latin. The rising was crushed with great bloodshed. Changes in prayer touch people's hearts deeply, and this one was not gentle.

But the man behind the book believed with all his heart that God's people should pray with understanding. His name was Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury. In the book's preface he explained why. Paul, he wrote, wanted the church to use "such language spoken to the people in the Church, as they might understand, and have profit by hearing the same." Instead, he said, the people "have heard with their ears only, and their heart, spirit, and mind, have not been edified thereby."

### Who he was

Thomas Cranmer was born in 1489 in Aslockton, a village in Nottinghamshire. He became a scholar at Cambridge, a quiet and careful man who read widely. He was drawn into the service of King Henry VIII, and in 1533 he became Archbishop of Canterbury, the chief bishop in England.

Slowly Cranmer came to share many of the Reformers' convictions. He worked to place an English Bible in every parish church, and in 1544 he prepared the first English service, a litany, or series of prayers for the people to answer. Under Henry's young son, Edward VI, he was free to do more. He prepared the prayer book of 1549, and a revised version in 1552.

Cranmer did not invent most of its prayers. He gathered them from the ancient Latin services, from prayers used for many centuries, and from other churches, and he translated them into English of great beauty. He also turned the many daily services of the monasteries into two, Morning Prayer and Evening Prayer, so that ordinary people could join the church's daily prayer. He arranged for the psalms to be read through every month and most of the Bible every year.

John Foxe, who wrote about him within a few years of his death, describes his own habits:

> "Diligent in duty, he rose at five in the morning, and continued in study and prayer until nine: between then and dinner, he devoted to temporal affairs."
> John Foxe, *Fox's Book of Martyrs*

("Temporal affairs" means the business of this world.)

### Praying with the whole church

The prayer book gave England a treasure of short prayers called collects. A collect gathers up the prayers of the people into a few clear sentences: it names God, remembers what He has done, asks for one thing, and ends in the name of Christ. One that Cranmer is thought to have written himself is about the Bible: "Blessed Lord, who hast caused all holy Scriptures to be written for our learning; Grant that we may in such wise hear them, read, mark, learn, and inwardly digest them." It echoes Paul: "Everything that was written in earlier times was written to teach us" (Romans 15:4).

In the 1552 book, Morning and Evening Prayer began with a confession that the whole congregation said together, on their knees: "Almighty and most merciful Father; We have erred, and strayed from thy ways like lost sheep. We have followed too much the devices and desires of our own hearts." Rich and poor, lord and servant, spoke the same words, because all needed the same mercy.

This is what Paul meant. Prayer in the spirit is good, but prayer must also go through the mind, so that others can say "Amen" (1 Corinthians 14:16) and be built up.

### The unworthy right hand

When Edward died in 1553, his Catholic sister Mary became queen. She restored the old faith, and Cranmer was arrested. We must say plainly that this was an age when Christians on both sides put one another to death. Cranmer himself, as archbishop, had taken part in condemning people for heresy. Now, under Mary, some three hundred Protestants were burned, and later, under Elizabeth, Catholics too were put to death for their faith.

In prison in Oxford, Cranmer watched his friends Nicholas Ridley and Hugh Latimer go to the fire. He was old, alone and afraid. Under great pressure, he signed several papers taking back what he had taught. Yet he was still condemned to die.

On 21 March 1556, he was brought to the University Church of St Mary in Oxford, where everyone expected him to read one last recantation. Foxe records how he knelt and prayed:

> "To Thee, therefore, O Lord, do I run; to Thee do I humble myself, saying, O Lord, my God, my sins be great, but yet have mercy upon me for Thy great mercy."
> John Foxe, *Fox's Book of Martyrs*

Then, to everyone's amazement, he withdrew his recantations. He said that his hand had written things contrary to his heart, for fear of death, and so his hand would be punished first. He was taken out to the place where Ridley and Latimer had died. When the fire was lit, he stretched out his right hand into the flames and held it there, saying, "This unworthy right hand." Foxe says that he used the words of Stephen, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Acts 7:59), until he died.

### Three key insights

1. **God wants us to pray with our minds as well as our hearts, in words we understand.** Paul would rather speak a few clear words than many that no one can follow. Prayer is not a charm; it is speaking with God, and with understanding.
2. **Praying the same words together makes a scattered people into one praying family.** When a whole church confesses, gives thanks and asks together, each person's faith is strengthened by all the others.
3. **After a great failure, God can still give a believer courage to stand at the end.** Cranmer fell, as Peter did. But he ran to God's mercy, and in the end he stood. No failure is too great for God to forgive.

### For us today

Cranmer's prayer book has traveled far beyond England. Today most Anglicans live not in England but in Africa, in countries such as Nigeria, Uganda and Kenya, and the prayer book's words have been translated into many languages. The same longing still drives Bible translators around the world: that every people should hear God's word and pray to Him in the language of their heart.

Some churches love written prayers; others pray freely as the Spirit leads. Both can honor God, as long as our minds are awake to what we say. A prayer read from a book can be empty, and so can a prayer made up on the spot. The question is whether the heart and the mind are both present.

And many believers know Cranmer's shame. Under threats, some have denied their faith, or kept silent when they should have spoken. Cranmer's last day says to them: it is not too late to run to God.

### Try this

This week, pray with your mind as well as your spirit.

- **Pray one collect slowly.** Each morning, pray the Collect for Purity, quoted above. Stop at each phrase and think about what you are asking.
- **Read, mark, learn and digest.** Choose one short Bible passage. Read it, mark one phrase, learn it by heart, and think about it again and again through the day.
- **Confess together.** If you live with family or friends, say a prayer of confession together once this week, aloud.
- **Pray for translators.** Ask God to help those who are translating the Bible into languages that still do not have it.

### A closing prayer

Almighty God, unto whom all hearts are open, all desires known, and from whom no secrets are hidden: cleanse the thoughts of my heart by Your Holy Spirit, that I may love You and praise You with my whole mind. Thank You for Your servant Thomas Cranmer, who gave Your people prayers in their own language, and who ran back to Your mercy after he fell. When I am weak and afraid, keep me faithful. When I fail, lead me back to You. Teach Your church in every land to pray together with understanding, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Have you ever said a prayer, or sung a song, without thinking about the words? What helps your mind stay awake when you pray?
- What is the value of praying the same words together with other believers? What might be lost if we only ever prayed alone?
- Cranmer failed, and then turned back. What does his story say to someone who feels they have failed God too badly to return?

## Give Me Scotland

> "Brothers and sisters, what my heart longs for, and what I ask God for them, is that they may be saved." (Romans 10:1)

In the summer of 1548, French galleys lay off the east coast of Scotland, between Dundee and St Andrews. A galley was a warship moved by oars, pulled by prisoners chained to their benches. Among the rowers were Scots captured the year before, when the castle of St Andrews surrendered. One of them was a preacher in his thirties named John Knox. He was so sick with fever that few thought he would live.

A fellow prisoner, James Balfour, told him to look at the land and asked if he knew it. Knox answered that he knew it well. He could see the steeple of the church where God had first opened his mouth in public to preach. And he said he was fully persuaded, however weak he looked, that he would not die until his tongue would glorify God's name in that same place again.

Knox tells this story himself, in the old Scots of his *History of the Reformation in Scotland*, writing about himself as if about another man. He notes that Balfour repeated these words before many witnesses, years before Knox came home.

He was set free early in 1549, after about nineteen months in the galleys. Ten years of exile followed. But in June 1559, Knox stood in the pulpit at St Andrews and preached again. The rest of his life would be spent pleading with God, and with his people, for Scotland.

### Who he was

John Knox was born around 1514 near Haddington, east of Edinburgh, and trained as a priest. In the 1540s he came to believe the teaching of the Reformation through a preacher named George Wishart, and he went about with Wishart, carrying a large two-handed sword to guard him.

Both sides shed blood in those years. In 1546 Wishart was burned for heresy on the order of Cardinal David Beaton. A few months later, a group of Protestant men murdered the cardinal in his castle at St Andrews and held the castle. Knox later joined them as their preacher. When they called him to preach, he says, he burst into tears and shut himself in his room. But he took up the task, and within a few months the castle fell.

After his release, Knox preached in England and served as one of the chaplains of the young King Edward VI. When the Catholic Queen Mary took the English throne in 1553, he fled, and spent most of the next years in Geneva, where John Calvin, whom we met in chapter 3, was teaching. In 1559 he came home. In August 1560 the Scottish Parliament accepted a Protestant confession of faith that Knox helped to write, and St Giles in Edinburgh was his church until he died in 1572.

He was not a gentle man. His preaching against images at Perth was followed by crowds tearing apart churches and friaries. He spoke harshly to Mary, Queen of Scots, and in one meeting, as Knox himself records, she wept in front of him. But we can still learn from the way he prayed.

### What he taught about prayer

In 1554, during his exile, Knox sent a small book on true prayer to the believers in England, who were then in danger of their lives. He addressed it to the small and scattered flock of Jesus Christ. It begins with a definition still worth learning. Prayer, he wrote, is "ane earnest and familiar talking with God," that is, an earnest and personal talk with God. In it we tell God our troubles, ask for His help, and praise Him for what He has given.

Trouble and fear, Knox wrote, are spurs to prayer. A spur is a sharp point on a rider's boot that makes a horse run. He knew this from his own life: he speaks of calling to the Lord in his worst trouble, when his pains were most cruel, and the editor of his works thought he meant the galleys.

He taught his readers that Paul commands us to pray for all people and at all times, and above all for believers who suffer persecution: "I urge that requests, prayers, intercessions and thanks be offered for everyone" (1 Timothy 2:1).

### "Give me Scotland, or I die"

Knox is famous for one short prayer: "Give me Scotland, or I die!" Many books repeat it, including E. M. Bounds's *The Necessity of Prayer*. But no one who knew Knox wrote it down. It is not in his own writings, or in his secretary's careful account of his last days, and it seems to appear in print only long after his death. It is a tradition, not a recorded saying, so this chapter's title has no quotation marks.

The same is true of another famous story: that Mary, Queen of Scots, feared Knox's prayers more than an army. We have not found it in any writing from Knox's own time; it was told by later writers, long after both had died. Charles Spurgeon passed it on to young preachers:

> "Nor will your people wholly engross you; the nation and the world will claim their share. The man who is mighty in prayer may be a wall of fire around his country, her guardian angel and her shield. We have all heard how the enemies of the Protestant cause dreaded the prayers of Knox more than they feared armies of ten thousand men."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Lectures to My Students*

"We have all heard," Spurgeon says, and that is exactly the point: everyone had heard it, but no one had seen it written at the time.

What we truly know is how Knox prayed at the end. His secretary, Richard Bannatyne, wrote down his last days in November 1572. Lying in bed, too weak to stand alone, Knox prayed aloud again and again. He asked God to give true pastors to His church, to keep its teaching pure, and to give peace again to the nation. On his last afternoon he said to his wife, "Goe reid whair I cast my first ancre!" That is: Go and read where I first cast my anchor. She read John 17, the prayer of Jesus for His people.

The famous words may be legend, but the longing behind them is true. Like Paul, Knox longed and prayed for his own people. Paul even wrote, "I could even wish that I myself were under a curse, cut off from Christ, on behalf of my brothers and sisters" (Romans 9:3). That is the real meaning of "Give me Scotland, or I die": a love for one's people so strong that it will not stop praying.

### Three key insights

1. **A heart that loves its own people will carry them to God in prayer.** Paul longed and prayed for his people, and Knox prayed for Scotland until his last breath. Love for our nation is shown best on our knees.
2. **Praying for faithful pastors and true teaching is a gift to the whole church.** On his deathbed Knox asked God for true pastors and pure doctrine. When we pray for those who preach and lead, we serve every believer who listens to them.
3. **Praying for a nation is the work of a lifetime, not of one dramatic night.** We remember one famous cry, but Knox's real prayers were spread over more than twenty-five years. God honors patient asking.

### For us today

Every believer belongs to a people: a family, a town, a tribe, a nation. Some of us live where believers are few, or suffer for their faith, as the English believers did when Knox wrote to them. In every place, God calls us to pray for those around us. The prophet told the exiles in Babylon to "Seek the peace of the city where I have sent you as exiles, and pray to the LORD for it" (Jeremiah 29:7).

A grandmother in a village in Ghana can pray for her country as truly as a pastor in a great city. A student in Seoul can pray for the believers of North Korea, and a factory worker in Brazil for the leaders of his land. We may wait many years for the answer, as Knox waited to preach again in St Andrews. But no prayer for a people is wasted.

Knox's life also warns us that zeal for our nation can turn harsh. The best prayer for our people is Paul's: not that they would agree with us, but "that they may be saved."

### Try this

This week, carry your own people to God in prayer.

- **Pray Romans 10:1.** Each day, pray Paul's words for the people of your town or nation, by name if you can: "what my heart longs for, and what I ask God for them, is that they may be saved."
- **Pray for pastors and leaders.** Use Knox's last prayer: ask God to give true pastors to His church, keep its teaching pure, and give peace to your land.
- **Pray in your trouble.** When fear or trouble comes this week, let it be a spur. Before you worry, turn and speak to God.
- **Read John 17.** Read slowly the chapter where Knox first cast his anchor, and see how Jesus prays for His people.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You love the nations, and You hear the prayers of Your people for their lands. Thank You for John Knox, who called on You in chains and in exile, and who prayed for his country until his last breath. Give me a heart that longs for my own people to be saved. Give true pastors to Your church in my land, keep its teaching pure, and give us peace. When trouble and fear come, let them drive me to You. Keep me from pride and harshness, and fill my prayers with love, through Jesus Christ my Lord. Amen.

- Who are "your people," the ones your heart longs for God to save? How often do you pray for them?
- Knox said trouble and fear are spurs to prayer. When has a hard time pushed you to pray?
- Why does it matter to separate true stories from legends in the lives of believers?

## Prayer as Friendship

> "I do not call you servants any longer, since a servant is not aware of what his master is doing. I have called you friends instead, because I have made known to you everything I heard from My Father." (John 15:15)

One day, around the year 1554, a nun walked into the small chapel of her convent in Ávila, a walled city in the hills of central Spain. Her name was Teresa, and she was nearly forty years old. She had been a nun for almost twenty years. Yet she later said that her life in those years was "the most painful life that can be imagined, because I had no sweetness in God, and no pleasure in the world." She loved God, but she also loved the friendships and talk of the visitors' room, and she could not give her whole heart to Him.

That day she saw a picture that had been set aside for a coming feast. It showed Christ "most grievously wounded." As she looked at it, she writes, "So keenly did I feel the evil return I had made for those wounds, that I thought my heart was breaking." She threw herself on the ground beside it, weeping, and begged Him to strengthen her once for all, so that she would never offend Him again. This time, she says, she did not trust herself at all, but put all her confidence in God. She told Him that she would not get up until He granted what she asked. Looking back, she wrote, "I have grown better ever since."

At about the same time, someone gave her the *Confessions* of Augustine. When she read how Augustine heard a voice in a garden and turned to God, it seemed to her that the Lord was speaking those words to her. She wept for a long time.

Teresa tells all this herself in the story of her life, which she wrote about ten years later at the request of the priests who guided her. We quote it here in the English of David Lewis, published in 1870.

### Who she was

Teresa was born in Ávila in 1515, into a large family. Her mother died when she was about twelve. Around the age of twenty, against her father's wishes at first, she entered the Carmelite Convent of the Incarnation, outside the city walls. Soon afterward she became so ill that she nearly died, and for a long time she could hardly move.

During these years she learned what she called "mental prayer," that is, prayer from the heart and mind in one's own words, not only reading set prayers aloud. But she found it hard. Her thoughts wandered, and she felt ashamed of how far her life was from God. For a year or more she gave up this kind of prayer altogether. She told herself that she was too wicked to come so close to God. Later she called it a "fearful delusion" to "give up prayer under the pretence of humility."

After the change of heart in the chapel, everything began to grow. In 1562 she opened a small, poor convent in Ávila, where a few women could live a simple life given to prayer. Later she traveled across Spain on rough roads, often in a covered cart, to begin new houses. Seventeen convents of women came from her work, and with the help of a young friar, John of the Cross, whom we will meet in the next chapter, houses of men as well. She wrote several books, among them the *Interior Castle* in 1577. She died in 1582. In 1970 the Catholic Church named her the first woman "Doctor of the Church," a title for its most trusted teachers.

### Friends of God

In the story of her life, Teresa stops to give a definition of prayer that is famous to this day: "mental prayer is nothing else, in my opinion, but being on terms of friendship with God, frequently conversing in secret with Him Who, we know, loves us."

Prayer is friendship. It is conversation, often and in secret. And it is with Someone who loves us, and we know it. Teresa had learned this the hard way. For years she had thought she had to become good before she could pray. Now she saw that prayer was the way to become good. So she begged her readers, "let him never cease from prayer who has once begun it, be his life ever so wicked; for prayer is the way to amend it."

This is the friendship Jesus offers in John 15:15. A servant only takes orders. A friend is told what is in the master's heart. Abraham "was called God's friend" (James 2:23). "The LORD would speak with Moses face to face, the way a person talks with a friend" (Exodus 33:11). Jesus offers the same gift to every disciple. It is not a friendship between equals. Jesus also said, "You are My friends if you carry out what I command you" (John 15:14). But it is real friendship, and it is offered to sinners. Teresa knew that our side of the friendship is weak. Still, she said, "no one ever took Him for his friend that was not amply rewarded."

### The door of the castle

Years later, in the *Interior Castle*, Teresa pictured the soul as "a castle, formed of a single diamond or a very transparent crystal," with many rooms. In the center room the King Himself lives. How do we go in?

> "As far as I can understand, the gate by which to enter this castle is prayer and meditation. I do not allude more to mental than to vocal prayer, for if it is prayer at all, the mind must take part in it. If a person neither considers to Whom he is addressing himself, what he asks, nor what he is who ventures to speak to God, although his lips may utter many words, I do not call it prayer."
> Teresa of Ávila, *Interior Castle*

Prayer, then, is not words alone. It is a person speaking, with attention, to a Person. And when our attention fails, as it often does, Teresa did not want us to lose heart. She wrote:

> "I only wish to warn you that to make rapid progress and to reach the mansions we wish to enter, it is not so essential to think much as to love much: therefore you must practise whatever most excites you to this."
> Teresa of Ávila, *Interior Castle*

She went on to say that love is not "great sweetness of devotion," but a firm desire to please God in everything. And she told her nuns not to imagine "that if your thoughts wander a little all is lost." She knew; her own thoughts wandered all her life.

Teresa was a Catholic nun, and some of what helped her, such as a painted image of Christ, belongs to her tradition. Many Protestant believers pray without such pictures. But every Christian can do what she did that day in the chapel: look at Christ's wounds with the eyes of the heart, and give up trusting in ourselves.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer is a friendship with Jesus, kept alive by frequent and honest conversation.** Teresa did not see prayer first as a duty or a skill. It is time spent, often and in secret, with the One who calls His disciples friends and tells them what is in His heart.
2. **Never stop praying because you feel unworthy, for prayer is the road back to God.** Teresa gave up prayer for a time because she felt too sinful. Later she saw that this was the worst thing she could have done. The sinner needs prayer most of all.
3. **In prayer, loving much matters more than thinking much.** We do not need clever thoughts or a quiet mind to pray well. A heart that wants to please God is what He looks for, even when our thoughts wander.

### For us today

Many people in our world are lonely. They live in crowded cities in Lagos or Mumbai, or in quiet villages where the young have left, and they feel that no one truly knows them. Teresa's words are good news for them. God is not only a Master who gives orders. In Jesus He offers to be a Friend.

Teresa also speaks to believers who feel they have failed too often to pray. Perhaps you stopped praying after a sin you are ashamed of. Perhaps you feel that God must be tired of you. Teresa would say: come back today. Prayer is not a reward for the good. It is the way home for the weak.

And she speaks to those who think prayer is only for the educated. Teresa often said that her mind was slow. Yet she became one of the great teachers of prayer. What she had was love, and the patience to keep coming back.

### Try this

This week, practice prayer as friendship.

- **Talk with Him as a friend.** Set aside ten minutes each day. Instead of a list of requests, tell Jesus simply what is in your heart, as you would tell a close friend.
- **Keep Him company.** Teresa often imagined she was with Jesus in the garden of Gethsemane, keeping Him company in His sorrow. Read Mark 14:32-42, and stay with Him there for a while.
- **Look at His wounds.** Read John 19:16-30 slowly, as Teresa looked at the picture of the wounded Christ, and tell Him what you see.
- **Love, not think.** When your thoughts wander, do not give up. Gently turn back to God with a short word of love, such as "Lord, You know that I love You."

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You no longer call me a servant, but a friend. Thank You for making known to me what You heard from Your Father. Thank You for Your servant Teresa, who learned after many years that prayer is friendship with You. Forgive me for the times I stayed away from You because I felt unworthy. Draw me back to Your wounds, and teach me to trust in You and not in myself. When my thoughts wander, keep my heart turned toward You. Help me to love much, and to keep coming to You, my Friend, who loves me. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Do you think of prayer more as a duty or as friendship? What difference would it make to see it as friendship?
- Have you ever stopped praying because you felt unworthy? What would Teresa say to you?
- What helps you to love much in prayer, even when you cannot think clearly?

## The Dark Night

> "Who among you fears the LORD and listens to the voice of His servant? Let the one who walks in darkness, with no glimmer of light, trust in the name of the LORD and lean on his God." (Isaiah 50:10)

On the night of 2 December 1577, a group of men broke into a small house in Ávila, in Spain, and carried off a friar who lived there. They were not robbers. They were Carmelite friars, members of his own religious order. The prisoner was John of the Cross, a small, quiet man of thirty-five. He had joined Teresa of Ávila, whom we met in the last chapter, in her work of renewal, and the leaders of the older branch of the order saw this as disobedience. They took him to their house in Toledo, judged him and locked him up.

His prison, as later witnesses described it, was a tiny room, only a few steps long, with no window of its own. A little light came in through a small opening from the next room, and it is said that he had to stand on a bench to read his prayer book. He was given bread, water and scraps of fish, and for months he had no change of clothes. Often he was brought before the community and beaten as a punishment. Through the cold of winter and the heat of summer, he stayed in the dark.

Yet in that dark room he made poems. At first he could only keep them in his memory. Later, it is said, a kinder guard gave him paper. After about eight months, in August 1578, he escaped. The story is told that he loosened his door at night and let himself down from a window high above the river, on a rope made from strips of his blankets. He made his way to a house of Teresa's nuns in Toledo, who hid him. There, still weak, he is said to have recited to them the poems he had made in prison.

What we know of his prison comes from what John later told his friends, written down after his death, so some details may have grown in the telling. Soon after his escape, or perhaps already in prison (scholars are not sure), he wrote a short poem that begins, in David Lewis's old English translation: "In a dark night, / With anxious love inflamed, / O, happy lot! / Forth unobserved I went, / My house being now at rest." The words sound like a prisoner slipping out at night. But John said they were about something deeper: the soul going out to meet God.

### Who he was

John was born in 1542 in Fontiveros, a small town near Ávila. His father died when he was small, and the family was very poor. He grew up in Medina del Campo, worked in a hospital for the poor, and became a Carmelite friar and a priest. In 1567 he met Teresa, who was then about fifty-two. She asked him to help her renew the order of friars as she had begun to renew the nuns, and in 1568 he joined the first small house of the reformed friars.

It is sad that his worst suffering came from fellow Christians, and even from brothers of his own order. The quarrel was about how strictly they should live, and power and pride were mixed into it on both sides. After his escape he served as a leader and spiritual guide in southern Spain, and he died in Úbeda in 1591, aged forty-nine.

### What the dark night is

Today people use the words "dark night of the soul" for any very hard time, but John meant something more exact. He wrote a book, *The Dark Night of the Soul*, to explain his poem. In it he describes times when God seems to take away all sweetness in prayer. The believer still loves God, but prayer feels dry and empty, and God seems far away.

John taught that such a night can be a gift. God, he says, sometimes weans the soul from sweet feelings, as a mother weans a child from milk, so that it will learn to love Him for Himself and not only for His gifts. It is God who leads the soul through the night.

He was careful, though, to say that not every dryness is this night. Dryness can come from sin, or from not caring about God, or from illness of body or mind. He gave three signs of the true night. First, the person finds no comfort "in the things of God, and none also in created things." Second, the person keeps thinking of God with a painful worry that they are not serving Him well. A lukewarm person does not worry like that. Third, they cannot pray in the old ways, however hard they try. And John added that the lack of joy may come from "bodily indisposition or a melancholy temperament," that is, from illness or a heavy sadness of mind.

We need this wisdom today. The dark night is not the same thing as depression. Depression is a real illness, and it often needs care from a doctor as well as from a pastor. Taking medicine for it is not a lack of faith. But the two can come together, and John himself admits that dryness may be "occasionally increased by melancholy or other infirmity." Whatever the cause, God does not leave His children alone in the dark. When Elijah lay under a tree and asked to die, God did not scold him. An angel touched him and said, "Get up and eat, for the journey is too much for you" (1 Kings 19:7). Sometimes the most spiritual thing we can do is sleep, eat and ask for help.

### Walking without light

What should we do in the night? John's advice is gentle. "They will do enough if they keep patience, and persevere in prayer," he writes. We should not try to force good feelings. We can simply turn our attention "lovingly and calmly towards God," and trust that He is at work, even when we feel nothing.

That is exactly what Isaiah says. The one "who walks in darkness, with no glimmer of light," is not told to make his own light. He is told to "trust in the name of the LORD and lean on his God." The Bible has room for such prayers. Psalm 88 ends, "my one companion is the dark" (Psalm 88:18). And Jesus Himself cried from the cross, "My God, My God, why did You abandon Me?" (Mark 15:34). Yet He was still praying, still saying "My God."

An American pastor of the 1800s, Richard Fuller, put it in words that D. L. Moody gathered into a book of daily readings:

> "Sometimes, like Paul, you can see neither sun nor stars, and no small tempest lies on you; and then you can do but one thing; there is only one way. Reason cannot help you. Past experiences give you no light. Even prayer fetches no consolation. Only a single course is left. You must put your soul in one position and keep it there."
> Richard Fuller, in D. L. Moody, *Thoughts for the Quiet Hour*

### Three key insights

1. **Feeling nothing in prayer is not the same as being left by God.** John of the Cross found God in a prison with no window. When prayer feels empty, God may be at work most deeply, out of our sight.
2. **Not every darkness is the same, and wise love helps us tell them apart.** Dryness can come from sin, from coldness, from illness or from God's own leading. We should be honest with ourselves, and we should not be afraid to seek help from a pastor or a doctor.
3. **In the dark we are asked to lean on God, not to make our own light.** Isaiah tells the one in darkness to trust and lean on God. We cannot force good feelings or make the light come back, but we can keep turning toward Him with patience.

### For us today

Many believers go through times when God seems silent. A young mother in Nairobi, worn out and sad after the birth of her baby, may feel that her prayers reach no one. A pastor in China, in prison for his faith, may sit in the dark as John did. A student far from home may find that the Bible feels dry and church feels empty. None of them should conclude that God has rejected them.

It also matters how we treat others in the dark. Do not tell a suffering friend that they simply need more faith. Sit with them, pray for them when they cannot pray, and help them find a doctor if they need one.

### Try this

If you are walking in darkness now, or when that time comes:

- **Pray Isaiah 50:10.** Say it slowly each day: "Lord, I walk in darkness, with no glimmer of light. I will trust in Your name and lean on You."
- **Borrow the psalmist's words.** When you have no words of your own, pray Psalm 13 or Psalm 88. God put these honest prayers in the Bible for nights like yours.
- **Tell someone.** Speak to a pastor, a wise friend or, if your sadness is deep and lasting, a doctor. Asking for help is not a failure of faith.
- **Rest and eat.** Remember Elijah. Care for your body as well as your soul.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, even the darkness is not dark to You. Thank You for Your servant John of the Cross, who found You in a prison without light and wrote songs of love there. When prayer feels empty and You seem far away, help me to trust in Your name and lean on You. Keep me from forcing feelings, and give me patience to keep praying. Comfort all who are sad, sick or in prison tonight, and lead us through every night until we see Your face, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Have you ever gone through a time when prayer felt dry or God seemed far away? What helped you?
- Why do you think God sometimes allows His children to walk without light?
- How can you care well for a friend who is in deep sadness or depression?

- Andrew Murray, *Waiting on God*, especially "In Times of Darkness"

## Search Me, O God

> "God, look deep into me and know my heart; put me to the test and know my restless thoughts. Look and see whether I am on a path that brings harm, and guide me along the road that never ends." (Psalm 139:23-24)

In May 1521, a French army surrounded the fortress of Pamplona, in the north of Spain. The Spanish soldiers inside knew they could not win, and most of them wanted to surrender. But one proud young officer, a Basque nobleman named Íñigo of Loyola, persuaded the commander to fight on. When the walls were broken, he stood fighting until a cannon ball struck him. It broke one of his legs and badly hurt the other.

The French treated him kindly and sent him home to his family's castle at Loyola. His leg had been set badly, so the doctors broke it again and reset it. He grew so ill that they expected him to die, but he recovered. Then, because a piece of bone stuck out below his knee and spoiled the look of his leg, he asked them to cut it away, though they warned him it would hurt more than everything before. He wanted to look fine at court.

For weeks he had to lie in bed. He loved stories of knights and adventure, and he asked for some to pass the time. But there were none in the house. Instead they gave him a *Life of Christ* and a book of the lives of the saints. He began to read, and his mind went two ways. For hours he daydreamed of the great deeds he would do to win the favor of a noble lady. Then he would read of the saints and think, "What if I should do what St. Francis did?"

Slowly he noticed something. "When he thought of worldly things it gave him great pleasure, but afterward he found himself dry and sad." But when he thought of following Christ, the joy stayed with him even after he stopped thinking. One day, the account says, "the eyes of his soul were opened." He "learned by experience that one train of thought left him sad, the other joyful." This, it adds, was "his first reasoning on spiritual matters."

We know this story because near the end of his life, Ignatius, as he was later called, told it to a younger friend, who wrote it down. It is told about him in the third person. We quote the English translation of J. F. X. O'Conor, published in 1900.

### Who he was

Íñigo was born in 1491 in the Basque country of northern Spain, the youngest son of a noble family. As a young man he served at court and as a soldier, and by his own account he cared mostly for fame. After his recovery he gave up that life. At the shrine of Montserrat he hung up his sword and dagger in the church, and then spent about a year in the town of Manresa, praying for many hours a day and begging for his food.

Manresa was not easy for him. He was tormented by guilt over his past sins, and for a time he could find no peace, even after confessing them again and again. But God brought him through, and he began to write down what he learned about prayer and about the movements of the heart. These notes became a small book, *The Spiritual Exercises*.

He went on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, and then, already over thirty, went back to school to learn Latin. He studied in Spain and in Paris, where he gathered a few friends. Among them was Francis Xavier, who would later carry the gospel to India and Japan, as we will see in chapter 10. In 1540 the pope approved their group as the Society of Jesus, often called the Jesuits. Ignatius led it from Rome until his death in 1556. The Jesuits started schools and missions across the world. They also worked hard to win back lands that had become Protestant, in an age when Catholics and Protestants were often bitter enemies.

### The examination of conscience

At the center of Ignatius's teaching is a simple belief: we were made for God. The first part of *The Spiritual Exercises* opens with these words:

> "Man is created to praise, reverence, and serve God our Lord, and by this means to save his soul."
> Ignatius of Loyola, *The Spiritual Exercises*

If that is true, then it matters every day whether we are moving toward God or away from Him. So Ignatius taught a short prayer to be made each day, which he called the "General Examen," the general examination of conscience. It has five steps, and it begins in a surprising place:

> "The first Point is to give thanks to God our Lord for the benefits received."
> Ignatius of Loyola, *The Spiritual Exercises*

Only after thanks does it go on. The second step is "to ask grace to know our sins and cast them out." The third is to look back over the day, "hour by hour, or period by period," first at our thoughts, then our words, then our deeds. The fourth is "to ask pardon of God our Lord for the faults." The fifth is "to purpose amendment with His grace," that is, to decide with God's help to do better. Then we pray the Lord's Prayer.

This was not only for monks or scholars. In his opening notes, Ignatius said that simple exercises like these, the examen among them, suit even those who cannot read: "This way is more proper for illiterate or less educated persons."

Notice the order. Thanks comes first, before any looking at sin. Ignatius knew from Manresa how easily an anxious person can sink into endless self-blame. The examen is not a courtroom where we accuse ourselves. It is a walk back through the day with God, who loves us and has given us every good thing in it.

### A prayer for every church

Many Protestant Christians use the examen too, and it is easy to see why. Each step is drawn from Scripture. David, who began Psalm 139 by saying, "LORD, You have looked deep into me, and You know me" (Psalm 139:1), ends by asking God to look again. He does not search himself alone. He asks God to search him, because "Who can catch all their own mistakes?" (Psalm 19:12). Paul tells believers, "Look closely at yourselves" (2 Corinthians 13:5).

The English Puritans of the next century taught daily self-examination in much the same way. Ignatius joined the examen to confession before a priest, as Catholics do. Many Protestants confess to God directly, trusting His promise: "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous: He will forgive our sins" (1 John 1:9). But all agree that the searching is God's work, and that forgiveness comes through Christ. R. A. Torrey, an American Bible teacher, wrote:

> "It is through prayer that my sin is brought to light, my most hidden sin."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

### Three key insights

1. **Looking back over the day with God begins with thanks, not with blame.** Ignatius put gratitude first. When we start by counting God's gifts, we can face our failures without fear.
2. **We cannot see our own hearts clearly, so we ask God to search them.** David did not trust himself to find every fault. He invited God to look deep into him, and to guide him on the road that never ends.
3. **Noticing what draws us toward God or away from Him helps us find His way.** In his sickbed Ignatius saw that some thoughts left him dry and sad, and others left him at peace with God. Paying attention to these movements, with Scripture and wise friends, helps us follow Christ.

### For us today

The examen is a gift for busy and ordinary people. It needs no book, no building and no money. A bus driver in Lima can pray it on the way home. A mother in Kampala can pray it after her children are asleep. A prisoner in a cell can pray it with no light at all. Ten or fifteen minutes are enough.

It is also a gift for anxious people. Some believers spend hours worrying over their sins, as Ignatius once did. The examen gives that worry a shape and an end: thanks, light, review, forgiveness, a new start. Then we rest, because God has heard.

And it is a gift for the whole church. Catholics and Protestants have often argued about many things. But we can all kneel and pray the same psalm: "God, look deep into me and know my heart."

### Try this

Tonight, before you sleep, pray through your day with God in five steps.

- **Give thanks.** Name three good gifts from today, large or small, and thank God for each.
- **Ask for light.** Pray Psalm 139:23: "God, look deep into me and know my heart."
- **Look back.** Walk through the day from morning to now. Where did you feel close to God? Where did you turn away from Him in thought, word or deed?
- **Ask forgiveness and begin again.** Confess what you have seen, receive Christ's forgiveness, and ask His help for one thing tomorrow. End with the Lord's Prayer.

### A closing prayer

God, You have looked deep into me, and You know me. Thank You for every gift of this day. Thank You for Your servant Ignatius, who learned on a sickbed to notice Your work in his heart. Search me, and show me what I cannot see. Where I have turned away from You, forgive me, for the sake of Jesus Christ. Where I have walked with You, let me be glad. Free me from fear and endless self-blame, and give me grace to do better tomorrow. Look and see whether I am on a path that brings harm, and guide me along the road that never ends. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Ignatius put thanksgiving first in the examen?
- Which parts of your life do you find hardest to let God search?
- When have you noticed that some choices leave you dry and sad, while others leave you at peace with God?

## Prayer for Busy People

> "In everything you say and everything you do, act in the name of the Lord Jesus, and through Him give thanks to God the Father." (Colossians 3:17)

In September 1594, a young priest and his cousin set out for the Chablais, a district of farms and villages on the southern shore of Lake Geneva. The priest was Francis de Sales, twenty-seven years old. For decades the Chablais had been ruled by the Protestant Swiss city of Bern, and most of its people had become Protestant. Now it was back under the Catholic Duke of Savoy, and the duke had asked for a preacher to win the people back to the Catholic Church.

It was hard and lonely work. For safety, Francis had to sleep each night in a fortress at Allinges, on a hill, and walk down each morning to preach. In the town of Thonon few people came to hear him. Some called him a sorcerer, and more than once men tried to kill him. So he began to write. He wrote short leaflets explaining his faith, one sheet at a time, and had them copied and passed from house to house. The story is told that he even slipped them under the doors of people who would not open to him. Years later these papers were gathered into a book.

We should tell this fairly. To the Protestants of the Chablais and Geneva, his mission was an attack on their faith. In the end many people did return to the Catholic Church. Part of that change came from his patient preaching and kindness, and part, as historians point out, came from the power of the duke, for in that age rulers on every side decided the religion of their lands.

Fifteen years later, Francis wrote another kind of paper. A young married woman whom he knew well, who lived a busy life in society, had asked him how she could live a devout life. Francis wrote her notes of advice, and she kept them. A learned priest who saw them urged him to publish them, and in 1609 they became a book, *Introduction to the Devout Life*. In the preface Francis explained that his "object is to teach those who are living in towns, at court, in their own households, and whose calling obliges them to a social life." He addressed the book to "Philothea," a name meaning "one who loves God," so that every reader could find herself or himself in it.

### Who he was

Francis was born in 1567 in the castle of Sales, in Savoy, a land in the Alps between France and Italy. He was the eldest son of a noble family, and his father planned for him to be a lawyer and to marry well. He studied in Paris and in Padua, Italy. In Paris, at about nineteen, he went through months of terror, believing that God had rejected him. The story is told that the darkness lifted as he prayed in a Paris church and gave himself completely to God's love.

He refused the marriage his father had arranged, became a priest in 1593, and in 1602 was made bishop of Geneva. Because Geneva was Protestant, he lived to the south, in Annecy. In 1604 he preached in Dijon, in France, and met Jane de Chantal, a widow with four children. He became her spiritual guide, mostly by letters, and in 1610 they founded a community of women, the Visitation, which welcomed women that other orders turned away because of poor health or age. Francis died in Lyon in 1622, aged fifty-five. He was famous for his gentleness, and Anglicans as well as Catholics remember him today.

### Devotion in every calling

Many people in his day thought that real holiness was only for monks and nuns. Francis disagreed. God made each tree to bear fruit "after its kind," he wrote, and in the same way each Christian bears fruit according to his or her calling. A bishop should not try to live like a hermit, and a workman should not spend all day in church like a monk. That kind of devotion would only be foolish. True devotion, he said, does not get in the way of our work. It makes us do it better:

> "It is an error, nay more, a very heresy, to seek to banish the devout life from the soldier's guardroom, the mechanic's workshop, the prince's court, or the domestic hearth."
> Francis de Sales, *Introduction to the Devout Life*

A "mechanic" here means any worker with tools, and "the domestic hearth" means the home. With devotion, Francis said, "family duties are lighter, married love truer," and every kind of work is "more acceptable and better performed." This is Colossians 3:17: everything we say and do, in the kitchen, the field or the office, can be done in the name of the Lord Jesus.

### A day shaped by prayer

How can a busy person live like this? Francis gave Philothea a simple pattern.

**A morning prayer.** Each morning, he said, thank God for the night, and remember that this day is a gift. Then think ahead to the day's work, its duties and its temptations. If you know that you will meet "some one who is hottempered and irritable," do not only resolve to keep your own temper; "consider by what gentle words to conciliate him," that is, to calm him. If you will visit someone sick, think how to comfort them. Then offer your heart to God. "LORD, at daybreak You hear my voice" (Psalm 5:3).

**A spiritual bouquet.** After times of quiet prayer, Francis said, we should do what people do in a garden: "gather a few flowers as they go, which they keep, and enjoy their scent during the day." Choose one thought, one verse, that struck you, and carry it with you all day.

**A retreat in the heart.** Busy people cannot often go away to pray. But Francis urged that "while externally occupied with business and social duties, you frequently retire within the solitude of your own heart." The crowd around us, he said, surrounds our body, not our soul. In a moment, in a market or on a crowded bus, the heart can turn to God and rest there.

**A hand in the Father's hand.** Francis did not want anxious, hurried believers. Jesus said to Martha, "Martha, Martha, you are fretting and in a stir about so many things" (Luke 10:41). Francis said that we should work carefully, but without worry, trusting God's care:

> "Imitate a little child, whom one sees holding tight with one hand to its father, while with the other it gathers strawberries or blackberries from the wayside hedge. Even so, while you gather and use this world's goods with one hand, always let the other be fast in your Heavenly Father's Hand, and look round from time to time to make sure that He is satisfied with what you are doing, at home or abroad."
> Francis de Sales, *Introduction to the Devout Life*

### Three key insights

1. **True devotion fits our calling and makes us better at it.** God does not ask a mother, a farmer or a soldier to live like a monk. He asks each of us to love Him in the place He has put us, and prayer makes that work lighter and truer.
2. **Thinking ahead in prayer about the people we will meet helps us meet them with gentleness.** Francis taught Philothea to think ahead and plan gentle words for the difficult person she would see. Prayer that looks ahead changes how we meet the day.
3. **We can work carefully without working anxiously, because our Father holds our hand.** A busy life does not have to be a worried life. Like a child picking berries, we can work with one hand and keep the other in His, looking up now and then to see that He is pleased.

### For us today

Most believers in the world are busy people. A woman selling vegetables in a market in Accra, a taxi driver in Jakarta, a nurse on the night shift in Manila, a farmer in the hills of Peru: none of them can spend hours each day in church. Francis de Sales wrote for exactly such people. Unusually for his time, he wrote a whole book on the spiritual life for ordinary Christians living in the world, and he addressed it to a woman with a household to run.

His message is freeing. You do not need to leave your work to be close to God; bring God into your work. A minute of prayer in the morning, a verse carried through the day, a quick turning of the heart in a crowd, a moment of thanks in the evening: these small things, kept faithfully, can make a whole life holy.

### Try this

This week, follow Francis's pattern for a busy day.

- **Pray before you leave your room.** Thank God for the night. Think of one person or task you will meet today, and ask God for the right words and patience.
- **Pick a flower.** Each morning choose one verse, such as Colossians 3:17, and repeat it to yourself through the day.
- **Retreat for a moment.** Three times today, in the middle of work, close your eyes for a few seconds and turn your heart to God.
- **Give thanks at night.** Before sleep, thank God for His help, and ask forgiveness where you failed.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You know how full my days are. Thank You for Your servant Francis de Sales, who taught busy people to love You in their homes and workplaces. Teach me to do everything I say and do in Your name, and to give thanks to the Father through You. Each morning, prepare my heart for the people I will meet, and give me gentle words for those who are difficult. In the crowd and the noise, let my heart rest in You. Keep me from hurry and worry. Hold my hand while I work, and let all I do please You. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Have you ever thought that real devotion was only for pastors, monks or nuns? How does Francis change that picture?
- Who is one difficult person you will meet this week? How could you prepare for that meeting in prayer?
- What usually makes your busy days anxious, and what would it mean to keep one hand in your Father's hand?

- Francis de Sales, *Treatise on the Love of God*, especially "Book VI: The Exercises of Holy Love in Prayer"
- Ochorus Originals, *Jesus, Teacher of Prayer*, especially "Very Early in the Morning"

## Prayer in a Secret Place

> "Whoever makes a home under the cover of the Most High will spend the night in the shade of the Almighty." (Psalm 91:1)

On 19 February 1865 a new church was opened on a hillside in Nagasaki, a port city in the south-west of Japan. It had been built for the French people living there, and it was dedicated to twenty-six Christians who had been put to death on a hill nearby in 1597. Local people called it the French temple and came to look at it. The French priest, Bernard Petitjean, let them in. He had a secret hope. Christianity had been forbidden in Japan for two and a half centuries. Had any Christians survived?

On 17 March, a little less than a month later, a group of twelve or fifteen Japanese men and women stood at the closed door. They seemed respectful rather than curious. Petitjean opened the door and walked to the front of the church, and they followed him. He knelt to pray, and three women of about fifty knelt beside him. Then one of them came close, with her hand on her heart. She looked around as if she feared that the walls might hear, and she whispered to him.

Petitjean reported her words in his letters. All of us here, she told him, have the same heart as you. She meant: we share your faith. She is remembered by the name Yuri.

They came from Urakami, a farming valley just outside the city, and there, they said, nearly everyone had the same heart. Then the woman asked where the statue of Holy Mary was. Petitjean led them to a statue of Mary holding the child Jesus. They knelt before it with joy, and told him that they were now in the "time of sorrow," the weeks before Easter that the church calls Lent. Then other people came in, and the visitors, afraid that officials might see them, slipped away.

Their families had been praying in secret for about two hundred and fifty years, and for more than two hundred of those years without a single priest.

### Who they were

The gospel first came to Japan on 15 August 1549, when Francis Xavier, a Jesuit missionary from Navarre in northern Spain, landed at Kagoshima in the far south. He came with two other Jesuits and with a Japanese believer named Anjirō, whom he had met in Malacca. Xavier, whom we met in chapter 8 as one of Ignatius's first friends, stayed about two years. Others followed, and many Japanese believed, from great lords to poor fishermen. By the year 1600 there may have been as many as three hundred thousand Christians in Japan.

But Japan's rulers came to fear the new faith as a step toward conquest by Spain or Portugal. In 1597 twenty-six believers were crucified on Nishizaka hill in Nagasaki: six friars from Spain, Mexico and India, and twenty Japanese, among them a Jesuit preacher named Paul Miki and three boys aged between about twelve and fourteen. They had been marched in winter for about a month from Kyoto. Accounts written soon afterward say that they sang as they died. One boy of thirteen, Anthony, began Psalm 113, which opens, "Praise the LORD! You who serve the LORD, give praise" (Psalm 113:1). The same accounts say that Paul Miki preached from his cross, prayed for his people and forgave those who killed him.

In 1614 the Tokugawa government banned Christianity across the whole country. Missionaries were sent away or killed. Thousands of believers were tortured and put to death, and many gave up their faith. Japan was closed to almost all foreigners, and every family had to register at a Buddhist temple. From 1629, people in Nagasaki were made to step on a *fumi-e*, a picture of Jesus or Mary cut in bronze or wood, to prove that they were not Christians. In some places this was done every year. By the 1640s the last priests had been killed or had died.

### How they prayed in secret

The church in Japan did not end. In villages around Nagasaki and on the islands nearby, families went on praying behind closed doors. Historians call them the *Kakure Kirishitan*, the hidden Christians.

They had no priests, no church buildings, and soon no printed books, because a book could cost a whole family their lives. So they organized themselves. In each community one person kept the church calendar, so that the people knew when to fast and when to celebrate. Another was the baptizer, who baptized each newborn child with water in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Their prayers were called *orasho*, from the Latin word for prayer. Parents taught them to children by mouth, and they were said softly, so that neighbors would not hear: the Lord's Prayer, the Hail Mary, the Creed, and prayers of sorrow for sin. Some families prayed before a statue of Mary made to look like a Buddhist figure.

They also kept a hope. Their tradition tells of a Japanese catechist named Bastian, put to death in the 1600s, who said that after seven generations priests would come again from across the sea. It is also said that they had learned to look for three signs of true priests: they would be unmarried, they would honor Mary, and they would be sent by the bishop of Rome. That may be why the woman at Ōura asked about the statue of Mary. She was testing whether this was the church her ancestors had known.

Psalm 91 describes this kind of life. The one who "makes a home under the cover of the Most High" is safe in God's shade, even through the night. The psalm goes on: "This is what I say about the LORD: 'He is my shelter and my stronghold, my God, the one I rely on'" (Psalm 91:2). The hidden Christians had no walls to protect them, but they had a hiding place in God. Charles Spurgeon preached on this verse:

> "The shadow of God is not the occasional resort, but the constant abiding-place, of the saint. Here we find not only our consolation, but our habitation. We ought never to be out of the shadow of God. It is to dwellers, not to visitors, that the Lord promises His protection."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Till He Come*

A "habitation" is a home. Jesus told us to pray to "your Father who is there in secret" (Matthew 6:6). E. M. Bounds wrote:

> "God's vision place is the closet. His dwelling place is in secret."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

The "closet" is the inner room. For the hidden Christians it was a farmhouse at night.

We must also be honest. After so many generations without the Bible or teachers, some communities' practice drifted. Many Latin and Portuguese words in their prayers became sounds whose meaning was forgotten, and customs from Buddhism and Japanese folk religion mixed with their faith. When Japan finally lifted the ban in 1873, tens of thousands of hidden Christians were living in the villages and islands around Nagasaki. Many joined the Catholic Church. Others kept their ancestors' ways apart, and only a few such communities remain today.

The cost of coming out was high. From 1867 the Christians of Urakami were arrested, and from 1868 more than three thousand of them were sent into exile in other parts of Japan. Several hundred died before they were allowed home in 1873. They called those years their journey.

### Three key insights

1. **God can keep His people praying when every outward support has been taken away.** The hidden Christians lost their priests, their churches and their books. Yet for generation after generation they kept calling on the Father, and God kept them.
2. **Ordinary believers can pass on prayer when no pastor is there to lead them.** Farmers and fishermen kept the calendar, baptized their babies and taught their children to pray.
3. **The secret place with God is a home to live in, not only a shelter to run to.** Psalm 91 speaks of those who make their home in God. Daily prayer holds us when the storm comes.

### For us today

Today many believers still pray in secret. In North Korea, in parts of Central Asia and the Middle East, and elsewhere, Christians meet quietly in homes and hide their Bibles. Some have no pastor. The hidden Christians of Japan say to them: you are not forgotten, and God can keep His church alive in the dark. The rest of us are told to "keep in mind those in prison, as if you were in chains alongside them" (Hebrews 13:3).

Most of us are free to pray openly, but the hidden Christians still teach us. A family in a crowded flat in Manila, a mother in a village in Malawi, a father on a night shift in São Paulo: each can make home a place where prayer is learned. The hidden Christians also warn us. Prayers handed down without understanding can slowly lose their meaning. So we must pass on not only the words, but the Scripture and the love of Christ behind them.

### Try this

This week, make your home with God.

- **Learn a prayer by heart.** Learn Psalm 91:1-2 and the Lord's Prayer, so that you could pray them even if you had no book.
- **Teach the meaning.** Pray the Lord's Prayer with a child or a new believer, and explain one line each day.
- **Pray for believers without a pastor.** Each day, pray for Christians who must meet in secret or who have no pastor, that God would keep their faith and hope alive and send them faithful teachers.

### A closing prayer

Most High God, You are my shelter and my stronghold. Thank You for the hidden Christians of Japan, who prayed to You in secret for many generations and did not let the light go out. Keep Your church wherever it must hide today. Give courage to those who meet in secret. Teach me to live under Your shadow every day, not only in times of danger. And help me to pass on Your word and Your love to those who come after me. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- The hidden Christians kept praying for more than two hundred years without a priest. What helped them, and what does that teach you about prayer at home?
- What is the difference between running to God only in trouble and making your home in Him?
- How can you make sure that the prayers you teach are understood, not only repeated?

## At Midnight I Rise

> "In the middle of the night I get up to thank You for Your righteous rulings." (Psalm 119:62)

In the autumn of 1626, people gathered in the church of Saint Saviour in Southwark, across the river Thames from the City of London, to bury their bishop. Lancelot Andrewes, bishop of Winchester, had died on 25 September at the age of about seventy-one. He had preached before Queen Elizabeth and King James. He had led one of the teams that translated the King James Bible. He was famous for his learning in many languages. Thomas Fuller, writing a generation later, said that some thought he could have served as "interpreter-general" at the tower of Babel.

His friend John Buckeridge, a fellow bishop, preached at the funeral. But he did not begin with all this learning. He said, using a Latin phrase, that Andrewes's life "was a life of prayer." Then he gave details. "A great part of five hours every day did he spend in prayer to God," he said. When Andrewes's brother Nicholas died, the bishop took it as a sign that his own death was near, and from then on he gave all his time to prayer. And "his prayer book, when he was in private, was seldom seen out of his hands."

What was that prayer book? It was not a printed book. It was a small notebook of prayers that Andrewes had written out for himself, in Greek and Latin with a little Hebrew. He never meant to publish it. A little before his death he gave a clean copy to his friend William Laud. After his death, other copies were passed from hand to hand.

In 1648 a minister named Richard Drake published an English translation. In his preface he described the bishop's own copy. If you had seen it, he wrote, you would have found it "slubbered with his pious hands and watered with his penitential tears." To "slubber" is to smudge or soil. The pages were worn by his fingers and marked by the tears he wept for his sins.

We must be careful here. In 1903 the scholar F. E. Brightman showed that the copy Andrewes gave to Laud is clean and shows little sign of use. So the worn, tear-stained notebook that Drake describes, if he described it rightly, has been lost. But the picture has stayed with Christians for nearly four hundred years: a great scholar and bishop, kneeling in his room, with a little book of prayers in his hands and tears on its pages.

### Who he was

Lancelot Andrewes was born in 1555 near the Tower of London. His father was a master of Trinity House, the company responsible for guiding ships safely along the English coast. Lancelot was a brilliant student. He went to Pembroke Hall at Cambridge in 1571 and later became its head. As a young minister in London he preached a famous series of sermons on the Lord's Prayer.

Under King James he became a bishop, first of Chichester in 1605, then of Ely, and in 1619 of Winchester. In 1604 he was chosen to lead the group of scholars at Westminster who translated the books of the Bible from Genesis to Second Kings. The King James Bible, finished in 1611, shaped the English language and the prayers of English-speaking Christians for centuries.

Andrewes lived in a hard age. In his day the church and the crown were joined, and both Catholics and Puritans who would not conform were fined or imprisoned. Andrewes defended the king's church with strong words, and he belonged to the world of the royal court. He was not a perfect man, and he knew it. In one of his lists of people to pray for, he included those he had caused to stumble.

### How he prayed

Andrewes's notebook is now called the *Preces Privatae*, Latin for "private prayers." It is arranged for each day of the week, with prayers of confession, faith, asking, intercession and thanksgiving. Most of its lines come from the Bible or from the ancient prayers of the Greek and Latin churches. Andrewes did not try to be original. He gathered the words of the Scriptures and of believers before him, and made them his own.

Near the beginning he made a list of the times of prayer he found in Scripture, from "a great while before day" through the hours of the day to evening and night, and last of all, "at midnight." Beside it he wrote two references: Psalm 119:62 and Acts 16:25, where "at midnight, as Paul and Silas prayed, they sang praises to God, and the prisoners listened." Later, in a long prayer that walks through the hours of the day and night, remembering what God did at each hour, he wrote (in Brightman's translation): "Thou who at midnight didst awaken David thy prophet and Paul the apostle to praise Thee: give us also songs by night and to remember Thee upon our beds." This echoes Job, who speaks of "God my Maker, who gives songs in the night" (Job 35:10).

We do not know how often Andrewes himself rose in the night to pray. But his book shows that he wanted even the dark hours to belong to God. E. M. Bounds, writing about praying men, gave him this short line:

> "Bishop Andrewes spent the greatest part of five hours every day in prayer and devotion."
> E. M. Bounds, *Power Through Prayer*

His intercessions are wide. He prayed for the whole church, which he listed as "Catholic, eastern, western, British": the worldwide church, its Eastern and Western branches, and the church in his own land. He prayed for rulers and for the people of every nation. He prayed for children, the hungry, the sick, "those in prison and bonds, the condemned to death; orphans, widows, strangers." He even prayed for those with an "entire want of intercessors," that is, people with no one at all to pray for them.

One prayer is especially kind. Andrewes knew that some people cannot pray as they would like, perhaps because of sickness, hard work or the care of others. So he asked: "them that for reasonable causes give not themselves to prayer remember, Lord, as if they did pray unto Thee."

Andrewes prayed for many people, but not in a vague way. He named them, group by group and often person by person. Andrew Murray taught the same:

> "Each believer has his own circle, his family, his friends, his neighbours. If he were to take one or more of these by name, he would find that this really brings him into the training-school of faith, and leads to personal and pointed dealing with his God."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

### Three key insights

1. **A life of prayer can be built from God's own words, gathered one by one.** Andrewes filled his book with Scripture and with the prayers of the ancient church. When we pray the Bible back to God, we pray with words we know He loves.
2. **The night hours, too, can be filled with thanksgiving.** The psalmist got up in the night to thank God, and Paul and Silas sang in prison at midnight. When we lie awake, we can turn our thoughts into praise.
3. **We can pray for those who have no one else to pray for them.** Andrewes remembered the forgotten, and even those too weak or too busy to pray for themselves. God sees every one of them.

### For us today

Many people are awake at night: a nurse on a night shift in Nairobi, a mother feeding her baby in a small room in Dhaka, a watchman at a gate in Lima, a sick man who cannot sleep, a student worried about tomorrow. The night can be a time of fear. Andrewes reminds us that it can also be a time of prayer. God gives "songs in the night."

Andrewes also shows that we do not need clever words. We can pray with the psalms, with the Lord's Prayer and with the prayers of believers who have gone before us. A worn Bible or a handwritten notebook can become a place where we meet God.

And Andrewes calls us to wide prayer. Who in your town has no one to pray for them? The prisoner, the refugee, the old man who lives alone, the street child: each one can be named before God.

### Try this

This week, gather a prayer book of your own.

- **Start a notebook.** Write down a few verses that you want to pray, and the names of people you will pray for on each day of the week.
- **Pray in the night.** If you wake in the night, thank God for one good thing, and pray Psalm 119:62 or Job 35:10.
- **Remember the forgotten.** Each day, pray for one group that few people pray for: prisoners, refugees, the lonely, the dying.
- **Pray for the busy.** Ask God to remember those who cannot pray today because of sickness or heavy work.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You give songs in the night. Thank You for Your servant Lancelot Andrewes, who filled his days and nights with prayer, and who gathered Your words to pray them back to You. Teach me to pray with Your Scriptures. When I lie awake, turn my worries into thanksgiving. Give me a heart that remembers the forgotten: the prisoner, the stranger, the widow and the orphan, and all who have no one to pray for them. Remember also those who cannot pray today, as if they prayed to You. And let my own life become a life of prayer, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Andrewes gathered his prayers from Scripture and from older believers. Which verse or prayer would you put first in your own prayer book?
- What do you usually think about when you lie awake at night? How could you turn those hours toward God?
- Who in your community might have no one praying for them? How could you begin to pray for them by name?

## Taste and See

> "Taste and see how good the LORD is! How blessed is the person who makes Him their refuge!" (Psalm 34:8)

In 1630 a new rector came to Bemerton, a small village about a mile from the city of Salisbury in the south of England. His name was George Herbert. He was thirty-seven, thin, and often ill. A few years earlier he had been a well-known speaker at Cambridge University, and many thought he would rise high at the king's court. Now he cared for two small country churches among farmers and shepherds.

Twice a day, at ten in the morning and at four in the afternoon, Herbert crossed the lane from his house to the little church of Saint Andrew to pray the daily services of the Prayer Book. His wife Jane came with him, with the three orphaned nieces they had taken into their home, and the rest of the household. A small bell was rung for prayers.

Izaak Walton, who wrote Herbert's life in 1670, tells what happened when the bell rang. Some of the poorer people of the parish loved and honored Herbert so much "that they would let their plough rest when Mr. Herbert's saint's-bell rung to prayers, that they might also offer their devotions to God with him." Then they went back to their work in the fields.

Herbert's time at Bemerton was short. Less than three years later he was dying of consumption, the lung disease we now call tuberculosis. As he lay dying, Walton tells us, he gave a friend a little book of poems to carry to Nicholas Ferrar, a close friend who led a household of prayer at Little Gidding. Herbert asked him to tell Ferrar that he would find in it "a picture of the many spiritual conflicts that have passed betwixt God and my soul, before I could subject mine to the will of Jesus my master: in whose service I have now found perfect freedom." If Ferrar thought it might "turn to the advantage of any dejected poor soul," he should publish it. If not, he should burn it.

George Herbert died on 1 March 1633, a month before his fortieth birthday. Ferrar did not burn the book. Later that year it was printed under the title *The Temple*. It has been read and loved ever since.

We should remember that Walton wrote nearly forty years after Herbert's death, and that he paints Herbert as a saint almost without fault. Some of his details may be polished. But the poems themselves remain, and they let us hear how Herbert prayed.

### Who he was

George Herbert was born on 3 April 1593 in Montgomery, in Wales, into a noble and wealthy family. His father died when he was three, and his mother Magdalen raised ten children with great care. George went to Westminster School in London and then to Trinity College, Cambridge. There he became the university's Public Orator, the man who spoke for the university on great occasions, often in Latin. In 1624 he sat in Parliament.

But the doors to a life at court closed. King James died in 1625, and several of Herbert's powerful friends died at about the same time. Herbert went through a long inner struggle. In the end he gave himself to the ministry. He married Jane Danvers in 1629, and in 1630 he was made a priest and sent to Bemerton. He repaired the church and the rectory with his own money, visited the sick and gave to the poor.

He also wrote a short book for country pastors, *A Priest to the Temple*, which he called "a mark to aim at." It describes a parson who prays with his people in church and teaches every person in his house to pray morning and night. Herbert even liked the old village custom of saying "God send us the light of heaven" when a lamp was brought in at evening. The parson, he wrote, "is rather glad of catching opportunities" to praise and pray to God at all times.

### How he prayed

Herbert's poems are prayers. Most of them speak directly to God. Some are full of joy, some full of complaint, and many move from one to the other.

One poem is simply called "Prayer." It has no main verb. It is a list of pictures, one after another, as if Herbert were trying to describe something too large for words. Prayer is "the soul in paraphrase, heart in pilgrimage." It is "Heaven in ordinarie," that is, heaven come down into ordinary life. And the poem ends with two quiet words: "something understood." When we pray, we do not explain God. But in His presence, something is understood.

Another poem, "The Call," is a prayer built on the words of Jesus, "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). It begins, "Come, my Way, my Truth, my Life," and goes on to call Christ "my Light, my Feast, my Strength" and "my Joy, my Love, my Heart."

In "The Elixir," Herbert asked God to let him see Him in everything he did. F. B. Meyer used some of its lines as a prayer at the end of one day's reading:

> "Teach me, my God and King, / In all things Thee to see, / And what I do in anything, / To do it as for Thee. / A servant with this clause / Makes drudgery divine! / Who sweeps a room as for Thy laws, / Makes that and th'action fine."
> George Herbert, quoted in F. B. Meyer, *Our Daily Walk*

"Drudgery" is dull, tiring work. Herbert's point is the reason behind the work: the same broom, pushed "as for Thee," becomes an offering. (In chapter 16 we will meet a kitchen brother in Paris who found another secret for the working day: keeping God company while he worked.)

The last poem in the main part of *The Temple* is called "Love." In it the soul is invited to a feast by Love, who is Christ. "Love bade me welcome: yet my soul drew back, Guiltie of dust and sinne." The guest says he is not worthy even to look at his host. Love takes his hand and reminds him who bore the blame. Then the poem ends: "You must sit down, sayes Love, and taste my meat: So I did sit and eat."

This is the heart of Psalm 34:8. David does not say, "Hear about the LORD," but "Taste and see how good the LORD is!" A person can describe honey all day, but only tasting tells you that it is sweet. Peter writes to new believers, "now that you have tasted that the Lord is kind" (1 Peter 2:3). Herbert's prayer is the prayer of a man who had tasted. He had argued with God, run from Him and complained to Him. But in the end he sat down at Christ's table and ate.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer is a way to taste God's goodness, not only to talk about it.** Psalm 34 invites us to taste and see. Herbert called prayer "Heaven in ordinarie," heaven brought into everyday life. In prayer we come to know God, not only to know about Him.
2. **We may come to Christ's table as we are, because He bore our blame.** In Herbert's poem the guest felt too dusty and sinful to sit down. Love answered that He had borne the blame. Our unworthiness is not a reason to stay away.
3. **Our struggles with God, told to Him honestly, can lead us into the freedom of His service.** Herbert's poems are full of conflict, and he did not hide it. At the end he said that in the service of Jesus he had found perfect freedom.

### For us today

Herbert wrote in a quiet English village, but his words are for every land. A farmer in Kenya who stops at noon to pray, a market woman in Accra, a factory worker in Vietnam, a student in Peru: each can find "Heaven in ordinarie" in the middle of an ordinary day.

Herbert also helps those who feel too unworthy to pray. Perhaps you have stayed away from God because of something you have done. Love still says, "You must sit down." Christ does not invite only the clean and the strong to His table. He invites sinners, and He feeds them Himself.

And Herbert gives permission to be honest. His poems include anger, sadness and doubt, all spoken to God. If your heart is in conflict, tell Him so. That too is prayer.

### Try this

This week, taste and see that the Lord is good.

- **Stop when the bell rings.** Choose a sign in your day, such as the call to prayer from a nearby church, a school bell or the noon hour, and stop for a moment to pray.
- **Write a list prayer.** Like Herbert's "Prayer," write down five pictures of what prayer means to you, and offer them to God.
- **Pray "The Call."** Pray slowly, "Come, my Way, my Truth, my Life," and think about each name of Christ.
- **Come to the table.** When you share the Lord's Supper, or simply eat your daily bread, thank Christ that He welcomes sinners.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, my Way, my Truth and my Life, I want to taste and see how good You are. Thank You for Your servant George Herbert, who argued with You, came back to You, and found perfect freedom in Your service. When I feel too dusty and sinful to come near, remind me that You bore the blame. Take my hand, and bring me to Your table. Teach me to see You in all things, and to do what I do as for You. Let my prayer be heaven in the middle of my ordinary days. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Herbert called prayer "something understood." Have you ever understood something in prayer that you could not put into words?
- In the poem "Love," the guest draws back because he feels unworthy. What makes you draw back from God, and what does Love say to that?
- Herbert's poems are honest about his struggles with God. What struggle could you bring to God in prayer this week?

## The Bruised Reed

> "He will not snap off a split reed or smother a wick that is barely burning; with faithfulness He will bring justice out." (Isaiah 42:3)

In the 1620s, in the chapel of Gray's Inn in London, a preacher stood up before a congregation of lawyers. Gray's Inn was one of the "inns of court," the colleges where young men trained for the law. Many powerful men in England had studied there. Their preacher was Richard Sibbes, a quiet scholar from Cambridge and the son of a wheelwright, a man who made wheels for carts.

His text was a promise about God's chosen Servant in Isaiah 42, which Matthew quotes in his Gospel about Jesus (Matthew 12:20). In the English Bible of Sibbes's day, it spoke of a "bruised reed" and of "smoking flax." Sibbes explained the pictures. A reed is a weak plant that grows by the water. A bruised reed is cracked and bent, almost useless. Flax is the plant used for the wick of an oil lamp, and smoking flax is a wick that gives more smoke than light, about to go out. Anyone else would snap the reed and throw it away, and pinch out the wick. But the Lord will not.

Sibbes said that these are pictures of God's people. They are, he said, "not trees, but reeds; and not whole, but bruised reeds." They are believers who feel their sin and their weakness, whose faith is small and mixed with doubt, whose prayers come out more like smoke than flame. And Christ does not break them. In fact, said Sibbes, more is meant than is spoken: Christ "will not only not break the bruised reed," but "he will cherish them." To cherish is to care for something tenderly because it is precious.

Sibbes's sermons on this verse were printed in 1630 as a small book, *The Bruised Reed*. It traveled far. Soon afterward a poor pedlar, a man who walked from house to house selling songs and small books, came to the door of a farmhouse in Shropshire. A teenage boy named Richard Baxter lived there, and his father bought *The Bruised Reed* from the pedlar. Baxter wrote long afterward that the book suited his state and seemed to come to him at just the right time. It showed him the love of God more clearly than before, and how much he owed to Jesus Christ. We will meet Baxter in the next chapter.

Sibbes died in 1635. Years later Izaak Walton, the biographer of George Herbert whom we met in the last chapter, wrote two lines in his own copy of one of Sibbes's books: "Of this blest man let this just praise be given: Heaven was in him, before he was in heaven."

### Who he was

Richard Sibbes was born in 1577 at Tostock, a village in Suffolk in the east of England. He went to school at Bury St Edmunds and in 1595 entered St John's College, Cambridge. He became a preacher at Holy Trinity Church in Cambridge, then from 1617 the preacher at Gray's Inn in London. In 1626 he also became Master of Catherine Hall, a college in Cambridge.

Sibbes was a Puritan, one of those who wanted the Church of England to be more fully reformed by the Bible and who stressed preaching and holy living. But he stayed in the Church of England all his life and worshiped with its Prayer Book. Like most Protestants of his time, he spoke strongly against the Church of Rome. But toward weak and troubled believers he was famously gentle. People came to call him "the heavenly Doctor Sibbes."

### God hears weak prayers

Sibbes knew that many believers feel that their prayers are too weak to count. He wrote, "Broken hearts can yield but broken prayers." Then he pictured a Christian who complains that he cannot pray, because his thoughts wander so much. Sibbes asks: has God put into your heart a desire to pray? Then God will hear the desires of His own Spirit in you. He goes on:

> "God can pick sense out of a confused prayer. These desires cry louder in his ears, than thy sins. Sometimes a Christian hath such confused thoughts, he can say nothing, but as a child crieth, O Father, not able to shew what it needs, as Moses at the Red Sea."
> Richard Sibbes, *The Bruised Reed*

Paul says the same: "What we should pray for, and how, we do not know. But the Spirit Himself pleads on our behalf, with groans that no words carry" (Romans 8:26). And David prays, "not one of my sighs escapes You" (Psalm 38:9).

Sibbes then asks the question that troubles many hearts: can a holy God really accept such a prayer? Yes, he answers: God "will accept that which is his own, and pardon that which is ours." Sibbes gives his reasons. Our weak prayers come from God's own children and from His own Spirit. They ask for what He Himself wills. And they are offered through Christ, who takes them and mixes them with His own sweet incense, as in the picture of Revelation 8:3. Then Sibbes adds:

> "There is never a holy sigh, never a tear we shed, lost. And as every grace increaseth by exercise of itself, so doth the grace of prayer; by prayer we learn to pray."
> Richard Sibbes, *The Bruised Reed*

So he tells us not to wait until we are strong. "Pray as we are able," he wrote, "hear as we are able, strive as we are able, do as we are able." When everything seems dark, he says, we should "do as mariners do, cast anchor in the dark." Sailors cannot always see the shore, but they can hold fast where they are.

Sibbes also had a word for pastors and leaders. He warned that "the ambassadors of so gentle a Saviour should not be over-masterly." Those who lead God's people must not lay heavy loads on weak believers. They should treat young and struggling Christians as Christ treats them.

### Three key insights

1. **Christ does not throw away bruised people; He bends down to mend them.** A bruised reed seems useless, and a smoking wick seems finished. But Jesus cherishes the weak, the doubting and the ashamed, and He will not give up on them.
2. **A weak and confused prayer that comes from a true desire is precious to God.** God can find sense in our stumbling words. Christ presents our prayers to the Father, and not one holy sigh is lost.
3. **We learn to pray by praying, so we must be patient with ourselves and with others.** No one begins strong. Sibbes calls us to pray as we are able, and to be as gentle with young believers as Christ is with us.

### For us today

Many believers feel like a bruised reed. A widow in Uganda whose prayers seem to bring no answer, a young man in Brazil who keeps falling into the same sin, a woman in Korea who is too depressed to pray, a believer in prison who denied Christ under pressure and now feels that he has failed: each may think that God has given up on them. Sibbes says that God has not. Christ does not break the bruised reed.

Sibbes also speaks to churches. In every land there are new believers whose faith is small and smoky. They need patient teachers, not harsh judges. Pastors, parents and leaders can ask themselves: do I stoop to the weakest, as Christ does?

And if your own prayers seem like smoke, keep praying. Say what you can. Even "I believe! Help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24) is a prayer that Jesus answered. The father who said those words was not strong in faith. He was honest about how weak his faith was, and he brought it to Jesus anyway. That is all Christ asks of a bruised reed.

### Try this

This week, rest in Christ's gentleness.

- **Pray a short, honest prayer.** When you cannot find words, simply say, "Father," or pray Romans 8:26 and trust the Spirit to pray for you.
- **Bring your weakness, not only your strength.** Tell God one area where you feel like a bruised reed, and ask Him to cherish and mend it.
- **Encourage one weak believer.** Visit, call or write to someone who is struggling in their faith. Listen first, and then pray with them gently.
- **Read one chapter.** Read Sibbes's chapter "Set upon Duties notwithstanding Weaknesses," and underline one sentence to pray over.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You do not break the bruised reed or put out the wick that is barely burning. Thank You for Your servant Richard Sibbes, who taught weak believers to trust Your gentle heart. You know how small my faith is and how weak my prayers are. Yet You hear the desires of Your Spirit in me, and You present my prayers to the Father with Your own sweet incense. Do not let me give up. Mend what is cracked in me, and fan my little flame into light. Make me gentle with others who are weak, as You are gentle with me. Amen.

- Why do you think God values the desire to pray, even when the words are confused?
- Who in your church or family is like a bruised reed? How could you treat them as Christ treats you?

## Set Your Mind on Things Above

> "You have been raised with Christ, then. So seek what is above, where Christ sits at the right hand of God. Fix your thoughts on what is above, not on what is on earth." (Colossians 3:1-2)

In 1647 a young English pastor lay sick in a borrowed bed, far from home. His name was Richard Baxter. He was about thirty-one years old, thin, pale and often ill. For five years England had been torn apart by civil war, the king's armies against the armies of Parliament. Baxter had served as a chaplain with Parliament's soldiers. Now his body had broken down.

Friends took him in. Sir Thomas Rous and his wife cared for him at their house at Rous Lench, a village in the county of Worcestershire. Baxter was sure he was going to die. A preface written for an edition of his book a century later says that he was "in continual expectation of death for many months." He had no library with him. He had only his Bible.

What does a man do when he expects to die soon? Baxter began to write about heaven, about the rest that God has promised to His people. He did not write for anyone else. He wrote, the same preface says, "merely for his own use."

But Baxter did not die. Slowly he grew stronger. He went back to his church in the town of Kidderminster, and he preached what he had written to his people, week after week. In 1650 it was printed as a large book, *The Saints' Everlasting Rest*. The title comes from Hebrews: "So a Sabbath rest is still waiting for God's people" (Hebrews 4:9). Baxter later said that thinking on this heavenly subject had done him more good "than all the studies of my life."

The book was loved in England for more than a hundred years. And the man who expected to die at thirty-one lived to be seventy-six. For forty-four more years he worked, wrote and suffered.

### Who he was

Richard Baxter was born in 1615 in Shropshire, in the west of England. He had little formal schooling, and he never went to a university. He taught himself by reading, especially earlier Puritans such as Richard Sibbes, whose *Bruised Reed*, bought from a pedlar at the door, had opened the love of God to him as a boy (see the last chapter).

In 1641 the people of Kidderminster asked him to be their preacher. It was a town of weavers. Baxter noticed that, as they stood at their looms, they could keep a book open in front of them. He gave a small book to every family, and a Bible to every poor family that had none. With an assistant, he spent every Monday and Tuesday, from morning almost to night, meeting the families of the parish one by one. There were more than eight hundred families, and he tried to speak with every one of them each year.

The town changed. Baxter later wrote that on Sundays "you might hear an hundred families singing psalms and repeating sermons, as you passed through the streets." When he first came, he said, there was "about one family in a street that worshipped God and called on his name." When he left, there were streets where nearly every family did.

After 1660 England had a king again, and new laws said that every minister must use the official prayer book and accept other rules. Baxter could not agree to all of them. In 1662 he was one of about two thousand ministers who had to leave their churches. He was fined, his books were taken, and when he was about seventy he was put in prison. Yet he kept writing and preaching wherever he could, and he worked all his life for peace among Christians who disagreed. He died in London in 1691.

### How he prayed

Baxter's great gift to us is a way of praying that he called heavenly contemplation, or meditation. It is not the same as asking God for things. It is taking time to think slowly and deliberately about God and the joy He has promised, until our hearts are warmed, and then turning those thoughts into prayer.

Paul's command in Colossians 3 is the root of it. Because we have been raised with Christ, we should "seek what is above, where Christ sits at the right hand of God." Paul does not tell us to think about clouds and harps. He tells us to think about Christ, who is in heaven, and who "is your life" (Colossians 3:4).

Baxter gave practical advice. Set a time for it, he said, and keep that time. He chose his own hour carefully:

> "I have always found that the fittest time for myself is, the evening, from sun-setting to the twilight. I the rather mention this, because it was the experience of a better and wiser man; for it is expressly said, Isaac went out to meditate in the field at the eventide."
> Richard Baxter, *The Saints' Everlasting Rest*

Baxter knew that not everyone owns their own time. Many are so poor, he wrote, that their families' needs leave them no quiet hour. For them he had kind advice: "Servants and laborers must take that season which their business will best afford: either while at work, or in travelling, or when they lie awake in the night."

Then, he said, take one great truth about heaven and think about it. Ask yourself questions. Speak to your own heart, as the psalmist did: "Why are you bent so low, my soul? Why this unrest inside me? Wait for God in hope" (Psalm 42:5). Let the truth stir up love, longing and joy. And then speak to God about it. Thinking leads to feeling, and feeling leads to prayer.

Why does this matter? Because, Baxter said, a heart that has tasted heaven is harder to pull away from God:

> "When thou hast had a fresh delightful taste of heaven, thou wilt not be so easily persuaded from it. You cannot persuade a child to part with his sweetmeats, while he hath the taste in his mouth."
> Richard Baxter, *The Saints' Everlasting Rest*

(Sweetmeats are sweets or candy.)

Heaven-mindedness did not make Baxter lazy about the earth. It made him work harder. In *The Reformed Pastor*, his book for ministers, he wrote that "Prayer must carry on our work as well as preaching: he preacheth not heartily to his people, that prayeth not earnestly for them." The man who thought most about heaven also visited the most homes in Kidderminster.

### Three key insights

1. **Remembering the joy ahead gives us courage for the troubles of today.** Baxter wrote about heaven when he expected to die, and it carried him through forty-four more hard years. Paul says our troubles are "light and last only a moment" compared to the glory to come (2 Corinthians 4:17).
2. **Meditation turns truths we know into joys we feel.** Many believers know that heaven is real, but the knowledge stays cold. When we take time to think slowly about God's promises and speak to our own hearts, the truth warms us and becomes prayer.
3. **Those who serve people must first plead with God for them.** Baxter knew that he could not change a single heart by his own words. He prayed earnestly for the families he visited, because only God can give faith and repentance.

### For us today

Some people think that Christians who talk about heaven are trying to escape from the real world. Baxter's life shows the opposite. A believer who knows where he is going can face prison, sickness and loss without being crushed. A believer whose treasure is in heaven is free to give generously on earth.

This is good news for the suffering church. In many lands, in Asia, Africa, the Middle East and Latin America, believers lose their homes, their jobs or their freedom because of Christ. Others live in poverty, or with long illness, or in crowded refugee camps. To them Paul says: "we hold our citizenship in heaven" (Philippians 3:20). No one can take that away.

It is also a word for those who are comfortable. In the busy cities of every continent it is easy to fill every hour with work, screens and plans. Baxter warned that our hearts can become so loaded with earthly cares that they cannot rise to God at all. A few minutes each day spent thinking about what is above can set our whole life in order.

### Try this

This week, practice a short time of heavenly meditation each day.

- **Choose a time.** Like Baxter, pick a time that suits your day, perhaps at sunset, on your way to work, or before you sleep, and keep it.
- **Take one promise.** Read one verse about heaven, such as John 14:2-3, Revelation 21:4 or Psalm 73:25-26, and think about it slowly.
- **Speak to your heart.** Ask yourself: What does this promise mean for me today? If it is true, why am I so troubled?
- **Turn it into prayer.** End by thanking God for what He has prepared, and pray for one person you know who needs this hope.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, You have been raised from the dead, and You sit at the right hand of God. You are my life, and my life is hidden with You. Forgive me for setting my heart on things that cannot last. Lift my thoughts to You, and let the joy that is waiting for Your people warm my cold heart. When I am sick, give me hope. When I am busy, call me back to You. When I am afraid of death, remind me that You have prepared a place for me. And as I think about heaven, make me more faithful and more loving here on earth, until the day I see You face to face. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Baxter wrote about heaven when he expected to die. How might thinking about heaven change the way you face a hard time in your own life?
- What usually fills your thoughts when your mind is free? What would help you to set your mind more often on what is above?
- Baxter said that we cannot help people without praying for them. Who are the people God has given you to serve, and how often do you pray for them by name?

## A Heart Without Words

> "People, rely on Him in every season; empty your hearts out before Him. God is our shelter." (Psalm 62:8)

On 12 November 1660, a small group of believers met in a farmhouse at Lower Samsell, near the village of Harlington in the English county of Bedfordshire. They had invited a preacher named John Bunyan. He was a tinker, a man who traveled from village to village mending pots and pans. He had little schooling, but for some years he had been preaching in the villages around Bedford.

That day a friend warned him that a warrant was out for his arrest. He could have slipped away. But he asked himself what the young believers would think if their preacher ran, so he stayed and began the meeting. Bunyan later wrote that when the constable came in, he found them "only with our Bibles in our hands, ready to speak and hear the Word of God." They had already begun in prayer.

England had a king again, and old laws were being used against church meetings held outside the parish church. In January 1661 Bunyan stood before the judges in Bedford. Bunyan wrote down the conversation himself, and his account was printed long after his death.

The chief judge, Justice Keelin, asked why he did not go to the parish church. Bunyan said he did not find it commanded in the Word of God.

"We were commanded to pray," said the judge.

"But not by the Common Prayer Book," Bunyan answered. He meant the official book of prayers that every parish was required to use.

"How then?"

"With the Spirit," said Bunyan, and he quoted Paul's words about praying with the Spirit and with the understanding (1 Corinthians 14:15).

Then another judge asked him a question: "What do you count prayer? Do you think it is to say a few words over before or among a people?"

Bunyan's answer is one of the best short descriptions of prayer ever given in a courtroom. "No, not so; for men might have many elegant, or excellent words, and yet not pray at all; but when a man prayeth, he doth, through a sense of those things which he wants, which sense is begotten by the Spirit, pour out his heart before God through Christ; though his words be not so many and so excellent as others are."

The judges agreed: "That was true." But they sent him back to prison. Bunyan would stay there, with a few short breaks, for twelve years.

### Who he was

John Bunyan was born in 1628 in Elstow, near Bedford, the son of a tinker. At sixteen he became a soldier in the civil war. Afterward he married a young woman who brought two Christian books into their poor home. Their first child, Mary, born in 1650, was blind.

For several years Bunyan went through a terrible spiritual struggle. He tells the whole story in *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners*. He was tormented by fear that he had committed a sin God would never forgive. Some days it seemed impossible even to pray. Yet he would not stop coming to Christ:

> "These places did pinch me very sore; yet, my case being desperate, I thought with myself I can but die; and if it must be so, it shall once be said, that such an one died at the foot of Christ in prayer."
> John Bunyan, *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners*

At last God gave him peace through the righteousness of Christ. He joined a small church in Bedford, and soon the members asked him to preach. His first wife died in 1658, and a year later he married a young woman named Elizabeth, who bravely cared for his four children while he was in prison. In prison he made laces to earn a little money for his family, and he wrote. *Grace Abounding* was written there, and later he began his most famous book, *The Pilgrim's Progress*. He was set free in 1672, became the pastor of the church in Bedford, and died in London in 1688.

His hardest pain in prison was his family, especially his blind daughter:

> "Poor child! thought I, what sorrow art thou like to have for thy portion in this world! Thou must be beaten, must beg, suffer hunger, cold, nakedness, and a thousand calamities, though I cannot now endure the wind should blow upon thee. But yet recalling myself, thought I, I must venture you all with God, though it goeth to the quick to leave you."
> John Bunyan, *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners*

("It goeth to the quick" means it cuts deep, to the most painful place.) Mary died some years before her father.

### What he taught about prayer

In 1662, in his prison cell, Bunyan wrote a small book on prayer. It is usually called *I Will Pray with the Spirit*, after the words of Paul in 1 Corinthians 14:15. In it he gave this definition: "Prayer is a sincere, sensible, affectionate pouring out of the heart or soul to God, through Christ, in the strength and assistance of the Holy Spirit." "Sincere" means honest, "sensible" means truly feeling our need, and "affectionate" means full of strong desire.

The heart of his teaching is the picture in our verse. "Empty your hearts out before Him," says the psalmist (Psalm 62:8). Bunyan quoted this psalm, with others like it, and said that pouring out the soul means that in prayer "there goeth the very life and whole strength to God." Hannah prayed this way. She said, "I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD" (1 Samuel 1:15).

Bunyan knew that a heart full of need often cannot find the right words. That does not stop the prayer, for, as we saw with Sibbes in chapter 13, God hears the Spirit's groans within us. Bunyan put it simply: "The best prayers have often more groans than words." A collection of his sayings, printed after his death, puts it in a line that many have remembered: "When thou prayest, rather let thy hearts be without words, than thy words without a heart."

We should be fair here. Bunyan refused to use the Book of Common Prayer, and he argued against it sharply, because the law was forcing it on everyone. Many faithful Christians, then and now, have prayed from that book with all their hearts, as we saw in chapter 4. Even in court Bunyan said that those who wanted to use it should have their liberty. His point is one that every Christian can share: words, whether written or spoken freely, are only prayer when the heart is in them.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer is pouring out the heart to God, not putting on a show of fine words.** God does not count our words or grade our speech. He looks for honest need, real desire and trust in Christ, even when the words are few and plain.
2. **Even in despair, the safest place to be is at the feet of Christ in prayer.** When Bunyan felt that heaven was shut against him, he decided that if he died, he would die praying. The darkest feelings are not a reason to stop coming to Him.
3. **A prison can take away our freedom, but it cannot shut the door between us and God.** Bunyan lost twelve years and could not protect his family, yet no jailer could stop his prayers. In his cell he poured out his heart, and God used those years to bless the whole church.

### For us today

Bunyan's story is the story of many believers today. In some countries Christians are arrested for meeting in homes, just as he was. Pastors sit in prison while their families struggle to find food, and parents carry the weight of children with special needs. Bunyan shows them that a prison cell can still be a place of prayer.

His teaching also frees ordinary believers. Many people think they cannot pray because they do not have fine words. Some cannot read. Some are too tired or too sad to put a sentence together. Bunyan says: come anyway. Empty your heart before God. A groan, a sigh or a few broken words offered through Christ are true prayer.

In *The Pilgrim's Progress*, when Christian walks through the dark valley and his sword is no use against the flames, he takes up "another weapon, called All-prayer," and cries, "O Lord, I beseech thee, deliver my soul." That weapon is in the hands of every believer, everywhere.

### Try this

This week, practice pouring out your heart to God.

- **Tell Him everything.** Find a quiet place, even a corner or a walk outside, and tell God honestly what is in your heart, as Psalm 62:8 invites you to do.
- **Speak plainly.** Pray this week without any fine words, as you would talk to someone you trust, and notice that God does not need them.
- **Check your heart, not only your words.** Before you pray the Lord's Prayer or a familiar prayer, stop and ask God to put your heart into it.
- **Entrust someone you cannot protect.** Like Bunyan with his blind daughter, name one person you love but cannot keep safe, and place them in God's hands.

### A closing prayer

Father, You invite me to rely on You in every season and to empty out my heart before You. Here is my heart, with all its fears, needs and sins. I come to You through Jesus Christ, Your Son, who opened the way. Thank You for Your servant John Bunyan, who prayed in prison and taught plain people to pray. When I have no words, let Your Spirit pray within me. When I feel shut out, keep me at the feet of Christ. Take care of the people I love when I cannot protect them. Strengthen every believer in prison for Your name, and comfort their families. Let my words never be without a heart, and let my heart always find its shelter in You. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Bunyan told the judges that people might use many excellent words "and yet not pray at all." When are you most in danger of praying with words but not with your heart?
- Have you ever felt too discouraged to pray? What does Bunyan's decision to die "at the foot of Christ in prayer" teach you?
- Bunyan placed his blind daughter in God's hands when he could not care for her. Who do you need to entrust to God in prayer today?

## Among the Pots and Pans

> "I have placed the LORD in front of me at all times; since He is there at my right hand, I will never stumble." (Psalm 16:8)

One winter, about four hundred years ago, a young peasant of eighteen stood looking at a tree. He lived in Lorraine, a region in the east of what is now France. His name was Nicolas Herman. The tree in front of him was bare. Every leaf was gone, and the branches looked dead.

But Nicolas knew that the tree was not dead. In a little while, when spring came, the leaves would grow again. After the leaves would come the flowers, and after the flowers, the fruit. As he thought about this, something happened inside him. He saw, all at once, how great and faithful God is, the God who brings life out of winter every year without fail.

Many years later, in 1666, a priest in Paris named Joseph de Beaufort sat down to talk with him. By then Nicolas was a man in his fifties, living in a monastery and known as Brother Lawrence. He told Beaufort that "in the winter, seeing a tree stripped of its leaves, and considering that within a little time the leaves would be renewed and after that the flowers and fruit appear, he received a high view of the Providence and Power of God, which has never since been effaced from his soul." That view, he said, set him free from the world and "kindled in him such a love for God" that he could not tell whether it had grown any greater in all the years since.

It was a quiet beginning. There was no preacher, no crowd and no miracle. There was only a young man, a cold day and a bare tree. But God used that ordinary moment to begin a life of prayer that has helped countless people.

Nicolas did not become a famous preacher or a great scholar. He became a cook. For about fifteen years he worked in the kitchen of a monastery in Paris, among the pots and pans, the fires and the noise. And there he learned to pray without stopping.

### Who he was

Nicolas Herman was born around 1614 into a poor family. He grew up during the Thirty Years' War, a terrible war that tore through much of Europe. As a young man he became a soldier, and he was wounded. For the rest of his life he was lame. Then he worked as a footman, a servant in a rich man's house. He later told Beaufort that he had been "a great awkward fellow who broke everything."

In 1640, at about the age of twenty-six, he entered a monastery of the Discalced Carmelites in Paris. This was the same reformed order that Teresa of Ávila had begun in Spain, whom we met in chapter 6. He did not become a priest. He was a lay brother, a member who did the practical work of the house. He took the name Lawrence of the Resurrection.

They put him in the kitchen. Beaufort says it was work "to which he had naturally a great aversion," which means he did not like it at all. Later he was given other work. Once he was sent on a journey to buy wine for the monastery. He could not walk around the boat, so he rolled himself over the barrels.

The life of prayer did not come easily. For four years, Lawrence was troubled by the fear that he would be lost forever. When he first tried to pray, his thoughts kept wandering, and he spent much of his prayer time pulling them back. But he kept on.

By the time Beaufort met him, people came to Lawrence for advice. After he died in 1691, Beaufort gathered his notes of their conversations, and some of Lawrence's letters, into a small book, *The Practice of the Presence of God*. Catholics and Protestants have both treasured it ever since.

### How he prayed

Lawrence's way of prayer was very simple. He tried to remember, all through the day, that God was with him, and to talk to Him as he worked. He did not keep prayer for the chapel only. He brought it into the kitchen.

Beaufort tells us that when Lawrence began his work, he would first think through what had to be done. Then he would say to God:

> "O my God, since Thou art with me, and I must now, in obedience to Thy commands, apply my mind to these outward things, I beseech Thee to grant me the grace to continue in Thy Presence; and to this end do Thou prosper me with Thy assistance, receive all my works, and possess all my affections."
> Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*

As he worked, he kept talking quietly with God, asking for help and offering each task to Him. When he finished, he looked back. If he had done well, he thanked God. If not, he asked forgiveness and started again, "without being discouraged."

This is what David meant: "I have placed the LORD in front of me at all times" (Psalm 16:8). Lawrence did not try to think deep thoughts about God all day long. He simply kept God before him, the way a man working beside a friend knows that his friend is there without staring at him, and speaks to him now and then.

George Herbert, as we saw in chapter 12, asked God to let him do each task "as for Thee." Lawrence agreed: Beaufort records his belief that "our sanctification did not depend upon changing our works, but in doing that for God's sake, which we commonly do for our own." But the heart of his practice was company. He did his work not only for God, but with God, talking to Him as he went.

In one of his letters he described this life, and warned against seeking it for the wrong reason:

> "There is not in the world a kind of life more sweet and delightful than that of a continual conversation with God. Those only can comprehend it who practice and experience it; yet I do not advise you to do it from that motive. It is not pleasure which we ought to seek in this exercise; but let us do it from a principle of love, and because God would have us."
> Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*

### Three key insights

1. **We can keep up a quiet conversation with God while our hands are busy.** Lawrence did not leave the kitchen to find God. He kept God before him at the stove and spoke to Him in short, simple words, asking for help and offering each task as he went.
2. **When we forget God, we can simply turn back to Him without long discouragement.** Lawrence did not punish himself for every failure. He asked for pardon, trusted Christ, and started again at once. Turning back quickly is part of the practice, not the end of it.
3. **In time, remembering God can become easier than forgetting Him.** Lawrence's thoughts wandered for years. But he said that by "rising after my falls," he came to a place where it was harder not to think of God than it had once been to remember Him.

### For us today

Most believers in the world do not spend their days in quiet rooms. They farm, sell in markets, drive buses, clean houses, cook for large families and care for children and the sick. Many work long hours for little pay. Some, like Lawrence, live with a body that is weak or injured. Lawrence's message to all of them is good news: God is not waiting for them somewhere else. He is beside them at the stove, the loom and the steering wheel, and He is glad to be spoken to there.

It is also a message for those who feel clumsy, unimportant or too ordinary to be useful. Lawrence was a lame, awkward man who broke things, and he spent his life in a kitchen. Yet God made him a teacher of prayer for the whole church.

And it is a message for anyone who has failed at prayer. Lawrence's wandering mind and his years of fear did not disqualify him. He kept coming back. So can you.

### Try this

This week, practice remembering God in your daily work.

- **Begin with an offering.** Before you start your work each morning, pray in your own words as Lawrence did: "Lord, You are with me. Help me to stay with You, and receive all my work today."
- **Talk with Him as you work.** Choose one task you do every day, such as washing dishes, sweeping or walking to work, and use it to speak with God in a few plain words while you do it.
- **Turn back without fear.** When you notice you have forgotten God for hours, do not scold yourself. Simply say, "Lord, here I am again," and go on.
- **Look at creation.** Find a tree, a plant or the sky, and thank God for His faithfulness, as the young Nicolas did.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You are with me in every place, in the kitchen and the field, the office and the market, the hospital and the home. Thank You for Your servant Brother Lawrence, who learned to love You among the pots and pans. Teach me to place You in front of me at all times, and to speak with You while I work. Receive my small tasks, and fill them with love for You. When my mind wanders, call me back gently. When I fail, help me to rise again and trust in the merits of Jesus Christ. Make my heart a quiet room where I can always speak with You. In the name of Jesus. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Nicolas Herman met God through a bare tree in winter. How has God used ordinary things in creation to speak to you?
- During which part of your day is it hardest to remember that God is with you? What might change if you spoke to Him there, even in a few words?
- How do you usually react when you realize you have forgotten God for a long time? What can you learn from Lawrence's way of turning back?

## Prayer in the Fire

> "Blessed are those who suffer persecution for the sake of righteousness: the kingdom of heaven is theirs." (Matthew 5:10)

Early in 1569, in the small town of Asperen in Holland, a man was running for his life. His name was Dirk Willems. The authorities wanted him because he had been baptized again as a young man, on his own confession of faith, and because he had let other believers meet, and be baptized, in his house. Both were crimes under the law of that land.

A thief-catcher, an officer whose job was to hunt down wanted men, was close behind him. There had been frost, and the water ahead of Dirk was covered with ice. Dirk ran out onto it. The ice was thin, but he got across, though it was very dangerous.

The officer followed him. Halfway across, the ice broke, and he fell into the freezing water. He could not get out. He was going to drown.

Dirk was safe on the other side. He was free. But he heard the man in the water, and he turned around. He went back across the ice and pulled his pursuer out, and so saved his life.

The officer wanted to let him go. But the burgomaster, the mayor of the town, called out sternly and reminded him of his oath to the authorities. So the man arrested the one who had just saved him. Dirk was taken to prison. On 16 May 1569 the judges sentenced him to be burned.

We know this story from a large book called the *Martyrs Mirror*, gathered by a Dutch Mennonite minister, Thieleman van Braght, and printed in 1660. He tells it from the report of "trustworthy persons," and he includes a copy of the court's sentence. (Later tellers add that Dirk had escaped from prison by a rope of knotted cloth; the *Martyrs Mirror* says only that he fled.)

On the day he died, a strong east wind blew the flames away from the top of his body, so that he suffered slowly. People in the next town, Leerdam, heard him cry out more than seventy times, "O my Lord; my God." The bailiff, the officer in charge, sat on his horse nearby. He was so troubled that he turned his horse away and told the executioner, "Dispatch the man with a quick death." Dirk Willems died calling on God.

### Who they were

Dirk belonged to a movement that its enemies called Anabaptists, which means "baptizers again." It began in Zurich, Switzerland, in January 1525, when a small group met in the house of Felix Manz and his mother and baptized one another on confession of faith. They believed that the church should be made up of people who had freely chosen to follow Christ. They tried to live by the Sermon on the Mount. Most of them refused to swear oaths or to use the sword, even to defend themselves.

To the rulers of that age, both Catholic and Protestant, this looked like a danger to church and society. And so Anabaptists were hunted. On 5 January 1527, the Protestant city council of Zurich had Felix Manz drowned in the river Limmat. He was the first Swiss Anabaptist put to death by fellow Protestants. A few months later, in May 1527, Catholic authorities in southern Germany tortured and burned Michael Sattler, a former monk who had become an Anabaptist leader. His wife Margaretha was drowned a few days later. Over the next century many hundreds more died, in Catholic and Protestant lands alike.

We should tell this history honestly and humbly. In the 1530s a violent group of radicals seized the German city of Münster, and many rulers feared that all Anabaptists were like them. But most, such as the Dutch leader Menno Simons, rejected violence completely. And the *Martyrs Mirror* was written by one side, with hard words for its enemies that we need not repeat. The truth is that Christians of that time, of almost every church, did terrible things to one another in the name of Christ. In 2010 the Lutheran World Federation, kneeling in prayer, asked Mennonites to forgive the persecution their forefathers had supported. That is the right spirit for all of us.

### How they prayed

The martyrs' letters are full of prayer. Felix Manz left a final message for his fellow believers that begins, "My heart rejoices in God." He wrote that "the true love of Christ shall not destroy the enemy." From prison, Michael Sattler wrote to his church, urging them to "watch and pray," and to meet together faithfully and "be united in prayer for all men." At his trial he said that he and his friends were "ready to suffer for the Word of God." A believer named Leopold Schneider, beheaded at Augsburg in 1528, left a prayer for his last hour: "My God, I will praise Thee in my last hour." In it he prayed for those who were killing him, with the words of Jesus on the cross: "Heavenly Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do."

One of the most moving prayers came from a young mother. On 24 January 1539, in the city of Rotterdam, Anneken Jans was put to death for her faith. Before she died she wrote a long letter to her young son, Isaiah, which the *Martyrs Mirror* calls her "Testament." She told him to follow Christ even if few others did: "where you hear of the cross, there is Christ; from there do not depart." She ended with a prayer: "O holy Father, sanctify the son of Thy handmaiden in Thy truth, and keep him from the evil, for Thy name's sake, O Lord."

Her words echo the prayer of Jesus for His disciples: "keep them safe from the evil one ... Set them apart for Yourself by the truth" (John 17:15-17). She could not stay with her son. She gave him to the Father with the words of the Son.

Jesus said that those who suffer persecution for righteousness are blessed, because "the kingdom of heaven is theirs" (Matthew 5:10). He did not say that they would escape suffering. He said that they already belong to God's kingdom, and no fire or river can take that away.

John Wesley, the English preacher we will meet in the next volume, wrote about how Christians should treat those who persecute them. His advice ended with prayer:

> "If you can do nothing more, at least 'pray for them that despitefully use you and persecute you.' You can never be disabled from doing this; nor can all their malice or violence hinder you."
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

### Three key insights

1. **Those who suffer for doing right are blessed, because the kingdom of heaven is already theirs.** The Anabaptist martyrs lost their homes, their freedom and their lives. But Jesus' promise stood: they belonged to His kingdom, and no one could take it from them.
2. **Mercy to an enemy in danger can speak louder than any argument.** Dirk Willems did not stop to ask whether the man in the water deserved help. His love moved the officer and has spoken to people for more than four hundred years.
3. **Remembering how Christians have wronged one another should lead us to repent and to pray for one another.** Most of these martyrs died at the hands of other Christians. When Lutherans knelt in 2010 to ask forgiveness for what their forefathers had done, they showed that honest memory, brought to God in prayer, can begin to heal old wounds.

### For us today

Every year many believers suffer for their faith. In some countries churches are burned, pastors are imprisoned and new believers are rejected by their families. The Anabaptist martyrs remind them that they are not forgotten. Jesus calls them blessed.

The story also asks something of all of us. Most of the Anabaptists were killed by other Christians. We must never use power, laws or violence to force anyone's faith, and we must defend the freedom of others to worship, even those we disagree with. When we pray for the persecuted church, we should also pray that Christians everywhere would never become persecutors.

And Dirk Willems asks the hardest question of all. When the person who has hurt us is in trouble, will we turn back to help?

The prayers of these martyrs did not end in the fire. In Volume 7, *The Fire on the Altar*, we will meet the Moravians, who kept prayer going day and night for a hundred years, together with the Wesleys and the praying people of the Great Awakening.

### Try this

This week, let these martyrs shape your prayers.

- **Pray for the persecuted.** Choose one country where Christians suffer today, and pray for believers there by name or by place each day.
- **Pray a blessing on a child.** Pray Anneken's prayer for a child you love: "Holy Father, keep this child from evil and set them apart for Yourself by Your truth."
- **Look for the man on the ice.** Ask God to show you someone who has opposed you and now needs help, and to give you courage to turn back for them.
- **Pray for unity.** Pray for Christians of other churches near you, and ask God to heal old wounds between traditions.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You were persecuted for righteousness, and You promised the kingdom of heaven to all who suffer for Your sake. Thank You for Dirk Willems, Felix Manz, Michael and Margaretha Sattler, Anneken Jans and all the faithful whose names only You know. Forgive us, Your church, for the times we have hurt one another in Your name. Strengthen every believer who suffers for You today. Give me the courage to turn back and help my enemy, and the faith to place those I love in the Father's hands. Keep me from the evil one, and set me apart by Your truth. Amen.

## Conclusion: Passing the Torch

> "After this I looked, and I saw a crowd so vast that no one could number it. They came from every nation and from all tribes and peoples and languages, and they stood before the throne and before the Lamb, dressed in white robes, with palm branches in their hands." (Revelation 7:9)

The people in this book did not agree with one another. Luther and Calvin argued about the Lord's Supper. Cranmer and the Catholic bishops of Mary's reign condemned each other. Puritans and bishops in England could not worship in the same way, and Bunyan spent twelve years in prison because of it. Catholics and Protestants alike hunted the Anabaptists. In Japan, Christians were put to death by rulers who saw the faith as a foreign threat.

Yet John, on the island of Patmos, saw a crowd from every nation standing together before the throne of God and the Lamb. When we read the prayers of this age side by side, we catch a glimpse of that crowd. Teresa's friendship with God, Luther's simple way to pray, Calvin's confident approach to the throne of grace, Andrewes's long list of people to pray for and the hidden Christians' whispered prayers in Japan all rise to the same Father, through the same Son, by the same Spirit.

That does not make the divisions of this age unimportant. Many of them were about the gospel itself, and believers still disagree about some of them today. But it reminds us that God was at work on every side, teaching His people to pray, and that the Lord Jesus is still praying that all who believe in Him may be one.

### What the witnesses taught us

Here, in a few lines, is some of what the witnesses of this book taught us about prayer:

- When the danger is greatest, prayer is our safest fortress (chapter 1, Martin Luther).
- The simple words we learned as children can become a lifelong school of prayer (chapter 2, Luther's letter to his barber).
- Prayer is how faith takes hold of the treasures God has promised (chapter 3, John Calvin).
- God wants us to pray with our minds as well as our hearts, in words we understand (chapter 4, Thomas Cranmer).
- A heart that loves its own people will carry them to God in prayer (chapter 5, John Knox).
- Prayer is a friendship with Jesus, kept alive by frequent and honest conversation (chapter 6, Teresa of Ávila).
- Feeling nothing in prayer is not the same as being left by God (chapter 7, John of the Cross).
- Looking back over the day with God begins with thanks, not with blame (chapter 8, Ignatius of Loyola).
- True devotion fits our calling and makes us better at it (chapter 9, Francis de Sales).
- God can keep His people praying when every outward support has been taken away (chapter 10, the hidden Christians of Japan).
- A life of prayer can be built from God's own words, gathered one by one (chapter 11, Lancelot Andrewes).
- Prayer is a way to taste God's goodness, not only to talk about it (chapter 12, George Herbert).
- Christ does not throw away bruised people; He bends down to mend them (chapter 13, Richard Sibbes).
- Remembering the joy ahead gives us courage for the troubles of today (chapter 14, Richard Baxter).
- Prayer is pouring out the heart to God, not putting on a show of fine words (chapter 15, John Bunyan).
- We can keep up a quiet conversation with God while our hands are busy (chapter 16, Brother Lawrence).
- Those who suffer for doing right are blessed, because the kingdom of heaven is already theirs (chapter 17, the Anabaptist martyrs).

You will find every one of these lessons gathered by theme in Appendix 1, and every Scripture in this book listed in Bible order in Appendix 2.

### A mighty fortress

The title of this book comes from Luther's best-known hymn, written on Psalm 46: "God is a shelter and a strength for us, a help always close at hand in times of distress" (Psalm 46:1).

The people in this book needed that shelter. Luther lived for years under a sentence of death. Cranmer and the Anabaptists went to the fire. Knox rowed in chains on a French galley. John of the Cross was locked in a dark cell by his own brothers in religion. Baxter preached through years of illness and lived to see himself silenced. Bunyan prayed in a Bedford jail while his family struggled without him. The hidden Christians of Japan prayed in secret for two hundred and fifty years, most of that time with no priest and no church, under the constant threat of death.

None of them found that God took every trouble away. What they found was that God Himself was a fortress around them in the trouble, and that prayer was the way in. Their prayers were sometimes long and learned, sometimes simple and short, sometimes no more than groans. But God heard them all.

### The torch is passed

By the middle of the 1600s the wars of religion were ending, and Europe was exhausted. In many churches, faith had become a matter of right belief and good order, and it was easy to belong to a church without knowing Christ. Some believers began to long for something warmer: a living, personal faith, and prayer that came from the heart.

In **Volume 7, *The Fire on the Altar***, we will see that longing answered. We will meet Jeanne Guyon, who taught simple people to pray, and the German Pietists, who gathered small groups to read the Bible and pray. We will go to the Moravian village of Herrnhut, where in 1727 a prayer meeting began that ran night and day for about a hundred years, and from which missionaries went out to the ends of the earth. We will watch Susanna Wesley pray with her apron over her head, and see her sons John and Charles carry the gospel across England. We will hear George Whitefield preach in the open air, join Jonathan Edwards's call for a "concert of prayer," and kneel with David Brainerd in the snow. And we will end with William Carey, a poor shoemaker who taught the church to expect great things from God and to attempt great things for God.

The Moravians traced their spiritual roots to the followers of Jan Hus, whom we met in Volume 5. The Wesleys read Thomas à Kempis, Jeremy Taylor and William Law. Brainerd's diary was published by Jonathan Edwards, who had read the Puritans. The torch was passed.

### Your turn

The witnesses of this book now pass the torch to you.

So when you are too busy to pray, pray more, as Luther did. Take the Lord's Prayer and turn each line into your own prayer, as he taught his barber. Come boldly to the throne of grace, as Calvin urged. Pray with your mind as well as your heart. Pray for your nation. Speak to God as a Friend who loves you. When God seems far away, keep waiting in the dark. Look back over each day with Him. Bring your work to God, whatever your work may be. If you must pray in secret, keep praying. Let your prayers grow longer and wider, until they reach the whole world. When your prayers feel weak, remember that Christ does not break a bruised reed. Set your mind on heaven. When you have no words, pour out your heart. And do even the smallest thing for the love of God.

### A closing prayer

God our shelter and our strength, thank You for the believers of every church who prayed through a divided and violent age. Thank You for Luther and Calvin, Cranmer and Knox, Teresa and John of the Cross, Ignatius and Francis de Sales, Andrewes and Herbert, Sibbes and Baxter, Bunyan and Brother Lawrence, the hidden Christians of Japan and the martyrs who prayed in the fire. Forgive Your church for the harm we have done to one another, and make us one, as You and Your Son are one. Be my fortress in every trouble. Teach me to pray simply, honestly and often, as a friend speaks to a friend. And bring me at last, with all Your people from every nation and language, to stand before Your throne and before the Lamb. Amen.
