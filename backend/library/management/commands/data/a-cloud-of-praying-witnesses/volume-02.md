## Introduction: Where We Are in the Story

> "So then, since we too have so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us, let us strip off every burden, and the sin that wraps itself around us so readily, and let us run the race laid out ahead of us, and run it with endurance." (Hebrews 12:1)

This book is part of a long story, and it helps to know where we are standing.

The series it belongs to, *A Cloud of Praying Witnesses*, follows the people of God at prayer from the earliest pages of the Bible to the church of today. The writer of Hebrews pictured all the faithful men and women who went before us as a great crowd of witnesses, gathered around us like spectators around a race. They have run their part. Now it is our turn. In the last book of the Bible, John sees the prayers of God's people held before the throne of God in "gold bowls full of incense" (Revelation 5:8). Not one sincere prayer is wasted. Not one is lost. When you pray, you join a prayer meeting that has been going on for thousands of years.

Volume 1 of the series, *The Prayers Jesus Learned*, listened to the prayers of the Old Testament: Abraham pleading for a city, Moses standing in the gap, Hannah pouring out her soul, David singing his psalms, Daniel kneeling at his open window. Those were the prayers Jesus grew up with. As a boy in Nazareth He would have heard them read in the synagogue every Sabbath and sung them on the road to Jerusalem at the great feasts.

Now, in Volume 2, we come to the center of the story. We come to Jesus Himself.

### The world Jesus prayed in

Jesus lived in a small land at the eastern end of the Mediterranean Sea, ruled by the Roman Empire. Most of His ministry took place in Galilee, a region of farms, fishing villages and busy roads in the north, and in Judea in the south, where Jerusalem and its temple stood. Most of the people He met were poor. They worked with their hands, paid heavy taxes, and lived close to hunger.

They were also people of prayer. Faithful Jewish families said the *Shema*, "Hear, Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4), every morning and evening. Many prayed at set hours of the day, as Daniel had done. The temple in Jerusalem had its hours of prayer, and every village had its synagogue, where people gathered to pray, read the Scriptures and sing the Psalms. The disciples of Jesus were not beginners at prayer. They had prayed all their lives.

And yet, when they watched Jesus pray, they saw something they had never seen before. That is what this book is about.

### What we will see

The four Gospels, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, tell us the story of Jesus. None of them gives us a complete record of His prayer life, but all of them show us that prayer was at the heart of it. Luke in particular seems to have noticed it everywhere. Again and again he writes, almost in passing, "Jesus was praying." He prays at His baptism, before choosing His disciples, on the mountain where He is transfigured, in the garden before His arrest, and on the cross.

E. M. Bounds, whose books on prayer you can read in full in the Ochorus library, studied these passages for years. His conclusion was simple:

> "Nothing is more conspicuous in the life of our Lord than prayer."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

This volume has twenty chapters, and they follow four movements:

- **Jesus at prayer** (chapters 1 to 3). We watch Jesus pray: before dawn, all night on a mountain, and in the place where His disciples finally asked, "Lord, teach us to pray."
- **The prayer He taught** (chapters 4 to 6). We walk slowly through the Lord's Prayer, the prayer that Christians in every nation and language have prayed for two thousand years, and we learn about the secret room.
- **His teaching on prayer** (chapters 7 to 16). We listen to His stories and promises: ask, seek and knock; never give up; humble yourself; believe; pray together; pray for the harvest; give thanks; ask in His name.
- **His final prayers** (chapters 17 to 20). We stand near Him as He prays for His followers on the night before He died, in the garden of Gethsemane, and on the cross. And we end where He is now, at the right hand of the Father, still praying for us.

### How each chapter works

Every chapter in this series has the same shape, so you will soon know your way around.

- **A key verse** opens the chapter. It is the Scripture the chapter rests on.
- **A story** sets the scene.
- **The teaching** looks at what the passage shows us about prayer, and how Jesus prayed.
- **Voices from the past** quote men and women of prayer from church history, word for word from their own books in the Ochorus library.
- **Three key insights** sum up what the chapter teaches.
- **For us today** brings the lesson into ordinary life, wherever in the world you live.
- **Try this** gives one simple practice for the coming week.
- **A closing prayer** and **Questions for reflection** help you respond, alone or with others.
- **Read more on Ochorus** points you to classic books where you can go deeper.

At the end of the book you will find two appendices. The first gathers all the key insights by theme, so you can see at a glance what this volume teaches about faith, persistence, forgiveness and more. The second lists every Scripture quoted in the book, in Bible order, with the chapters where it appears.

### How to read this book

You can read it straight through, but it is best taken slowly. One chapter a day will carry you through the book in twenty days. If you are reading with a small group, one or two chapters a week works well, and the questions at the end of each chapter will get your conversation started.

Most of all, read it with your Bible open and your heart ready to pray. This is not a book about prayer so much as a book to pray with. The disciples did not learn to pray by reading about it. They learned by being with Jesus. Our hope is that as you read, you will find yourself saying with them, "Lord, teach us to pray," and that He will.

## Very Early in the Morning

> "Long before daylight, He got up and went out. He went off to a lonely place and spent time there in prayer." (Mark 1:35)

It had been an extraordinary day.

It began on the Sabbath in the synagogue at Capernaum, a fishing town on the shore of the Sea of Galilee. Jesus stood up to teach, and the people were amazed, because He taught like someone with authority. In the middle of the service a man with an evil spirit cried out, and Jesus set him free with a word. From the synagogue He walked to the house of Simon Peter, where Peter's mother-in-law lay in bed with a fever. He took her by the hand and lifted her up, and the fever left her.

By evening the news had reached every house in town. Mark tells us what happened when the sun went down and the Sabbath ended:

> "When evening came and the sun had set, people began bringing to Him everyone who was ill or controlled by demons. The entire town had crowded together outside the door." (Mark 1:32-33)

Picture that doorway. Parents carrying sick children. Old men leaning on their sons. People who had been in pain for years, pushing forward in the dark. Jesus healed many of them, one by one, late into the night.

Then, a few hours later, while the town was still asleep, He got up. He did not wait for the light. He walked out past the quiet houses, out beyond the edge of town, to a place where no one was. And there He prayed.

When Peter and the others woke up, the crowd was already gathering again. They searched for Him, found Him, and said what every busy person has heard a thousand times: "Everybody is out looking for You!" (Mark 1:37).

His answer surprised them. He did not go back to the house in Capernaum. He said, "Let us move on to the market towns nearby, so that I can proclaim the message there as well. This is what I came out to do" (Mark 1:38).

### The Son who prayed

We are beginning a journey through two thousand years of prayer. We will meet martyrs and monks, reformers and revivalists, mothers and missionaries, from every part of the world. But we begin with Jesus, because every one of them learned to pray by looking at Him.

That is the first thing that should stop us. Jesus is the eternal Son of God. He was there before the world began. He healed the sick and cast out demons with a word. And yet He prayed. He did not pray as a show for His disciples, or only when He was in trouble. He prayed because He lived His whole life in dependence on His Father. He once said, "The Son cannot do anything on His own; He does only what He sees the Father doing" (John 5:19). Prayer was where He saw what the Father was doing.

Mark 1:35 is not a one-time event. Luke, describing the same months, uses a word that tells us it was His habit:

> "But word about Jesus went out farther than ever. Large crowds kept gathering to listen and to be healed of their illnesses. Yet He kept slipping away to places in the wilderness to pray." (Luke 5:15-16)

Look at that little word "yet." The crowds grew larger; yet He kept slipping away. The more people needed Him, the more He prayed. Most of us do the opposite. When life gets busy, prayer is the first thing we cut.

### How He prayed

The Gospels never give us a timetable of Jesus' prayer life, but they show us a pattern.

He prayed **alone**. Mark says He went to "a lonely place." Matthew tells us that after feeding the five thousand "He climbed up into the hills by Himself to pray" (Matthew 14:23).

He prayed **early**, and sometimes **all night**. Before He chose the twelve apostles, "He stayed up all night in prayer to God" (Luke 6:12).

He prayed at **turning points**: at His baptism (Luke 3:21), before choosing His disciples, before the cross. The great decisions of His life were made on His knees.

And He prayed **when He was tired**. That is what makes Mark 1:35 so striking. He had every reason to sleep in. He had worked late. No one would have blamed Him. But He knew where His strength came from.

Andrew Murray, the South African pastor who wrote some of the most loved books on prayer ever published, looked at this verse and saw what Jesus knew:

> "He knew how the holiest service, preaching and healing, can exhaust the spirit; how too much intercourse with men could cloud the fellowship with God; how time, time, full time, is needed if the spirit is to rest and root in Him; how no pressure of duty among men can free from the absolute need of much prayer."
> Andrew Murray, *The Ministry of Intercession*

"Time, time, full time." Murray does not mean that we earn God's love by the hour. He means that friendship takes time. Roots grow slowly, out of sight. Jesus gave His Father unhurried time, and He came back from that lonely place knowing exactly what He had come to do.

### Three key insights

1. **If Jesus needed to pray, so do we.** Prayer is not only for the weak, the anxious or the extra-spiritual. It is how the Son of God Himself lived. If He depended on His Father, we can stop pretending that we can manage without Him.
2. **Busyness is a reason to pray, not an excuse not to.** The busier Jesus became, the more He slipped away. Prayer was not something He fitted in after the work. It was where the work began.
3. **Prayer gives direction.** Everyone in Capernaum had a plan for Jesus' day. Alone with His Father, He remembered His own: "This is what I came out to do." In prayer we stop being driven by the crowd's needs and start being led by God.

### For us today

Perhaps you are thinking, "But I don't have a lonely place." Many readers of this book live in small homes full of family. Some share a room with brothers and sisters or with other workers. Some live in cities where it is never quiet. Jesus did not have a private room either. He had a hillside before dawn. Your lonely place may be a corner of the kitchen, a bench on your way to work, a walk around the field, or a few minutes in a parked car or on a crowded bus with your eyes closed. In a later volume we will meet a mother of many children who prayed with her apron pulled over her head, and her children knew not to disturb her. The place matters less than the meeting.

Perhaps you are thinking, "But I can't pray early." You may work at night, or care for a newborn baby, or rise at four to reach the fields. The point of Mark 1:35 is not the clock. It is that Jesus gave His Father the *first* of His day, before the demands came knocking. Find your "first," whenever it is, and give it to God before you give it to anyone else, including your phone.

The psalmist knew this rhythm long before Jesus was born:

> "LORD, at daybreak You hear my voice. At daybreak I set everything out before You and keep watch." (Psalm 5:3)

Notice the two parts: setting everything out before God, and then keeping watch. Prayer is not only speaking. It is also waiting, watching and listening. The prophet Isaiah, speaking of the Servant of the Lord, said, "Each morning He rouses me; He rouses my ear to listen as a learner listens" (Isaiah 50:4). Christians have always heard Jesus in those words. Every morning, the Teacher became the learner, and listened.

Two thousand years later, E. M. Bounds, an American Methodist pastor who rose at four every morning to pray, put it plainly:

> "The men who have done the most for God in this world have been early on their knees."
> E. M. Bounds, *Power Through Prayer*

You do not need to become famous to follow that pattern. You only need to show up.

### Try this

For the next seven days, before you look at your phone or hear the news, take ten minutes with God. Go to your "lonely place," wherever it is.

- **Be still.** Read Mark 1:35 slowly. Picture Jesus walking out in the dark to meet His Father.
- **Set everything out.** Like Psalm 5:3, name the things your day holds, one by one: the people you will see, the work you will do, the worries you are carrying. Place each one before God.
- **Listen.** Ask, "Father, what have I come out to do today?" Sit quietly for a minute or two. You may not hear anything special. That is fine. You are learning to listen, as Jesus did.

At the end of the week, notice what changed.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You rose while it was still dark to be alone with Your Father. You were tired, and the whole town needed You, and still You prayed. Teach me to love Your Father as You do. Give me a place to meet Him and the will to keep the appointment. When my day is crowded, draw me away to pray. When I don't know what to do, show me what I have come out to do. And as I learn from You, make me a person of prayer. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What usually fills the first minutes of your day? What would it cost you to give them to God?
- Jesus prayed more as the crowds grew larger. Why do you think we tend to do the opposite?
- Where is your "lonely place"? If you don't have one yet, where could it be?

## All Night on the Mountain

> "During those days Jesus went off to the mountain to pray, and He stayed up all night in prayer to God." (Luke 6:12)

The sun was going down over the hills of Galilee when Jesus left the crowds and began to climb.

By now He was famous. People came to Him from all over the land, from Jerusalem in the south and from the coast of Tyre and Sidon in the north. Some came to listen. Many came to be healed. There were also people who were angry with Him. Just before this, Luke tells us, the religious leaders had watched Him heal a man's withered hand on the Sabbath, and they had been "filled with mindless rage" and had "talked together about what they could do to Jesus" (Luke 6:11). The pressure was rising.

And Jesus had a decision to make, perhaps the most important decision of His ministry. Out of all the people who followed Him, He was about to choose twelve men to be His closest companions. They would live with Him, learn from Him, and one day carry His message to the ends of the earth. The future of His church would rest, humanly speaking, on these twelve.

So He went up the mountain, alone, and He prayed. Not for an hour. Not until He felt tired. He stayed there all night.

We are not told what He said. We are not told whether He knelt, or stood, or walked about under the stars. We only know that the night passed in prayer, and that when the sky began to grow light, He was ready.

> "At daybreak He summoned His disciples and picked out twelve of them, giving them the name apostles." (Luke 6:13)

### A strange list of names

Read the names Luke gives and you may be surprised. Simon, whom Jesus renamed Peter, a fisherman who often spoke before he thought. His brother Andrew. James and John, two more fishermen, so fiery that Jesus nicknamed them "Sons of Thunder" (Mark 3:17). Matthew, a tax collector, who had worked for the Roman system that most Jews hated. And Simon the Zealot, from a movement that wanted to drive the Romans out by force. In any other setting, Matthew and Simon might have been enemies.

At the end of the list is a name that stops us: "Judas Iscariot, the one who turned traitor" (Luke 6:16).

Jesus chose Judas after a whole night of prayer. That should make us think. Prayer did not give Jesus an easy path. It did not protect Him from betrayal and suffering. Later He said plainly, "Did I not choose the twelve of you Myself? Still, one of you is a devil" (John 6:70). Jesus was not deceived. He chose in obedience to His Father, knowing that the road ahead led to a cross. Prayer did not lead Him away from the Father's plan. It led Him into the heart of it.

Mark, telling the same story, adds a detail about why Jesus chose them: "He appointed twelve to be with Him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message" (Mark 3:14). First, to be with Him. Then, to be sent. The men who would one day lead the church in prayer began by keeping company with a man who prayed all night.

### How He prayed

This was not the only time Jesus prayed through the night or withdrew to a mountain. After feeding the five thousand, He sent everyone away and "went off up the mountain to pray" (Mark 6:46), and He was still there in the dark hours when the disciples were struggling against the wind on the lake. Before He was transfigured, He "went up the mountain to pray" (Luke 9:28). On His last night, He prayed in Gethsemane while His friends slept.

What do we see in these nights?

He prayed **before great decisions**, not only after them. He did not choose the twelve and then ask His Father to bless the choice. He asked first.

He prayed **at length** when the moment required it. Most of Jesus' recorded prayers are short. But when a turning point came, He gave it time, as much time as it needed.

He prayed **under pressure**. His enemies were plotting. The crowds were pressing. His answer to pressure was not to work harder, but to pray longer.

E. M. Bounds, looking at the whole of Jesus' life, saw the same pattern:

> "The great eras of His life were created and crowned with prayer."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

And again:

> "His campaigns were arranged and His victories were gained in the struggles and communion of His all night praying."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

### Three key insights

1. **Big decisions deserve big prayer.** Jesus did not make the most important choice of His ministry quickly. He gave the whole night to His Father first. The weight of a decision is a call to pray, not a reason to rush.
2. **Praying does not promise an easy road.** Jesus prayed all night and still chose Judas, because He was following His Father's plan, not His own comfort. Prayer does not always keep us out of hard places, but it keeps us in the will of God, and that is the safest place to be.
3. **The church was born on a mountain of prayer.** Before there were apostles, there was a night of prayer. The first leaders of the church learned this lesson well. When they chose a replacement for Judas, they prayed, "Lord, You who know the hearts of all people, make plain which one of these two is Your choice" (Acts 1:24). When they appointed leaders in the new churches, they did it "after praying and fasting" (Acts 14:23).

### For us today

Most of us will never choose apostles. But we all face decisions that will shape our lives and the lives of others. Whom should I marry? Should I take this job, or move to that city? Should our church call this pastor? What should we do about our child, our parents, our land, our money?

It is natural to talk these things over with friends, to think them through, to make lists of reasons for and against. All of that is good. But the example of Jesus asks us a question: have you prayed about it as much as you have worried about it?

You may not be able to stay up all night. Your body, your work or your family may not allow it. That is not the point. The point is that Jesus gave the decision unhurried time with His Father, more time than usual, before He acted. For you that might mean an evening set aside, a morning without your phone, a day of fasting, or a half-night of prayer with a few trusted friends. In many churches around the world, especially in Africa and Asia, believers still gather for whole nights of prayer before important events. We will meet some of them in later volumes of this series. They are following a path that Jesus Himself walked on a mountain in Galilee.

And when you pray, you can stand on this promise:

> "If any of you is short of wisdom, ask God, who gives to everyone with an open hand and without scolding, and it will be given to you." (James 1:5)

Notice the words "without scolding." God is not annoyed that you need guidance. He wants you to ask.

### Try this

Think of one decision that is in front of you right now, large or small. This week, set apart one longer time of prayer for it: an hour, an evening, or longer if you are able.

- **Write the decision down** in one sentence at the top of a page.
- **Pray James 1:5** and ask God for wisdom, believing that He wants to give it.
- **Bring the people.** Write the names of everyone the decision will affect, and pray for each one by name.
- **Wait.** Spend some of the time in silence. You may not receive a clear answer that night. Jesus Himself acted "at daybreak," after the waiting was done. Keep praying until peace and direction come, and talk with mature believers you trust.

### A closing prayer

Father, Your Son climbed a mountain and prayed all night before He chose His friends. I confess that I often decide first and pray later, if I pray at all. Teach me to bring my decisions to You before I make them. Give me wisdom, as You have promised. When the road You show me is hard, give me courage to walk it, as Jesus did. And let everything I choose be shaped by time spent with You. In His name I pray. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Think of an important decision you made in the past. How much did you pray about it before you decided?
- Why do you think Jesus chose Judas, knowing what he would do? What does that teach us about prayer and the will of God?
- What would a longer time of prayer look like in your life? What would you need to change to make room for it?

## "Lord, Teach Us to Pray"

> "Once Jesus was praying in a certain place. When He had finished, one of His disciples asked Him, 'Lord, teach us how to pray, the way John taught his own disciples.'" (Luke 11:1)

Luke does not tell us where it happened. He calls it simply "a certain place." It might have been a hillside, or a garden, or a quiet spot along the road. The disciples were waiting nearby while Jesus prayed, as they must have done many times before.

They had seen a great deal by now. They had watched Him calm a storm with a word. They had seen a leper made clean, the blind given sight, and a dead girl lifted from her bed. They had heard Him teach crowds of thousands. They had even been sent out themselves to preach and to heal.

And yet, when He finished praying that day, one of them came to Him with a request. He did not say, "Lord, teach us to work miracles." He did not say, "Lord, teach us to preach." He said, "Lord, teach us to pray."

Something in the way Jesus prayed made them hungry. They had prayed all their lives. They knew the psalms and the set prayers of their people. But watching Him, they realized that they did not really know how to pray at all.

### Why they asked

The disciple mentioned John the Baptist. John had taught his followers a way to pray, as Jewish teachers often did. A teacher's prayer was a kind of badge: it showed whose school you belonged to and what you believed. The disciples wanted that too. They wanted to belong to Jesus' school of prayer.

But there was more to it than a badge. They had noticed that prayer was the secret of His life. The power they saw in public came from the time He spent in private. They had seen Him slip away before dawn (Mark 1:35) and come down from a mountain after a night of prayer (Luke 6:12). They had seen that when He prayed, heaven seemed very near.

Andrew Murray began his famous book *With Christ in the School of Prayer* with this very verse. He imagined the disciples' thoughts:

> "The disciples had been with Christ, and seen Him pray. They had learnt to understand something of the connection between His wondrous life in public, and His secret life of prayer."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

That connection is still true. What we are in public flows from what we are with God in private.

### How Jesus answered

Jesus did not tell them that prayer was too deep to explain, or that they would pick it up in time. He answered at once, and He answered in three ways.

First, He gave them **words to pray**:

> "He said to them, 'When you pray, say: "Father, may Your name be honored as holy. May Your kingdom come. Each day give us the bread we need. Forgive us our sins, since we also forgive all who owe us. And do not bring us into temptation."'" (Luke 11:2-4)

This is the shorter form of the prayer we call the Lord's Prayer. We will look at it closely in the next two chapters.

Second, He told them **a story**, about a man who knocks on his friend's door at midnight to borrow bread, and keeps knocking until he gets it (Luke 11:5-8). We will return to that story in chapter 7. Its point is plain: keep asking.

Third, He gave them **a promise**:

> "You are evil, and yet you know how to give your children good gifts. Then how much more will the Father in heaven give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him!" (Luke 11:13)

Words, a story and a promise. Jesus taught prayer the way a good teacher teaches anything: by giving His students a pattern to follow, a picture to remember, and the confidence to try.

### Prayer can be learned

This is one of the most encouraging verses in the Bible for anyone who struggles to pray. And that is most of us.

Many Christians feel that they are "not good at prayer." Their minds wander. They don't know what to say. They feel that other people pray beautifully while they stumble. Some have quietly given up.

Luke 11:1 says to all of us: you are in good company. The apostles themselves had to ask to be taught. Prayer is natural to a child of God, the way talking is natural to a child. But just as a child learns to speak by listening to its parents, we learn to pray by listening to Jesus and practicing with Him.

Andrew Murray put it well:

> "Though in its beginnings prayer is so simple that the feeblest child can pray, yet it is at the same time the highest and holiest work to which man can rise."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

Both halves of that sentence are true. You can begin today, with simple words. And you will never finish learning.

The best news of all is that the Teacher has not left the school. Jesus is still teaching His people to pray. Paul wrote that when we do not know what to pray, "the Spirit Himself pleads on our behalf, with groans that no words carry" (Romans 8:26). The Holy Spirit, the very gift Jesus promised in Luke 11:13, is the teacher of prayer who lives inside every believer.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer can be learned.** The apostles were not born knowing how to pray, and neither are we. It is not a gift for a special few. Anyone can begin, and everyone can grow.
2. **We learn to pray by watching people who pray.** The disciples asked because they had seen Jesus at prayer. That is why this series tells the stories of praying men and women: their example stirs us to ask, as the disciples did, "Teach us too."
3. **Jesus is a willing teacher.** He did not refuse the request or make it complicated. He gave words, a story and a promise, and He still gives His Spirit to everyone who asks.

### For us today

If you want to learn to pray, start where the disciples started. Ask. The simplest and most honest prayer you can pray is the one in Luke 11:1: "Lord, teach me to pray." It is a prayer He loves to answer.

Then look for people who pray. Every church, in every land, has a few: an old woman who has prayed for her neighbors for fifty years, a pastor who rises early, a young believer whose prayers are full of faith. Don't be shy. Ask them how they pray. Most will be glad to tell you, and some will invite you to pray with them. The disciples learned by being near Jesus. We often learn by being near those who are near Him.

And don't be discouraged by your weakness. If your prayers feel small and clumsy, remember that the Father is not grading your words. He is listening to His child. A father does not mind if his little one stumbles over a sentence. He is simply glad to hear the child's voice.

### Try this

This week, enroll yourself in "the school of prayer."

- **Begin every time of prayer with Luke 11:1.** Before anything else, say, "Lord, teach me to pray." Mean it.
- **Pray the short Lord's Prayer from Luke 11:2-4** once a day, slowly. After each line, stop and say it again in your own words.
- **Find one person of prayer** in your church or community and ask them, "How do you pray?" Listen, and if they invite you, pray with them.
- **Keep a short note** of anything you learn. At the end of the week, read it back and thank God for what He taught you.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, Your disciples watched You pray and asked You to teach them. I ask You the same: teach me to pray. You know how weak my prayers are, how easily my mind wanders, how often I don't know what to say. Thank You that You are a patient teacher. Give me Your Holy Spirit, as You promised, to help me when I have no words. Lead me to people who pray, and make me one of them. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What do you find hardest about prayer? Have you ever asked Jesus to teach you in that area?
- Who has taught you the most about prayer, by their words or by their example?
- Murray says prayer is both "so simple that the feeblest child can pray" and "the highest and holiest work." Which half of that sentence do you need to hear today?

## Our Father in Heaven

> "So this is how you should pray: 'Our Father in heaven, may Your name be kept holy. May Your kingdom come. May Your will be done on earth, as it is done in heaven.'" (Matthew 6:9-10)

There is a prayer that is spoken somewhere in the world at every hour of every day.

It is whispered by mothers putting their children to bed in Lagos and in Lima. It is sung by monks in the mountains of Ethiopia and recited by miners in Bolivia before they go underground. It is prayed in great cathedrals and in tiny house churches that must meet in secret. It was prayed by martyrs on their way to death and is prayed by people in hospital beds today. It has been translated into more languages than almost any other words ever written.

It is the prayer Jesus taught on a hillside in Galilee, and it begins with two words: "Our Father."

Jesus gave this prayer twice. In Luke, as we saw in the last chapter, He gave it when a disciple asked to be taught. In Matthew, it comes in the middle of the Sermon on the Mount. Just before it, Jesus warns against two wrong ways of praying. Some people pray to be seen by others (Matthew 6:5). Others pile up words, thinking God will listen if they talk long enough:

> "When you pray, do not keep piling up empty words as the pagans do. They think they will be heard because they say so much. So do not imitate them. Your Father already knows what you need before you ask Him." (Matthew 6:7-8)

Then He says, "So this is how you should pray." What follows is not long. It is not complicated. Most children can learn it by heart in a day. And yet Christians have spent their whole lives exploring it and never reached the bottom.

In this chapter we look at the first half of the prayer, which is all about God. In the next chapter we will look at the second half, which is about us.

### "Our Father"

The first word is "our." Jesus did not teach us to pray "my Father," even when we pray alone in a closed room. Every time we pray this prayer, we pray it with the whole family of God: with believers in every nation, in every century, rich and poor, persecuted and free. You are never the only child at the Father's table.

The second word is "Father." This was the word Jesus Himself used when He prayed. In the garden of Gethsemane He cried, "Abba, Father" (Mark 14:36). *Abba* is the Aramaic word for "father" that was used at home, by little children and grown sons and daughters alike. It is warm and close, full of trust. The Jewish people knew God as the Father of their nation, but to speak to Him with such closeness in prayer was something new.

And here is the wonder: Jesus invites us to share His own word. Paul wrote to the Christians in Rome:

> "What you received was not a spirit of slavery that would put you back under fear. You received the Spirit of adoption as children, and in the Spirit we call out, 'Abba, Father!'" (Romans 8:15)

Through faith in Jesus, we are adopted into God's family. The Son of God shares His Father with us.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Watson, who preached a long series of sermons on the Lord's Prayer in London in the 1600s, loved this opening. He wrote:

> "The name Jehovah carries majesty in it: the name Father carries mercy in it."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

And:

> "No father like God for love; if thou art his child thou canst not love thy own soul so entirely as he loves thee."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

Some readers find the word "father" painful. Your own father may have been absent, harsh or cruel. If so, please hear this: God is not like the worst father you have known. He is better than the best. Jesus showed us what He is like in the story of the lost son, where the father sees his son "while he was still far away," runs to meet him, and throws his arms around him (Luke 15:20). That is the Father you are speaking to.

### "In heaven"

"Our Father" tells us that God is near. "In heaven" tells us that He is great. He is not a grandfather who can be talked into anything, or a small god who belongs to one family or tribe. He rules over all things. Both truths belong together. We come to Him with the trust of children and the reverence of people standing before a King.

### "May Your name be kept holy"

The first thing Jesus teaches us to ask for is not for ourselves at all. It is that God's name, His character and reputation, would be honored as holy. In the Bible, God's name stands for who He is. When Isaiah saw a vision of God, the angels were calling to each other, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of Armies! All that fills the earth is His glory" (Isaiah 6:3). This petition asks that the whole earth would come to see Him as the angels do, and that our own lives would show it.

### "May Your kingdom come"

A kingdom is wherever a king's rule is welcomed and obeyed. Jesus came announcing that the kingdom of God had come near. It arrives in part whenever a person turns to Christ, whenever the sick are healed, whenever justice is done and the poor are cared for. One day it will come in full, when Jesus returns and "the rule over the world now belongs to our Lord and to His Christ" (Revelation 11:15). Every time we pray "Your kingdom come," we are asking God to bring that day closer, and to begin His rule in our own hearts today.

### "May Your will be done"

This is the hardest line in the prayer. It is easy to ask for God's will in general. It is much harder to want it when it costs us something. Jesus knew that better than anyone. On the night before He died, He prayed with tears, "Father, if You are willing, remove this cup from Me. Yet let Your will be done, not Mine" (Luke 22:42). We will stand with Him in Gethsemane in chapter 18.

"On earth, as it is done in heaven." In heaven, God's will is done gladly, immediately and completely. We are asking for our homes, our churches, our cities and our nations to become a little more like that.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer begins with relationship, not requests.** Before we ask for anything, Jesus reminds us whom we are speaking to: our Father. Everything else in prayer grows out of knowing that we are loved children.
2. **We never pray alone.** "Our Father" joins us to the whole family of God on earth and in heaven. Even the most private prayer is prayed with brothers and sisters around the world.
3. **God's concerns come first.** The first three requests are about His name, His kingdom and His will. When we put God's concerns first, our own needs find their proper place, as Jesus promised: "Above all, seek the kingdom and His righteousness, and you will be given all these things as well" (Matthew 6:33).

### For us today

Many of us have prayed the Lord's Prayer so often that we hardly hear the words anymore. It can become like the "empty words" Jesus warned about, said quickly without thought. The answer is not to stop praying it. The answer is to slow down.

Early Christians prayed the Lord's Prayer three times a day. We will meet them in Volume 4. They did not find it boring, because they did not rush it. Each phrase was a doorway into a room of prayer.

Try praying just the first line, "Our Father in heaven," and then stop. Think about it. You are speaking to the Creator of the universe, and He is your Father. Thank Him. Tell Him what that means to you. Then move to the next line, "May Your name be kept holy," and pray about the places in your own life and your own land where His name is not honored. Then, "May Your kingdom come." Pray for your family, your neighbors, your nation, the persecuted church. Then, "May Your will be done." Bring to Him the thing you are struggling to surrender.

Prayed this way, these few lines can fill an hour. Or they can fill one minute in a busy day, and still lift your heart to God.

### Try this

Each day this week, pray through the first half of the Lord's Prayer slowly, one line at a time. After each line, pause and add your own words.

- **"Our Father in heaven."** Thank God for one way He has been a Father to you.
- **"May Your name be kept holy."** Ask Him to be honored in one specific place: your home, your workplace, your nation.
- **"May Your kingdom come."** Pray for one person who does not yet know Him.
- **"May Your will be done on earth, as it is done in heaven."** Name one area of your life where you find His will hard to accept, and give it to Him.

### A closing prayer

Our Father in heaven, thank You that through Jesus I can call You Father. You are near, and You are great. May Your name be kept holy in my life, in my family and in my land. May Your kingdom come, in my heart today and in all the earth when Jesus returns. May Your will be done in me, even when it is hard, as it is done gladly in heaven. I pray this with all Your children everywhere, in the name of Your Son. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- When you pray, do you think of God more as a King, a Judge or a Father? How does Jesus' word "Abba" change that?
- Why do you think Jesus put God's name, kingdom and will before our own needs?
- Which line of this half of the prayer is hardest for you to pray honestly? Why?

## Bread, Forgiveness, Deliverance

> "Give us today the bread we need. Forgive us what we owe You, as we too have forgiven those who owe us. And do not lead us into testing, but rescue us from the evil one." (Matthew 6:11-13)

Many of the people listening to Jesus on that hillside were day laborers. They had no savings. Each morning they stood in the marketplace hoping someone would hire them, and each evening they were paid enough to buy food for the next day. God's law commanded employers to pay such workers before sunset (Deuteronomy 24:15), because their families could not wait. If there was no work today, there might be no bread tomorrow.

When Jesus taught them to pray, "Give us today the bread we need," they understood exactly what He meant.

They would also have remembered a story from their history. When Israel wandered in the wilderness after leaving Egypt, God fed them with manna, bread from heaven. But He gave it one day at a time:

> "The LORD said to Moses, 'I am about to rain bread from heaven for you. Each day the people are to go out and gather the amount needed for that day.'" (Exodus 16:4)

Anyone who tried to store it overnight found it full of worms in the morning. God was teaching His people to trust Him daily.

In the last chapter we looked at the first half of the Lord's Prayer, which turns our eyes to God: His name, His kingdom, His will. Now the prayer turns to us. In three short requests, Jesus covers every part of our lives: our bodies (bread), our past (forgiveness) and our future (protection from testing and evil).

### "Give us today the bread we need"

It is striking that the first thing Jesus teaches us to ask for ourselves is something so ordinary. Not wisdom, not holiness, not power, but bread. God cares about our bodies. He is not too spiritual to be interested in our rent, our harvest, our children's school fees or our next meal. Nothing is too small to bring to Him.

Notice the word **today**. We ask for what we need now, not a lifetime supply. That keeps us coming back. It teaches us to trust our Father one day at a time, as Israel did in the wilderness.

Notice the words **we need**. This is not a prayer for luxury. A wise man once prayed, "Give me neither poverty nor wealth, but feed me with the bread I need each day" (Proverbs 30:8). Too little can tempt us to steal; too much can tempt us to forget God.

And notice the word **us**. When we pray this prayer, we are not only asking for our own bread. We are praying for the hungry family down the road, for refugees and for nations facing famine. And if God has given us more than we need today, we may be the answer to someone else's prayer.

Thomas Watson saw one more thing in this line:

> "God gives us daily bread, let us give him daily praise."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

### "Forgive us what we owe You"

Jesus moves straight from bread to forgiveness, as if to say: you need both every day. Our bodies need food, and our souls need mercy.

Jesus calls our sins "what we owe." Every sin is a debt, a failure to give God the love and obedience we owe Him. And it is a debt we cannot pay. All we can do is ask Him to cancel it. The wonderful news of the gospel is that He does. "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous: He will forgive our sins and cleanse us from every kind of wrongdoing" (1 John 1:9). On the cross, Jesus paid the debt we could not pay.

Watson put it in a way that is easy to remember:

> "Daily bread may make us live comfortably, but forgiveness of sins will make us die comfortably."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

### "As we too have forgiven those who owe us"

Then comes the line that many of us would rather skip. We ask God to forgive us in the same way that we forgive others.

Jesus took this so seriously that it is the only part of the prayer He explains afterward:

> "For if you forgive others when they wrong you, your Father in heaven will forgive you as well. But if you refuse to forgive others, your Father will not forgive your wrongs either." (Matthew 6:14-15)

This does not mean we earn God's forgiveness by forgiving others. We can never earn it. But a heart that has truly received mercy cannot hold on to hatred forever. As Watson explained, our forgiving others "is not a cause of God's forgiving us, but it is a condition without which he will not forgive us." A closed fist cannot receive a gift. When we refuse to forgive, we close our hands to the mercy we need most.

Forgiving is not pretending the wrong did not happen. It is not always the same as trusting the person again, especially where there has been abuse or danger. It is a choice to release the debt, to hand the person over to God's justice rather than our own revenge. Sometimes it has to be chosen many times. When Peter asked whether seven times was enough, Jesus said, "Not just up to seven times, but up to seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:22).

Jesus never asks us to do what He has not done. Hanging on the cross, He prayed for the men who had nailed Him there: "Father, forgive them. They do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34).

### "Do not lead us into testing, but rescue us from the evil one"

The last request looks ahead. We know we are weak. We know there is an enemy, "the evil one," who wants to destroy our faith. So we ask our Father to guard our steps.

God does not tempt anyone to sin. James is clear: "God is beyond the reach of evil's temptation, and He never tempts anyone" (James 1:13). But God does allow His children to pass through times of testing, and testing can become temptation if we face it in our own strength. This prayer is the honest cry of someone who knows they could fall: "Father, don't let me face more than I can bear. Keep me close. Rescue me."

And God promises to answer:

> "The testing that has gripped you is only what all people face. But God is faithful, and He will not allow the testing to go past your strength. Along with the testing He will also make the way out, so that you are able to hold up under it." (1 Corinthians 10:13)

In Gethsemane, Jesus told His sleepy disciples, "Stay alert and pray that you will not come into testing" (Matthew 26:41). Then, a few hours later, He prayed for them to His Father: "Keep them safe from the evil one" (John 17:15). He is still praying that prayer for us.

### "For Yours is the kingdom"

Many Christians end the Lord's Prayer with the words "For Yours is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, forever. Amen." These words do not appear in the oldest manuscripts of Matthew's Gospel, which is why many modern Bibles leave them out. But Christians began adding them very early. The *Didache*, a guide for churches written around the end of the first century, already ends the prayer with praise: "for Yours is the power and the glory forever." It is a fitting end. The prayer that began with God's name ends with God's glory.

### Three key insights

1. **God cares about our daily needs.** Bread is the first thing Jesus teaches us to ask for ourselves. Nothing in our lives is too ordinary to pray about, and we are invited to trust Him one day at a time.
2. **Forgiven people forgive.** We cannot ask God to cancel our debts while we hold others to theirs. Receiving mercy and giving mercy belong together.
3. **We need God's protection every day.** We are weaker than we think, and the evil one is real. Praying "rescue us" keeps us humble, alert and close to the Father who has promised a way out.

### For us today

These three requests are a simple checklist for every day. Have I asked my Father for what I need today, and thanked Him for what He has already given? Have I asked Him to forgive me, and have I forgiven others? Have I asked Him to guard me from the testing and the traps I will face?

For some readers, the hardest of these will be bread. You may be facing real hunger, unemployment or debt. Jesus did not teach this prayer to the comfortable. He taught it to the poor, and He meant it. Pray it boldly, and look for the ways God provides, often through His people.

For others, the hardest will be forgiveness. There may be a name that comes to mind as soon as you read these words: a parent, a spouse, a former friend, an enemy of your people. Start where you are. You may only be able to pray, "Lord, I am not ready to forgive, but I am willing to be made willing." That is a real prayer, and God honors it.

### Try this

Each day this week, pray the second half of the Lord's Prayer slowly, adding your own words after each line.

- **"Give us today the bread we need."** Name your needs for today, then name one other person's need and pray for them. If you can help meet that need, do it.
- **"Forgive us what we owe You."** Ask the Holy Spirit to show you anything you need to confess, and receive His forgiveness with thanks.
- **"As we too have forgiven those who owe us."** Write the name of one person you need to forgive. Pray for God to bless them. Do it again tomorrow.
- **"Do not lead us into testing, but rescue us from the evil one."** Name the place where you are most likely to fall, and ask your Father to guard you there today.

### A closing prayer

Father, thank You for every meal You have given me. Give me today the bread I need, and make me generous to those who have less. Forgive me what I owe You; I cannot pay it, and I thank You that Jesus has. Help me to forgive those who have wronged me, as You have forgiven me. When testing comes, do not let it overwhelm me. Keep me close to You, and rescue me from the evil one. For Yours is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, forever. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What does it mean to you to pray for bread "today" rather than for a lifetime of security?
- Is there someone you find it hard to forgive? What would be a first step for you this week?
- Where are you most likely to be tested? How can praying "rescue us from the evil one" change the way you face that place?
