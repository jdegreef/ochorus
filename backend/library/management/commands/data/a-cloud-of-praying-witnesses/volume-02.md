## Introduction: Where We Are in the Story

> "So then, since we too have so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us, let us strip off every burden, and the sin that wraps itself around us so readily, and let us run the race laid out ahead of us, and run it with endurance." (Hebrews 12:1)

This book is part of a long story, and it helps to know where we are standing.

The series it belongs to, *A Cloud of Praying Witnesses*, follows the people of God at prayer from the earliest pages of the Bible to the church of today. The writer of Hebrews pictured all the faithful men and women who went before us as a great crowd of witnesses, gathered around us like spectators around a race. They have run their part. Now it is our turn. In the last book of the Bible, John sees the prayers of God's people held before the throne of God in "gold bowls full of incense" (Revelation 5:8). Not one sincere prayer is wasted. Not one is lost. When you pray, you join a prayer meeting that has been going on for thousands of years.

Volume 1 of the series, *The Prayers Jesus Learned*, listens to the prayers of the Old Testament: Abraham pleading for a city, Moses standing in the gap, Hannah pouring out her soul, David singing his psalms, Daniel kneeling at his open window. Those were the prayers Jesus grew up with. As a boy in Nazareth He would have heard them read in the synagogue every Sabbath and sung them on the road to Jerusalem at the great feasts.

Now, in Volume 2, we come to the center of the story. We come to Jesus Himself.

### The world Jesus prayed in

Jesus lived in a small land at the eastern end of the Mediterranean Sea, ruled by the Roman Empire. Most of His ministry took place in Galilee, a region of farms, fishing villages and busy roads in the north, and in Judea in the south, where Jerusalem and its temple stood. Most of the people He met were poor. They worked with their hands, paid heavy taxes, and lived close to hunger.

They were also people of prayer. Faithful Jewish families said the *Shema*, "Hear, Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4), every morning and evening. Many prayed at set hours of the day, as Daniel had done. The temple in Jerusalem had its hours of prayer, and every village had its synagogue, where people gathered to pray, read the Scriptures and sing the Psalms. The disciples of Jesus were not beginners at prayer. They had prayed all their lives.

And yet, when they watched Jesus pray, they saw something they had never seen before. That is what this book is about.

### What we will see

The four Gospels, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, tell us the story of Jesus. None of them gives us a complete record of His prayer life, but all of them show us that prayer was at the heart of it. Luke in particular seems to have noticed it everywhere. Again and again he writes, almost in passing, "Jesus was praying." He prays at His baptism, before choosing His disciples, on the mountain where He is transfigured, in the garden before His arrest, and on the cross.

E. M. Bounds, whose books on prayer you can read in full in the Ochorus library, studied these passages for years. His conclusion was simple:

> "Nothing is more conspicuous in the life of our Lord than prayer."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

This volume has twenty chapters, and they follow four movements:

- **Jesus at prayer** (chapters 1 to 3). We watch Jesus pray: before dawn, all night on a mountain, and in the place where His disciples finally asked, "Lord, teach us to pray."
- **The prayer He taught** (chapters 4 to 6). We walk slowly through the Lord's Prayer, the prayer that Christians in every nation and language have prayed for two thousand years, and we learn about the secret room.
- **His teaching on prayer** (chapters 7 to 16). We listen to His stories and promises: ask, seek and knock; never give up; humble yourself; believe; pray together; pray for the harvest; give thanks; ask in His name.
- **His final prayers** (chapters 17 to 20). We stand near Him as He prays for His followers on the night before He died, in the garden of Gethsemane, and on the cross. And we end where He is now, at the right hand of the Father, still praying for us.

### How each chapter works

Every chapter in this series has the same shape, so you will soon know your way around.

- **A key verse** opens the chapter. It is the Scripture the chapter rests on.
- **A story** sets the scene.
- **The teaching** looks at what the passage shows us about prayer, and how Jesus prayed.
- **Voices from the past** quote men and women of prayer from church history, word for word from their own books in the Ochorus library.
- **Three key insights** sum up what the chapter teaches.
- **For us today** brings the lesson into ordinary life, wherever in the world you live.
- **Try this** gives one simple practice for the coming week.
- **A closing prayer** and **Questions for reflection** help you respond, alone or with others.
- **Read more on Ochorus** points you to classic books where you can go deeper.

At the end of the book you will find two appendices. The first gathers all the key insights by theme, so you can see at a glance what this volume teaches about faith, persistence, forgiveness and more. The second lists every Scripture quoted in the book, in Bible order, with the chapters where it appears.

### How to read this book

You can read it straight through, but it is best taken slowly. One chapter a day will carry you through the book in twenty days. If you are reading with a small group, one or two chapters a week works well, and the questions at the end of each chapter will get your conversation started.

Most of all, read it with your Bible open and your heart ready to pray. This is not a book about prayer so much as a book to pray with. The disciples did not learn to pray by reading about it. They learned by being with Jesus. Our hope is that as you read, you will find yourself saying with them, "Lord, teach us to pray," and that He will.

## Very Early in the Morning

> "Long before daylight, He got up and went out. He went off to a lonely place and spent time there in prayer." (Mark 1:35)

It had been an extraordinary day.

It began on the Sabbath in the synagogue at Capernaum, a fishing town on the shore of the Sea of Galilee. Jesus stood up to teach, and the people were amazed, because He taught like someone with authority. In the middle of the service a man with an evil spirit cried out, and Jesus set him free with a word. From the synagogue He walked to the house of Simon Peter, where Peter's mother-in-law lay in bed with a fever. He took her by the hand and lifted her up, and the fever left her.

By evening the news had reached every house in town. Mark tells us what happened when the sun went down and the Sabbath ended:

> "When evening came and the sun had set, people began bringing to Him everyone who was ill or controlled by demons. The entire town had crowded together outside the door." (Mark 1:32-33)

Picture that doorway. Parents carrying sick children. Old men leaning on their sons. People who had been in pain for years, pushing forward in the dark. Jesus healed many of them, one by one, late into the night.

Then, a few hours later, while the town was still asleep, He got up. He did not wait for the light. He walked out past the quiet houses, out beyond the edge of town, to a place where no one was. And there He prayed.

When Peter and the others woke up, the crowd was already gathering again. They searched for Him, found Him, and said what every busy person has heard a thousand times: "Everybody is out looking for You!" (Mark 1:37).

His answer surprised them. He did not go back to the house in Capernaum. He said, "Let us move on to the market towns nearby, so that I can proclaim the message there as well. This is what I came out to do" (Mark 1:38).

### The Son who prayed

We are beginning a journey through two thousand years of prayer. We will meet martyrs and monks, reformers and revivalists, mothers and missionaries, from every part of the world. But we begin with Jesus, because every one of them learned to pray by looking at Him.

That is the first thing that should stop us. Jesus is the eternal Son of God. He was there before the world began. He healed the sick and cast out demons with a word. And yet He prayed. He did not pray as a show for His disciples, or only when He was in trouble. He prayed because He lived His whole life in dependence on His Father. He once said, "The Son cannot do anything on His own; He does only what He sees the Father doing" (John 5:19). Prayer was where He saw what the Father was doing.

Mark 1:35 is not a one-time event. Luke, describing the same months, uses a word that tells us it was His habit:

> "But word about Jesus went out farther than ever. Large crowds kept gathering to listen and to be healed of their illnesses. Yet He kept slipping away to places in the wilderness to pray." (Luke 5:15-16)

Look at that little word "yet." The crowds grew larger; yet He kept slipping away. The more people needed Him, the more He prayed. Most of us do the opposite. When life gets busy, prayer is the first thing we cut.

### How He prayed

The Gospels never give us a timetable of Jesus' prayer life, but they show us a pattern.

He prayed **alone**. Mark says He went to "a lonely place." Matthew tells us that after feeding the five thousand "He climbed up into the hills by Himself to pray" (Matthew 14:23).

He prayed **early**, and sometimes **all night**. Before He chose the twelve apostles, "He stayed up all night in prayer to God" (Luke 6:12).

He prayed at **turning points**: at His baptism (Luke 3:21), before choosing His disciples, before the cross. The great decisions of His life were made on His knees.

And He prayed **when He was tired**. That is what makes Mark 1:35 so striking. He had every reason to sleep in. He had worked late. No one would have blamed Him. But He knew where His strength came from.

Andrew Murray, the South African pastor who wrote some of the most loved books on prayer ever published, looked at this verse and saw what Jesus knew:

> "He knew how the holiest service, preaching and healing, can exhaust the spirit; how too much intercourse with men could cloud the fellowship with God; how time, time, full time, is needed if the spirit is to rest and root in Him; how no pressure of duty among men can free from the absolute need of much prayer."
> Andrew Murray, *The Ministry of Intercession*

"Time, time, full time." Murray does not mean that we earn God's love by the hour. He means that friendship takes time. Roots grow slowly, out of sight. Jesus gave His Father unhurried time, and He came back from that lonely place knowing exactly what He had come to do.

### Three key insights

1. **If Jesus needed to pray, so do we.** Prayer is not only for the weak, the anxious or the extra-spiritual. It is how the Son of God Himself lived. If He depended on His Father, we can stop pretending that we can manage without Him.
2. **Busyness is a reason to pray, not an excuse not to.** The busier Jesus became, the more He slipped away. Prayer was not something He fitted in after the work. It was where the work began.
3. **Prayer gives direction.** Everyone in Capernaum had a plan for Jesus' day. Alone with His Father, He remembered His own: "This is what I came out to do." In prayer we stop being driven by the crowd's needs and start being led by God.

### For us today

Perhaps you are thinking, "But I don't have a lonely place." Many readers of this book live in small homes full of family. Some share a room with brothers and sisters or with other workers. Some live in cities where it is never quiet. Jesus did not have a private room either. He had a hillside before dawn. Your lonely place may be a corner of the kitchen, a bench on your way to work, a walk around the field, or a few minutes in a parked car or on a crowded bus with your eyes closed. In a later volume we will meet a mother of many children who prayed with her apron pulled over her head, and her children knew not to disturb her. The place matters less than the meeting.

Perhaps you are thinking, "But I can't pray early." You may work at night, or care for a newborn baby, or rise at four to reach the fields. The point of Mark 1:35 is not the clock. It is that Jesus gave His Father the *first* of His day, before the demands came knocking. Find your "first," whenever it is, and give it to God before you give it to anyone else, including your phone.

The psalmist knew this rhythm long before Jesus was born:

> "LORD, at daybreak You hear my voice. At daybreak I set everything out before You and keep watch." (Psalm 5:3)

Notice the two parts: setting everything out before God, and then keeping watch. Prayer is not only speaking. It is also waiting, watching and listening. The prophet Isaiah, speaking of the Servant of the Lord, said, "Each morning He rouses me; He rouses my ear to listen as a learner listens" (Isaiah 50:4). Christians have always heard Jesus in those words. Every morning, the Teacher became the learner, and listened.

Two thousand years later, E. M. Bounds, an American Methodist pastor who rose at four every morning to pray, put it plainly:

> "The men who have done the most for God in this world have been early on their knees."
> E. M. Bounds, *Power Through Prayer*

You do not need to become famous to follow that pattern. You only need to show up.

### Try this

For the next seven days, before you look at your phone or hear the news, take ten minutes with God. Go to your "lonely place," wherever it is.

- **Be still.** Read Mark 1:35 slowly. Picture Jesus walking out in the dark to meet His Father.
- **Set everything out.** Like Psalm 5:3, name the things your day holds, one by one: the people you will see, the work you will do, the worries you are carrying. Place each one before God.
- **Listen.** Ask, "Father, what have I come out to do today?" Sit quietly for a minute or two. You may not hear anything special. That is fine. You are learning to listen, as Jesus did.

At the end of the week, notice what changed.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You rose while it was still dark to be alone with Your Father. You were tired, and the whole town needed You, and still You prayed. Teach me to love Your Father as You do. Give me a place to meet Him and the will to keep the appointment. When my day is crowded, draw me away to pray. When I don't know what to do, show me what I have come out to do. And as I learn from You, make me a person of prayer. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What usually fills the first minutes of your day? What would it cost you to give them to God?
- Jesus prayed more as the crowds grew larger. Why do you think we tend to do the opposite?
- Where is your "lonely place"? If you don't have one yet, where could it be?

## All Night on the Mountain

> "During those days Jesus went off to the mountain to pray, and He stayed up all night in prayer to God." (Luke 6:12)

The sun was going down over the hills of Galilee when Jesus left the crowds and began to climb.

By now He was famous. People came to Him from all over the land, from Jerusalem in the south and from the coast of Tyre and Sidon in the north. Some came to listen. Many came to be healed. There were also people who were angry with Him. Just before this, Luke tells us, the religious leaders had watched Him heal a man's withered hand on the Sabbath, and they had been "filled with mindless rage" and had "talked together about what they could do to Jesus" (Luke 6:11). The pressure was rising.

And Jesus had a decision to make, perhaps the most important decision of His ministry. Out of all the people who followed Him, He was about to choose twelve men to be His closest companions. They would live with Him, learn from Him, and one day carry His message to the ends of the earth. The future of His church would rest, humanly speaking, on these twelve.

So He went up the mountain, alone, and He prayed. Not for an hour. Not until He felt tired. He stayed there all night.

We are not told what He said. We are not told whether He knelt, or stood, or walked about under the stars. We only know that the night passed in prayer, and that when the sky began to grow light, He was ready.

> "At daybreak He summoned His disciples and picked out twelve of them, giving them the name apostles." (Luke 6:13)

### A strange list of names

Read the names Luke gives and you may be surprised. Simon, whom Jesus renamed Peter, a fisherman who often spoke before he thought. His brother Andrew. James and John, two more fishermen, so fiery that Jesus nicknamed them "Sons of Thunder" (Mark 3:17). Matthew, a tax collector, who had worked for the Roman system that most Jews hated. And Simon the Zealot, from a movement that wanted to drive the Romans out by force. In any other setting, Matthew and Simon might have been enemies.

At the end of the list is a name that stops us: "Judas Iscariot, the one who turned traitor" (Luke 6:16).

Jesus chose Judas after a whole night of prayer. That should make us think. Prayer did not give Jesus an easy path. It did not protect Him from betrayal and suffering. Later He said plainly, "Did I not choose the twelve of you Myself? Still, one of you is a devil" (John 6:70). Jesus was not deceived. He chose in obedience to His Father, knowing that the road ahead led to a cross. Prayer did not lead Him away from the Father's plan. It led Him into the heart of it.

Mark, telling the same story, adds a detail about why Jesus chose them: "He appointed twelve to be with Him, and to be sent out to proclaim the message" (Mark 3:14). First, to be with Him. Then, to be sent. The men who would one day lead the church in prayer began by keeping company with a man who prayed all night.

### How He prayed

This was not the only time Jesus prayed through the night or withdrew to a mountain. After feeding the five thousand, He sent everyone away and "went off up the mountain to pray" (Mark 6:46), and He was still there in the dark hours when the disciples were struggling against the wind on the lake. Before He was transfigured, He "went up the mountain to pray" (Luke 9:28). On His last night, He prayed in Gethsemane while His friends slept.

What do we see in these nights?

He prayed **before great decisions**, not only after them. He did not choose the twelve and then ask His Father to bless the choice. He asked first.

He prayed **at length** when the moment required it. Most of Jesus' recorded prayers are short. But when a turning point came, He gave it time, as much time as it needed.

He prayed **under pressure**. His enemies were plotting. The crowds were pressing. His answer to pressure was not to work harder, but to pray longer.

E. M. Bounds, looking at the whole of Jesus' life, saw the same pattern:

> "The great eras of His life were created and crowned with prayer."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

And again:

> "His campaigns were arranged and His victories were gained in the struggles and communion of His all night praying."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

### Three key insights

1. **Big decisions deserve big prayer.** Jesus did not make the most important choice of His ministry quickly. He gave the whole night to His Father first. The weight of a decision is a call to pray, not a reason to rush.
2. **Praying does not promise an easy road.** Jesus prayed all night and still chose Judas, because He was following His Father's plan, not His own comfort. Prayer does not always keep us out of hard places, but it keeps us in the will of God, and that is the safest place to be.
3. **The church was born on a mountain of prayer.** Before there were apostles, there was a night of prayer. The first leaders of the church learned this lesson well. When they chose a replacement for Judas, they prayed, "Lord, You who know the hearts of all people, make plain which one of these two is Your choice" (Acts 1:24). When they appointed leaders in the new churches, they did it "after praying and fasting" (Acts 14:23).

### For us today

Most of us will never choose apostles. But we all face decisions that will shape our lives and the lives of others. Whom should I marry? Should I take this job, or move to that city? Should our church call this pastor? What should we do about our child, our parents, our land, our money?

It is natural to talk these things over with friends, to think them through, to make lists of reasons for and against. All of that is good. But the example of Jesus asks us a question: have you prayed about it as much as you have worried about it?

You may not be able to stay up all night. Your body, your work or your family may not allow it. That is not the point. The point is that Jesus gave the decision unhurried time with His Father, more time than usual, before He acted. For you that might mean an evening set aside, a morning without your phone, a day of fasting, or a half-night of prayer with a few trusted friends. In many churches around the world, especially in Africa and Asia, believers still gather for whole nights of prayer before important events. We will meet some of them in later volumes of this series. They are following a path that Jesus Himself walked on a mountain in Galilee.

And when you pray, you can stand on this promise:

> "If any of you is short of wisdom, ask God, who gives to everyone with an open hand and without scolding, and it will be given to you." (James 1:5)

Notice the words "without scolding." God is not annoyed that you need guidance. He wants you to ask.

### Try this

Think of one decision that is in front of you right now, large or small. This week, set apart one longer time of prayer for it: an hour, an evening, or longer if you are able.

- **Write the decision down** in one sentence at the top of a page.
- **Pray James 1:5** and ask God for wisdom, believing that He wants to give it.
- **Bring the people.** Write the names of everyone the decision will affect, and pray for each one by name.
- **Wait.** Spend some of the time in silence. You may not receive a clear answer that night. Jesus Himself acted "at daybreak," after the waiting was done. Keep praying until peace and direction come, and talk with mature believers you trust.

### A closing prayer

Father, Your Son climbed a mountain and prayed all night before He chose His friends. I confess that I often decide first and pray later, if I pray at all. Teach me to bring my decisions to You before I make them. Give me wisdom, as You have promised. When the road You show me is hard, give me courage to walk it, as Jesus did. And let everything I choose be shaped by time spent with You. In His name I pray. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Think of an important decision you made in the past. How much did you pray about it before you decided?
- Why do you think Jesus chose Judas, knowing what he would do? What does that teach us about prayer and the will of God?
- What would a longer time of prayer look like in your life? What would you need to change to make room for it?

## "Lord, Teach Us to Pray"

> "Once Jesus was praying in a certain place. When He had finished, one of His disciples asked Him, 'Lord, teach us how to pray, the way John taught his own disciples.'" (Luke 11:1)

Luke does not tell us where it happened. He calls it simply "a certain place." It might have been a hillside, or a garden, or a quiet spot along the road. The disciples were waiting nearby while Jesus prayed, as they must have done many times before.

They had seen a great deal by now. They had watched Him calm a storm with a word. They had seen a leper made clean, the blind given sight, and a dead girl lifted from her bed. They had heard Him teach crowds of thousands. They had even been sent out themselves to preach and to heal.

And yet, when He finished praying that day, one of them came to Him with a request. He did not say, "Lord, teach us to work miracles." He did not say, "Lord, teach us to preach." He said, "Lord, teach us to pray."

Something in the way Jesus prayed made them hungry. They had prayed all their lives. They knew the psalms and the set prayers of their people. But watching Him, they realized that they did not really know how to pray at all.

### Why they asked

The disciple mentioned John the Baptist. John had taught his followers a way to pray, as Jewish teachers often did. A teacher's prayer was a kind of badge: it showed whose school you belonged to and what you believed. The disciples wanted that too. They wanted to belong to Jesus' school of prayer.

But there was more to it than a badge. They had noticed that prayer was the secret of His life. The power they saw in public came from the time He spent in private. They had seen Him slip away before dawn (Mark 1:35) and come down from a mountain after a night of prayer (Luke 6:12). They had seen that when He prayed, heaven seemed very near.

Andrew Murray began his famous book *With Christ in the School of Prayer* with this very verse. He imagined the disciples' thoughts:

> "The disciples had been with Christ, and seen Him pray. They had learnt to understand something of the connection between His wondrous life in public, and His secret life of prayer."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

That connection is still true. What we are in public flows from what we are with God in private.

### How Jesus answered

Jesus did not tell them that prayer was too deep to explain, or that they would pick it up in time. He answered at once, and He answered in three ways.

First, He gave them **words to pray**:

> "He said to them, 'When you pray, say: "Father, may Your name be honored as holy. May Your kingdom come. Each day give us the bread we need. Forgive us our sins, since we also forgive all who owe us. And do not bring us into temptation."'" (Luke 11:2-4)

This is the shorter form of the prayer we call the Lord's Prayer. We will look at it closely in the next two chapters.

Second, He told them **a story**, about a man who knocks on his friend's door at midnight to borrow bread, and keeps knocking until he gets it (Luke 11:5-8). We will return to that story in chapter 7. Its point is plain: keep asking.

Third, He gave them **a promise**:

> "You are evil, and yet you know how to give your children good gifts. Then how much more will the Father in heaven give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him!" (Luke 11:13)

Words, a story and a promise. Jesus taught prayer the way a good teacher teaches anything: by giving His students a pattern to follow, a picture to remember, and the confidence to try.

### Prayer can be learned

This is one of the most encouraging verses in the Bible for anyone who struggles to pray. And that is most of us.

Many Christians feel that they are "not good at prayer." Their minds wander. They don't know what to say. They feel that other people pray beautifully while they stumble. Some have quietly given up.

Luke 11:1 says to all of us: you are in good company. The apostles themselves had to ask to be taught. Prayer is natural to a child of God, the way talking is natural to a child. But just as a child learns to speak by listening to its parents, we learn to pray by listening to Jesus and practicing with Him.

Andrew Murray put it well:

> "Though in its beginnings prayer is so simple that the feeblest child can pray, yet it is at the same time the highest and holiest work to which man can rise."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

Both halves of that sentence are true. You can begin today, with simple words. And you will never finish learning.

The best news of all is that the Teacher has not left the school. Jesus is still teaching His people to pray. Paul wrote that when we do not know what to pray, "the Spirit Himself pleads on our behalf, with groans that no words carry" (Romans 8:26). The Holy Spirit, the very gift Jesus promised in Luke 11:13, is the teacher of prayer who lives inside every believer.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer can be learned.** The apostles were not born knowing how to pray, and neither are we. It is not a gift for a special few. Anyone can begin, and everyone can grow.
2. **We learn to pray by watching people who pray.** The disciples asked because they had seen Jesus at prayer. That is why this series tells the stories of praying men and women: their example stirs us to ask, as the disciples did, "Teach us too."
3. **Jesus is a willing teacher.** He did not refuse the request or make it complicated. He gave words, a story and a promise, and He still gives His Spirit to everyone who asks.

### For us today

If you want to learn to pray, start where the disciples started. Ask. The simplest and most honest prayer you can pray is the one in Luke 11:1: "Lord, teach me to pray." It is a prayer He loves to answer.

Then look for people who pray. Every church, in every land, has a few: an old woman who has prayed for her neighbors for fifty years, a pastor who rises early, a young believer whose prayers are full of faith. Don't be shy. Ask them how they pray. Most will be glad to tell you, and some will invite you to pray with them. The disciples learned by being near Jesus. We often learn by being near those who are near Him.

And don't be discouraged by your weakness. If your prayers feel small and clumsy, remember that the Father is not grading your words. He is listening to His child. A father does not mind if his little one stumbles over a sentence. He is simply glad to hear the child's voice.

### Try this

This week, enroll yourself in "the school of prayer."

- **Begin every time of prayer with Luke 11:1.** Before anything else, say, "Lord, teach me to pray." Mean it.
- **Pray the short Lord's Prayer from Luke 11:2-4** once a day, slowly. After each line, stop and say it again in your own words.
- **Find one person of prayer** in your church or community and ask them, "How do you pray?" Listen, and if they invite you, pray with them.
- **Keep a short note** of anything you learn. At the end of the week, read it back and thank God for what He taught you.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, Your disciples watched You pray and asked You to teach them. I ask You the same: teach me to pray. You know how weak my prayers are, how easily my mind wanders, how often I don't know what to say. Thank You that You are a patient teacher. Give me Your Holy Spirit, as You promised, to help me when I have no words. Lead me to people who pray, and make me one of them. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What do you find hardest about prayer? Have you ever asked Jesus to teach you in that area?
- Who has taught you the most about prayer, by their words or by their example?
- Murray says prayer is both "so simple that the feeblest child can pray" and "the highest and holiest work." Which half of that sentence do you need to hear today?

## Our Father in Heaven

> "So this is how you should pray: 'Our Father in heaven, may Your name be kept holy. May Your kingdom come. May Your will be done on earth, as it is done in heaven.'" (Matthew 6:9-10)

There is a prayer that is spoken somewhere in the world at every hour of every day.

It is whispered by mothers putting their children to bed in Lagos and in Lima. It is sung by monks in the mountains of Ethiopia and recited by miners in Bolivia before they go underground. It is prayed in great cathedrals and in tiny house churches that must meet in secret. It was prayed by martyrs on their way to death and is prayed by people in hospital beds today. It has been translated into more languages than almost any other words ever written.

It is the prayer Jesus taught on a hillside in Galilee, and it begins with two words: "Our Father."

Jesus gave this prayer twice. In Luke, as we saw in the last chapter, He gave it when a disciple asked to be taught. In Matthew, it comes in the middle of the Sermon on the Mount. Just before it, Jesus warns against two wrong ways of praying. Some people pray to be seen by others (Matthew 6:5). Others pile up words, thinking God will listen if they talk long enough:

> "When you pray, do not keep piling up empty words as the pagans do. They think they will be heard because they say so much. So do not imitate them. Your Father already knows what you need before you ask Him." (Matthew 6:7-8)

Then He says, "So this is how you should pray." What follows is not long. It is not complicated. Most children can learn it by heart in a day. And yet Christians have spent their whole lives exploring it and never reached the bottom.

In this chapter we look at the first half of the prayer, which is all about God. In the next chapter we will look at the second half, which is about us.

### "Our Father"

The first word is "our." Jesus did not teach us to pray "my Father," even when we pray alone in a closed room. Every time we pray this prayer, we pray it with the whole family of God: with believers in every nation, in every century, rich and poor, persecuted and free. You are never the only child at the Father's table.

The second word is "Father." This was the word Jesus Himself used when He prayed. In the garden of Gethsemane He cried, "Abba, Father" (Mark 14:36). *Abba* is the Aramaic word for "father" that was used at home, by little children and grown sons and daughters alike. It is warm and close, full of trust. The Jewish people knew God as the Father of their nation, but to speak to Him with such closeness in prayer was something new.

And here is the wonder: Jesus invites us to share His own word. Paul wrote to the Christians in Rome:

> "What you received was not a spirit of slavery that would put you back under fear. You received the Spirit of adoption as children, and in the Spirit we call out, 'Abba, Father!'" (Romans 8:15)

Through faith in Jesus, we are adopted into God's family. The Son of God shares His Father with us.

The Puritan pastor Thomas Watson, who preached a long series of sermons on the Lord's Prayer in London in the 1600s, loved this opening. He wrote:

> "The name Jehovah carries majesty in it: the name Father carries mercy in it."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

And:

> "No father like God for love; if thou art his child thou canst not love thy own soul so entirely as he loves thee."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

Some readers find the word "father" painful. Your own father may have been absent, harsh or cruel. If so, please hear this: God is not like the worst father you have known. He is better than the best. Jesus showed us what He is like in the story of the lost son, where the father sees his son "while he was still far away," runs to meet him, and throws his arms around him (Luke 15:20). That is the Father you are speaking to.

### "In heaven"

"Our Father" tells us that God is near. "In heaven" tells us that He is great. He is not a grandfather who can be talked into anything, or a small god who belongs to one family or tribe. He rules over all things. Both truths belong together. We come to Him with the trust of children and the reverence of people standing before a King.

### "May Your name be kept holy"

The first thing Jesus teaches us to ask for is not for ourselves at all. It is that God's name, His character and reputation, would be honored as holy. In the Bible, God's name stands for who He is. When Isaiah saw a vision of God, the angels were calling to each other, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of Armies! All that fills the earth is His glory" (Isaiah 6:3). This petition asks that the whole earth would come to see Him as the angels do, and that our own lives would show it.

### "May Your kingdom come"

A kingdom is wherever a king's rule is welcomed and obeyed. Jesus came announcing that the kingdom of God had come near. It arrives in part whenever a person turns to Christ, whenever the sick are healed, whenever justice is done and the poor are cared for. One day it will come in full, when Jesus returns and "the rule over the world now belongs to our Lord and to His Christ" (Revelation 11:15). Every time we pray "Your kingdom come," we are asking God to bring that day closer, and to begin His rule in our own hearts today.

### "May Your will be done"

This is the hardest line in the prayer. It is easy to ask for God's will in general. It is much harder to want it when it costs us something. Jesus knew that better than anyone. On the night before He died, He prayed with tears, "Father, if You are willing, remove this cup from Me. Yet let Your will be done, not Mine" (Luke 22:42). We will stand with Him in Gethsemane in chapter 18.

"On earth, as it is done in heaven." In heaven, God's will is done gladly, immediately and completely. We are asking for our homes, our churches, our cities and our nations to become a little more like that.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer begins with relationship, not requests.** Before we ask for anything, Jesus reminds us whom we are speaking to: our Father. Everything else in prayer grows out of knowing that we are loved children.
2. **We never pray alone.** "Our Father" joins us to the whole family of God on earth and in heaven. Even the most private prayer is prayed with brothers and sisters around the world.
3. **God's concerns come first.** The first three requests are about His name, His kingdom and His will. When we put God's concerns first, our own needs find their proper place, as Jesus promised: "Above all, seek the kingdom and His righteousness, and you will be given all these things as well" (Matthew 6:33).

### For us today

Many of us have prayed the Lord's Prayer so often that we hardly hear the words anymore. It can become like the "empty words" Jesus warned about, said quickly without thought. The answer is not to stop praying it. The answer is to slow down.

Early Christians prayed the Lord's Prayer three times a day. We will meet them in Volume 4. They did not find it boring, because they did not rush it. Each phrase was a doorway into a room of prayer.

Try praying just the first line, "Our Father in heaven," and then stop. Think about it. You are speaking to the Creator of the universe, and He is your Father. Thank Him. Tell Him what that means to you. Then move to the next line, "May Your name be kept holy," and pray about the places in your own life and your own land where His name is not honored. Then, "May Your kingdom come." Pray for your family, your neighbors, your nation, the persecuted church. Then, "May Your will be done." Bring to Him the thing you are struggling to surrender.

Prayed this way, these few lines can fill an hour. Or they can fill one minute in a busy day, and still lift your heart to God.

### Try this

Each day this week, pray through the first half of the Lord's Prayer slowly, one line at a time. After each line, pause and add your own words.

- **"Our Father in heaven."** Thank God for one way He has been a Father to you.
- **"May Your name be kept holy."** Ask Him to be honored in one specific place: your home, your workplace, your nation.
- **"May Your kingdom come."** Pray for one person who does not yet know Him.
- **"May Your will be done on earth, as it is done in heaven."** Name one area of your life where you find His will hard to accept, and give it to Him.

### A closing prayer

Our Father in heaven, thank You that through Jesus I can call You Father. You are near, and You are great. May Your name be kept holy in my life, in my family and in my land. May Your kingdom come, in my heart today and in all the earth when Jesus returns. May Your will be done in me, even when it is hard, as it is done gladly in heaven. I pray this with all Your children everywhere, in the name of Your Son. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- When you pray, do you think of God more as a King, a Judge or a Father? How does Jesus' word "Abba" change that?
- Why do you think Jesus put God's name, kingdom and will before our own needs?
- Which line of this half of the prayer is hardest for you to pray honestly? Why?

## Bread, Forgiveness, Deliverance

> "Give us today the bread we need. Forgive us what we owe You, as we too have forgiven those who owe us. And do not lead us into testing, but rescue us from the evil one." (Matthew 6:11-13)

Many of the people listening to Jesus on that hillside were day laborers. They had no savings. Each morning they stood in the marketplace hoping someone would hire them, and each evening they were paid enough to buy food for the next day. God's law commanded employers to pay such workers before sunset (Deuteronomy 24:15), because their families could not wait. If there was no work today, there might be no bread tomorrow.

When Jesus taught them to pray, "Give us today the bread we need," they understood exactly what He meant.

They would also have remembered a story from their history. When Israel wandered in the wilderness after leaving Egypt, God fed them with manna, bread from heaven. But He gave it one day at a time:

> "The LORD said to Moses, 'I am about to rain bread from heaven for you. Each day the people are to go out and gather the amount needed for that day.'" (Exodus 16:4)

Anyone who tried to store it overnight found it full of worms in the morning. God was teaching His people to trust Him daily.

In the last chapter we looked at the first half of the Lord's Prayer, which turns our eyes to God: His name, His kingdom, His will. Now the prayer turns to us. In three short requests, Jesus covers every part of our lives: our bodies (bread), our past (forgiveness) and our future (protection from testing and evil).

### "Give us today the bread we need"

It is striking that the first thing Jesus teaches us to ask for ourselves is something so ordinary. Not wisdom, not holiness, not power, but bread. God cares about our bodies. He is not too spiritual to be interested in our rent, our harvest, our children's school fees or our next meal. Nothing is too small to bring to Him.

Notice the word **today**. We ask for what we need now, not a lifetime supply. That keeps us coming back. It teaches us to trust our Father one day at a time, as Israel did in the wilderness.

Notice the words **we need**. This is not a prayer for luxury. A wise man once prayed, "Give me neither poverty nor wealth, but feed me with the bread I need each day" (Proverbs 30:8). Too little can tempt us to steal; too much can tempt us to forget God.

And notice the word **us**. When we pray this prayer, we are not only asking for our own bread. We are praying for the hungry family down the road, for refugees and for nations facing famine. And if God has given us more than we need today, we may be the answer to someone else's prayer.

Thomas Watson saw one more thing in this line:

> "God gives us daily bread, let us give him daily praise."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

### "Forgive us what we owe You"

Jesus moves straight from bread to forgiveness, as if to say: you need both every day. Our bodies need food, and our souls need mercy.

Jesus calls our sins "what we owe." Every sin is a debt, a failure to give God the love and obedience we owe Him. And it is a debt we cannot pay. All we can do is ask Him to cancel it. The wonderful news of the gospel is that He does. "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous: He will forgive our sins and cleanse us from every kind of wrongdoing" (1 John 1:9). On the cross, Jesus paid the debt we could not pay.

Watson put it in a way that is easy to remember:

> "Daily bread may make us live comfortably, but forgiveness of sins will make us die comfortably."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

### "As we too have forgiven those who owe us"

Then comes the line that many of us would rather skip. We ask God to forgive us in the same way that we forgive others.

Jesus took this so seriously that it is the only part of the prayer He explains afterward:

> "For if you forgive others when they wrong you, your Father in heaven will forgive you as well. But if you refuse to forgive others, your Father will not forgive your wrongs either." (Matthew 6:14-15)

This does not mean we earn God's forgiveness by forgiving others. We can never earn it. But a heart that has truly received mercy cannot hold on to hatred forever. As Watson explained, our forgiving others "is not a cause of God's forgiving us, but it is a condition without which he will not forgive us." A closed fist cannot receive a gift. When we refuse to forgive, we close our hands to the mercy we need most.

Forgiving is not pretending the wrong did not happen. It is not always the same as trusting the person again, especially where there has been abuse or danger. It is a choice to release the debt, to hand the person over to God's justice rather than our own revenge. Sometimes it has to be chosen many times. When Peter asked whether seven times was enough, Jesus said, "Not just up to seven times, but up to seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:22).

Jesus never asks us to do what He has not done. Hanging on the cross, He prayed for the men who had nailed Him there: "Father, forgive them. They do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34).

### "Do not lead us into testing, but rescue us from the evil one"

The last request looks ahead. We know we are weak. We know there is an enemy, "the evil one," who wants to destroy our faith. So we ask our Father to guard our steps.

God does not tempt anyone to sin. James is clear: "God is beyond the reach of evil's temptation, and He never tempts anyone" (James 1:13). But God does allow His children to pass through times of testing, and testing can become temptation if we face it in our own strength. This prayer is the honest cry of someone who knows they could fall: "Father, don't let me face more than I can bear. Keep me close. Rescue me."

And God promises to answer:

> "The testing that has gripped you is only what all people face. But God is faithful, and He will not allow the testing to go past your strength. Along with the testing He will also make the way out, so that you are able to hold up under it." (1 Corinthians 10:13)

In Gethsemane, Jesus told His sleepy disciples, "Stay alert and pray that you will not come into testing" (Matthew 26:41). Then, a few hours later, He prayed for them to His Father: "Keep them safe from the evil one" (John 17:15). He is still praying that prayer for us.

### "For Yours is the kingdom"

Many Christians end the Lord's Prayer with the words "For Yours is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, forever. Amen." These words do not appear in the oldest manuscripts of Matthew's Gospel, which is why many modern Bibles leave them out. But Christians began adding them very early. The *Didache*, a guide for churches written around the end of the first century, already ends the prayer with praise: "for Yours is the power and the glory forever." It is a fitting end. The prayer that began with God's name ends with God's glory.

### Three key insights

1. **God cares about our daily needs.** Bread is the first thing Jesus teaches us to ask for ourselves. Nothing in our lives is too ordinary to pray about, and we are invited to trust Him one day at a time.
2. **Forgiven people forgive.** We cannot ask God to cancel our debts while we hold others to theirs. Receiving mercy and giving mercy belong together.
3. **We need God's protection every day.** We are weaker than we think, and the evil one is real. Praying "rescue us" keeps us humble, alert and close to the Father who has promised a way out.

### For us today

These three requests are a simple checklist for every day. Have I asked my Father for what I need today, and thanked Him for what He has already given? Have I asked Him to forgive me, and have I forgiven others? Have I asked Him to guard me from the testing and the traps I will face?

For some readers, the hardest of these will be bread. You may be facing real hunger, unemployment or debt. Jesus did not teach this prayer to the comfortable. He taught it to the poor, and He meant it. Pray it boldly, and look for the ways God provides, often through His people.

For others, the hardest will be forgiveness. There may be a name that comes to mind as soon as you read these words: a parent, a spouse, a former friend, an enemy of your people. Start where you are. You may only be able to pray, "Lord, I am not ready to forgive, but I am willing to be made willing." That is a real prayer, and God honors it.

### Try this

Each day this week, pray the second half of the Lord's Prayer slowly, adding your own words after each line.

- **"Give us today the bread we need."** Name your needs for today, then name one other person's need and pray for them. If you can help meet that need, do it.
- **"Forgive us what we owe You."** Ask the Holy Spirit to show you anything you need to confess, and receive His forgiveness with thanks.
- **"As we too have forgiven those who owe us."** Write the name of one person you need to forgive. Pray for God to bless them. Do it again tomorrow.
- **"Do not lead us into testing, but rescue us from the evil one."** Name the place where you are most likely to fall, and ask your Father to guard you there today.

### A closing prayer

Father, thank You for every meal You have given me. Give me today the bread I need, and make me generous to those who have less. Forgive me what I owe You; I cannot pay it, and I thank You that Jesus has. Help me to forgive those who have wronged me, as You have forgiven me. When testing comes, do not let it overwhelm me. Keep me close to You, and rescue me from the evil one. For Yours is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, forever. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What does it mean to you to pray for bread "today" rather than for a lifetime of security?
- Is there someone you find it hard to forgive? What would be a first step for you this week?
- Where are you most likely to be tested? How can praying "rescue us from the evil one" change the way you face that place?

## The Secret Room

> "But when you pray, go into an inner room, shut the door, and pray to your Father who is there in secret. Your Father sees what is hidden, and He will repay you." (Matthew 6:6)

In the villages of Galilee, most families lived in a small house with one main room. The whole family ate there, worked there and slept there, often with their animals close by at night. There was little privacy. But many houses had one more space: a small storeroom at the back, where grain, oil and tools were kept. It was often the only room with a door that could be shut.

When Jesus said, "Go into an inner room, shut the door," His listeners could picture it. Step away from everyone. Go into the storeroom among the jars of oil and sacks of grain. Pull the door closed behind you. And there, in the dark, speak to your Father.

It was a startling picture, because Jesus was contrasting it with something His listeners saw every day.

### Prayer on the street corner

Faithful Jews prayed at set times of the day. When the hour of prayer came, a man might stop wherever he happened to be and pray. Some people made sure they happened to be in the busiest place in town:

> "And when you pray, do not be like the hypocrites. They love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners where others can see them. Truly I tell you, they have been paid in full." (Matthew 6:5)

"Paid in full." They wanted people to admire them, and people did. That was their reward, and it was all the reward they would get. Their prayers were really performances, and the audience they cared about was human.

In this part of the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus talks about three things that God's people do: giving to the poor, praying and fasting (Matthew 6:1-18). He gives the same warning about all three. Do not do them to be seen. Do them for your Father, who sees in secret. "Take care not to put your righteousness on show for others to see," He begins (Matthew 6:1). The secret room is the place where we learn to live for an audience of One.

### Jesus in the secret place

Jesus did not only teach this. He lived it. We have already seen Him slip away before dawn and climb a mountain to pray all night. After feeding the five thousand, "He climbed up into the hills by Himself to pray. Evening came, and He was still there by himself" (Matthew 14:23). By Himself. No crowd, no disciples, no one to impress. Just the Son and the Father.

He was following a path that the people of God had walked for centuries. When the prophet Elisha came to the house where a little boy lay dead, "He went in, shut the door on the two of them, and prayed to the LORD" (2 Kings 4:33). Daniel went up to his upstairs room to pray three times a day (Daniel 6:10). Notice that Daniel's windows were open. The secret room is not about hiding our faith from danger. Daniel prayed "just as he had always done," even when it could cost him his life. The secret room is about motive. Whom are we praying to? Whose approval do we want?

### What happens behind the door

Why does Jesus send us into a room alone? Not because God cannot hear us in a crowd. He hears every prayer, everywhere. Jesus sends us there because something happens behind the closed door that does not happen anywhere else. We meet our Father without distraction and without pretending.

Andrew Murray wrote a whole book about the secret room, called *The Inner Chamber*. He believed that the most important thing in private prayer is not our list of requests, or even how well we pray. It is the meeting itself:

> "The first and chief thing is to see that there in secret you have the Father's presence and attention. Know that He sees and hears you."
> Andrew Murray, *The Inner Chamber*

Murray also warned of a danger. It is possible to go into the secret room, read the Bible, say prayers and come out again without really meeting God at all:

> "You are in danger of substituting Prayer and Bible Study for living fellowship with God, the living interchange of giving Him your love, your heart, and your life, and receiving from Him His love, His life, and His spirit."
> Andrew Murray, *The Inner Chamber*

The secret room is not a duty to complete. It is an appointment with a Person.

### "He will repay you"

Jesus promises a reward for secret prayer: "Your Father sees what is hidden, and He will repay you." What is the reward? Answers to prayer, certainly. But the greatest reward of the secret room is the Father Himself: His nearness, His peace, His strength for the day. And that reward does not stay hidden. People who spend time with God in secret are changed, and others notice, even if they never know why. Their patience, their courage and their kindness grow out of a place no one else can see.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer is for an audience of One.** The question is not whether people see us pray, but whom we are praying to. In the secret room there is no one to impress, so we can be completely honest with God.
2. **The secret room is a meeting, not a ritual.** Reading and praying are the doorway, not the destination. What matters most is that we meet our Father and know that He sees and hears us.
3. **What happens in secret shapes our public life.** The strength Jesus showed in public came from the hours He spent alone with His Father. The same is true for us.

### For us today

There have always been ways to pray "at the street corner." Today there are new ones. We can post our prayers online for others to see, or make sure people know how spiritual we are. Praying with others is good; the early church did it constantly, as we will see in Volume 3. But the warning of Jesus asks us to check our hearts. If no one ever knew that we prayed, would we still pray?

Some readers will say, "I have no inner room." Many people in the world live in crowded homes, share sleeping space with family, or live in places where privacy is very rare. Remember that the storeroom Jesus described was not a comfortable study. It was simply a place where a person could be alone with God for a while. For you, the door you shut may be your own eyes, closed on a crowded bus. It may be an early walk, a corner of a field, or a few minutes in the washroom at work. In a later volume we will meet Christians in Japan who kept their faith alive in secret for more than two hundred years, and believers in prisons who found that God met them in a cell. God is not limited by the size of your room.

The harder door to shut today may not be a wooden one. It may be the door of the phone, the screen and the constant noise of messages and news. Many of us never truly shut the door, because we carry the whole world in our pocket into the place of prayer. Shutting the door may mean leaving the phone in another room.

### Try this

This week, keep a daily appointment with your Father behind a closed door.

- **Choose your room and your time.** Pick a place and a time you can keep each day, even if it is only ten minutes.
- **Shut the door.** Close the door if you have one. Turn off your phone or leave it outside.
- **Begin with the meeting.** Before you ask for anything, say quietly, "Father, You see me. You hear me. I am here with You." Stay with that thought until you know it is true.
- **Keep a secret with God.** This week, do one good thing for someone without telling anyone at all, not even your closest friend. Let it be a secret between you and your Father.

### A closing prayer

Father, You see what is hidden. You see me now. Thank You that I do not have to perform for You or impress You. Teach me to shut the door on the noise of the world and the opinions of others, and to meet You in secret. Let my prayers be for You alone. Be my reward. And let the strength I find with You in secret shine out in the way I live, for Your glory and not my own. In Jesus' name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- If no one ever knew that you prayed, would your prayer life change? How?
- Where is your "inner room"? What makes it hard to shut the door there?
- Murray warns that prayer and Bible reading can become a substitute for meeting God. Have you experienced that? What helps you to meet Him?

- Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*, especially "Alone with God"

## Ask, Seek, Knock

> "So I tell you: ask, and God will give to you; search, and you will find; knock, and God will open the door for you." (Luke 11:9)

It is the middle of the night. The village is dark and silent. Suddenly there is a knock at the door. A friend has arrived after a long journey on foot, tired, dusty and hungry.

In the world of Jesus, hospitality was sacred. You could not let a guest go to bed hungry. It would bring shame on you, your family and your whole village. But you have a problem. There is no bread in the house. You used the last of it at supper.

So you go out into the darkness to the house of another friend and knock.

Jesus told this story right after teaching His disciples the Lord's Prayer:

> "Imagine that one of you has a friend, and you go to his house in the middle of the night and say to him, 'My friend, lend me three loaves. Another friend of mine has come to my house from a journey, and I have nothing to set out for him to eat.' Would that friend call back from inside, 'Do not trouble me. The door is already shut for the night, and my children are in bed with me. I cannot get up now to hand you anything'? I tell you, even if friendship is not enough to get him out of bed to give you the bread, your boldness in asking without shame will get him up, and he will give you as much as you need." (Luke 11:5-8)

Jesus' listeners would have smiled at the picture. In a one-room village house, the whole family slept together on mats on the floor. To get up, the man would have to climb over his sleeping children, unbolt the heavy door, and wake everyone up. No wonder he called out, "Do not trouble me!"

But the man outside keeps knocking. He is not ashamed to ask. And in the end, his friend gets up and gives him everything he needs.

### Not a reluctant God

It would be easy to misunderstand this story. Is Jesus saying that God is like a grumpy neighbor who must be pestered until He gives in? No. Jesus is using a kind of argument His listeners knew well, from the lesser to the greater. If even a sleepy, irritated friend will get up in the middle of the night to help, how much more will your Father in heaven, who never sleeps, and who loves you?

Jesus makes the same point a moment later with a picture from family life:

> "Is there a father among you who would hand his son a snake when the boy asked for a fish? Or if he asked for an egg, would he give him a scorpion? You are evil, and yet you know how to give your children good gifts. Then how much more will the Father in heaven give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him!" (Luke 11:11-13)

God is not the reluctant neighbor. He is the Father who is eager to give. The story is not about God's unwillingness. It is about our boldness.

### Ask, search, knock

Then Jesus gives one of the greatest invitations in the Bible:

> "So I tell you: ask, and God will give to you; search, and you will find; knock, and God will open the door for you. For all who ask receive, all who search find, and all who knock have the door opened to them." (Luke 11:9-10)

There are three verbs, and they grow stronger. To **ask** is to speak a request. To **search** is to look for something with effort. To **knock** is to stand at a closed door and keep pounding until it opens. Matthew records the same words with a sense of continuing action: "Keep asking, and you will receive. Keep seeking, and you will find. Keep knocking, and the door will be opened for you" (Matthew 7:7).

Jesus is teaching us not to give up after one try. Some answers come the moment we ask. Others come only after long searching and much knocking. But the promise stands: all who ask receive, all who search find, all who knock have the door opened.

Charles Spurgeon, who preached to thousands in London every week and was known for the power of his church's prayer meetings, put it this way:

> "Be importunate, heaven's gate does not open to every runaway knock. Knock, and knock, and knock again; and add to thy knocking and to thy asking seeking, and be not satisfied till thou gettest a real answer."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Mighty Power in Prayer*

"Importunate" is an old word for someone who keeps on asking, boldly and without giving up. A "runaway knock" is the knock of a child who bangs on a door and runs away before anyone comes. Spurgeon was saying: don't pray like that. Stay at the door.

### Asking for someone else

Look again at the story. The man at midnight was not asking for himself. He had a guest who was hungry, and he had nothing to give. "My friend, lend me three loaves. Another friend of mine has come... and I have nothing to set out for him."

That is a picture of much of our praying. People come into our lives with needs we cannot meet: a friend in despair, a neighbor who does not know God, a child who is ill, a church that is struggling. We have nothing in ourselves to give them. But we know Someone who has bread. So we go to Him and knock, on their behalf. In *With Christ in the School of Prayer*, Andrew Murray called this "the boldness of God's friends." God loves to give through people who are bold enough to ask for others.

### The best gift

Notice what Jesus says the Father will give: "the Holy Spirit." In Matthew's version it is "good things" (Matthew 7:11). Luke makes it plain that the greatest good thing of all is God's own presence. We may come asking for bread, for healing or for help, and God may give those things gladly. But above all, He wants to give us Himself.

### Three key insights

1. **God invites us to ask boldly.** The man in the story asked "without shame" at midnight. We do not need to be shy with our Father. He wants us to come to Him with our needs, at any hour.
2. **Ask, search, knock: keep at it.** Prayer sometimes requires persistence. Some doors open at the first knock; others only after long knocking. Keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking.
3. **Much of our asking is for others.** The man knocked at midnight for a hungry friend. When we have nothing to give the people around us, we can go to the Father who has everything.

### For us today

Some of us find it hard to ask God for things. We think it is selfish, or that we should only pray "spiritual" prayers, or that God is too busy for our small needs. Jesus says: ask. James wrote that some Christians go without simply "because you do not ask" (James 4:2). He also warned that some prayers are not answered "because your asking is wrong: you want to spend what you get on your pleasures" (James 4:3). So we ask with clean hearts, for things that honor God, but we do ask.

Others of us have asked once and given up. We prayed for something, nothing seemed to happen, and we decided God was not listening. Jesus' words "keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking" are for us. We will think more about this in the next chapter.

And all of us have "midnight friends": people who have come into our lives with needs we cannot meet. Their names are your invitation to knock.

### Try this

This week, make an "asking list" with three parts.

- **Ask.** Write one need of your own that you have not yet brought to God. Bring it to Him every day this week, simply and boldly.
- **Knock for a friend.** Write the name of one person who has come to you with a need you cannot meet. Each day, knock on heaven's door on their behalf. If God shows you a way to help them, do it.
- **Ask for the best gift.** Each morning, pray Luke 11:13: "Father, You have promised to give the Holy Spirit to those who ask. I am asking. Fill me with Your Spirit today."

At the end of the week, write down anything you have seen God do.

### A closing prayer

Father, thank You that You are not a sleepy neighbor, but a loving Father who delights to give. Forgive me for the times I have not asked, and for the times I have given up too soon. Teach me to ask, to search and to knock until the door opens. I bring You my own needs, and the needs of my friends, which I cannot meet. And above all, give me Your Holy Spirit, as You have promised. In Jesus' name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Do you find it easy or hard to ask God for things? Why?
- Who are the "midnight friends" in your life right now? What would it look like to knock on their behalf?
- Why do you think Jesus ends this teaching with the promise of the Holy Spirit, rather than bread or other gifts?

## Always Pray and Never Give Up

> "Jesus told them a parable about their need to pray at all times and not lose heart." (Luke 18:1)

In the world of Jesus, a widow was one of the most helpless people in society. She had no husband to speak for her in court, often no money of her own, and no power to make anyone listen. The Old Testament law commanded God's people again and again to protect widows, because they were so easy to cheat and so easy to ignore.

Jesus told a story about one such woman. Someone had wronged her. Perhaps a neighbor had taken her land, or a relative had stolen what her husband left her. She had only one hope: the judge of her town. But the judge was a hard man.

> "He said, 'There was once a judge in a certain city who did not fear God and had no regard for anyone. In that city there was also a widow who came to him again and again. She said, "Give me my rights against my opponent." For a while he refused. But later he said to himself, "I do not fear God and I have no regard for anyone. Still, this widow is such a nuisance that I will give her justice. If I do not, she will keep coming until she wears me out."'" (Luke 18:2-5)

Picture her standing outside his house every morning. Following him through the market. Calling out to him at the city gate where he sat to hear cases. Day after day, week after week, the same words: "Give me my rights." She had no money for a bribe and no friends in high places. All she had was her voice and her refusal to give up.

And in the end, she won.

### God is not the unjust judge

Luke tells us why Jesus told this story before the story even begins: "about their need to pray at all times and not lose heart." Jesus knew that His followers would be tempted to give up praying. Answers would seem slow. Injustice would seem to win. Prayers would seem to bounce off a closed heaven.

So Jesus told them this story, and then He explained it:

> "The Lord said, 'Listen to what the unjust judge says. Then surely God will give justice to the people He has chosen, when they call out to Him day and night. Will He be slow to help them? I tell you, He will see them vindicated, and without delay. Even so, when the Son of Man comes, will He find this kind of faith on the earth?'" (Luke 18:6-8)

Like the story of the friend at midnight in the last chapter, this is an argument from the lesser to the greater. If even a corrupt judge who cares for no one will finally give justice to a widow who keeps coming back, how much more will God, who is perfectly just and who loves His people, answer those who cry out to Him day and night?

The widow was a stranger to the judge. We are God's chosen children. The judge had to be worn down. God is eager to act. The widow had no promise to hold on to. We have the promises of God.

### "Without delay"

But here is the puzzle. Jesus says God will act "without delay." Yet in the same breath He tells us to keep praying and not lose heart, which suggests that sometimes the answer will seem slow.

Both are true. From heaven's side, God is never late. He acts at exactly the right time. From our side, the waiting can feel very long. Peter wrote about this: "The Lord is not delaying the promise, as some people think of delay. Rather, He is being patient" (2 Peter 3:9). God's timing is not our timing. What feels like delay to us may be God's patience, His preparation, or His working in ways we cannot yet see.

That is why Jesus ends with a question: "When the Son of Man comes, will He find this kind of faith on the earth?" The kind of faith He is looking for is the widow's kind. It keeps coming back. It does not take silence for an answer. It goes on praying until God acts.

### Voices from the past

Few people in history have shown this kind of faith more clearly than George Müller, who cared for thousands of orphans in Bristol, England, in the 1800s by prayer alone. Müller often met Christians who were tempted to give up. He told of one man who had been praying for many years for his six sons, who showed no interest in God. Müller told him, "Continue to pray for your sons, and expect an answer to your prayer, and you will have to praise God." The man took the advice and gave himself to prayer more earnestly than ever. Two months after that conversation, five of his six sons came to faith in Christ within eight days. When Müller returned to the city six years later, all five were still walking with the Lord. Müller wrote:

> "May the Christian reader be encouraged by this, should his prayers not at once be answered; and, instead of ceasing to pray, wait upon God all the more earnestly and perseveringly, and expect answers to his petitions."
> George Müller, *Answers to Prayer*

Müller lived what he taught. The American preacher R. A. Torrey, who knew of Müller's work, gave this account of Müller's own persistence, honestly noting where his memory was unsure:

> "George Muller prayed for two men daily for upwards of sixty years. One of these men was converted shortly before his death, I think at the last service that George Muller held, the other was converted within a year after his death."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

Sixty years. And one of the answers came after Müller himself had gone to be with the Lord.

### Three key insights

1. **Delay is not denial.** When an answer seems slow, God has not stopped listening. He is never late, even when He is not early. Our part is to keep praying and not lose heart.
2. **We do not pray to wear God down.** The widow had to pester an unjust judge. We come to a just and loving Father. We persist not because God is unwilling, but because persistence is what trust looks like over time.
3. **Persistent prayer is faith in action.** The faith Jesus is looking for when He returns is the widow's faith: it keeps coming back. Every time we pray again for the same thing, we are saying, "I still believe You will act."

### For us today

What have you stopped praying for? Many of us have a prayer we gave up on long ago: a son or daughter who walked away from God, a husband or wife who does not believe, a sickness that did not go away, a broken relationship, a nation suffering injustice. We prayed, nothing seemed to happen, and slowly we stopped asking.

Jesus' story invites us to start again. In Volume 4 of this series we will meet Monica, a mother in North Africa who prayed with tears for many years for her wayward son. That son became Augustine, one of the greatest teachers the church has ever known.

The widow's prayer was a cry for justice. Around the world today, many Christians pray this kind of prayer: for the persecuted, for victims of war and corruption, for the poor who are cheated and the weak who are crushed. The book of Revelation shows the martyrs in heaven crying out, "How long until You judge... and give justice?" (Revelation 6:10). Jesus says: keep crying out. God hears.

One word of balance. Persistent prayer is not the same as refusing to accept God's answer. Paul prayed three times for a painful "thorn" to be taken away, and God answered, "My grace is enough for you, for power comes to its fullness in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Sometimes the answer is not what we asked for, but something better. Persistence keeps us talking with God until we hear His answer, whatever it is.

### Try this

Choose one long-term request that you have stopped praying for, or that you are tempted to give up on.

- **Write it down** with today's date, and keep it somewhere you will see it.
- **Pray for it every day this week.** Use the widow's simple, honest words if you like: "Father, please act."
- **Pray it with thanks.** Paul wrote, "Keep on praying, and stay alert as you pray, giving thanks" (Colossians 4:2). Thank God in advance that He hears you and will answer in His time.
- **Find a partner.** Ask one trusted friend to pray with you for this request over the coming months. When the answer comes, write the date beside the first one.

### A closing prayer

Father, You are not an unjust judge. You are my loving Father, and You hear me. Forgive me for the prayers I have stopped praying. Give me the widow's faith, that keeps coming back and does not lose heart. I bring You again the things I have long asked for. I trust Your timing, even when I do not understand it. And when Your Son returns, may He find this kind of faith in me. In His name I pray. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Is there a prayer you have given up on? What made you stop?
- How does it help to know that God is not like the unjust judge?
- What is the difference between persistent prayer and refusing to accept God's answer?

## "God, Have Mercy on Me, a Sinner"

> "The tax collector, though, kept back at a distance and would not even lift his eyes toward heaven. Instead he struck his chest again and again and said, 'God, be merciful to me, the sinner!'" (Luke 18:13)

Luke tells us exactly whom this story was for:

> "There were some who relied on themselves, sure that they were righteous, and who looked down on all others. Jesus told them this parable." (Luke 18:9)

Then Jesus painted a scene that everyone listening could see in their minds. It is the hour of prayer at the temple in Jerusalem. The great courts are filling with worshipers. The smoke of the sacrifice rises from the altar. And two men come up the steps to pray.

> "Two men went up to the temple to pray. One was a Pharisee, and the other was a tax collector." (Luke 18:10)

To Jesus' first listeners, the story seemed to be over before it began. Everyone knew who the good man was and who the bad man was.

The Pharisees were the most respected religious people of their day. They studied the Scriptures, kept the law carefully, prayed at the set hours and gave generously. Ordinary people looked up to them.

Tax collectors were among the most hated people in the land. They worked for the Roman occupiers, collecting money from their own people, and many of them took far more than they should and kept the difference. They were seen as traitors and thieves. Respectable people would not eat with them.

So the listeners waited to hear how the good man would be praised and the bad man condemned.

### Two prayers

The Pharisee stood up and prayed. Luke's wording is striking: he "prayed this prayer about himself."

> "God, I thank You that I am not like the rest of humanity, who are swindlers, crooks and adulterers, or even like this tax collector. Twice every week I fast, and I give a tenth of everything I take in." (Luke 18:11-12)

Everything he said may have been true. He probably did fast twice a week and give a tenth of his income. Those are good things. But listen to his prayer. He begins with "God," and then he never mentions God again. He asks for nothing. He confesses nothing. His prayer is a list of his own achievements, and a comparison with people he thinks are worse than him. He even glances across the court at the tax collector and uses him as an example.

Then Jesus turns our eyes to the other man. He "kept back at a distance." He would not even lift his eyes toward heaven. He struck his chest again and again, the way people did in deep grief. And he prayed just seven words in our English Bible:

> "God, be merciful to me, the sinner!"

He did not compare himself with anyone. He did not list his good deeds. He did not make excuses. He called himself not *a* sinner, but *the* sinner, as if there were no one worse. He threw himself completely on the mercy of God.

The word he used for "be merciful" is connected in the Bible with the sacrifice that covered sin. As the smoke of the offering rose from the altar, he was asking God to accept that sacrifice for him. He had nothing to offer God but his need.

### The shock

Then Jesus gave His verdict, and it turned the whole world upside down:

> "I tell you, it was this man, not the other, who went home put right with God. For everyone who lifts themselves up will be brought low, and the one who takes the low place will be lifted up." (Luke 18:14)

The religious man went home exactly as he came. The sinner went home forgiven.

Dwight L. Moody, the American evangelist who preached to millions in the 1800s, loved this short prayer. He noticed how quickly it was answered:

> "How short the publican's prayer was: 'God be merciful to me a sinner!' The Syrophenician woman's was shorter still: 'Lord help me!' She went right to the mark, and she got what she wanted."
> Dwight L. Moody, *Prevailing Prayer*

("Publican" is the old English word for a tax collector.)

### Humility, the doorway of prayer

The Bible says the same thing in many places. God is high and holy, and yet, as Isaiah heard Him say, "I am also with the one who is crushed and low in spirit" (Isaiah 57:15). David, after his own great sin, prayed, "A heart that is broken and crushed, God, You will never reject" (Psalm 51:17). James wrote, "God sets Himself against the proud, but to the humble He gives grace" (James 4:6).

Andrew Murray wrote a whole book on humility, and he saw this parable as part of a pattern that runs through all of Jesus' teaching:

> "The lowliest is the nearest to God."
> Andrew Murray, *Humility*

And:

> "Humiliation is the only ladder to honour in God's kingdom."
> Andrew Murray, *Humility*

This is not about pretending to be worse than we are, or hating ourselves. It is simply about telling the truth. "Everyone has sinned, and all come short of God's glory" (Romans 3:23). The Pharisee did not see that truth about himself. The tax collector did. And the truth set him free.

The tax collector's prayer became one of the best-loved prayers in the history of the church. A blind beggar on the road to Jericho cried something very like it to Jesus, "Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!" (Luke 18:38), only a few verses later in Luke's Gospel. In Volume 4 we will meet Christians in the deserts of Egypt and the monasteries of the East who joined these cries into a short prayer they prayed thousands of times: "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner." Christians all over the world still pray it today.

### Three key insights

1. **Humility is the doorway of prayer.** God hears the prayer that comes from an honest and humble heart. We do not need fine words or a good record. We need to come as we are and ask for mercy.
2. **Comparing ourselves with others poisons prayer.** The Pharisee's prayer was ruined by his eye on the tax collector. When we pray, our eyes belong on God, not on the people we think are worse than us.
3. **God answers the cry for mercy at once.** The tax collector went home put right with God that same day. No one is too sinful to pray this prayer, and no one who prays it sincerely is turned away.

### For us today

This parable is a warning especially for religious people, and that includes most of us who read books like this one. It is possible to pray, fast, give, go to church and read the Bible, and still be the Pharisee. The danger is not in the good things we do. It is in trusting them, and in looking down on others.

The Pharisee's prayer can take many forms. "Thank God I'm not like those people." Those people might be another church, another tribe, another nation, another political party, or the neighbor whose life is a mess. Whenever we find ourselves thinking that way, the tax collector's prayer is the cure. We stop looking sideways and look up.

And if you are the tax collector, if you feel too sinful, too broken or too far gone to pray, this parable is for you. You do not need to clean yourself up first. You do not need to find the right words. Seven words are enough. Jesus told this story so that you would know that God is waiting to hear them.

### Try this

This week, let the tax collector teach you to pray.

- **Pray his prayer each morning.** Say slowly, "God, be merciful to me, the sinner." Then be quiet, and receive His mercy with thanks.
- **Pray it through the day.** Moody noticed how short the best prayers are. Whenever you remember during the day, pray it again silently: while you work, walk or wait.
- **Check your comparisons.** Ask the Holy Spirit to show you anyone you look down on. Then pray for that person, asking God to bless them.
- **Confess one thing honestly.** Bring to God one sin you have been excusing, and ask for His forgiveness without making excuses.

### A closing prayer

God, be merciful to me, the sinner. I come to You with nothing to offer but my need. Forgive me for the times I have trusted in my own goodness and looked down on others. Forgive me for comparing myself with anyone but Jesus. Thank You that You do not turn away a broken and humble heart. Thank You that Jesus Himself is the sacrifice for my sin. Send me home today put right with You. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- In what ways are you tempted to pray like the Pharisee?
- Who are the people you are most tempted to look down on? What would it mean to pray for them?
- Why do you think such a short prayer was enough for the tax collector? What does that teach you about prayer?

- John Bunyan, *The Jerusalem Sinner Saved*

## Faith That Moves Mountains

> "So I tell you: everything you request when you pray, believe that you received it, and you will have it." (Mark 11:24)

It was the last week of Jesus' life. Each morning He walked with His disciples from the village of Bethany, where they were staying, over the Mount of Olives and into Jerusalem. Each evening they walked back.

One morning, Jesus was hungry. Beside the road He saw a fig tree, covered in leaves. Usually, leaves on a fig tree promised that some early fruit would be there too. But when He reached it, there was nothing. Only leaves. Jesus spoke to the tree: "Let no one eat fruit from you ever again" (Mark 11:14). The disciples heard Him.

They went on into the city, where Jesus drove the traders out of the temple, saying that God's house should be "a house of prayer for all the nations" (Mark 11:17). We will return to that scene in chapter 12. The temple, like the fig tree, was full of leaves and empty of fruit: busy with religion, but missing the heart of prayer.

The next morning, as they walked the same road, Peter saw the tree:

> "Early the next day, as they went along, they saw the fig tree withered from the roots up. Peter remembered and said to Him, 'Look, Rabbi! The fig tree You cursed is all dried up.'" (Mark 11:20-21)

The disciples were amazed. Jesus used their amazement to teach them about prayer.

### "Have faith in God"

> "Jesus answered them, 'Have faith in God. Truly I tell you, if anyone tells this mountain, "Rise up and be hurled into the sea," and does not waver in their heart, but believes that what they say is taking place, it will be done for them. So I tell you: everything you request when you pray, believe that you received it, and you will have it.'" (Mark 11:22-24)

They were standing on or near the Mount of Olives. Jesus may well have pointed to the mountain under their feet, or across to the hill where the temple stood. "This mountain." It was a vivid picture. Jewish writings use the phrase "uprooter of mountains" for a teacher who could solve the hardest problems. Jesus was saying that nothing, no problem and no obstacle, is too big for God.

Notice where Jesus begins. Not with "Have faith in your prayers" or "Have faith in your faith," but "Have faith in God." Everything else rests on that. The power is not in our words, or in how hard we believe. The power is in the God we believe in.

Andrew Murray saw this clearly:

> "The power to believe a promise depends entirely, but only, on faith in the promiser. Trust in the person begets trust in his word."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

If you want stronger faith, do not stare at the mountain. Look at God. Get to know Him in His Word and in prayer. The more you know Him, the easier it becomes to trust what He says.

### "Believe that you received it"

Then Jesus gives a promise so large that many Christians have been nervous of it: "Everything you request when you pray, believe that you received it, and you will have it."

Murray admitted that our first instinct is to shrink it down to something safe:

> "What a promise! so large, so Divine, that our little hearts cannot take it in, and in every possible way seek to limit it to what we think safe or probable."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

We should not shrink this promise. But we should read it in the light of the whole of Scripture, as Jesus meant it. The same Gospel of Mark, only three chapters later, shows us Jesus praying in Gethsemane: "Abba, Father, for You all things are possible. Remove this cup from Me. Yet let it be what You will, not what I will" (Mark 14:36). Jesus believed that all things were possible for God, and He surrendered to God's will. The two belong together.

John put it this way: "Whenever we ask for something in line with His will, He listens to us. And if we know that He hears us in whatever we ask, we know that the requests we have made of Him are ours" (1 John 5:14-15). Faith that moves mountains is not a technique for getting whatever we want. Some teachers have twisted this verse into a promise of wealth and comfort for anyone who believes hard enough. That is not what Jesus taught. Faith is trusting a God who is good and wise, believing that He will do what He has promised, and leaving the how and the when to Him.

### Faith and forgiveness

Then Jesus adds something we might not expect:

> "And whenever you stand up to pray, if you are holding anything against anyone, forgive them, so that your Father in heaven may forgive you your wrongdoings as well." (Mark 11:25)

In the middle of teaching about mountain-moving faith, Jesus talks about forgiveness. Why? Because bitterness blocks faith. A heart full of resentment cannot rest in God's goodness. We saw the same link in the Lord's Prayer (chapter 5). Before we ask God to move mountains, we may need to let Him move the mountain of unforgiveness in our own hearts.

### Three key insights

1. **Faith looks at God, not at the mountain.** Jesus said, "Have faith in God." The size of the problem matters less than the greatness of the One we bring it to. Strong faith grows from knowing God well.
2. **Faith trusts God's will as well as His power.** Jesus believed all things were possible for His Father, and He also prayed, "Let it be what You will, not what I will." Mountain-moving faith and humble surrender are not enemies. They are partners.
3. **Faith and forgiveness go together.** Jesus linked believing prayer with a forgiving heart. Unforgiveness is a mountain that can stand between us and God.

### For us today

Everyone faces mountains. A sickness that doctors cannot cure. An addiction that will not let go. Poverty that seems endless. A child far from God. A nation closed to the gospel. A church that has lost its first love. Sometimes the mountain is so big that we stop praying about it at all.

Jesus says: bring it to God. Speak to the mountain in His name. Believe that He hears you. In later volumes of this series we will meet men and women who did exactly that. Hudson Taylor prayed for workers for the unreached provinces of inland China, and hundreds came. George Müller prayed for bread for thousands of orphans, and God provided for them for more than sixty years. Christians in Korea, Africa and Latin America prayed for revival, and whole regions were changed. They were ordinary people with extraordinary faith in an extraordinary God.

But perhaps your faith feels very small. Take heart. Jesus said that faith "even the size of a mustard seed," one of the smallest seeds people knew, could move a mountain (Matthew 17:20). It is not the size of your faith that matters, but the size of your God. When a desperate father brought his son to Jesus, he could only say, "I believe! Help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24). Jesus did not send him away. He healed the boy. That father's honest prayer can be ours too.

### Try this

This week, bring one mountain to God in faith.

- **Name your mountain.** Write down the one problem that seems most impossible to you right now.
- **Look at God, not the mountain.** Find one promise or truth about God in Scripture that speaks to your situation. Read it every day this week. Let it fill your view.
- **Clear the way.** Ask God whether you are holding anything against anyone. If so, forgive them before Him, as Mark 11:25 says.
- **Pray with thanks.** Bring your request to God each day, and thank Him in advance that He hears you and will act in His way and His time (Philippians 4:6). If your faith is weak, pray honestly, "I believe! Help my unbelief!"

### A closing prayer

Father, You are the God for whom all things are possible. I bring You the mountain in front of me. I cannot move it, but You can. Help me to look at You and not at the problem. Teach me to believe Your promises, and to trust Your will even when I do not understand it. Show me anyone I need to forgive, and give me grace to do it. I believe; help my unbelief. In the name of Jesus, who prayed, "Let it be what You will, not what I will." Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What is the "mountain" in your life right now? Have you brought it to God, or have you stopped praying about it?
- Why do you think Jesus said "Have faith in God" before giving His great promise about prayer?
- How do you hold together faith that "all things are possible" and the prayer "let it be what You will, not what I will"?

- Dwight L. Moody, *Prevailing Prayer*, especially "Faith"

## Where Two or Three Agree

> "And again, truly I tell you: if two of you here on earth are of one mind about anything they ask for, it will be done for them by My Father in heaven. For whenever two or three come together in My name, I am there among them." (Matthew 18:19-20)

It was the middle of the night in Jerusalem, and a small house was full of people praying.

King Herod had arrested Peter and thrown him into prison. He had already put the apostle James to death with the sword, and the people had approved. Now Peter was chained between two soldiers, with guards at the door, waiting to be brought out for trial after the Passover. Everyone knew what that trial would mean.

The church had no army, no money and no friends at court. But it had prayer. Luke writes:

> "So Peter was under guard in the prison, but the church was praying to God for him without letup." (Acts 12:5)

That night an angel woke Peter, his chains fell off, and the gates of the prison opened on their own. Peter walked out into the dark street, hardly believing it was real. Then he went to the house where he knew his friends would be:

> "He made his way to the home of Mary, mother of John whose other name was Mark. A good number of people had come together there and were praying." (Acts 12:12)

When he knocked, a servant girl named Rhoda came to the door, recognized his voice, and was so excited that she ran back inside to tell everyone, leaving Peter standing in the street. The people praying for his release told her she was out of her mind. Peter kept knocking. When they finally opened the door, they were astonished.

It is a story full of warmth and even humor. The church prayed together, God answered more quickly than they could believe, and the answer stood knocking at the door while they were still praying for it.

### The promise of united prayer

The early church prayed together because Jesus had taught them to. In Matthew 18 He gave a special promise to those who pray as one: "If two of you here on earth are of one mind about anything they ask for, it will be done for them by My Father in heaven."

The word Matthew uses for being "of one mind" is the word from which we get the English word "symphony." It describes different instruments sounding together in harmony. Two or three believers, each with their own voice and story, come before God with one desire. Jesus says the Father delights to answer that kind of prayer.

Then He adds a promise of His presence: "Whenever two or three come together in My name, I am there among them." Not two or three thousand. Two or three. The smallest prayer meeting in the poorest village, in a prison cell or a hospital room, has the Lord Jesus Himself in the middle of it.

### Agreement begins with reconciliation

It is important to see where Jesus gave this promise. Matthew 18 is about broken relationships in the family of God. Just before these verses, Jesus teaches what to do when a brother or sister sins against you: "Go and confront them about it, with only the two of you present. If they will listen to you, you have won your brother or sister back" (Matthew 18:15). Right after them, Peter asks how many times he must forgive.

So the promise of agreement in prayer sits in the middle of teaching about reconciliation. That is no accident. People cannot truly pray as one while they are divided. Jesus said elsewhere that if you remember at the altar that someone has something against you, "Go and make peace with them first, and then come back and offer your gift" (Matthew 5:24). The symphony of united prayer begins when the instruments are in tune with each other.

### The church that prayed together

From the very beginning, the followers of Jesus prayed together. After His ascension, the apostles, together with Mary His mother and the other women, waited in Jerusalem: "All of these were devoted to prayer and of one mind in it" (Acts 1:14). The Holy Spirit came on them at Pentecost while "they were all gathered in one place" (Acts 2:1).

When the apostles were threatened, the believers "lifted their voices to God together and prayed" (Acts 4:24). And "when they finished praying, the place where they had met shook, and every one of them was filled with the Holy Spirit, and they went on proclaiming God's word fearlessly" (Acts 4:31).

Again and again in church history, great movements of God have begun with a few people praying together. In later volumes we will meet the Moravians in Germany, who began a prayer meeting in 1727, after a season of bitter quarrels had been healed, that lasted for a hundred years. We will meet five students in America who prayed under a haystack in a thunderstorm and started a missionary movement. We will meet a businessman in New York who began a lunchtime prayer meeting that grew to thousands. Each began with two or three.

### A voice from the past

Andrew Murray, having taught about the secret room, turned to this passage and showed how both kinds of prayer belong together:

> "As a tree has its root hidden in the ground and its stem growing up into the sunlight, so prayer needs equally for its full development the hidden secrecy in which the soul meets God alone, and the public fellowship with those who find in the name of Jesus their common meeting-place."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

He also pointed out how strange it would be for God's children never to pray together:

> "Nothing would be more unnatural than that the children of a family should always meet their father separately, but never in the united expression of their desires or their love."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

### Three key insights

1. **United prayer carries a special promise.** Jesus promised that the Father will answer when two or three agree in prayer, and that He Himself is present where they gather in His name. No prayer meeting is too small.
2. **True agreement begins with reconciliation.** Jesus gave this promise in the middle of His teaching on restoring broken relationships. We cannot pray as one while we refuse to be at peace with one another.
3. **We need both the secret room and the prayer meeting.** Private prayer is the root; united prayer is the tree in the sunlight. A healthy prayer life has both.

### For us today

In many churches, the prayer meeting is the smallest meeting of the week, if it happens at all. People will come to hear a sermon or a concert, but few will come to pray. Yet the history of the church shows that God has often done His greatest works through small groups of people who simply met to pray together.

You do not need a large group or a special program. You need one or two others. A husband and wife praying together before bed. Two friends meeting before work once a week. A few mothers praying for their children's school. Workers on a factory floor praying together during a break. A family gathering at the end of the day. Jesus said, "I am there among them."

And if there is someone in your church you cannot pray with because of a quarrel, this passage asks you to go to them first. United prayer is worth the cost of making peace.

### Try this

This week, practice the prayer of agreement.

- **Find one prayer partner.** Ask a friend, family member or fellow believer to pray with you at least once this week, in person or by phone.
- **Agree on one specific thing.** Murray wrote that true united prayer needs "agreement as to the thing asked." Choose one clear request together, and both pray for it.
- **Remember who is there.** Before you begin, read Matthew 18:20 aloud and thank Jesus that He is present with you.
- **Make peace first.** If there is someone you are at odds with, take one step this week toward reconciliation before you pray.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for Your promise that where two or three gather in Your name, You are there among them. Forgive me for the times I have prayed only alone, and for the times I have let quarrels divide me from Your people. Give me partners in prayer. Tune our hearts together, so that we pray with one mind. And do in answer to our united prayers what we could never do alone, for the glory of Your Father. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- When did you last pray with another person, not only for them? What was that like?
- Why do you think Jesus placed this promise in a passage about reconciliation?
- Who could you ask to be a prayer partner with you? What one thing could you agree to pray for together?

## A House of Prayer for All Nations

> "Then He taught them, saying, 'Does Scripture not say, "My house will be known as a house of prayer for all the nations"? But you have turned it into a hideout for bandits.'" (Mark 11:17)

The temple in Jerusalem was one of the wonders of the ancient world. Its white stone and gold shone in the sun and could be seen from miles away. Around the holy building itself were great open courts, one inside another. The innermost courts were for the priests and the people of Israel. But the largest court of all, on the outside, was open to everyone. It was the one place in the temple where people from other nations, who worshiped the God of Israel but were not Jews, could come to pray.

That was the court Jesus walked into in the last week of His life.

It was Passover season, and the city was crowded with pilgrims. The outer court had become a marketplace. There were stalls selling animals for sacrifice: oxen, sheep, and doves for the poor who could not afford anything larger. There were tables where money changers exchanged pilgrims' coins for the special coins needed to pay the temple tax, often at a profit. The air was filled with the noise of bargaining, the smell of animals and the clink of coins. People used the court as a shortcut through the city, carrying their goods across it.

Imagine a man from a distant land who had traveled for weeks to pray to the God of Israel. He arrives at the only place in the temple where he is allowed to pray, and he cannot hear himself think.

> "He went into the temple courts and began driving out the people who were selling and buying there. He turned over the money changers' tables and the benches of the dove sellers, and He would not let anyone carry goods through the temple courts." (Mark 11:15-16)

### Two prophets

When Jesus explained what He had done, He quoted two prophets.

The first was Isaiah. God had promised that foreigners who turned to Him would be welcome in His house:

> "I will lead them to My holy mountain and make them glad in My house of prayer. Their burnt offerings and sacrifices will find favor on My altar, for My house will be named a house of prayer for all peoples." (Isaiah 56:7)

This had always been God's plan. When Solomon dedicated the first temple a thousand years earlier, he prayed especially for "the foreigner who is not one of Your people Israel but comes from a distant land," asking God to hear their prayers too, "Then all the peoples of the earth will come to know Your name" (1 Kings 8:41-43).

The second was Jeremiah, who had warned the people of his day that they were treating the temple as "a hideout for robbers" (Jeremiah 7:11). A hideout is where robbers go to feel safe after they have done wrong. Jeremiah's point was that the people were living unjust lives and then hiding behind their religion, as if being near the temple would protect them. Jesus saw the same thing in His day. The house of God had become a place for profit and for hiding, instead of a place for prayer.

### The zeal of Jesus

This is one of the few times in the Gospels that we see Jesus angry. It was not a loss of temper. It was holy zeal for His Father's house. When John tells of Jesus clearing the temple, he says the disciples remembered the words of the psalm, "Zeal for Your house will devour Me" (John 2:17).

What made Him so angry? Not that people were buying and selling. That has its place. It was that the buying and selling had pushed out the prayer. The one space set aside for the nations to pray had been filled with business. The outsiders who were seeking God had been crowded out by insiders who were seeking money.

E. M. Bounds wrote about this scene in *The Necessity of Prayer*:

> "Prayer is always in place in the house of God. When prayer is a stranger there, then it ceases to be God's house at all."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Necessity of Prayer*

And:

> "Prayer is perfectly at home in the house of God. It is no stranger, no mere guest; it belongs there."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Necessity of Prayer*

### For all the nations

Notice the words "for all the nations." From the very beginning, God's heart was for the whole world. He chose Israel not to keep His blessing for one people, but so that through them "all the peoples of the earth" would come to know Him. The psalmist prayed:

> "May God show us grace and bless us; may He let the light of His face shine on us. Then the earth will know Your ways, and every nation Your power to save." (Psalm 67:1-2)

And the Bible ends with that prayer answered. John sees "a crowd so vast that no one could number it. They came from every nation and from all tribes and peoples and languages, and they stood before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9).

Today God's house is not a building in Jerusalem. Through Jesus, His people themselves have become His temple, the place where He lives by His Spirit. That means the church in every land is called to be what the temple was meant to be: a house of prayer, open to all nations.

### Three key insights

1. **God's house is first of all a house of prayer.** Many good things happen in church, but prayer is at the center. When prayer becomes a stranger in God's house, something has gone badly wrong.
2. **God's heart is for all the nations.** Our prayers should be as wide as His love. The church's prayer is meant to embrace every people, tribe and language on earth.
3. **Jesus clears away what keeps others from God.** He drove out the business that crowded the outsiders away from prayer. He still challenges anything in our churches that makes it hard for others to come near to God.

### For us today

It is worth asking honest questions about our own churches. Is prayer at the center, or at the edges? Is the prayer meeting the heart of the church, or a small gathering that most people skip? Do we spend more energy on buildings, programs and money than on seeking God together?

And who is being crowded out? The court of the nations was meant for outsiders. Our churches can become places where only one kind of person feels at home: one tribe, one language, one social class, one generation. A house of prayer for all nations is a place where the stranger, the refugee, the poor and the person from another people can come and pray and be welcomed.

There is good news here too. In our time, the church has become more truly global than ever before. There are believers today in almost every nation on earth, and many of the most vibrant praying churches are in Africa, Asia and Latin America. In Volume 10 we will meet some of them. The prayer of Psalm 67 is being answered before our eyes.

### Try this

This week, let your prayers become a house of prayer for all nations.

- **Pray for one nation each day.** Choose seven countries, perhaps your own, your neighbors', and some far away. Each day, learn one thing about one of them, and pray for its people and its church.
- **Pray in God's house.** If your church building is open, go there once this week simply to pray, not for a service.
- **Go to the prayer meeting.** If your church has a prayer meeting, attend it this week. If it does not, ask your pastor whether you could help to start one.
- **Welcome a stranger.** Look for one person at church or in your neighborhood who is an outsider, and make them welcome.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You were consumed with zeal for Your Father's house. Make my church a house of prayer. Clear away anything in me and in us that has crowded out prayer, or crowded out the people You love. Give me a heart as wide as Yours, for every nation, tribe and language. Let Your light shine on us, so that the whole earth may know Your ways and every nation Your power to save. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- If Jesus walked into your church this week, what would He find at the center?
- Who might feel crowded out or unwelcome in your church? What could change that?
- Which nation or people group has God put on your heart to pray for?

## Ask the Lord of the Harvest

> "Then He said to His disciples, 'The harvest is large, but there are few workers. So pray to the Lord of the harvest, and ask Him to send out workers into His harvest.'" (Matthew 9:37-38)

Anyone who has lived in a farming community knows the urgency of harvest time.

For months the farmer has plowed, sown, weeded and waited. Now the grain stands golden in the fields, ready to be gathered. But a harvest cannot wait. If it is not brought in within a few days, the grain will fall to the ground, rot in the rain or be eaten by birds. A whole year's work can be lost. So at harvest time the whole village goes out into the fields, from dawn until dark. Every pair of hands is needed.

Jesus had grown up in a farming land. When He looked at the crowds of Galilee, He saw a harvest field.

### What Jesus saw

Matthew tells us what Jesus had been doing:

> "Jesus went through all the towns and villages, teaching in their synagogues, announcing the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every disease and every illness." (Matthew 9:35)

Everywhere He went, people came to Him. And He did not see them simply as a crowd. He saw each one:

> "When He saw the crowds, He felt deep pity for them, because they were worn out and abandoned, like sheep with no shepherd." (Matthew 9:36)

The word Matthew uses for "deep pity" describes a feeling deep in the stomach, a love so strong it can be felt in the body. Jesus looked at the crowds and His heart broke for them. They were tired, confused, sick and lost. Their religious leaders had failed them. They were like sheep wandering on the hills with no shepherd to lead them to food and water or protect them from wolves.

Then Jesus turned to His disciples. They might have expected Him to say, "Go and help them." Instead, He said, "Pray."

### Why pray?

At first this may seem strange. The need was urgent. Why not just send the disciples out at once? Andrew Murray noticed something unusual about this command:

> "The Lord frequently taught His disciples that they must pray, and how; but seldom what to pray."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

Here is one of the few times Jesus told His followers exactly what to pray for: workers for the harvest. It shows how important this prayer is to Him.

Notice whose harvest it is. Jesus calls God "the Lord of the harvest," and He calls the harvest "His harvest." The people in the fields do not belong to us. They belong to God. The work of bringing them in is His work, and the workers are His to call and send. We do not create the harvest, and we cannot produce the workers. We ask the Lord of the harvest to do it.

Murray believed this prayer truly makes a difference:

> "Prayer is no form or show. The Lord Jesus was Himself the truth; everything He spake was the deepest truth."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

If Jesus told us to pray for workers, it is because our prayers really matter in God's plan. God has chosen to send workers in answer to the prayers of His people.

### The ones who prayed became the answer

Now look at what happens next. Matthew's very next sentence is this:

> "Jesus called His twelve disciples to Him and gave them authority over unclean spirits, to drive them out, and to heal every disease and every illness." (Matthew 10:1)

A few verses later, "Jesus sent these twelve out" (Matthew 10:5). The men He had just told to pray for workers were the workers He sent.

This happens again and again. When we pray honestly for God to send workers, He often answers by sending us. Isaiah had a vision of God asking, "Whom am I to send? Who will go on our behalf?" and he answered, "I am ready. Send me" (Isaiah 6:8). Praying for the harvest changes the person who prays. It is hard to keep asking God to send someone to your neighbor without beginning to wonder whether that someone is you.

### A prayer that changed the world

The history of Christian mission is full of answers to this prayer. In later volumes we will meet William Carey, a poor shoemaker in England who prayed and then went to India. We will meet the Moravians, whose hundred-year prayer meeting sent missionaries to the Caribbean, Greenland and Africa. We will meet five students who prayed in a field in America and started a movement that sent hundreds abroad. We will meet Hudson Taylor, who prayed for seventy new workers for China and saw them come.

But this prayer is not only for faraway places. The harvest is also in your street, your school, your workplace and your village. And today the workers come from everywhere. Missionaries now go out from Nigeria, Brazil, Korea, India, the Philippines and many other nations, carrying the gospel to every part of the world. The prayer of Matthew 9:38 is being answered from every direction.

Jesus said elsewhere, "Raise your eyes and look at the fields. They have turned white, ready for harvest" (John 4:35). The harvest is still large. The workers are still few. And the command is still the same: pray.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer for the harvest begins with compassion.** Jesus prayed for workers because He saw the crowds with deep pity. When we ask God to let us see people as He sees them, our prayers for them become urgent and real.
2. **The harvest belongs to the Lord.** It is His harvest, and the workers are His to call and send. We cannot produce them; we can only ask Him. And He has chosen to answer our prayers.
3. **Those who pray for workers often become workers.** The disciples who were told to pray were sent out themselves. Be ready: when you pray this prayer, God may answer by sending you.

### For us today

Who are the "sheep with no shepherd" around you? Perhaps the young people in your town who have no one to guide them. Perhaps the migrant workers who live far from home. Perhaps a people group in your country who have never heard the gospel in their own language. Perhaps a neighbor who is "worn out and abandoned."

Ask God to open your eyes to see them as Jesus does. Then pray for workers: pastors, evangelists, teachers, Bible translators, doctors, ordinary believers who will love them in Jesus' name. Pray for the workers already in the field, that God will strengthen them. And pray with an open hand, ready for God to say, "You go."

Some Christians set a daily reminder to pray this prayer, for example at 9:38 each morning or evening, to remember Matthew 9:38. However you do it, make it a regular part of your praying. It is one of the few prayers Jesus told us by name to pray.

### Try this

This week, pray every day for the harvest.

- **See the crowd.** Each day, take a moment to look at the people around you: on the street, at work, at the market. Ask God to give you His deep pity for them.
- **Pray Matthew 9:38.** Ask the Lord of the harvest to send workers into a specific place: your neighborhood, your city, an unreached people, a nation you care about.
- **Pray for one worker by name.** Choose a missionary, pastor or evangelist you know of, and pray for them each day. If you can, send them a message to tell them you are praying.
- **Offer yourself.** End your prayer each day with Isaiah's words: "I am ready. Send me." Then watch for how God answers.

### A closing prayer

Lord of the harvest, the harvest is Yours, and it is large. Open my eyes to see the crowds as Jesus saw them, worn out and abandoned, like sheep with no shepherd. Give me His deep pity. I pray now: send out workers into Your harvest, in my own town and to the ends of the earth. Strengthen those who are already in the fields. And if You want to send me, here I am. Send me. In Jesus' name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Who are the "sheep with no shepherd" in your community? How might Jesus see them?
- Why do you think Jesus told His disciples to pray before He sent them out?
- Are you willing for God to answer your prayer for workers by sending you? What would that mean for you?

## Thanks Before the Miracle

> "So they took the stone away. Then Jesus raised His eyes upward and said, 'Father, I give You thanks that You have heard Me. I Myself knew that You hear Me at all times. But I have spoken for the sake of the crowd standing around, so that they may believe that it was You who sent Me.'" (John 11:41-42)

The message came from Bethany, a village just outside Jerusalem. It was from two sisters, Martha and Mary, close friends of Jesus. Their words were short and urgent:

> "Lord, look, the one You love is ill." (John 11:3)

The one He loved was their brother Lazarus. The sisters did not ask Jesus to come. They did not need to. They knew He loved Lazarus, and they were sure He would hurry to help.

But He did not hurry. John tells us something that must have puzzled the disciples: "So when He heard that Lazarus was ill, He stayed where He was for two more days" (John 11:6).

By the time Jesus arrived in Bethany, Lazarus had died and had been in the tomb for four days. The house was full of mourners. Martha came out to meet Jesus on the road, and her first words carried all the pain of those days of waiting: "Lord, had You been here, my brother would not have died" (John 11:21). Mary said the same words when she came, falling at His feet in tears.

And then we read the shortest verse in the Bible: "Jesus wept" (John 11:35).

### At the tomb

They went to the tomb, a cave with a large stone across the entrance. Jesus said, "Take the stone away." Martha, practical as always, objected: "Lord, he already stinks; this is the fourth day" (John 11:39). There was no doubt at all that Lazarus was dead.

They rolled the stone away. Everyone was watching. And Jesus lifted His eyes to heaven and prayed.

Notice what He said. He did not ask the Father to raise Lazarus. He thanked Him: "Father, I give You thanks that You have heard Me."

Jesus gave thanks before anything had happened. Lazarus was still lying dead in the tomb. The smell of death was in the air. The crowd was weeping. And Jesus said, "Thank You."

It seems that Jesus had already prayed about Lazarus, perhaps during those two days of waiting, and He knew that His Father had heard. So He thanked Him for the answer before it could be seen. Then He explained why He prayed out loud: "For the sake of the crowd standing around, so that they may believe." He wanted the people to know that what was about to happen came from the Father.

Then He cried out with a loud voice, "Lazarus, come out!" And the dead man came out, still wrapped in his grave clothes. "Untie him," Jesus said, "and let him go" (John 11:43-44).

### A thankful Savior

Thanksgiving was part of the way Jesus always prayed. When He fed the five thousand with five loaves, "Jesus took the loaves, gave thanks, and shared them out" (John 6:11). He gave thanks before the bread multiplied, while it was still a small boy's lunch. On the night He was betrayed, knowing that He was about to suffer and die, "He took bread, and after giving thanks He broke it and handed it to them" (Luke 22:19). Even with the cross before Him, He gave thanks.

Thanksgiving runs through the prayers of God's people in every age. Daniel, when he heard that praying to God had been made a crime, went home and prayed three times a day, "giving thanks to his God, just as he had always done" (Daniel 6:10). Jonah, from inside the great fish, prayed, "I will sacrifice to You with a song of thanksgiving... It is the LORD who saves" (Jonah 2:9). And Paul, writing from prison, said:

> "Do not worry about anything. Instead, in everything, bring your requests to God through prayer and petition, with thanksgiving." (Philippians 4:6)

### A voice from the past

R. A. Torrey, the American Bible teacher who led great evangelistic missions around the world in the early 1900s, believed that thanksgiving was one of the most neglected parts of prayer:

> "In approaching God to ask for new blessings, we should never forget to return thanks for blessings already granted."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

He noticed that we are often very specific in our requests, but vague in our thanks:

> "We should be just as definite in returning thanks as we are in prayer."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

And he saw that thanksgiving makes faith stronger:

> "Returning thanks for blessings already received increases our faith and enables us to approach God with new boldness and new assurance."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

### Tears and thanks together

There is something very important in this story. Jesus wept, and then He gave thanks. He did not pretend that death was not terrible, or that the sisters' grief did not matter. He felt it deeply. His thanksgiving was not a way of covering up sorrow. It was faith speaking in the middle of sorrow.

That matters for us. Sometimes Christians feel they must hide their pain and always look happy, as if thanksgiving means pretending. Jesus shows us a better way. We can weep honestly and still say, "Father, thank You that You hear me." Paul wrote, "Whatever happens, give thanks" (1 Thessalonians 5:18). He did not say to give thanks *for* everything, as if evil were good. He said to give thanks *in* everything, because even in the darkest place, God is still good and still hears.

### Three key insights

1. **Thanksgiving is faith speaking before the answer is seen.** Jesus thanked His Father while Lazarus was still in the tomb. When we thank God before we see the answer, we are saying that we trust Him.
2. **Thanksgiving and tears can go together.** Jesus wept, and then He gave thanks. Thankful prayer is not pretending that pain does not hurt. It is trusting God in the middle of it.
3. **Remembering past answers builds faith for new ones.** Torrey saw that thanking God for what He has already done gives us new boldness to ask again. A thankful heart is a believing heart.

### For us today

Think about how most of our prayers begin. Often we rush straight to our requests. We have a list of needs, and we hand it to God like a shopping list. There is nothing wrong with asking; Jesus told us to ask. But how often do we stop to thank Him?

Torrey suggested an exercise: think back over the prayers God has already answered in your life. Most of us will find that we have forgotten many of them, and thanked Him for few. Remembering them is like turning on a light. Suddenly we see how faithful He has been, and our faith for today grows stronger.

And think of the "tombs" in your life: the situations that seem dead and hopeless. A marriage, a child, a church, a dream, a body. Like Martha, you may say, "Lord, it's too late." Jesus may be waiting there for you to roll away the stone, and to hear you say, even through tears, "Father, thank You that You hear me."

### Try this

This week, let thanksgiving lead your prayers.

- **Start with thanks.** Before you ask for anything each day, thank God for three specific things. Be as definite in your thanks as you are in your requests.
- **Remember answered prayers.** Write a list of prayers God has answered in your life. Add to it each day this week. Read it back on the last day and give thanks.
- **Thank Him before the answer.** Choose one request you are still waiting for. Each day, pray for it and then say, "Father, thank You that You have heard me," as Jesus did.
- **Thank Him in a hard place.** If you are in a season of sorrow, bring your tears to God honestly, and then thank Him for one way He is still with you.

### A closing prayer

Father, thank You that You hear me. Thank You for every prayer You have answered in my life, even the ones I have forgotten. Forgive me for coming to You so often with requests and so seldom with thanks. Teach me to thank You before I see the answer, as Jesus did at the tomb. When I weep, let me weep in Your presence, and still say, "Thank You." And let my thankful heart become a believing heart. In Jesus' name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- How much of your prayer time is spent asking, and how much giving thanks?
- Can you remember a prayer God answered that you never thanked Him for? Thank Him now.
- What is the "tomb" in your life where it is hardest to give thanks? What would it mean to thank God there?

## Asking in His Name

> "So far you have asked for nothing in My name. Ask and you will receive, so that your joy will be complete." (John 16:24)

In the city of Ephesus, some years after Jesus had risen and returned to the Father, a group of men decided to try a new technique.

They were traveling Jewish exorcists, men who went from town to town claiming to drive out evil spirits. They had seen the apostle Paul cast out demons in the name of Jesus, and they thought they had discovered the secret. So they began to use the same words:

> "'I charge you by the Jesus whom Paul preaches,' they would say. A Jewish high priest named Sceva had seven sons who were doing this. The evil spirit answered them, 'I know Jesus, and I am acquainted with Paul. But you, who are you?' Then the man who had the evil spirit threw himself on them. He mastered every one of them and was too strong for them, so that they fled from that house stripped of their clothes and covered with wounds." (Acts 19:13-16)

It is a frightening story, and also a little comic: seven men running out into the street, bruised and without their clothes. But the lesson is serious. The name of Jesus is not a magic word. It does not work like a key that anyone can turn. The sons of Sceva used His name, but they did not know Him.

So what does it mean to pray "in the name of Jesus"?

### The night before the cross

Jesus gave His great promises about praying in His name on the last night before He died. He was with His disciples in an upper room in Jerusalem. He had washed their feet and shared the bread and the cup. Now He was preparing them for His departure.

In those hours He spoke again and again about asking in His name:

> "And whatever you ask in My name, I will do it, so that in the Son the Father may be glorified. If you ask Me anything in My name, I Myself will do it." (John 14:13-14)

> "So that the Father may give you whatever you ask Him in My name." (John 15:16)

> "So far you have asked for nothing in My name. Ask and you will receive, so that your joy will be complete." (John 16:24)

"So far you have asked for nothing in My name." Something new was about to begin. While Jesus was with them on earth, the disciples could simply turn to Him and ask. Soon He would be gone from their sight. But they would not be left alone. They would be able to come to the Father directly, in His name, and the Father would hear them.

### What is in a name?

In the Bible, a person's name stands for the person: their character, their authority and everything they are. To come in someone's name is to come as their representative, with their authority, on their business.

R. A. Torrey explained it with a picture from everyday life. Imagine going to a bank with a check. If you sign it yourself and you have no money in that bank, it will not be paid. But if the check is signed by someone who has a great deal of money in that bank, it will be paid at once, even though you have nothing there yourself:

> "I have nothing deposited there, I have absolutely no credit there, and if I go in my own name I will get absolutely nothing; but Jesus Christ has unlimited credit in heaven, and He has granted to me the privilege of going to the bank with His name on my checks."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

We do not come to God because we are good enough, or because we have prayed long enough, or because we have earned an answer. We come because of who Jesus is and what He has done. "It is through Him that both of us can approach the Father, in the one Spirit" (Ephesians 2:18). Jesus said, "Only through Me does anyone come to the Father" (John 14:6). His name is our only plea, and it is enough.

### Not a formula

Many Christians end their prayers with the words "in Jesus' name, amen." It is a good habit, and it is a good reminder. But Torrey was careful to warn:

> "Praying in the name of Christ is not merely adding the phrase 'I ask these things in Jesus' name' to my prayer."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

The sons of Sceva used the words, and the words did them no good. To pray in Jesus' name means to pray as one who belongs to Him, who trusts in Him, and who wants what He wants. Andrew Murray put it this way:

> "When I go in the name of another, I deny myself, I take not only his name, but himself and what he is, instead of myself and what I am."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

This explains why the promise is so large: "whatever you ask in My name." If we truly pray in His name, we will not ask for things He would be ashamed to sign. We will ask for what serves His purposes and brings glory to the Father. Jesus said He would answer "so that in the Son the Father may be glorified" (John 14:13). A prayer in Jesus' name is a prayer He would be glad to put His own name to.

### The Father Himself loves you

There is one more beautiful promise in this part of the Upper Room teaching:

> "On that day you will ask in My name, and I do not say that I will ask the Father on your behalf. For the Father Himself loves you, since you have loved Me and believed that I came out from God." (John 16:26-27)

Jesus is not saying that He no longer prays for us. Hebrews tells us that He always lives to intercede for us, and we will come to that in the last chapter of this book. He is saying something else: we do not need to think of the Father as unwilling, as if Jesus has to persuade Him to listen. "The Father Himself loves you." Praying in Jesus' name brings us into the love between the Father and the Son. And that is why Jesus says our joy will be "complete."

### Three key insights

1. **We come to God on Jesus' credit, not our own.** We have nothing to offer God that earns an answer. We come in the name of Jesus, who has "unlimited credit in heaven." His name is our only plea, and it is enough.
2. **Praying in His name means praying His will.** The name of Jesus is not a magic formula. It means asking as His representatives, for the things He wants, for the glory of the Father.
3. **The Father Himself loves us.** Jesus' name opens the way into the Father's love. Praying in His name is not only powerful; it is full of joy.

### For us today

In many parts of the world, Christians pray boldly "in the name of Jesus" against sickness, fear and evil spirits. This is good and right. The apostles did it: "In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, walk!" Peter said to a man who had been lame from birth (Acts 3:6). There is power in His name, because "Salvation comes through no one but Him" (Acts 4:12).

But the story of the sons of Sceva reminds us that the power is in the Person, not in the phrase. The name of Jesus belongs to those who belong to Jesus.

Next time you end a prayer with "in Jesus' name," pause for a moment. Ask yourself: Is this something Jesus would sign? Am I asking as His representative, for His purposes? Then say it with confidence and joy, because the Father Himself loves you.

### Try this

This week, learn to pray in the name of Jesus.

- **Pause before "in Jesus' name."** Each time you end a prayer, stop before those words and ask, "Lord, is this a prayer You would sign?" If not, change it.
- **Thank Him for access.** Each day, read Ephesians 2:18 and thank Jesus that through Him you can come to the Father.
- **Do your work in His name.** Paul wrote, "In everything you say and everything you do, act in the name of the Lord Jesus, and through Him give thanks to God the Father" (Colossians 3:17). Choose one task each day and do it consciously in His name.
- **Ask for joy.** Pray John 16:24 for yourself and for one other person: "Father, let our joy be complete."

### A closing prayer

Father, I come to You not in my own name, for I have nothing to offer You. I come in the name of Jesus, Your Son, who loved me and gave Himself for me. Thank You that through Him I can approach You, and that You Yourself love me. Teach me to pray as one who belongs to Jesus, asking for the things that He wants, so that You may be glorified in Him. And make my joy complete. In the name of Jesus. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What do you usually mean when you say "in Jesus' name" at the end of a prayer?
- Torrey compared praying in Jesus' name to using a check signed by someone with "unlimited credit." What does that picture teach you?
- Jesus said, "The Father Himself loves you." How does that change the way you come to Him in prayer?

## "I Have Prayed for You"

> "Simon, Simon, Satan has demanded to have all of you, to sift you like wheat. But I have prayed for you, so that your faith will not give out. And you, once you have turned back, must strengthen your brothers." (Luke 22:31-32)

It was the last supper Jesus would eat with His disciples before He died. The bread had been broken and the cup shared. Then, of all things, the disciples began to argue about which of them was the greatest (Luke 22:24).

Into that moment Jesus spoke to Peter. He used his old name, twice, the way you might speak to a dear friend when you have something serious to say: "Simon, Simon."

What He said next must have shocked them all. Satan, the enemy of God and of His people, had "demanded to have all of you." The word "you" here is plural in the original language. Satan wanted all of them. He wanted to "sift" them like wheat.

In the villages of Jesus' day, sifting wheat was hard, violent work. After the harvest, the grain was beaten and then shaken hard in a sieve, back and forth, again and again, until the husks and dirt fell through. Satan wanted to shake the disciples like that, until their faith broke apart.

Then Jesus' words change from plural to singular: "But I have prayed for *you*." He was speaking directly to Peter.

### Peter's answer

Peter did not understand. He was sure of himself:

> "Peter answered Him, 'Lord, I am ready to go to prison with You, and even to die with You.' Jesus replied, 'I tell you, Peter, before a rooster crows today, you will have said three times that you do not know Me.'" (Luke 22:33-34)

We know what happened. A few hours later Jesus was arrested, and Peter followed at a distance into the courtyard of the high priest's house. There, warming himself by a fire, he was asked three times whether he was one of Jesus' followers. Three times he said no. The third time, while he was still speaking, a rooster crowed.

> "And the Lord turned and looked at Peter. Then Peter remembered the Lord's words... He went outside and wept bitterly." (Luke 22:61-62)

### What Jesus prayed

Look carefully at what Jesus said He had prayed. He did not pray that Peter would be spared the test. He did not even pray that Peter would never fall. He knew Peter would fall that very night. He prayed "so that your faith will not give out."

Peter's courage failed. His words failed. His promises failed. But his faith, the deep root of trust in Jesus, did not die. That is why, when Jesus looked at him, Peter did not run away in despair. He wept the bitter tears of repentance. And three days later, when the women came with news of the empty tomb, Peter ran to see.

The Puritan Thomas Watson saw great comfort here:

> "Satan would have sifted Peter till he sifted out all his grace, but Christ would not suffer him."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

And:

> "It is a comfort that Satan cannot go a hair's breadth beyond God's permission."
> Thomas Watson, *The Lord's Prayer*

Like Job long before, Peter was tested within limits that God had set (Job 1:12). The enemy could shake him, but he could not destroy him, because Jesus had prayed.

### "Once you have turned back"

Notice the last part of Jesus' words: "And you, once you have turned back, must strengthen your brothers." Jesus did not only predict Peter's fall. He predicted his return. "Once," not "if." Before Peter had denied Him, Jesus was already planning his restoration, and giving him a job to do afterward.

After the resurrection, Jesus met Peter on the shore of Lake Galilee. Around a charcoal fire, like the one where Peter had denied Him, Jesus asked him three times, "Simon, son of John, do you love Me?" Three times Peter said yes. And three times Jesus gave him his task: "Feed My lambs... Shepherd My sheep... Feed My sheep" (John 21:15-17).

Peter went on to become a leader of the church. On the day of Pentecost he preached, and three thousand people believed. He wrote letters that we still read, full of encouragement for suffering Christians. The man who fell was the man God used to strengthen others, because he knew what it was to fall and to be lifted up.

### A voice from the past

E. M. Bounds, writing about this moment, could hardly contain himself:

> "Happy Peter, to have such an one as the Son of God to pray for him! Unhappy Peter, to be so in the toils of Satan as to demand so much of Christ's solicitude!"
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

"Solicitude" means loving care and concern. Bounds saw both sides: Peter was in great danger, and Peter was greatly loved. The same is true of us.

### Three key insights

1. **Jesus prays for us by name, even before we fall.** He saw Peter's failure coming and prayed for him in advance. He knows our weaknesses better than we do, and He prays for us.
2. **Jesus prays most of all for our faith.** He did not pray that Peter would escape the test, but that his faith would not give out. Our greatest need in times of testing is not comfort, but a faith that holds.
3. **Restored people strengthen others.** Jesus spoke of Peter's return before his fall, and gave him work to do afterward. Our failures, when we turn back, can become the very place from which we help others.

### For us today

Perhaps you have fallen badly. You made promises to God, like Peter, and broke them. You are ashamed, and you wonder whether God could ever use you again. Hear Jesus' words: "I have prayed for you." He is not surprised by your failure. He prayed for you before it happened. And He has already said, "Once you have turned back." He is waiting for you to come back, and He has work for you to do when you do.

Perhaps you are being "sifted" right now. Life is shaking you hard: sickness, loss, persecution, temptation, doubt. You may feel your faith is about to break. Remember that Jesus prays for you. The enemy cannot go one hair's breadth beyond what God allows.

And this passage teaches us how to pray for others. When someone we love is being tested, our first prayer should not only be "Lord, take this away," but "Lord, keep their faith from failing." When someone has fallen, we can pray for their return, and be ready to welcome them back.

### Try this

This week, learn from the way Jesus prayed for Peter.

- **Hear His prayer for you.** Read Luke 22:31-32 slowly, putting your own name in place of "Simon." Thank Jesus that He prays for you.
- **Pray for someone's faith.** Choose one person you know who is being "sifted" by hardship or temptation. Each day, pray by name: "Lord, keep their faith from giving out."
- **Pray for someone's return.** Think of someone who has fallen away from God. Pray for them as Jesus prayed for Peter, and ask God to show you how to welcome them back.
- **Strengthen a brother or sister.** If God has restored you from a fall, look for one person this week whom you can encourage with what you have learned.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You that You prayed for Peter, and that You pray for me. You know my weaknesses better than I do. When I am sifted, keep my faith from giving out. When I fall, turn and look at me with love, and bring me back. And when I have turned back, use me to strengthen my brothers and sisters. I pray now for those I love who are being tested: keep their faith strong, and bring them safely through. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- How does it feel to know that Jesus prayed for Peter before he fell, and that He prays for you?
- Why do you think Jesus prayed for Peter's faith, rather than praying that he would avoid the test?
- Has God ever used one of your failures to help someone else? How?

## That They May All Be One

> "Father, I ask that all of them may be one. Just as You are in Me and I am in You, so may they too be in Us, so that the world may come to believe that You sent Me." (John 17:21)

The meal was over. Judas had gone out into the night. Jesus had washed His disciples' feet, given them a new commandment to love one another, and spoken to them for a long time about the Holy Spirit who would come. Soon they would leave the house and walk down into the Kidron Valley toward the garden where He would be arrested.

But first, He prayed.

> "After Jesus had said these things, He raised His eyes toward heaven and prayed: 'Father, the hour has come. Glorify Your Son, so that the Son may glorify You.'" (John 17:1)

What follows in John 17 is the longest prayer of Jesus recorded in the Bible. Christians have often called it His "high priestly prayer," because in it Jesus stands before the Father on behalf of His people, as the high priest did in the temple. It is like being allowed to stand in the room and overhear the Son speaking to the Father on the night before He died.

The prayer moves in three widening circles.

### Three circles

First, Jesus prays **for Himself** (John 17:1-5). He asks the Father to glorify Him in the hour that has come, so that He may glorify the Father. And He says what eternal life really is: "that they know You, the only true God, and the one You sent, Jesus Christ" (John 17:3).

Second, He prays **for His disciples** (John 17:6-19), the eleven men in the room with Him. "I am asking on their behalf," He says (John 17:9). He asks the Father to keep them, to protect them, to make them holy. He does not ask that they be taken out of the hard world they live in: "I am not asking You to take them out of the world, but to keep them safe from the evil one" (John 17:15). And He asks, "Set them apart for Yourself by the truth; Your word is truth" (John 17:17).

Third, the circle widens to include people who were not even born yet:

> "I am not asking on behalf of these alone. My prayer is also for those who are going to believe in Me through their message." (John 17:20)

Stop and read that again. "Those who are going to believe in Me through their message." The apostles preached. Those who believed passed on the message, and so on, generation after generation, across every continent, until it reached you. If you believe in Jesus, you are one of the people He was praying for that night. Before you were born, Jesus prayed for you.

### What He asked

And what did He pray for us? Out of everything He could have asked, He asked this: "That all of them may be one."

Not one in the sense that every Christian must think the same thoughts, belong to the same organization or worship in the same style. Jesus asked for something much deeper: "Just as You are in Me and I am in You, so may they too be in Us." The unity He prayed for is a sharing in the love between the Father and the Son. It is a family likeness, a oneness of heart that comes from all of us being joined to the same Lord.

And He gave the reason: "so that the world may come to believe that You sent Me." A little later He says it again: "So may they be brought to perfect oneness, and the world will know that You sent Me and loved them just as You loved Me" (John 17:23). The unity of Christians is meant to be a sign to the world. When people who would naturally be divided, by race, language, class, history or politics, love one another as one family, the world sees something it cannot explain except by the love of God.

E. M. Bounds felt the weight of this:

> "The oneness of God's people was to be the one credential to the world of the divinity of Christ's mission on earth."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

And then he asked a hard question:

> "Let us ask in all candor, are we praying for this unity as Christ prayed for it?"
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

### An honest look

We must be honest. The history of the church has many shameful pages of division. Christians have quarreled, split, condemned one another and even fought wars against one another. In many towns today there are churches on the same street that will not pray together. Families have been torn apart over church disputes. The world has often looked at Christians and seen not oneness, but rivalry.

This series deliberately includes believers from many traditions: Orthodox, Catholic, Protestant and Pentecostal, from every part of the world. We will not pretend that they agreed on everything. They did not, and some of their disagreements were about important things. But we will listen to them all as brothers and sisters who prayed to the same Father through the same Son. That is one small way of taking Jesus' prayer seriously.

Unity is not something we create. It is something the Spirit gives, and something we are called to protect. Paul wrote, "Spare no effort to keep the unity the Spirit gives, with peace as the bond that holds you together. There is one body and one Spirit... one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all" (Ephesians 4:3-6).

### Three key insights

1. **Jesus prayed for you before you were born.** "My prayer is also for those who are going to believe in Me." Every believer in every generation is included in the prayer of John 17.
2. **Christian unity is a sharing in the life of God.** Jesus prayed that we would be one as He and the Father are one. Our unity is not a human agreement but a family bond, rooted in His love.
3. **Unity is a witness to the world.** Jesus prayed for our oneness "so that the world may come to believe." When divided people love one another in Christ, the world sees the love of God.

### For us today

Where are the divisions in your world? Perhaps between your church and the one down the road. Perhaps between Christians of different tribes or nations, or between rich and poor believers, or between older and younger generations. Perhaps there is a broken friendship in your own congregation.

Jesus' prayer asks us not to accept these divisions as normal. We may not be able to solve every disagreement, but we can pray for unity as He did. We can refuse to speak badly about other believers. We can look for ways to pray, worship and serve with Christians who are different from us. And we can begin with the person nearest to us with whom we need to make peace.

It is also a great comfort to know that Jesus prayed for your protection. "Keep them safe from the evil one." "Set them apart for Yourself by the truth." The prayers Jesus prayed that night are still being answered, every day, in the lives of His people.

### Try this

This week, pray the prayer of John 17.

- **Read it slowly.** Read all of John 17 once this week, and notice the three circles: Jesus prays for Himself, for His disciples, and for you.
- **Pray for your church.** Use Jesus' words to pray for your own congregation: that they would be kept from the evil one, set apart by the truth, and made one.
- **Pray for another church.** Choose one church of a different tradition or language in your town or nation. Pray for its people by name if you can. Ask God to bless them.
- **Take one step toward unity.** Is there a believer you have been divided from? Pray for them, and if possible, reach out to them this week.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, on the night before You died, You prayed for me. Thank You. Keep me safe from the evil one, and set me apart by Your truth. Make Your people one, as You and the Father are one. Forgive us for our divisions, our pride and our quarrels. Heal what is broken between Christians in my town, my nation and the world. Let the world see our love for one another and believe that the Father sent You. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- How does it affect you to know that Jesus prayed for you in John 17?
- What divisions among Christians do you see around you? Which of them grieve you most?
- What is one practical thing you could do to "keep the unity the Spirit gives"?

## Not My Will but Yours

> "He went a little farther, threw Himself face down on the ground, and prayed, 'My Father, if there is any way, let this cup pass by Me. But not what I want; what You want.'" (Matthew 26:39)

The name Gethsemane means "oil press." It was a garden of olive trees on the slope of the Mount of Olives, just across the valley from Jerusalem. In the press, olives were crushed under heavy stones until the oil ran out. On the night Jesus was betrayed, He went there to pray, and He Himself was crushed.

It was late. The Passover moon was full. Jesus left most of His disciples near the entrance: "Stay seated here while I go on over there and pray" (Matthew 26:36). He took Peter, James and John a little farther, the same three who had seen His glory on the mountain of transfiguration. Now they would see His agony.

> "He began to be filled with grief and anguish. Then He said to them, 'My soul is weighed down with grief, even to death. Stay here and keep awake with Me.'" (Matthew 26:37-38)

Then He went a little farther still, alone, and fell with His face to the ground.

### The cup

What was the "cup" Jesus asked His Father to take away? In the Old Testament, the cup is often a picture of God's judgment against sin. Jesus was about to bear that judgment for us. It was not only the physical pain of the cross, terrible as it was. It was the weight of the sin of the world, and the darkness of being separated from His Father. "It was for our rebellions that He was pierced through, for our guilt that He was crushed" (Isaiah 53:5).

Luke, who was a doctor, adds details the others leave out:

> "Then an angel from heaven appeared to Him and gave Him strength. In His anguish He prayed all the more intensely, and His sweat became like thick drops of blood dripping down onto the ground." (Luke 22:43-44)

(Some of the oldest copies of Luke's Gospel do not include these two verses, but they have been read in the church since very early times.)

The writer of Hebrews remembered this night too: "He offered up prayers and pleas, crying aloud and weeping, to the one who was able to save Him out of death, and He was heard for His godly fear" (Hebrews 5:7).

### An honest prayer

Look at the first half of Jesus' prayer: "My Father, if there is any way, let this cup pass by Me."

Jesus was completely honest with His Father. He did not pretend to be calm. He did not hide what He wanted. He said, in effect, "Father, I do not want this. If there is any other way, please take it away." Here is the Son of God, sinless and perfect, telling the Father plainly what His heart desired.

That gives us permission to be honest too. Prayer is not the place for pretending. If you are afraid, tell God. If you want something desperately, tell Him. If the path ahead looks unbearable, tell Him that too. Jesus did.

### A surrendered prayer

But then comes the second half: "But not what I want; what You want."

Jesus prayed this prayer three times that night (Matthew 26:44). The second time, His words had already moved: "My Father, if there is no way for this to pass unless I drink it, then let Your will be done" (Matthew 26:42). In those hours of prayer, Jesus brought His human will into perfect agreement with His Father's will.

This is the heart of Gethsemane. It is not a prayer of giving up. It is a prayer of giving over. Jesus did not stop wanting the cup to pass because He had lost hope, but because He trusted His Father more than His own desires. And Hebrews says He "was heard." The cup was not taken away, but He was heard. He was given strength to drink it, and through His suffering He won salvation for the world.

E. M. Bounds wrote movingly about this prayer. He knew that every believer will have a Gethsemane of their own:

> "'Not my will, but thine, be done,' our broken hearts shall say."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

But Bounds also gave a warning. Some people use "Your will be done" as an excuse not to pray with faith and effort, as if it meant "whatever happens, happens":

> "We are ever ready to excuse our lack of earnest and toilsome praying, by a fancied and delusive view of submission."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

Jesus' surrender in Gethsemane was not lazy or passive. He prayed so intensely that His sweat fell like drops of blood. True surrender comes at the end of honest, wrestling prayer, not instead of it.

### Sleeping friends

While Jesus prayed, His closest friends fell asleep. Three times He came back and found them sleeping. "So none of you could stay awake with Me for even one hour?" He said to Peter. "Stay alert and pray that you will not come into testing. The spirit is eager, but human nature is weak" (Matthew 26:40-41).

There is sadness in those words, but also great kindness. Jesus understood their weakness. And His warning came true: within hours they had all run away. Jesus, who had prayed, stood firm. They, who had slept, fell.

Then, at the end, Jesus rose from the ground, calm and ready: "Get up; let us be going. Here comes the man who is betraying Me" (Matthew 26:46). He had gone into the garden crushed with sorrow. He came out with the strength to face the cross. Prayer had not changed the path, but it had prepared Him to walk it.

### Three key insights

1. **We can be honest with God about what we want.** Jesus told His Father plainly that He wanted the cup to pass. Prayer is not the place for pretending; we can pour out our true desires to Him.
2. **Surrender is giving over, not giving up.** "Not what I want; what You want" is not the prayer of someone who has lost hope. It is the prayer of someone who trusts the Father more than their own desires.
3. **Prayer prepares us for what we must face.** The cup did not pass, but Jesus rose from prayer strengthened and ready. Sometimes God answers not by changing the road, but by giving us strength to walk it.

### For us today

Everyone who follows Jesus will come to a Gethsemane. It may be a diagnosis, a death, a betrayal, a persecution, a call from God to do something very hard. It may be a moment when we must choose between our own will and God's. In that place, we can pray as Jesus prayed.

First, tell God honestly what you want. Do not be ashamed of your fear or your tears. Jesus wept and cried aloud. Then, as you keep praying, ask Him for the grace to say, "Not what I want; what You want." That may take time. Jesus prayed three times. It may take a whole night, or many nights. But it is the way to peace and to strength.

And if you have friends who are in their Gethsemane, do not fall asleep on them. Stay awake with them. Pray with them. It is one of the greatest gifts we can give.

### Try this

This week, learn to pray the Gethsemane prayer.

- **Name your cup.** Think of the hardest thing you are facing, or the thing you fear most. Write it down.
- **Pray honestly.** Tell God exactly what you want about it, holding nothing back. "Father, if there is any way, let this pass."
- **Pray surrender.** Then pray, "But not what I want; what You want." If you cannot mean it yet, ask God to help you mean it. Pray it again tomorrow.
- **Watch with someone.** Think of one person who is going through a hard time. Stay alert for them this week: call them, visit them, or pray with them.

### A closing prayer

Father, Your Son knelt in the garden and prayed with tears. Thank You that I can be honest with You about what I want and what I fear. Here is my cup: You know it. If there is any way, take it from me. But not what I want; what You want. Give me the trust to say that and mean it. And when the path does not change, give me the strength to walk it, as You gave strength to Jesus. Keep me awake and praying, for myself and for those who need me. In His name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What is your "cup" right now, the thing you most wish God would take away?
- What is the difference between giving up and giving over? How did Jesus show it?
- Bounds warns that "Your will be done" can become an excuse not to pray earnestly. Have you ever used it that way?

## Prayers from the Cross

> "Jesus kept saying, 'Father, forgive them. They do not know what they are doing.' They shared out His clothes among themselves by casting lots." (Luke 23:34)

They led Him outside the city walls to a place called the Skull. There the Roman soldiers stretched out His arms, drove nails through His hands and feet, and lifted Him up on a cross between two criminals.

Crucifixion was designed to be the most painful and shameful death imaginable. It could last for hours, even days. Every breath was agony. Men dying this way often cursed and screamed at their executioners, at the crowd, at God.

Jesus prayed.

The Gospels record seven sayings of Jesus from the cross. Three of them are prayers to His Father. Together they show us how Jesus prayed in the darkest hour the world has ever known, and they teach us how to pray in ours.

### "Father, forgive them"

The first prayer came at the very beginning, as the soldiers were nailing Him to the wood and gambling for His clothes at His feet. Luke says Jesus "kept saying" it, again and again: "Father, forgive them. They do not know what they are doing."

Think of who "them" included. The soldiers swinging the hammers. The religious leaders who had plotted His death. The crowd that had shouted "Crucify Him!" Pilate, who washed his hands. The disciples who had run away. And in a deeper sense, all of us, whose sins put Him there.

Jesus did not wait for them to say sorry. He did not wait until the pain was over. He prayed for His enemies while they were hurting Him. He was doing what He had taught on the mountainside: "Love your enemies, and pray for the people who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). The prophet Isaiah had said of the suffering Servant, "He pleads on behalf of the rebels" (Isaiah 53:12). On the cross, that prophecy came true.

This prayer has echoed through the centuries. A few years later, Stephen, the first Christian martyr, was stoned to death in Jerusalem. As the stones struck him, "he sank to his knees and cried out in a loud voice, 'Lord, do not charge them with this sin'" (Acts 7:60). In later volumes we will meet many more who died with this prayer on their lips, in Rome, in Bohemia, in Uganda and in many other places. They learned it from Jesus.

### "My God, My God, why?"

The second prayer came after hours of suffering. From noon until three in the afternoon, darkness covered the land. Then:

> "At about three o'clock Jesus gave a loud cry: 'Eli, Eli, lema sabachthani?' That means, 'My God, My God, why have You abandoned Me?'" (Matthew 27:46)

This is the most mysterious prayer in the Bible. How could the Son of God cry out that God had abandoned Him? We cannot fully understand it. But we know that in this moment Jesus was bearing the full weight of our sin, and experiencing the separation from God that sin brings. He went into the deepest darkness so that we would never have to go there alone.

Notice something else. These words are not Jesus' own invention. They are the opening words of Psalm 22, a psalm of David. In Volume 1 we saw that Jesus grew up praying the Psalms. Now, in His agony, the words that came to His lips were the words of Scripture. And Psalm 22 does not end in despair. It ends in praise: "He did not turn His face away, but heard him when he called for help" (Psalm 22:24).

This prayer gives us permission to be honest when God feels far away. Jesus Himself cried "Why?" It is not a sin to ask God why. It is still prayer. Even in His cry of abandonment, Jesus still said "My God." He did not let go.

### "Into Your hands"

The third prayer was the last thing Jesus said before He died:

> "Then Jesus cried out in a loud voice, 'Father, I place My spirit in Your hands.' With these words He breathed out His last breath." (Luke 23:46)

Again Jesus prayed a psalm: "I put my spirit into Your hand for safekeeping" (Psalm 31:5). This psalm was traditionally prayed by faithful Jews in the evening, before sleep. Jesus may have prayed it every night of His life. Now He prayed it at the end of His life, but He added one word at the beginning: "Father."

After the darkness and the cry of "Why?", the last prayer is one of complete trust. The same Father He felt had abandoned Him is the Father into whose hands He places His spirit. He dies like a child falling asleep in his father's arms.

E. M. Bounds, looking at the whole life of Jesus, saw that it was prayer from beginning to end:

> "With the breath of prayer in His last sigh, He commits His spirit to God."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Reality of Prayer*

### A prayer answered at once

There was another prayer at the cross, not by Jesus but to Him. One of the criminals crucified beside Him had joined in mocking Him at first. But as the hours passed, something changed. He turned his head and said, "Jesus, remember me when You come in Your kingdom." And Jesus answered him, "Truly I tell you, this very day you will be with Me in paradise" (Luke 23:42-43).

It was the last prayer anyone prayed to Jesus before He died, and it was answered at once. A dying thief, with nothing to offer, asked to be remembered, and was promised paradise. Even on the cross, Jesus was still hearing prayer.

### Three key insights

1. **Jesus prayed for His enemies.** On the cross, in agony, He asked His Father to forgive the people who were killing Him. That prayer shows us the heart of God, and it calls us to pray for those who hurt us.
2. **An honest cry of "Why?" is still prayer.** Jesus cried out that God had abandoned Him, using the words of a psalm. When God feels far away, we can tell Him so, and still call Him "my God."
3. **We can die, and live, in trust.** Jesus' last words were a prayer of trust: "Father, I place My spirit in Your hands." Whatever happens to us, we can place ourselves in the Father's hands.

### For us today

Is there someone who has hurt you deeply? The prayer "Father, forgive them" may be the hardest prayer in the world to pray. But it is the prayer of Jesus, and He can give us the grace to pray it too. Like Him, we may need to keep saying it, again and again, before our hearts follow our words.

Is God silent in your life right now? Do you feel abandoned? Jesus has been there. You can cry out with Him, "My God, My God, why?" He understands. And He will not let go of you, even when you feel you are letting go of Him.

Are you afraid of death, or of the future? Jesus' last prayer can be ours every night: "Father, I place my spirit in Your hands." Many Christians through the centuries have prayed these words before they slept, and many have prayed them as they died. They are words of perfect trust.

### Try this

This week, pray the prayers of the cross.

- **"Father, forgive them."** Think of one person who has hurt you. Pray this prayer for them by name each day, even if your feelings do not follow yet.
- **"My God, why?"** If there is a place in your life where God feels far away, tell Him honestly. Then read Psalm 22 all the way to the end.
- **"Into Your hands."** Each night before you sleep, pray Luke 23:46 or Psalm 31:5: "Father, I place my spirit in Your hands." Place yourself, your family and tomorrow into His care.
- **"Remember me."** Thank Jesus that He heard the dying thief, and that He hears you.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, on the cross You prayed for those who were killing You. Forgive me, and teach me to forgive those who have hurt me. You cried out in the darkness, "My God, My God, why?" Thank You that You went into that darkness for me, so that I would never be truly abandoned. And You died with words of trust: "Father, into Your hands." Teach me to live and die that way. Remember me, Lord, in Your kingdom. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Which of Jesus' three prayers from the cross speaks most to you right now? Why?
- Is there someone you need to pray "Father, forgive them" for? What makes it hard?
- How does it help to know that Jesus prayed the Psalms, even on the cross?

## He Always Lives to Pray for Us

> "For this reason He is able to save completely those who approach God through Him, since He is always alive to plead on their behalf." (Hebrews 7:25)

Forty days after He rose from the dead, Jesus led His disciples out of Jerusalem toward the village of Bethany, on the Mount of Olives. They had seen Him alive many times by now. They had eaten with Him, touched Him and listened to Him explain the Scriptures. Now He had one more thing to do.

> "He led them out until they were near Bethany. There He raised His hands and blessed them. He was still blessing them when He parted from them and was borne up into heaven." (Luke 24:50-51)

The last thing the disciples saw of Jesus on earth was His hands raised over them in blessing. Then a cloud hid Him from their sight (Acts 1:9).

Where did He go? And what is He doing now?

### At the right hand of God

The New Testament answers with one picture again and again: Jesus "was taken up into heaven and took His seat at the right hand of God" (Mark 16:19). In the ancient world, the place at a king's right hand was the place of highest honor and authority. Jesus, who was crucified in weakness, now reigns over all things.

But the New Testament also tells us what He is doing there. He is praying for us.

> "Who is going to condemn them? Christ is the one who died; still more, He was raised, and He is at the right hand of God, where He pleads on our behalf." (Romans 8:34)

> "He went into heaven itself, and there He now appears before the face of God on our behalf." (Hebrews 9:24)

> "Yet if anyone does sin, we have an advocate in the Father's presence, Jesus Christ, who is righteous." (1 John 2:1)

And our key verse: "He is always alive to plead on their behalf."

### The praying Christ

Think back over this book. We have seen Jesus rise before dawn to pray, pray all night before choosing His apostles, teach His disciples to pray, pray at the tomb of Lazarus, pray for Peter, pray for all His people in John 17, pray in agony in Gethsemane, and pray from the cross. His whole life on earth was a life of prayer.

His ascension did not end that life. It moved it to the throne of heaven. The same Jesus who prayed for Peter by name prays for you by name. The same Jesus who asked the Father to keep His disciples safe from the evil one is asking it now for you. He never grows tired. He never forgets. He never sleeps. "He is always alive to plead on their behalf."

The writer of Hebrews explains why this matters so much. In the Old Testament, the priests who prayed for Israel grew old and died, and others had to take their place. "But He remains forever, and so the priesthood He holds will never pass to another" (Hebrews 7:24). Jesus is a priest who will never die. His prayers for us will never stop.

That is why He "is able to save completely." Not partly. Not for a while. Completely. Our salvation does not depend on how strong our own grip on Him is. It depends on His grip on us, and on His prayers for us, which never fail.

### Our prayers and His

And here is something even more wonderful. Our prayers are joined to His.

Andrew Murray loved this truth. He wrote:

> "Because He prays, we pray too."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

When we pray, we are not speaking into an empty sky, hoping someone hears. We are joining a prayer that is already going on, the prayer of the Son to the Father on our behalf. Murray put it in a beautiful way: "What He prays passes through us, what we pray passes through Him." Our weak, wandering, imperfect prayers are taken up into His perfect prayer and presented to the Father.

The Holy Spirit is part of this too. Paul wrote that "the Spirit Himself pleads on our behalf, with groans that no words carry," and that He "pleads for God's holy people in line with what God wants" (Romans 8:26-27). So we have two who pray for us: the Son at the right hand of the Father, and the Spirit within our own hearts. We are never praying alone.

### Golden bowls

At the beginning of this book, we saw a picture from the book of Revelation: the prayers of God's people held before the throne of God in "gold bowls full of incense." Later in that same book John sees it again:

> "Then another angel came, holding a gold censer, and took his place at the altar. Much incense was handed to him, so that he could add it to the prayers of all God's holy people on the gold altar before the throne. The smoke of the incense rose with the prayers of God's holy people from the angel's hand before God." (Revelation 8:3-4)

Every prayer you have ever prayed in the name of Jesus has risen before God like incense. The prayers you whispered in the dark, the prayers you could not put into words, the prayers that seemed to go unanswered, the prayers of saints in every century and every land: none of them has been lost. They are gathered before the throne, joined to the prayers of the One who always lives to pray for us.

### Boldness

Because of all this, the writer of Hebrews calls us to come to God with confidence:

> "We have, then, a great high priest who has gone through the heavens: Jesus the Son of God... So let us, with confidence, approach the throne of grace, to receive mercy and to find grace that brings help just when we need it." (Hebrews 4:14-16)

We do not come to a throne of judgment, but a throne of grace. We do not come alone, but with Jesus our High Priest beside us. We do not come hoping to persuade an unwilling God, but to a Father who loves us, through a Son who prays for us, in a Spirit who helps us. There is no safer, more welcoming place in the universe.

### Three key insights

1. **Jesus is still praying for us.** His ascension did not end His life of prayer. At the right hand of God He always lives to plead on our behalf, and His prayers never fail.
2. **Our salvation rests on His prayers, not our strength.** Because He is a priest forever, He is able to save completely. Our hope does not depend on our grip on Him, but on His grip on us.
3. **Our prayers are joined to His.** We never pray alone. The Son prays for us in heaven, the Spirit prays within us, and our prayers rise before God like incense, mingled with His.

### For us today

This is the truth that holds together everything in this book. Every lesson Jesus taught us about prayer, about the secret room, about asking, seeking and knocking, about persistence and humility and faith and forgiveness, rests on this foundation: Jesus Himself is praying.

So when you feel that your prayers are too weak, remember that His are strong. When you do not know what to say, remember that the Spirit prays within you. When you fall, like Peter, remember that you have an advocate before the Father. And when you are tempted to give up on prayer altogether, remember that you are not the only one praying. The Son of God is praying for you right now.

### Try this

This week, let the praying Christ change the way you pray.

- **Begin with Him.** Each time you pray this week, start by reminding yourself, "Jesus is praying for me right now." Then join your prayer to His.
- **Pray with confidence.** Read Hebrews 4:14-16 each day. Come to God boldly, with your needs, your failures and your fears.
- **Look back.** Look back over the twenty chapters of this book. Choose the one lesson that has meant the most to you, and ask God to make it part of your life.
- **Give thanks.** End the week by thanking Jesus for His whole life of prayer: on the mountain, in the garden, on the cross, and now at the right hand of God.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You prayed on the mountain and in the garden and on the cross, and now You always live to pray for me. Thank You. When my prayers are weak, Yours are strong. When I fall, You plead for me. When I do not know what to pray, Your Spirit prays within me. Teach me to come boldly to the throne of grace, knowing that You are there. Take my prayers and join them to Yours. And keep me praying, all my life, until I see You face to face. Amen.

- Hebrews says Jesus is "able to save completely." What does that mean for you on the days when your faith feels weak?
- Looking back over this book, what is the most important thing Jesus has taught you about prayer?

## Conclusion: Passing the Torch

> "All of these were devoted to prayer and of one mind in it. With them were the women, Mary the mother of Jesus, and His brothers." (Acts 1:14)

We began this book on a dark road outside Capernaum, watching Jesus slip away before dawn to pray. We end it on the Mount of Olives, watching Him rise into heaven with His hands raised in blessing.

In between, we have walked with Him through the whole of His life of prayer. We have climbed the mountain where He prayed all night. We have stood with the disciples in "a certain place" and heard them ask, "Lord, teach us how to pray." We have knelt with Him in the garden and stood beneath His cross. And we have seen Him take His seat at the right hand of the Father, where He always lives to pray for us.

### What the Teacher taught us

If you have read this far, you have sat in the school of the greatest Teacher of prayer the world has ever known. Here, in a few lines, is some of what He taught:

- Give God the first of your day, and slip away to pray when life is busiest (chapter 1).
- Bring your biggest decisions to God before you make them (chapter 2).
- Prayer can be learned, and Jesus is a willing teacher (chapter 3).
- Begin with "Our Father." Put His name, His kingdom and His will first (chapter 4).
- Ask for today's bread, forgive as you have been forgiven, and ask to be kept from the evil one (chapter 5).
- Pray in secret, for an audience of One (chapter 6).
- Ask, search and knock, and keep knocking, especially for others (chapter 7).
- Pray at all times and do not lose heart (chapter 8).
- Come humbly, asking for mercy, and never look down on others (chapter 9).
- Have faith in God, and trust His will as well as His power (chapter 10).
- Pray together, and make peace so that you can (chapter 11).
- Let God's house be a house of prayer for all the nations (chapter 12).
- Ask the Lord of the harvest to send out workers, and be ready to go (chapter 13).
- Give thanks, even before you see the answer and even through tears (chapter 14).
- Ask in Jesus' name, on His credit and for His glory (chapter 15).
- Remember that Jesus prays for your faith, even before you fall (chapter 16).
- Pray for the unity of all His people (chapter 17).
- Tell God honestly what you want, and then say, "Not what I want; what You want" (chapter 18).
- Pray for your enemies, cry out honestly in the dark, and place your life in the Father's hands (chapter 19).
- Come boldly to the throne of grace, because Jesus is praying for you there (chapter 20).

You will find all of these lessons gathered by theme in Appendix 1, and every Scripture we have read listed in Bible order in Appendix 2.

No one learns all of this at once. The disciples did not. They fell asleep in Gethsemane on the very night Jesus taught them most. Be patient with yourself. Choose one lesson and practice it until it becomes part of you. Then choose another. The school of prayer lasts a lifetime.

### The torch is passed

But the story does not end with the ascension. It begins again.

Before He left, Jesus told His disciples to wait in Jerusalem "for what the Father had promised" (Acts 1:4), the gift of the Holy Spirit. And so they went back to the city, to an upstairs room, and did the one thing He had taught them above all else. They prayed.

> "All of these were devoted to prayer and of one mind in it." (Acts 1:14)

Look at who was in that room. The eleven apostles, including Peter, who had denied Him and been restored. The women who had followed Him from Galilee and stood at the cross. Mary His mother. And His brothers, who had once refused to believe in Him. About a hundred and twenty people in all, waiting and praying together.

The Teacher had gone. Now His students were praying without Him in the room, and yet He was with them, just as He had promised. The torch had been passed.

In **Volume 3, *Devoted to Prayer***, we will follow them out of that upper room and into the streets of Jerusalem and the roads of the Roman world. We will be there when the Spirit falls at Pentecost and three thousand believe. We will see the church pray until the building shakes, pray through the night for Peter in prison, and send out its first missionaries from a prayer meeting in Antioch. We will hear Paul and Silas singing at midnight in a prison cell, and read the prayers Paul wrote for the churches he loved. We will watch ordinary men and women put into practice everything Jesus taught them.

And after them, in the volumes that follow, comes the great cloud of witnesses: martyrs and monks, reformers and revivalists, mothers and missionaries, from every nation and every century, all of them students in the same school, all of them learning from the same Teacher.

### Your turn

The writer of Hebrews, having described that great cloud of witnesses, does not tell us to look at them. He tells us where to look:

> "Let us keep our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer who leads the way in faith and brings it to perfection." (Hebrews 12:2)

That is what this book has tried to do. Before we meet the witnesses, we have looked at Jesus. Everything they knew about prayer, they learned from Him. Everything we will learn from them points back to Him.

Now it is your turn. The same Jesus who rose before dawn to pray, who taught His friends to say "Our Father," who prayed for Peter by name and for you before you were born, is praying for you now. The same Father who heard Him is ready to hear you. The same Spirit who filled that upper room lives in every believer.

So take up the torch. Go into your inner room and shut the door. Find two or three others and pray together. Ask, search and knock. Give thanks. Forgive. Do not lose heart. And every time you pray, remember that you are joining a prayer meeting that began with Jesus, that has never stopped, and that will one day fill the whole earth.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for teaching me to pray. Thank You for every lesson in this book: for the early morning and the long night, for the secret room and the prayer meeting, for the Lord's Prayer and the prayer from the cross. Forgive me for how slowly I learn. Keep me in Your school all my life. Fill me with Your Holy Spirit, as You filled Your first disciples. Make me devoted to prayer, and of one mind with Your people everywhere. And as I go on from here, keep my eyes on You, the pioneer of my faith, who always lives to pray for me. Amen.
