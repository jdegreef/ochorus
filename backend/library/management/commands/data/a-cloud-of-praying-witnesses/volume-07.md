## Introduction: Where We Are in the Story

> "That is why I remind you to stir up into flame the gift God gave you when I laid my hands on you." (2 Timothy 1:6)

A fire does not stay hot by itself. Wood must be added, and the coals must be stirred, or the flames die down to ashes. Paul knew this when he wrote to young Timothy. Timothy had received a real gift from God, but he needed to "stir up into flame" what he had been given.

By the middle of the 1600s, many churches in Europe needed the same reminder. The great struggles of the Reformation were over. The wars of religion had ended, leaving whole regions poor and exhausted. In many places the church had settled into right belief and good order. People were baptized, confirmed and buried by the church. They knew the catechism and attended on Sundays. But for many of them, faith had grown cold. It was a matter of belonging, not of knowing Christ.

This book tells how God stirred the fire again. And it shows that, almost every time, the fire began with people who prayed.

### Where we are in the series

This is the seventh volume of *A Cloud of Praying Witnesses*, a series that follows the people of God at prayer through the whole story of the church. The name comes from Hebrews 12:1, which pictures all the faithful who went before us as "so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us."

The first volumes followed prayer from the Old Testament, through the life of Jesus and the first church, to the martyrs, the desert fathers and the monks, mystics and missionaries of the Middle Ages. Volume 6, *A Mighty Fortress*, followed the age of the Reformation, when believers on every side of a divided church learned to pray with new honesty and depth.

This volume follows what came next, from about 1650 to 1800.

### The world of these witnesses

This was the age of new ideas. Scientists were explaining the movements of the planets, and many thinkers began to trust human reason more than God's word. In some places it became fashionable to treat faith as old-fashioned. It was also an age of great empires, of trade across the oceans and, to the shame of nations that called themselves Christian, of the Atlantic slave trade, which carried millions of Africans in chains to the Americas.

Into that world came a series of awakenings. In Germany, the Pietists gathered believers in small groups to read the Bible and pray. In a village called Herrnhut, refugees from Moravia began a prayer meeting that is said to have continued, night and day, for about a hundred years, and sent missionaries around the world. In Britain and America, preachers like John Wesley and George Whitefield preached in fields and town squares to crowds of thousands, and many were changed. In the American colonies, Jonathan Edwards called the churches to join together in a concert of prayer, an agreement to pray at the same set times for the same things.

These awakenings had their shadows. Some revivals were marked by excess and quarrels. Some churches that were full of fire still shut out their Black members or sat them apart, and in this book we will see two men pulled from their knees in a Philadelphia church because of the color of their skin. We will tell these stories honestly, because the witnesses themselves did.

### What we will see

This volume has seventeen chapters, in four movements:

- **Hearts set on fire** (chapters 1 to 5). Jeanne Guyon teaches ordinary people that anyone can pray. Philipp Spener gathers little churches within the church, and August Hermann Francke builds an orphanage on prayer. At Herrnhut the Moravians begin a prayer watch that will not go out, and send missionaries to the ends of the earth.
- **The Awakening in Britain and America** (chapters 6 to 9). Susanna Wesley, as her family remembered, prays with her apron over her head, and her son John learns that God does great things in answer to prayer. George Whitefield prays for God to tear open the heavens, and Jonathan Edwards calls the churches to pray together.
- **Prayer in hard places** (chapters 10 to 13). David Brainerd prays in the snow for the Native peoples he served. Praying societies in a Scottish village see God revive His work. Marie Durand prays in a prison tower for thirty-eight years. John Newton, once a slave trader, teaches a small town to ask God for large things.
- **Prayer that reaches the nations** (chapters 14 to 17). Absalom Jones is pulled from his knees in a Philadelphia church, and he and Richard Allen go on to found churches of their own. Samson Occom, a Mohegan minister, prays with the Native peoples he serves, so that every family of the nations may worship God in its own tongue. A group of English Baptists begins to pray once a month for the spread of the gospel, and from their prayer meetings William Carey sails for India.

### How each chapter works

Every chapter in this series has the same shape:

- **A key verse** opens the chapter, the Scripture it rests on.
- **A story** sets the scene.
- **The teaching** looks at what the story shows us about prayer.
- **Voices from the past** quote men and women of prayer, often the witnesses themselves, word for word from their own books in the Ochorus library.
- **Three key insights** sum up what the chapter teaches.
- **For us today** brings the lesson into ordinary life, wherever in the world you live.
- **Try this** gives one simple practice for the coming week.
- **A closing prayer** and **Questions for reflection** help you respond, alone or with others.
- **Read more on Ochorus** points you to classic books where you can go deeper.

At the end of the book, two appendices gather all the key insights by theme and list every Scripture quoted, in Bible order, with the chapters where it appears.

### A word about these stories

Many of the people in this book kept diaries and journals, and wrote letters and sermons that we still have. We can often let them speak for themselves. Some famous stories and sayings, though, were remembered by their families and friends and written down later, and in each chapter we say where a story comes from and when a detail is only a tradition.

### How to read this book

One chapter a day will carry you through the book in seventeen days. With a small group, one or two chapters a week works well.

If your own faith feels more like ashes than flame, this book is for you. None of the people in it could light the fire by themselves. They asked God to do it, and they kept on asking. As you read, ask Him to stir up the flame in you.

## Be Still and Know

> "Stop, and acknowledge that I am God. Among the nations I will be lifted high; over all the earth I will be lifted high." (Psalm 46:10)

In 1668, in the small French town of Montargis, south of Paris, a young wife of nineteen or twenty went to visit a stranger. Her name was Jeanne Guyon. Her father had urged her to go. A Franciscan friar had come to the town, a man who had just spent five years alone in prayer, and her father hoped he could help his unhappy daughter.

Jeanne had been married at fifteen to a rich man more than twenty years older than she was, in an arranged match, and the house she entered was not a kind one. She loved God and longed to pray. For years she had tried to pray by method and effort, as she had been taught: choosing a subject, thinking hard about it, trying to stir up her feelings. Almost nothing came of it. Prayer had become a weight.

She tells the story herself, in the account of her life that she wrote many years later. The friar was shy. He was not used to speaking with women, and for some time he said nothing at all. So she spoke first and told him plainly about her trouble in prayer. At last he answered: "It is, madame, because you seek without what you have within. Accustom yourself to seek God in your heart, and you will there find Him." Then he left her.

Those words went into her, she said, like an arrow. She had been running everywhere to find God, and He had been near her all along. Prayer was no longer a task she performed but a turning of her heart toward a God who was already present. "O my Lord," she wrote, "Thou wast in my heart, and demanded only a simple turning of my mind inward, to make me perceive Thy presence."

### Who she was

Jeanne-Marie Bouvier de la Mothe was born in 1648, the very year the Thirty Years' War ended. In France, King Louis XIV ruled with great power, and the church was strong, rich and closely watched by the state. Many people were Christians in name and habit; fewer seemed to know God as a living Friend.

Her own life was full of sorrow. Smallpox scarred her face, and some of her children died young. Her husband died in 1676. Some years later she traveled, with her small daughter, to the region near Geneva and then to Grenoble. Wherever she went, people came to her to ask about prayer: nuns and priests, but also servants, workmen and poor women. For them she wrote a little book. A friend had it printed at Grenoble in 1685. In English it is called *A Short and Easy Method of Prayer*, and it is in the Ochorus library.

That same year, King Louis revoked the Edict of Nantes, the law that had protected French Protestants, and thousands of them fled the country. Later in this book we will meet Huguenot women who stayed and prayed in prison. It was an age when the powerful wanted everyone to pray in the same approved way.

### What she taught

Jeanne Guyon's little book begins with a simple definition:

> "Prayer is nothing else but the application of the heart to God, and the interior exercise of love."
> Jeanne Guyon, *A Short and Easy Method of Prayer*

By "application" she means the heart turning toward God and staying with Him, the way a child turns toward a parent's voice. And because prayer is a matter of the heart, not of cleverness, she insisted that everyone can pray. She did not write for scholars. She wrote, she said, for "the little ones" who were not capable of extraordinary things. She wanted a way of prayer that would fit every kind of life:

> "What we need, then, is an attitude of prayer, in which we can constantly abide, and out of which exterior occupations cannot draw us; a prayer which can be offered alike by princes, kings, prelates, magistrates, soldiers, children, artisans, labourers, women, and the sick."
> Jeanne Guyon, *A Short and Easy Method of Prayer*

Her first step was simple. Read a short passage of Scripture or a good book, she said, but read it slowly. Read two or three lines, and stay with them as long as they feed you. She gave a picture from the garden: "the bees can only draw the juice from the flowers by resting on them, not by flying round them."

Then let the words lead you into quiet attention to God. Speak to Him, then be silent for a little while and listen. She warned beginners not to work too hard at stirring up their feelings. "The fire must be blown softly," she wrote, "and as soon as it is lighted, cease to blow it, or you will put it out."

This is the heart of Psalm 46:10. Older English Bibles begin it with the words of our title, *Be still, and know*. The Open Word Bible gives the plain sense: "Stop, and acknowledge that I am God." The Hebrew word means to let go, to stop striving. In the psalm, God is speaking to warring nations: put down your weapons, and know who I am. Jeanne Guyon heard it as a word for the restless heart too. Stop running. Stop straining. God is here.

### Speak in your own words

Some of the most beautiful lines in her book are written to preachers. She begged them to teach ordinary people to pray from the heart, not to frighten them with fine words and long rules. Then she turned to the people themselves:

> "Go, poor children, and speak to your Heavenly Father in your natural language: however uncultivated it may be, it is not so to Him."
> Jeanne Guyon, *A Short and Easy Method of Prayer*

### The storm

In 1687 the church in Rome condemned the Spanish priest Miguel de Molinos for teaching a prayer so passive that, his critics said, it made effort, repentance and even asking seem unnecessary. His followers were called Quietists, and in France Madame Guyon was soon accused of the same errors. Some of her language was extreme, and some of her claims about her own experience made wise people uneasy. But court politics, jealousy and fear of a woman who taught played their part too.

A gifted priest, François Fénelon, later Archbishop of Cambrai, became her friend and defender, and the great Bishop Bossuet opposed them both. At the end of 1695 she was arrested, and in 1698 she was taken to the Bastille, the royal prison in Paris, where she stayed until 1703. In 1699 the Pope condemned parts of a book by Fénelon, and he submitted at once.

By all accounts she bore her prison years with deep peace, praying and writing poems of love to God. She died near Blois in 1717, still a member of the Catholic Church that had imprisoned her. Strangely, her books were kept alive above all by Protestants: German Pietists, Quakers, English Methodists and, much later, Christians in China. John Wesley published a shortened version of her life for his Methodists, admiring her devotion and warning plainly against what he judged her mistakes. That is still a wise way to read her.

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer is the heart turning to God, so even the simplest believer can pray.** Jeanne Guyon wrote for servants and workmen, children and the sick. God does not wait for us to become clever.
2. **A few words of Scripture, read slowly, can feed the soul more than many words read in a hurry.** Like the bee resting on a flower, we draw the sweetness from God's word by staying with it, not by rushing past it.
3. **God is often found not by straining harder, but by stopping and turning to Him.** When prayer has become a weight, the answer may be to stop striving and remember that He is already near.

### For us today

Many believers today feel that real prayer belongs to pastors, monks or people with long hours to spare. A market woman in Accra who leaves home before dawn, a factory worker in Dhaka on a twelve-hour shift, a grandmother in a village in Bolivia who never learned to read: Jeanne Guyon would say to each of them, you can pray. Speak to your Father in your own language, in your own simple words. He understands.

Her story speaks also to believers who live where prayer meetings are watched or forbidden. Guyon was not perfect, and the church of her day had real reasons for caution. But no prison could stop her from turning her heart to God. And to restless, anxious people everywhere, pulled in every direction by work and worry, Psalm 46:10 still says: "Stop, and acknowledge that I am God."

### Try this

This week, practice turning your heart to God slowly and simply.

- **Read like a bee.** Each day read just two or three verses, such as Psalm 46:10-11. Stay with them until they have fed you before you move on.
- **Pray with pauses.** Pray the first words of the Lord's Prayer, "Our Father," and then be silent for a little while before you go on.
- **Use your own language.** Pray once a day in the plainest words you have, the words you would use at home, not the words you think a preacher would use.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You are near to me, nearer than I know, and yet I have looked for You everywhere else. Thank You for Your servant Jeanne Guyon, who learned to find You in her heart and taught simple people to pray. Quiet my restless mind. Teach me to read Your word slowly and to rest in it. When I strain and struggle, help me to stop and acknowledge that You are God. Hear me when I speak to You in my own plain words, as a child speaks to a father. And be lifted high among the nations and over all the earth, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Have you ever felt that prayer was a weight or a task you could not do well? What does the friar's advice, to seek God in your heart, say to you?
- Why do you think Jeanne Guyon believed that "the little ones" are well suited to prayer?
- In your own life, what is God asking you to stop, so that you can know that He is God?

## Little Churches Within the Church

> "Then those who feared the LORD talked with one another, and the LORD listened and heard them. A book of remembrance was written in His presence about those who feared the LORD and honored His name." (Malachi 3:16)

In the summer of 1670, in the German city of Frankfurt, a few men came to their pastor with a request. Frankfurt was a busy trading city on the river Main. The pastor was Philipp Jakob Spener, the senior minister of the Lutheran churches there. He was thirty-five years old, a careful scholar and a warm preacher.

The men wanted more than sermons. They wanted to meet together during the week, to talk about the things of God and to help one another live as Christians. Spener hesitated. He knew that in other places such private meetings had led people to break away from the church, and he did not want that. But he also saw their hunger. So he agreed, on one condition: they would meet in his own house, and he would be with them.

So a small group began to gather in Spener's study, twice a week. At first only a few men came. Spener opened with prayer and read aloud from a devotional book. Then they talked freely together about what they had heard. There were rules. They were to speak only of what would build up faith and godliness. They were not to argue for the sake of arguing. They were not to talk about people who were not there. If they spoke of the sins of the church or the town, they were to speak in general, without naming names.

The group grew. Later the doors were opened to women and to ordinary working people as well as to the learned. After a few years they set aside the other books and read the Bible itself. Spener called these meetings by a Latin name, *collegia pietatis*, which means "gatherings for godliness." He also spoke of them as *ecclesiola in ecclesia*, "a little church within the church." He did not want them to leave their parish churches. He wanted them to bring new life into them.

### Who he was

Spener was born in 1635 in Alsace, on the border of France and Germany, while the Thirty Years' War was still burning across the land. As a young man he read Johann Arndt's *True Christianity*, and English Puritan books such as Lewis Bayly's *The Practice of Piety*, which had been translated into German. These books taught him that faith is not only right belief but a new heart and a changed life.

When the war ended in 1648, much of Germany lay in ruins. The Lutheran churches had kept the true teaching of the gospel, and they were rightly proud of it. But Spener saw that in many places faith had grown cold. Pastors argued about fine points of doctrine. Many church members heard the sermon with their ears, he said, but it never reached their hearts. Drunkenness was common, and so were lawsuits between neighbors. People went to church, took Holy Communion and went home unchanged.

Malachi lived in a time something like that. The people of Judah had come back from exile and rebuilt the temple. But they had grown tired and bitter. They said, "It is useless to serve God" (Malachi 3:14). Then something quiet happened. A few people who still feared the LORD began to talk with one another. They did not start a war or a new nation. They simply met and spoke about God. And the LORD listened. He heard them, and He had their names written in a book of remembrance.

### Heartfelt longings

In 1675 Spener was asked to write a preface for a new edition of Arndt's sermons. What he wrote was so important that it was soon printed as a little book of its own. It is called *Pia Desideria*, which means "Pious Desires" or "Heartfelt Longings." In it he grieved over the state of the church. But he also wrote with hope. He believed that God could make His church better, and he offered some simple proposals.

The book is not in the Ochorus library, and the good English translations of it are modern, so we will describe his ideas in our own words.

First, Spener urged that God's word be used far more widely among the people: read at home, read in the gatherings, talked about together, not only heard on Sunday. Second, he reminded the church of something Luther had taught: every believer is a priest. Peter calls the whole church "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9). This does not do away with pastors, Spener said. But it means every Christian is called to pray for others, to teach and encourage the household, and to warn and comfort fellow believers. Third, he insisted that knowing the Christian faith is not enough. It must be practiced, above all in love.

His fourth proposal is one we still need. When Christians disagree, he wrote, they should argue with humility and love, not to defeat one another but to help one another toward the truth. They should pray for those who are in error. The last proposals were for the training of pastors: they should be godly as well as learned, and they should preach to build up faith in ordinary people, not to show off their learning.

More than two hundred years later, Andrew Murray put Spener's third point in a way that goes straight to the heart of prayer:

> "Not according to what I try to be when praying, but what I am when not praying, is my prayer dealt with by God."
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

### Light and shadow

The little gatherings spread to other towns in Germany. People who joined them were soon given a nickname by their critics: "Pietists," the pious ones. At first the name was meant as mockery. In time they wore it gladly.

There were real dangers, and Spener knew it. Some groups grew proud. They began to think of themselves as the only true Christians and of their neighbors as lost. Some left the church altogether. In Frankfurt itself, a few of the first members broke away. Later, in Dresden and Berlin, Spener no longer led such meetings himself, and gave more of his effort to teaching the catechism to the young. He was attacked fiercely by other Lutheran theologians, who accused him of many errors. He answered his critics and stayed in the church he loved until his death in 1705.

His influence went far beyond his own life. He helped a young scholar named August Hermann Francke, whom we will meet in the next chapter. And Nikolaus Ludwig von Zinzendorf, who met Spener as a boy at his grandmother's home, later shaped the Moravian village of Herrnhut as another "church within the church." The small groups, house fellowships and cell groups that meet in homes around the world today owe more to Spener's little gatherings than most of them know.

### Three key insights

1. **When believers who fear God talk together about Him, the Lord listens and remembers.** Malachi's friends did not do anything the world would notice. They met and spoke of God. God listened, and their names were written before Him.
2. **A small group stays healthy when it builds others up instead of tearing them down.** Spener's rules were simple: speak only what helps faith, do not argue for victory, and do not talk about those who are absent. Without love, even a prayer meeting can become a place of pride and division.
3. **Because every believer is a priest, praying for others belongs to the whole church, not only to the pastor.** Peter calls the whole church a royal priesthood. A mother, a farmer or a student can pray for others, open the Scriptures with them and comfort them in trouble.

### For us today

In many parts of the world, the church grows most in small groups. In China, believers have long met in homes, often without a building or a trained pastor. In Nigeria, Brazil and Korea, cell groups gather in apartments and courtyards on weeknights. Spener's little gatherings speak to all of them.

His rules speak to us too. It is easy for a fellowship group to slide into gossip, or into arguments about things that do not build anyone up. It is easy to feel that our little group is better than the big church down the road. Spener wanted the opposite: little churches that would bring life back into the whole church.

And Malachi reminds us that no meeting is too small for God to notice. Three believers talking about Jesus over a cup of tea are heard in heaven.

### Try this

This week, try a little gathering for godliness.

- **Invite two or three.** Ask one or two believing friends, neighbors or family members to meet with you once this week for half an hour.
- **Open the word together.** Read a short passage, such as Malachi 3:16-18 or 1 Peter 2:4-10. Let each person say one thing they notice.
- **Keep Spener's rules.** Agree together: speak only to build up, do not argue to win, and do not talk about anyone who is not there.
- **Pray for one another by name.** Before you part, let each person pray for the one beside them.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You listen when those who fear You speak together, and You remember their names. Thank You for Your servant Philipp Spener and for the little gatherings that brought new life to Your church. Give me friends who love You, and make me a friend who helps them. Guard our words from gossip and pride. Let us read Your word together and live what we read. Make every one of us a faithful priest, praying for others and pointing them to Christ. And renew Your whole church, in every land, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Malachi's friends met when others said, "It is useless to serve God." Where do you hear that kind of discouragement today, and who could you talk with about God instead?
- Which of Spener's rules for his gatherings would most help the groups you belong to?
- What does it mean for you that every believer is a priest? Whom could you pray for and encourage this week?

## An Orphanage Built on Prayer

> "From His holy dwelling God acts as father to orphans and defender of widows." (Psalm 68:5)

At the beginning of 1694, in Glaucha, a poor village just outside the walls of the German city of Halle, the beggars came as usual on a Thursday. Thursday was the day when better-off households handed out alms, and the poor knew which doors to knock on. One of those doors belonged to the village pastor, a young professor named August Hermann Francke.

That Thursday, Francke invited the beggars into his house, put the adults on one side of the room and the children on the other, and asked the children, kindly, what they knew of the Christian faith from Luther's catechism. They knew almost nothing. After about a quarter of an hour he closed with a prayer and gave out the alms as usual. Then he told them that from now on they would receive food for the soul as well as for the body, and invited them to come back every Thursday. They did.

Francke soon saw that a few minutes a week could not undo the ignorance of a whole childhood. Many children in Glaucha never went to school because their parents were too poor. So at the beginning of 1695 he fixed a box for gifts to the poor on the wall of his parlor. Above it he wrote 1 John 3:17, which asks how the love of God can remain in someone who has enough of this world's goods, sees a brother or sister in need, and shuts their heart against them.

About three months later someone put in a gift that was larger than usual, though still small. Francke held it in his hands and, as he later wrote, said "in full assurance of faith" that this was "a considerable fund, worthy to be laid out in some important undertaking." That same day he bought schoolbooks and hired a poor student to teach the children for two hours a day. A school for the poor had begun.

That autumn, four orphaned sisters were brought to him. He had money to care for one. "I ventured," he wrote, "in the Name of God, to take them all four." Within a few weeks there were nine orphans. He had no house for them, so he placed them with Christian families and paid for their keep. In July 1698, still without any settled income, he laid the foundation of a large stone orphan house.

### Who he was

August Hermann Francke was born in 1663 in Lübeck, in northern Germany. He was a brilliant student who mastered Hebrew and Greek. At Leipzig, with Philipp Spener's encouragement, he and some friends began a group of young scholars who met to study the Bible, not only for its grammar but for their own lives.

Yet, as he later told it, his faith was still mostly in his head. In 1687, in the town of Lüneburg, he was asked to preach on John 20:31, a verse about believing in Jesus. As he prepared, he realized that he did not have the living faith he was meant to preach. He fell into deep doubt, even about whether God existed. At last he knelt and cried out to God, and, he wrote, his doubts were taken away and his heart was filled with joy and assurance. From then on his learning served his faith.

In 1692 he became a professor at the new university in Halle and the pastor of Glaucha. He stayed in Halle for the rest of his life.

### The footsteps of God

Francke wrote down how God supplied the orphan house, so that others might trust God too. An English translation printed in London in the 1780s calls the book *The Footsteps of Divine Providence*. It reads like a diary of answered prayer.

Once, he wrote, all their supplies were gone. The steward came with his account book to be paid, and Francke saw no way to pay him. He decided to go into his room to pray, but first finished the work in front of him. As he was preparing to pray, a letter arrived from a merchant who had been told to pay him a thousand crowns for the orphan house. Francke remembered God's promise: "I answer them before they call" (Isaiah 65:24). He went into his room anyway, but instead of asking, he spent the time praising God.

Another Saturday the steward came for money to pay the builders. "Is there any money brought in?" he asked. Francke answered, "No; but I believe in God." Almost at once a student arrived with thirty crowns from someone who would not give his name. Francke asked the steward how much he needed. Exactly thirty crowns.

Not every answer came through the rich. In 1698 Francke sent a gold coin, a ducat, to a very poor woman who was suffering many trials. She wrote back that it had come just when she needed it, and that she had at once prayed God to reward the orphan house "with a great many more ducats." Soon gifts of ducats began to arrive, and then a prince left the orphan house five hundred ducats in his will. When Francke saw the heap of gold, he remembered her prayer. "Thus the believing poor," he wrote, "can help with their prayers, if they cannot with their cash."

Francke was not idle while he waited on God. He wrote letters and told the story of the work widely. In time the orphan house supported itself in part with a pharmacy that sold medicines and a press that printed cheap Bibles. His schools were strict, and some of his pupils later remembered them as harsh. But the foundation was prayer. When he died in 1727, more than two thousand children were being taught in his schools, and Halle had become a center from which missionaries were sent as far as India.

### A young man in Bristol

About a hundred years after Francke's death, a German student at the university of Halle named George Müller stayed for about two months in free rooms that Francke's orphan houses kept for poor divinity students. Some years later, in England, he read Francke's life. In 1836 he opened his own orphan house in Bristol (we will meet him again in Volume 8), and over the next sixty years he cared for many thousands of orphans without ever asking anyone for money, telling his needs to God alone. In his own account he explains why:

> "I well knew that the Word of God ought to be enough, and it was, by grace, enough, to me; but still, I considered that I ought to lend a helping hand to my brethren, if by any means, by this visible proof to the unchangeable faithfulness of the Lord, I might strengthen their hands in God; for I remembered what a great blessing my own soul had received through the Lord's dealings with His servant A. H. Franke, who in dependence upon the living God alone, established an immense Orphan-House, which I had seen many times with my own eyes."
> George Müller, *Answers to Prayer*

His biographer put it more simply:

> "Half unconsciously George Müller's whole life-work at Bristol found both its suggestion and pattern in Francke's orphanage at Halle."
> Arthur T. Pierson, *George Müller of Bristol*

### Three key insights

1. **God is a Father to orphans, and He loves to meet their needs through people who pray.** Psalm 68 says God "acts as father to orphans." In Glaucha He did it through a pastor who believed Him, a poor widow who prayed, and many people who gave.
2. **A small gift placed in God's hands can be the start of a great work.** Francke did not wait until he had enough. He began with one small gift and one poor student teacher, and trusted God for the next step.
3. **The prayers of the poor are a real gift to God's work.** A woman with almost nothing prayed for the orphans, and Francke counted her prayer as part of God's supply. No one is too poor to help with prayer.

### For us today

There are millions of orphans in the world today: children who have lost parents to war, to disease, to famine or to the dangers of the road. In Africa, Asia and Latin America it is often grandmothers, aunts, neighbors and small churches who take them in, with very little. Francke's story says to all of them: God is the Father of these children, and He hears when you pray for them.

It also speaks to those of us who feel we have nothing to give. You may not be able to open a school or support an orphan. But like the poor woman who received a ducat, you can pray. Francke believed her prayers did as much as the prince's gold.

### Try this

This week, let Francke's faith shape your prayers.

- **Pray for orphans by name.** If you know a child without parents, or a family caring for one, pray for them by name each day.
- **Pray before you ask.** When you have a need, tell God first, before you tell anyone else, as Francke and Müller did.
- **Turn answers into praise.** When God answers a prayer, even before you have finished praying, stop and thank Him, as Francke did.
- **Give something small.** Find one small way to help a child in need this week, and offer it to God as Francke offered his first gift.

### A closing prayer

Father in heaven, from Your holy dwelling You care for orphans and defend widows. Thank You for Your servant August Hermann Francke, who trusted You for bread and books and buildings, and for George Müller, who learned from him to trust You too. Be a Father to every child who has no one today. Hear the prayers of the poor, and let none of us think our prayers too small to matter. Teach me to bring my needs to You first, to begin with what is in my hand, and to praise You when You answer. In the name of Jesus Christ, who welcomed the children. Amen.

- Francke said, "No; but I believe in God." What need in your life or church do you find hardest to leave with God?
- Why do you think God so often uses small beginnings, like one gift or one poor teacher?
- Whose prayers, perhaps from someone poor or unnoticed, have helped you in your life?

## The Fire Shall Never Go Out

> "Fire is to burn on the altar at all times, never going out." (Leviticus 6:13)

On Monday, 13 August 1727, the people of a young village in Saxony, in eastern Germany, walked down a gentle slope to the parish church of Berthelsdorf to take Holy Communion together. Their village was called Herrnhut. It was only five years old.

Most of them were refugees. They had come over the mountains from Moravia and Bohemia, where Protestants were being hunted and imprisoned. In Volume 5 we met Jan Hus, who was burned at Constance in 1415. Some of his followers later formed the Unity of the Brethren. After the Brethren were outlawed in their homeland in the 1620s, some families had kept the faith in secret for a hundred years. These refugees were their children. A young nobleman, Count Nikolaus Ludwig von Zinzendorf, had let them settle on his land.

But the village had not been a peaceful place. Other people had come too, with all kinds of beliefs, and quarrels broke out about doctrine, about the parish church, about leaders. The Moravian historian J. E. Hutton, writing in 1909, describes it sadly: "As the settlers learned to know each other better they learned to love each other less." One loud preacher told them that the Lutheran church was Babylon and that they must leave it. Some called the local pastor a false prophet.

That spring Zinzendorf stepped in. On 12 May he called them all together, spoke for more than three hours about the sin of division, and set out rules for life together, which they promised to keep. Then he came to live among them, and in July they signed a brotherly agreement to end their quarrels and to live in fellowship with Christians of every church. Through that summer, people who had been enemies began to meet in small groups to pray. In the evenings the whole village gathered to pray and sing.

So they came to the church on 13 August. As they walked down the hill, those who had quarreled in the past made peace with each other. During the service, the people later said, a deep sense of the presence of God came over them all at once. They left the church sure that they were one in Christ. Years afterward they would sum up that day very simply: "We learned," they said, "to love."

### A fire that must be kept

The Brethren did not want that fire to go out. Two weeks later, on 27 August 1727, they began what they called the hourly intercession. Hutton explains their thinking: "As the fire on the altar in the Jewish Temple was never allowed to go out, so the Brethren resolved that in this new temple of the Lord the incense of intercessory prayer should rise continually day and night." The day and night were divided into hours, and each brother or sister took a turn. A later tradition says that at the start twenty-four men and twenty-four women each took one hour, so that someone was always praying.

The verse they had in mind is Leviticus 6:13. In the tabernacle, God Himself had first lit the fire on the altar: "Fire went out from before the LORD and devoured the burnt offering" (Leviticus 9:24). After that, the priests' work was to keep it burning. "Every morning the priest is to put more wood on it" (Leviticus 6:12). God lit the fire. His servants fed it, morning by morning, so that it never went out.

That is what happened at Herrnhut. On 13 August God came near. On 27 August His people began to add wood, one hour at a time.

Even the name of the village pointed that way. When the first refugees arrived in 1722, the count's steward wrote that he hoped they would build a town that would live "under the Lord's Watch," and whose people would keep "the Lord's Watch, so that no silence may be there by day or night." *Herrnhut* means "the Lord's watch" or "the Lord's keeping." The Lord was watching over them; now they would keep watch with Him.

### How long did it last?

It is often said that the Herrnhut prayer watch went on for a hundred years. Hutton is careful: it "is said to have lasted without interruption for over a hundred years." We cannot check every hour of every night across a whole century, and the way the watch was kept surely changed over the years. But there is no doubt that for a very long time the people of Herrnhut and their sister communities kept prayer rising day and night.

Nor is there doubt about what grew from it. In 1732, only five years after the watch began, the first two Moravian missionaries left Herrnhut for the Caribbean. We will follow their story in the next chapter. Nearly two centuries later, a leader of the student missionary movement wrote of the Moravians:

> "Theirs is also a praying Church. There is probably no other branch of the Church of Christ whose members devote themselves so earnestly to intercession on behalf of world-wide missions."
> John R. Mott, *The Evangelization of the World in This Generation*

Andrew Murray wondered what any church might see if it did the same:

> "Who can say what power a Church could develop and exercise, if it gave itself to the work of prayer day and night for the coming of the kingdom, for God's power on His servants and His word, for the glorifying of God in the salvation of souls?"
> Andrew Murray, *With Christ in the School of Prayer*

### One hour

There is something very humble about an hourly watch. No one at Herrnhut was asked to pray all night, every night. Each person was asked for one hour. A carpenter, a weaver, a young mother, an old man: each took a turn, and then went to sleep while another took up the watch.

In the garden of Gethsemane, Jesus came back to His sleeping disciples and asked, "So none of you could stay awake with Me for even one hour?" (Matthew 26:40). At Herrnhut, ordinary believers answered that question with their lives. None of them could keep the fire burning alone. Together, they could.

### Three key insights

1. **God often begins a work of prayer by first teaching His people to love one another.** Herrnhut was a village of quarrels before it was a village of prayer. The fire fell when enemies made peace and came to the Lord's table together.
2. **A prayer watch shared by many can keep the fire burning when any one of us grows tired.** No one at Herrnhut prayed every hour. Each took a turn, and the whole community kept the altar fire alive.
3. **The fire of prayer is kept alive by small, faithful offerings, made day after day.** God lit the fire on the altar, but the priests added wood every morning. Our part is the daily wood: an hour, a few minutes, a faithful habit.

### For us today

Many churches today know quarrels like the ones in Herrnhut: disputes over leaders, over doctrine, over money, over who belongs. Herrnhut reminds us that prayer and reconciliation belong together. Before you ask God to send His fire, ask Him to show you whom you need to forgive.

The Herrnhut watch also shows that a great work of prayer can be built from small pieces. Some churches in Korea pray early every morning. In parts of Africa and Latin America, believers keep all-night prayer meetings. Around the world, groups of Christians have formed prayer chains, with each person taking a set hour, inspired by the Moravians. A busy nurse, a student, a farmer with cattle to feed can all give one hour, or even a quarter of an hour, at a time they promise to keep.

And for believers who pray alone, in prison or in places where Christians must hide, Herrnhut says: you are not the only one keeping watch. Somewhere, at every hour of the day and night, God's people are praying.

### Try this

This week, add some wood to the fire.

- **Make peace first.** If there is someone in your church or family you have quarreled with, take one step toward peace before you pray for revival.
- **Choose your hour.** Pick one hour, or one part of an hour, that you will give to prayer for others each day this week, and keep it as a watch.
- **Share the watch.** Ask a few friends to take different times in the same day, so that prayer rises from your group from morning to night.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You lit the fire on the altar, and You ask Your people to keep it burning. Thank You for the believers of Herrnhut, refugees and strangers who learned to love one another and to pray day and night. Show me where I need to make peace, and give me grace to do it. Light Your fire in my heart, and make me faithful to add wood to it each morning. Join my small prayers to the prayers of Your people in every land and every hour, until Your name is known among all the nations, through Jesus Christ, who stays awake and prays for us. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think God's presence came to Herrnhut after the people made peace with one another, and not before?
- What is the "wood" you could faithfully add to the fire of prayer each day?
- Who could you invite to share a prayer watch with you, and what would you pray for together?

## Worthy Is the Lamb

> "They sing a new song: 'Worthy are You to receive the scroll and to break open its seals, for You were slaughtered, and at the cost of Your blood You bought for God those from every tribe and language and people and nation.'" (Revelation 5:9)

At three o'clock in the morning on 21 August 1732, two young men stood outside a house in Herrnhut, the village in Saxony where the Moravian Brethren had found a home. One was Leonard Dober, a potter. The other was David Nitschmann, a carpenter. They carried bundles on their backs and had very little money. They were setting out to walk to Copenhagen, in Denmark, and from there to sail to St Thomas, a Danish island in the Caribbean Sea, to tell the slaves on its sugar plantations about Jesus.

The house belonged to Count Nikolaus Ludwig von Zinzendorf, on whose land Herrnhut stood. J. E. Hutton's history of the Moravian Church (1909) says that the Count had spent the whole night in prayer. He drove the two men in his carriage as far as the town of Bautzen. There, by the quiet roadside, they knelt down and prayed together. Zinzendorf laid his hands on them and blessed them, and they walked away to the west.

It had begun a year earlier, in Copenhagen. Zinzendorf was there for the crowning of the new Danish king, and he met a baptized slave from St Thomas named Anton Ulrich. Anton spoke of his sister and brother, who were still slaves on the island, and of their longing to hear the gospel. When the Count told the story at Herrnhut, Dober could not sleep. He felt God calling him to go. Walking in the evening with his friend Tobias Leupold, he found that Tobias had felt the same call, and the two young men knelt down side by side and asked God to guide them. The church at Herrnhut hesitated for a year. At last, as the Brethren often did, they asked the Lord's will by drawing a lot, and Dober drew a slip of paper that read, "Let the lad go, for the Lord is with him."

A story is often told that the first missionaries sold themselves as slaves so that they could preach among the slaves. Hutton calls this "a popular misconception." What we know is that when an official in Copenhagen asked how they would live, Nitschmann answered, "We shall work as slaves among the slaves." Told that no white man would be allowed to work as a slave, he said that he was a carpenter and would ply his trade.

They reached St Thomas in December 1732. Others soon followed, to Greenland, to Suriname in South America, to South Africa and beyond.

### Who he was

Count Zinzendorf was born in Dresden, in Saxony, in 1700. He was raised by his grandmother, a woman of warm faith, and went to school at Halle under August Hermann Francke, whom we met in chapter 3. There he and some friends formed a little society to pray for one another and for the spread of the gospel.

As a young man, in the city of Düsseldorf he saw a painting of Christ wearing the crown of thorns, with words beneath it that Hutton gives as "All this I did for thee; what doest thou for Me?" He resolved again to live for Christ. In 1722 he allowed a group of refugees from Moravia, the heirs of the old Unity of the Brethren that grew from the followers of Jan Hus, to build a village on his land. That village was Herrnhut, "the Lord's watch." In chapter 4 we saw how God united its quarreling people in 1727, and how they began to pray in turn, hour by hour, night and day.

Zinzendorf was not a perfect man. He could be proud and impatient, and Hutton writes plainly that he "was a firm believer in slavery." When he visited St Thomas in 1739, he urged the enslaved believers to obey their masters. Yet God used the Moravians to bring the gospel to people whom the powerful churches had ignored. For fifty years, Hutton says, they worked among the slaves of the West Indies with no help from any other church.

### The Lamb at the center

The heart of Moravian prayer was the Lamb of God. Their old seal shows a lamb carrying a banner, with the Latin words "Vicit agnus noster, eum sequamur": "Our Lamb has conquered; let us follow Him." A motto often linked with the first missionaries says that the Lamb who was slain must receive the reward of His suffering. The words are traditional, and we cannot be sure who first said them. But they say exactly what Revelation 5:9 says. Jesus "bought for God" people "from every tribe and language and people and nation." Every person who has never heard of Him is someone He paid for.

This changed the way they preached. In Greenland, where Moravians arrived in 1733, they spent years explaining doctrine with almost no fruit. Then, one evening in June 1738, a missionary named John Beck read to a group of Greenlanders from the Gospel story of Jesus praying in the garden, His sweat falling "as it were great drops of blood." A man named Kajarnak came forward and said, in Hutton's words, "How was that? Tell me that again, for I, too, would be saved." From then on the missionaries simply told the story of the cross.

Their prayer was also calm. In 1735 a group of Moravians sailed to the colony of Georgia in America. On the same ship was a young Anglican minister named John Wesley, whom we will meet in chapter 7. On 25 January 1736 a terrible storm struck the ship during the Moravians' evening service. Wesley wrote in his journal:

> "In the midst of the psalm wherewith their service began, the sea broke over, split the mainsail in pieces, covered the ship, and poured in between the decks, as if the great deep had already swallowed us up. A terrible screaming began among the English. The Germans calmly sang on. I asked one of them afterward, 'Were you not afraid?' He answered, 'I thank God, no.' I asked, 'But were not your women and children afraid?' He replied, mildly, 'No; our women and children are not afraid to die.'"
> John Wesley, *The Journal of John Wesley*

Wesley knew that he did not have their peace. That discovery set him on the road to Aldersgate Street.

More than a century later, the London preacher Charles Spurgeon urged a gathering of ministers not merely to admire the Moravians but to follow them, for the same Lamb had bought them all:

> "It is not enough for us to say, 'Those Moravians are very wonderful people.' We ought to be wonderful people, too. Christ did not purchase the Moravians any more completely than He purchased us; they are under no more obligation to make sacrifices than we are."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *An All-Round Ministry*

### Three key insights

1. **Prayer for the nations begins with seeing what the Lamb paid for them.** Revelation 5:9 says that Jesus bought people from every tribe and language at the cost of His blood. When we remember that price, we cannot pray only for our own family, church or nation.
2. **Those who are at peace with God can sing when the storm breaks.** The Moravians on Wesley's ship were not braver by nature than the English. They had already given their lives to Christ, so even death could not frighten them.
3. **We ask God to show us His way, and then we go where He sends us.** Dober and Leupold knelt side by side and asked to be guided. When the answer came, Dober did not wait for an easy road. He walked to Copenhagen with a bundle on his back.

### For us today

The Moravians were not rich or famous. They were potters, carpenters and farmers, many of them refugees. That is good news, because most believers in the world are ordinary people too. Today believers in Nigeria, Brazil, Korea, India and the Philippines are sending workers to other lands, often with very little money, just as Herrnhut did.

Most of us will not be sent far away. But all of us can pray for the peoples who have not yet heard of Jesus, and all of us can tell the story of the cross to someone near us. When we pray, we remember that Christ has already paid for them. We are asking Him to gather what is His own.

### Try this

This week, let the Lamb who was slain shape your prayers.

- **Pray for one people.** Choose one people group or nation where few know Christ. Pray for them each day, using the words of Revelation 5:9.
- **Tell the old story.** Look for one chance to tell someone, simply, what Jesus suffered for them.
- **Sing in the storm.** When fear comes this week, sing a hymn or psalm of praise to the Lamb, as the Moravians did on the ship.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, Lamb of God, You were slaughtered, and at the cost of Your blood You bought for God people from every tribe and language and people and nation. Thank You for Leonard Dober and David Nitschmann, for Count Zinzendorf and the Brethren of Herrnhut, who prayed and went so that You would receive the reward of Your suffering. Give me a heart that prays for the nations. Give me peace in the storm, so that I can sing when others are afraid. Show me where You want me to go, and give me courage to follow You. You are worthy, Lord Jesus, to receive power and glory and blessing for ever. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Jesus bought people from every nation. How might this change the way you pray for people of other tribes and languages?
- The Moravians' calm in the storm troubled John Wesley. What gives you peace when you are afraid?
- Dober and Leupold knelt together to ask God's guidance. Is there a decision you could bring to God in prayer with a trusted friend this week?

## The Apron over Her Head

> "You hide them under the cover of Your presence, away from human plotting; You keep them in a shelter, safe from quarrelsome tongues." (Psalm 31:20)

The rectory at Epworth was never quiet. It stood in a small market town in Lincolnshire, in the flat, wet country of eastern England, and it was full of children. There were babies to feed, little ones to teach, older girls sewing and arguing, servants coming in from the yard, and often no money to pay the bills. The rector, Samuel Wesley, was a clever and stubborn man who was often away, sometimes in London on church business, once in prison for debt. The one who held the house together was his wife, Susanna.

The best-known story about her prayer comes down to us as a family memory, retold in many books since. It is not in her own letters, and we cannot be sure of its details. But it fits everything else we know of her. In a house with no quiet room, the story says, Susanna would sit down and throw her long apron up over her head. Underneath it she was alone with God. Her children learned that when their mother's head was covered, she was praying, and they must not disturb her.

We may smile at the picture, but think of what it means. Susanna did not wait for a peaceful house, which she never had. She made a little room for God in the middle of the noise, with the cloth she wore every day for cooking and cleaning. Under that apron she was hidden, as the psalm says, "under the cover of Your presence."

What we know for certain from her letters is that she kept set times for God. Writing to her eldest son, Samuel, when he was a schoolboy in London, she told him:

> "I will tell you what rule I used to observe when I was in my father's house, and had as little, if not less liberty than you have now. I used to allow myself as much time for recreation as I spent in private devotion; not that I always spent so much, but I gave myself leave to go so far but no farther."
> Susanna Wesley, quoted in Eliza Clarke, *Susanna Wesley*

In the same letter she urged him: "Begin and end the day with Him who is the Alpha and Omega." Another time, when he hoped to win a place as a scholar at his school, she asked him to set apart the hours from four to six on Sunday afternoon to pray about it, and told him that she would spend the same hours praying too. Mother and son, many miles apart, would be on their knees together.

### Who she was

Susanna Annesley was born in London in 1669. She was the youngest of a very large family; her father, Dr. Samuel Annesley, was a well-known Puritan minister. She was a thoughtful girl who read widely, and while still young she decided to join the Church of England, though her father had left it. In 1688 she married Samuel Wesley, a young clergyman, and in the 1690s they moved to Epworth.

Her life was hard. She gave birth to nineteen children, but only about half of them lived to grow up. Her health was often poor. The family was always in debt. In 1709 the rectory burned down at night. Five-year-old John was trapped upstairs and pulled out of a window just before the roof fell in. Susanna believed God had saved him for a purpose.

She was also a remarkable teacher. For about twenty years she kept school for her children six hours a day, teaching the girls as well as the boys to read well, which was unusual then. Once her husband counted while she told one child the same thing twenty times. When he wondered at her patience, she answered, Clarke tells us, that if she had stopped at nineteen she would have lost all her labor.

### How she prayed

Susanna's prayers were not only for herself. One winter, while her husband was away in London, her daughter Emilia read aloud to her a book about two young German missionaries, Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg and Heinrich Plütschau, who were working in Tranquebar in South India under the king of Denmark. Susanna wrote to her husband that she spent a good part of that evening "praising and adoring the Divine goodness" for those men. Then a new thought came to her:

> "I thought I might live in a more exemplary manner, I might pray more for the people, and speak with more warmth to those with whom I have opportunity of conversing. However, I resolved to begin with my own children, and accordingly I proposed and observed the following method."
> Susanna Wesley, quoted in Eliza Clarke, *Susanna Wesley*

Each evening she spent time alone with one child, talking about the things of their soul. She named the evenings: Monday for Molly, Tuesday for Hetty, Thursday for Jacky (her name for John), and so on through the week.

About the same time, because there was no afternoon service in the church, she began to read a sermon and pray with her own household on Sunday evenings in the rectory kitchen. A servant boy told his parents, and they asked to come. Others followed. Soon, she wrote, more than two hundred people came, and many went away for lack of room. (Her biographer Eliza Clarke wonders whether the kitchen could really hold so many, and whether the meetings moved to a barn.) The curate complained, and her husband wrote from London to ask whether it was proper for a woman to lead such meetings. In February 1712 she answered him plainly:

> "To your second, I reply that as I am a woman, so I am also a mistress of a large family. And though the superior charge of the souls contained in it lies upon you, as head of the family, and as their minister, yet in your absence I cannot but look upon every soul you leave under my care as a talent committed to me, under a trust, by the great Lord of all the families of heaven and earth."
> Susanna Wesley, quoted in Eliza Clarke, *Susanna Wesley*

She was not afraid of the crowd. She wrote that "those who have the honour of speaking to the great and holy God need not be ashamed to speak before the whole world." If he wanted the meetings stopped, she asked him to say so by a clear command. His reply is lost, and the trouble ended when he came home.

Susanna lived to see her sons John and Charles preach across England. In 1739, at the age of seventy, she told John that as she received the cup at the Lord's Supper, the words "The blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, which was given for thee" struck through her heart, and she knew that God had forgiven all her sins. She died in London in July 1742. Her last request to her children was, "Children, as soon as I am released, sing a psalm of praise to God."

### Three key insights

1. **A crowded, noisy home can still hold a hidden place for meeting God.** Whether or not the apron story is exactly true, Susanna kept set times with God in a house that was never calm. We do not need perfect conditions to pray, only a place, however small, and the will to keep it.
2. **Children learn to pray when they see prayer kept faithfully at home.** Susanna's children saw her pray, and each one had an evening alone with her each week. Two of them grew up to lead a revival that reached the whole English-speaking world.
3. **Every person placed in our care is a trust from God, and prayer is the first way we keep that trust.** Susanna looked on each soul in her house as "a talent committed to me." She began with her own children and servants, and neighbors came because they found God there.

### For us today

Many believers today live as Susanna did: in small houses full of people, with little money and no room of their own. A mother in a one-room home in Nairobi, a family sharing a flat in Mumbai, a grandmother caring for grandchildren in a village in Guatemala, all know how hard it is to find a quiet moment. Susanna shows that it can be done. A scarf over the head, a corner of the room, a walk to the well, a few minutes before the house wakes: God can meet us in any of them.

Her kitchen meetings also speak to places where there are few pastors, or where believers cannot meet openly. In many lands the church lives in homes, led by ordinary people, often women, who read the Scriptures and pray with their neighbors. Susanna's letter is a reminder that every soul under our care is a trust from God.

### Try this

This week, make a small place for God in a busy life.

- **Choose your apron.** Find one simple sign that tells your family you are praying: a scarf, a shawl, a chair in a corner. Use it for ten minutes each day.
- **Give each child an evening.** If you have children or grandchildren, spend a short time with each one alone this week, asking about their life and praying with them.
- **Open your door.** Invite a neighbor or friend to read a passage of Scripture and pray with you in your home.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You hide Your people under the cover of Your presence and keep them in Your shelter. Thank You for Your servant Susanna Wesley, who found room for You in a crowded and troubled house, who prayed for her children and taught them to love You. Teach me to make a quiet place for You in the middle of my noise. Help me to begin and end each day with You. Bless the children and the people You have placed in my care, and make my home a place where others can find You. Through Jesus Christ, whose blood was given for me. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Where in your home or your day could you make a small, regular place to meet God?
- Susanna gave each of her children an evening alone with her. Who has given you that kind of attention in your walk with God, and to whom could you give it?
- Susanna wrote that every soul in her care was a trust from God. Whom has God placed in your care, and how can you pray for them this week?

## "God Does Nothing but in Answer to Prayer"

> "You crave, and you do not have. You murder and you envy, and you cannot get what you want. You fight and you wage war. You lack, because you do not ask." (James 4:2)

Wednesday, 24 May 1738, began early for John Wesley. He was thirty-four years old, a clergyman of the Church of England and a teacher from Oxford University. He had prayed for years, fasted twice a week, visited prisoners and given away much of his money. He had sailed to America as a missionary. Yet he had come home in failure, with "continual sorrow and heaviness" in his heart. He was sure that he did not have the living faith he had seen in the Moravians on the ship in the storm (chapter 5).

His journal tells us what happened that day. At about five in the morning he opened his New Testament and read that God has given us "exceeding great and precious promises." Before he went out, he opened it again and read the words of Jesus, "Thou art not far from the kingdom of God." In the afternoon he went to St Paul's Cathedral in London and heard the choir sing Psalm 130: "Out of the deep have I called unto Thee, O Lord: Lord, hear my voice."

In the evening he did something he did not want to do. He went, "very unwillingly," to a small religious society that met in a house in Aldersgate Street. Someone was reading aloud from Martin Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans, the same Luther we met in Volume 6. Wesley wrote:

> "About a quarter before nine, while he was describing the change which God works in the heart through faith in Christ, I felt my heart strangely warmed. I felt I did trust in Christ, Christ alone, for salvation; and an assurance was given me that He had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death. I began to pray with all my might for those who had in a more especial manner despitefully used me and persecuted me."
> John Wesley, *The Journal of John Wesley*

Notice the last sentence. The first thing Wesley did with his warmed heart was to pray. And he did not pray first for himself. He prayed "with all my might" for the people who had treated him worst.

The struggle was not over. That very night, Wesley says, temptations came back "again and again." But now, he writes, "I as often lifted up my eyes, and He 'sent me help from his holy place.'" The difference was not that the battle stopped. It was that he now fought it as a child who could call on his Father.

### Who he was

John Wesley was born in 1703 at Epworth, the fifteenth child of Samuel and Susanna Wesley, whom we met in chapter 6. At the age of five he was pulled from the burning rectory. He studied at Oxford, became a fellow of Lincoln College and was ordained in the Church of England. With his brother Charles and a few friends he formed a small group that met to pray, study the Bible, take Communion often and visit the poor and the prisoners. Other students mocked them as the "Holy Club" and the "Methodists," because they lived by such a careful method.

After Aldersgate, Wesley spent more than fifty years preaching the gospel across Britain and Ireland, usually on horseback. He preached in churches when they let him, and in fields, market squares and mines when they did not. Mobs threw stones at him. He gathered those who were awakened into societies and small "classes" that met each week to pray and watch over one another's souls. He wrote against the slave trade, and one of his last letters encouraged William Wilberforce in his fight to end it. He died in London in 1791, still preaching in his late eighties.

He could be stubborn and controlling, and his marriage was unhappy. But few people in history have prayed and worked so steadily for so long.

### How he prayed

Wesley believed that prayer was not an extra but the means by which God chooses to work. In 1763 he published a short book of counsel for believers, later reprinted in *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*. One of its sayings has become famous:

> "God does nothing but in answer to prayer; and even they who have been converted to God without praying for it themselves, (which is exceeding rare,) were not without the prayers of others. Every new victory which a soul gains is the effect of a new prayer."
> John Wesley, *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*

Wesley did not mean that God is weak and cannot act unless we ask. He meant that God has chosen to make His people partners in His work. That is exactly what James says: "You lack, because you do not ask" (James 4:2). James also warns that God does not answer prayers that only serve our own pleasures (James 4:3). But when we ask for His kingdom and His help, He loves to give.

Wesley also believed that no one comes to Christ without someone's prayers. Behind every conversion there is a mother, a friend, a church or a stranger who prayed. That was true of Wesley himself. His mother had prayed for him, and talked with him alone about his soul, since he was a small boy.

Wesley gave God the first hours of his day. On his birthday in 1774, wondering why he was still so strong in old age, he listed among the reasons "my constantly rising at four, for about fifty years." Two years later he gave thanks again for his health, and added: "But still 'the help that is done upon earth, He doeth it Himself.' And this He doeth in answer to many prayers."

Wesley also taught his people to give themselves wholly to God. From the 1750s he led Methodists in services, usually at the start of the year, in which they renewed their covenant with God; on 1 January 1776, his journal says, about eighteen hundred met in London to do so. In 1780 he printed his directions for the service. The best-known words of that service were not his own; he took them from an older Puritan writer, Richard Alleine. In the second edition, printed in 1781, the believer says to Christ: "put me to what thou wilt, rank me with whom thou wilt; put me to doing, put me to suffering, let me be employed for thee, or laid aside for thee, exalted for thee, or trodden under foot for thee." Methodists around the world still pray a form of these words.

### Three key insights

1. **God has chosen to do His work in answer to the prayers of His people.** He does not need our prayers to be strong. But He has made us His partners, and He waits to be asked. "You lack, because you do not ask."
2. **Knowing that our sins are forgiven turns prayer from an anxious duty into a child's call to the Father.** For years Wesley prayed as a duty and found no peace. After Aldersgate, when temptations came back, he could write, "I as often lifted up my eyes." He prayed as a son, and even his enemies were now in his prayers.
3. **Giving ourselves wholly to God makes every other prayer honest.** In the covenant prayer we hand God our whole life: our work, our rank, our success and our suffering. After that, our requests are no longer orders to God. They are the prayers of servants who trust their Master.

### For us today

Wesley's words are a call and a comfort. They are a call, because many good things do not happen simply because no one asks. A church may stay cold, a neighborhood may stay without the gospel, a child may stay far from God, because believers did not pray. They are also a comfort, because God does answer the prayers of ordinary people. Wesley's societies were full of miners, servants, weavers and soldiers, and their prayers helped to change a nation.

Many who read this book will never have a quiet study or much free time. Wesley rose at four. A market seller in Accra, a fisherman in Indonesia or a nurse working night shifts in São Paulo may rise even earlier. The hour does not matter. What matters is that we give God some of our best time, and that we ask Him, specifically and often, for what we need and for what others need.

And the covenant prayer is for every believer, in every land. Some are "employed" for God in visible ways. Others are "laid aside" by illness, prison or old age. Both can belong wholly to Him.

### Try this

This week, take James 4:2 seriously.

- **Ask for one thing.** Write down one specific need, your own or someone else's, that you have worried about but never brought to God. Pray for it every day.
- **Pray for your enemies first.** Before you pray for yourself one morning, pray "with all your might" for someone who has hurt you.
- **Remember those who prayed for you.** Thank God for the people whose prayers helped bring you to Christ, and pray for someone who does not yet know Him.
- **Pray the covenant.** At the end of the week, read the covenant words above slowly, and give your whole life to God again.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You have chosen to do Your work in answer to the prayers of Your people. Forgive me for the times I have worried instead of asking, and for the good I have missed because I did not pray. Thank You for Your servant John Wesley, whose heart You warmed, and who rose early to seek You for so many years. Warm my heart with the assurance that Christ has taken away my sins, even mine. Teach me to pray for those who hurt me. I am no longer my own but Yours. Put me to doing or to suffering, use me or lay me aside, as You will, for I belong to You, through Jesus Christ my Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- James says, "You lack, because you do not ask." Is there something you have needed but never really asked God for?
- Whose prayers do you think helped bring you to Christ? How could you pray for someone in the same way?
- Which part of the covenant prayer is hardest for you to say honestly to God, and why?

## Oh, That You Would Tear the Heavens

> "For a long time now we have been like a people never under Your rule, a people that never bore Your name. If only You would split the heavens apart and come down, and the mountains would shake as You came near!" (Isaiah 64:1)

On the afternoon of Saturday, 17 February 1739, a young clergyman climbed a small hill at Kingswood, outside the city of Bristol in the west of England. His name was George Whitefield, and he was twenty-four years old. Around the hill lived the colliers, the men who dug coal from the pits and carried it into the city. They were poor, rough and often drunk. They had no church of their own, and the churches of Bristol did not want them. Whitefield's own journal says that he had long "felt my bowels yearn" toward them, an old way of saying that he felt deep pity for them.

Whitefield had become famous as a preacher in London and Bristol. But many pulpits were now closed to him. Some clergy disliked his message that even baptized churchgoers must be born again. Others disliked the crowds he drew. So at Kingswood he did something that almost no clergyman of the Church of England had done. He stood on the hill and preached in the open air to as many as came to hear. He thought there were upwards of two hundred.

His journal, as he printed it in 1756, records what he felt: "Blessed be God that the ice is now broke, and I have now taken the field." He knew that some would blame him. "But is there not a cause?" he asked. "Pulpits are denied, and the poor colliers ready to perish for lack of knowledge."

He went back to Kingswood again and again. The crowds grew to thousands. Many colliers were deeply moved, and tears ran down faces black with coal dust. Within six weeks Whitefield had persuaded his friend John Wesley, whom we met in chapter 7, to come to Bristol and take up the work. Wesley was shocked at first. He wrote in his journal that he could scarcely reconcile himself "to this strange way of preaching in the fields," because all his life he had thought "the saving of souls almost a sin if it had not been done in a church." But he went, and for the next fifty years Wesley preached in the open air too.

### Who he was

George Whitefield was born in 1714 at the Bell Inn in Gloucester, where his mother kept the inn. His father died when he was two. At Oxford University he was a servitor, a poor student who paid his way by waiting on richer students. There he joined the Wesley brothers' "Holy Club." For a long time he tried to earn peace with God by fasting and strict rules, until he became seriously ill. In 1735, as he lay ill, he came to trust in Christ alone, and he never forgot the joy.

He preached for thirty-four years in Britain and America, crossing the Atlantic Ocean thirteen times. In the American colonies he became the best-known voice of the Great Awakening, and Jonathan Edwards, whom we will meet in chapter 9, welcomed him to Northampton in 1740. He founded an orphan house in Georgia called Bethesda.

But we must also tell the hardest part of his story honestly. Whitefield campaigned for slavery to be allowed in Georgia, where its founders had forbidden it, and he owned enslaved people to support his orphan house. He preached that Africans had souls as precious as any, and he rebuked masters for their cruelty, yet he did not see the evil of the system he joined. It is the darkest mark on his life, and believers today should grieve it.

Whitefield and Wesley also quarreled bitterly in 1740 and 1741, because they understood God's choosing of His people differently. Yet they were reconciled, and remained friends. When Whitefield died in Massachusetts in September 1770, Wesley preached a sermon in his memory in London, as Whitefield had asked.

### How he prayed

Whitefield's open-air preaching grew out of a life of prayer. After his conversion, he wrote, "I began to read the holy Scriptures (upon my knees) laying aside all other books, and praying over, if possible, every line and word." He said it proved to be "meat indeed, and drink indeed" to his soul.

He believed that prayer is the mark of every true believer, not only of preachers. In a sermon on the words "Brethren, pray for us," he said:

> "Now Prayer is a duty founded on natural religion; the very heathens never neglected it, though many Christian heathens amongst us do: and it is so essential to Christianity, that you might as reasonably expect to find a living man without breath, as a true Christian without the spirit of prayer and supplication."
> George Whitefield, *Selected Sermons of George Whitefield*

In the same sermon he warned that Christians who pray only for themselves soon lose their love for one another. Intercession, praying for others, keeps love alive. Perhaps this is one reason why Whitefield and Wesley could disagree so strongly and still love each other.

When Whitefield preached, he did not only speak to the crowd. He prayed for God to act while he spoke. Older English Bibles translate Isaiah 64:1, "Oh that thou wouldest rend the heavens, that thou wouldest come down." Whitefield used those words in a sermon on the raising of Lazarus:

> "And O that he would now rend the heavens, and come down amongst you! O that there may be a stirring among the dry bones this day!"
> George Whitefield, *Selected Sermons of George Whitefield*

He knew that a preacher could not raise the spiritually dead. Only God could do that. So he asked God to come down.

Wesley, who heard him pray many times, said in his funeral sermon that the love of God in Whitefield's heart was the source of his preaching and of his prayers:

> "This it was which enabled him to pour out his soul in prayer, in a manner peculiar to himself, with such fullness and ease united together, with such strength and variety both of sentiment and expression."
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

Whitefield prayed to the end. Wesley tells us that on his last morning he rose at four and went into his closet, his private room for prayer, and that his companion noticed he stayed "unusually long in private." A little later he died.

### Three key insights

1. **A true believer needs prayer as a living body needs breath.** Whitefield said that we might as well expect a living man without breath as a Christian without the spirit of prayer. Prayer is not a special skill for a few. It is the life of every child of God.
2. **We may boldly ask God to come down in power where people have been forgotten.** The colliers of Kingswood had been left alone by the churches. Whitefield went to them and asked God to split the heavens and come down. God answered with a great awakening.
3. **Praying for one another keeps love alive, even between friends who disagree.** Whitefield taught that Christians who stop praying for others soon stop loving them. He and Wesley disagreed for thirty years, yet they remained friends to the end.

### For us today

Around the world today there are many "Kingswoods": crowded settlements on the edge of great cities, mining towns, refugee camps, prisons, fishing villages and places where the church has never gone. People there are often poor, and many feel that no one cares about them. Whitefield's example calls us to go to them, but it also calls us to pray for them first, and to ask God to come down in power among them.

Few of us will preach to thousands. But all of us can pray over the Bible on our knees, a line at a time. All of us can ask God to work while we speak to a neighbor about Christ. And all of us can pray for believers with whom we disagree. In many lands Christians of different churches are divided by old quarrels. Prayer for one another is the first step toward love.

### Try this

This week, let Isaiah's prayer become your own.

- **Pray through the sermon.** The next time you hear a sermon, pray silently all through it that God will come down and open hearts, beginning with your own.
- **Name your Kingswood.** Think of one place or group near you that the church has largely left alone. Pray each day that God will come down in power there.
- **Pray while you speak.** Before you talk with someone about Jesus, ask God silently to open their heart.
- **Pray for someone you disagree with.** Choose one believer with whom you have argued, and pray for God's blessing on them every day.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, we have often been like a people never under Your rule. If only You would split the heavens apart and come down! Thank You for Your servant George Whitefield, who prayed over Your word on his knees and went out to the poor colliers when the churches were closed to him. Give me his love for the forgotten and his boldness in prayer. Breathe into me the spirit of prayer, so that I can no more live without it than without breath. Teach me to pray for those with whom I disagree. Come down in power on my town and my nation, for the glory of Your name, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Whitefield compared prayer to breathing. How does that picture describe your own prayer life right now?
- Who are the "colliers of Kingswood" in your town, the people the church has left alone? How could you begin to pray for them?
- Is there a believer you disagree with whom you have stopped praying for? What would change if you began to pray for them again?

## A Concert of Prayer

> "The people of one city will go to another and say, 'Come, let us go at once to seek the favor of the LORD and to seek the LORD of Armies. I am going myself.'" (Zechariah 8:21)

In October 1744 a group of ministers in Scotland met to talk about the state of the church and the world. Revival had come to their land and to America in the years before. Thousands had turned to Christ. But now the fire seemed to be dying down, and much of the world had never heard the gospel at all. What could they do?

They decided to pray, and to ask others to pray with them. After seeking God for direction, they agreed on a simple plan. For the next two years, believers who wished to join would set apart some time on Saturday evening and Sunday morning each week, and a longer time on the first Tuesday of every three months. They could pray alone, in small praying groups, or in public meetings. All of them would ask the same thing: that God would pour out His Holy Spirit on the churches and on the whole earth, revive true religion and spread the kingdom of Christ.

There was no great organization behind it. The ministers simply spoke to friends and wrote letters. Yet many joined. In Glasgow, about forty-five praying societies agreed to it. When the two years ended, the ministers printed a short paper, called a memorial, dated 26 August 1746, asking believers everywhere to continue for seven more years. They sent nearly five hundred copies across the ocean to New England.

One of those copies reached Jonathan Edwards, pastor of the church in Northampton, a town in the colony of Massachusetts. Edwards had seen revival in his own town in 1734 and 1735, and again in the Great Awakening a few years later. He read the memorial and was deeply stirred. In 1747 he wrote a book to urge Americans to join. Its title, like many titles of that time, was very long. It began: *An Humble Attempt to Promote Explicit Agreement and Visible Union of God's People in Extraordinary Prayer.* In it he called the Scottish plan "a certain concert for prayer."

In the English of that time, a "concert" meant an agreement, a plan in which many people act together, as musicians play together in harmony. Believers who might never meet, in Scotland, England and America, would kneel at the same hours, with the same requests, like one great choir of prayer before God.

### Who he was

Jonathan Edwards was born in 1703 in Connecticut, the only son among the eleven children of a pastor. Prayer came early. In his *Personal Narrative*, written years later, he remembers a time of revival in his father's church when he was a boy. "I used to pray five times a day in secret," he wrote, and he and some school friends "built a booth in a swamp, in a very retired spot, for a place of prayer." Later, he says honestly, those feelings faded, and he came to think that his boyish delight in religion had not yet been true grace. Real faith came when he was a student at Yale College, as he read of the glory of God in 1 Timothy 1:17.

Edwards became one of the greatest thinkers America has produced. He wrote deep books about God's glory, the human will and true religion. He also made mistakes. His church dismissed him in 1750 after a bitter quarrel, and he went to serve as a missionary among the Mahican people at Stockbridge, on the frontier. Like many ministers of his time, he owned enslaved Africans, a sin that he did not see clearly and that we must name honestly. He died in 1758, soon after becoming president of the college at Princeton.

He loved to pray alone, often outdoors. In 1737, he writes, "as I rode out into the woods for my health," he got off his horse in a quiet place, "as my manner commonly has been, to walk for divine contemplation and prayer." There he was given a view of the glory of Christ that kept him, "as near as I can judge, about an hour," in "a flood of tears, and weeping aloud."

His wife, Sarah, shared this love of prayer. Before they married, when she was only about thirteen, Edwards wrote a note about her. "She loves to be alone," he said, "walking in the fields and groves, and seems to have some one invisible always conversing with her." Together they raised eleven children in a home where family prayer was kept each day. In 1747 their house also became the place where the young missionary David Brainerd came to die. We will meet him in the next chapter.

### Seeking the Lord Himself

Edwards built his book on Zechariah 8:20-22. In the Bible he used, verse 21 reads, "Let us go speedily to pray before the LORD, and to seek the LORD of hosts: I will go also." He pointed out that the union in the verse is an open agreement: one city proposes it, and others join. He read the words "I will go also" as the answer of one person after another who hears the call and says yes. He also noted that the Hebrew for "speedily" is literally "let us go in going," a doubled phrase that shows great earnestness.

Then he asked what they were seeking. Their goal, he wrote, was "the good, that shall be sought by prayer; which is God himself." They were not only asking for blessings or success. They were asking that God, who had seemed to hide Himself, would come back to His church and make Himself known.

Edwards did not think that praying in a crowd was enough. In an earlier book, *Religious Affections*, he had warned that some people are deeply moved in public but have nothing like it when they are alone with God. He wrote:

> "A true Christian doubtless delights in religious fellowship, and Christian conversation, and finds much to affect his heart in it; but he also delights at times to retire from all mankind to converse with God in solitary places. And this also has its peculiar advantages for fixing his heart, and engaging its affections. True religion disposes persons to be much alone in solitary places, for holy meditation and prayer."
> Jonathan Edwards, *A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections*

The concert of prayer joined both. Each believer prayed, often alone, but at agreed times and for agreed things, so that all were praying together. The plan also guarded against forgetfulness. Edwards explained that duties with no set time are easily put off, but fixing certain seasons for prayer "tends to prevent forgetfulness."

The seed grew slowly. In 1784 a group of Baptist ministers in England read Edwards's book and began a monthly prayer meeting for the spread of the gospel. In chapter 16 we will see what came of it.

### Three key insights

1. **When believers agree to pray together for God's kingdom, they take part in His plan for the world.** Zechariah saw the people of one city calling those of another to seek the Lord, and others answering, "I am going myself." Edwards believed that God brings revival in answer to such united prayer, and history has shown him right many times.
2. **Prayer with others must grow from prayer alone with God.** Edwards loved praying societies, but he also walked alone in the woods to pray. Public prayer that has no root in private prayer soon withers.
3. **A plan for prayer, agreed ahead of time, guards us against forgetting to pray.** Edwards saw that a duty with no set time is easily put off. The concert did not depend on how people felt each week, and its fixed times helped ordinary believers keep praying for years.

### For us today

Today a believer in Kenya can pray at the same hour as a believer in Korea, Peru or Ukraine, asking the same things of the same Father. Many churches already do this. Some join in days of prayer for the persecuted church. Some pray at an agreed hour each week for their nation. Some small groups in villages and cities meet faithfully, month after month, to pray for revival and for the gospel to reach those who have never heard it. Edwards would have rejoiced to see it.

A concert of prayer also helps those who feel alone. A believer in a hostile land, or a sick person who cannot leave home, may not be able to join a meeting. But they can pray at the agreed time, knowing that thousands of others are praying with them.

### Try this

This week, join a concert of prayer.

- **Agree with someone.** Ask one or two believers, near or far, to pray with you at the same time each week for the same things.
- **Use the old times.** Try praying on Saturday evening and Sunday morning, as the Scottish believers did, for God's blessing on His church and His word.
- **Seek the Lord Himself.** In your prayer, ask not only for blessings but for God's presence: "Lord, make Yourself known among us."

### A closing prayer

Lord of Armies, Your people have gone from city to city saying, "Come, let us go at once to seek the favor of the LORD." Thank You for the ministers of Scotland, for Jonathan and Sarah Edwards, and for all who have agreed together to pray for Your kingdom. Join my prayers to the prayers of Your people in every land. Teach me to seek You Yourself, not only Your gifts. Meet me when I am alone with You, and keep me faithful at the times I set apart for You. Pour out Your Spirit on Your church, revive true religion, and fill the whole earth with the knowledge of Your glory, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Zechariah pictures each person saying, "I am going myself." What keeps you from praying with others for revival, and what would help you begin?
- Edwards warned against being moved in public but cold in private. How does your prayer alone compare with your prayer in church?
- Which believers could you agree with to pray at the same time each week? What would you pray for together?

## Praying in the Snow

> "My children, I am suffering birth pains over you a second time, and I will until Christ is formed in you." (Galatians 4:19)

In April 1743 a thin young preacher of twenty-four rode into a place called Kaunaumeek, in the forest between Stockbridge in Massachusetts and Albany in New York. A small community of Mahican people lived there, and he had been sent to tell them about Christ. His name was David Brainerd. On his first night he slept, as he wrote in his diary, "on a little heap of straw, in a log room without any floor."

He stayed almost a year. For a time he lived with the people in one of their wigwams, and then he built a small house of his own. Bread had to be fetched from ten or fifteen miles away, and sometimes it was moldy before he could eat it. That winter he was told to learn the language from John Sergeant, the missionary at Stockbridge, so he rode back and forth again and again. Jonathan Edwards, who later edited his diary, explains that these rides took him "twenty miles through the uninhabited woods between Stockbridge and Kaunaumeek; which many times exposed him to extreme hardship in the severe seasons of the winter." Brainerd himself looked back on those months and wrote: "I have been often exposed to cold and hunger in the wilderness, where the comforts of life were not to be had; have frequently been lost in the woods; and sometimes obliged to ride much of the night; and once lay out in the woods all night."

The diary does not describe one famous night of kneeling in the snow. The title of this chapter is a picture of those hard winters, and of what Brainerd did in them. Again and again his entries begin the same way: he went out into the woods to pray. "I love to live alone in my own little cottage," he wrote that November, "where I can spend much time in prayer."

Two years later, in the summer of 1745, he came to a settlement of Lenape (Delaware) people at Crossweeksung, in New Jersey. On 8 August he preached to about sixty-five men, women and children. He wrote that evening:

> "There was much visible concern among them while I was discoursing publicly; but afterward, when I spoke to one and another more particularly, whom I perceived under much concern, the power of God seemed to descend upon the assembly 'like a mighty rushing wind,' and with an astonishing energy bore down all before it."
> David Brainerd, *The Life and Diary of David Brainerd*

Old men and women, and children of six or seven, cried to God for mercy. Brainerd noticed that each one seemed to be praying alone, though they were all in one house: "every one praying apart, although all together."

### Who he was

David Brainerd was born in 1718 in Haddam, Connecticut. His father died when he was nine and his mother when he was about fourteen. In July 1739, walking alone in a quiet place, he found peace with God through Christ. He went to Yale College in the years of the Great Awakening, but in 1742 he was expelled, partly because he had said of one of his tutors that he had "no more grace than this chair." The words were careless and unkind, and he later asked to be forgiven for them. Yale still refused him his degree.

A Scottish missionary society then sent him to the Native peoples of New York, Pennsylvania and New Jersey. Like many settlers of his time, he used harsh words for them in his diary, words that hurt to read today. Yet he loved them, and it grieved him that many settlers, as he wrote, had "no regard to the souls of the poor Indians."

He was also a man of deep sadness. Edwards did not hide it. In his preface he wrote:

> "There is one thing, easily discernible in the life of Brainerd, which by many may be considered an objection to the extraordinary evidences of his religion and devotion, viz. that he was, by his constitution and natural temper, so prone to melancholy and dejection of Spirit."
> Jonathan Edwards, *The Life and Diary of David Brainerd*

(*Melancholy* is an old word for deep and lasting low spirits.) Edwards added, gently, that Brainerd also worked too hard and did not care for his body. He had tuberculosis, a disease of the lungs. In 1747, too ill to work, he came to Edwards's house in Northampton, Massachusetts. Edwards's daughter Jerusha nursed him. He died there on 9 October 1747, aged twenty-nine. Jerusha died a few months later. In 1749 Edwards published Brainerd's life and diary, and it has sent missionaries into all the world ever since.

### How he prayed

Brainerd's diary is full of prayer, much of it for other people. Before he ever became a missionary, on 14 June 1742, he set apart a day for fasting and prayer. Near evening, he wrote:

> "I wrestled for absent friends, for the ingathering of souls, for multitudes of poor souls, and for many that I thought were the children of God, in many distant places. I was in such an agony, from half an hour before sunset, till near dark, that I was all over wet with sweat: but yet it seemed to me that I had wasted away the day, and had done nothing."
> David Brainerd, *The Life and Diary of David Brainerd*

*Ingathering* means bringing in the harvest. He longed to see many people brought to Christ, as a farmer longs to see the crop brought home.

This is what Paul meant when he wrote of "birth pains" in our key verse (Galatians 4:19). A mother cannot make her baby be born by trying harder. But she cannot be calm and careless about it either. Paul loved the Galatians so much that their need for Christ hurt him. Edwards used the same picture of Brainerd: after years of "agonizing in prayer, and travailing in birth for their conversion," he wrote, "at length the day dawns."

Notice too what Brainerd felt afterward: he thought he "had done nothing." Prayer often feels like that. His sad moods often told him that he was useless. But God was not measuring his prayers by his feelings. Brainerd prayed in 1742 for "multitudes" in "distant places." In 1745, in a place he had never planned to go, God answered.

His prayers were also humble. In his last months at Northampton, Edwards noticed that he "generally made it one petition in his prayer, 'that we might not outlive our usefulness.'"

### Three key insights

1. **Longing prayer for others is a kind of birth pain, and God uses it to bring new life.** Paul suffered birth pains for the Galatians, and Brainerd wrestled in prayer for people he had not yet met. We cannot make anyone believe, but love that aches in prayer is never wasted.
2. **A believer who struggles with sadness can still be a mighty intercessor.** Brainerd's moods were often dark, and Edwards said so honestly. Yet God heard his prayers and used his life. Our feelings are not the measure of our usefulness to God.
3. **The answer to long prayer may come at a time and place we never expected.** Brainerd prayed for years with little to show for it. Then, among people he had not planned to visit, the Spirit came "like a mighty rushing wind." Edwards said it came "at a time, in a place, and upon subjects, that scarce ever entered his heart."

### For us today

Many believers today carry a burden of prayer for people who do not yet know Christ: a son who has left the faith, neighbors of another religion, a people group in a distant land. Some pray in places where preaching is forbidden. Some pray for years and see nothing. Brainerd's story says: keep praying. The birth pains are not the end of the story.

His story also speaks to those who struggle with depression. In many churches, in Africa, Asia, Latin America and the West, people still whisper that a sad Christian must have weak faith. Brainerd's life says otherwise. If you carry that heaviness, you are not alone, and you are not useless to God. But learn from his mistakes too. Rest, eat, sleep and accept help, as Brainerd accepted Jerusha's care at the end. Your body is not your enemy. And if someone near you is struggling, be a Jerusha to them.

### Try this

This week, let your love for others become prayer.

- **Name your harvest.** Write down the names of three people who do not yet know Christ, and pray for them by name each day.
- **Find a quiet place.** Brainerd went into the woods. Find your own place: a field, a rooftop, a corner of a room, a walk at dawn.
- **Pray when you feel useless.** On a day when you feel you have done nothing, offer God that feeling, and pray for someone else anyway.
- **Care for a tired servant.** Think of a pastor, missionary or caregiver who is worn out, and give them a meal, a visit or a day of rest.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You poured out Your soul in prayer for us, and You still pray for us at the Father's right hand. Thank You for Your servant David Brainerd, who prayed in the cold woods for people he did not yet know, and for the Lenape believers of Crossweeksung, who cried out to You together. Give me a heart that aches for others until Christ is formed in them. When my spirit is heavy and I feel I have done nothing, hold me fast and teach me to keep praying. Send Your Spirit like a mighty wind on the places I pray for, in Your time and in Your way. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Paul felt "birth pains" for the Galatians. Is there anyone for whom you feel that kind of longing in prayer? If not, whom could you begin to carry?
- Brainerd often felt that he had "done nothing." How do your feelings affect your prayers, and what does his story teach you about them?
- How can your church care better for people who struggle with deep sadness, without telling them that their faith is weak?

## Revive Your Work

> "LORD, I have heard of Your fame, and I stand in awe. LORD, renew Your work in our own time; in our own time make it known; in wrath, remember mercy." (Habakkuk 3:2)

Cambuslang was a small country parish in Scotland, on the south side of the river Clyde, about five miles from Glasgow. In 1742 it had only about nine hundred people. Most were farmers, weavers and tradesmen, and most lived within a mile of the church.

In January of that year two working men went from house to house with a paper. One was Ingram More, a shoemaker. The other was Robert Bowman, a weaver. The paper was a petition to their minister, William McCulloch. About ninety heads of families signed it. They were not asking for lower rents or better roads. They were asking for more of God's word: a weekly lecture, that is, a teaching sermon, on a weekday as well as on Sunday. McCulloch gladly agreed, and Thursday was chosen.

The parish already had small groups that met to pray. A letter written that May, which McCulloch read and signed as true, tells what happened next. On Monday 15 February there was "a general meeting, at the minister's house, of the particular societies for prayer, which had subsisted in the parish for several years before." On Tuesday they met again, joining other Christians elsewhere in "solemn prayer relating to the public interests of the gospel." They found the time so good that they met a third time on Wednesday. Then, the letter adds, they all went home at a sensible hour. The rumors that they had prayed for days and nights without stopping were not true.

On Thursday 18 February McCulloch preached his weekly lecture. Afterwards about fifty people came to his house, deeply troubled about their sins and asking what they must do to be saved. He spent the whole night with them. From then on people came every day. McCulloch preached almost daily, and by May more than three hundred people had been awakened to a deep concern for their souls.

In the summer the famous preacher George Whitefield came. The parish held its communion, the Lord's Supper, on 11 July, and again on 15 August. Crowds gathered on the grassy slope beside the church, a natural bowl in the hillside. At the second communion some said fifty thousand were present. The careful counters said over thirty thousand, and about three thousand received the bread and wine. Whitefield wrote to a friend: "Such a passover has not been heard of. The voice of prayer and praise was heard all night."

### What came before

The story of Cambuslang is often told as the story of Whitefield's preaching. But the work had begun before he came, and it began with prayer.

William McCulloch was born at Whithorn, in Galloway, where his father kept the school. He was a scholar who knew Hebrew well, taught astronomy, and made small globes with his own hands. He became minister of Cambuslang in 1731. He was not a great preacher. His son wrote that he was not "a very ready speaker," and that his manner was "slow and cautious." But his son also wrote: "He greatly encouraged private Christians to meet for social prayer, and particularly that God would revive His work everywhere."

That word *revive* came from the Bible these believers knew. The old English versions read Habakkuk 3:2 as "revive thy work." The Open Word Bible has "renew Your work in our own time." Habakkuk prayed in a dark age, when he knew that judgment was coming on his people. He did not ask God to ignore their sin. He asked Him to remember mercy.

McCulloch later counted the fellowship meetings in his parish: "In 1731, when I came to this parish, there were only three of these; in 1742, they increased to a dozen or more." For years, a few farmers and weavers had met each week in a kitchen or a barn to pray, sing a psalm and talk about their souls. Nobody outside the parish noticed them. Yet these hidden meetings were the soil in which the revival grew.

George Whitefield understood why such meetings matter. In an early sermon on Ecclesiastes 4:9-12, the passage that says two are better than one, he said:

> "It is an observation no less true than common, that kindled coals, if placed asunder, soon go out, but if heaped together, quicken and enliven each other, and afford a lasting heat."
> George Whitefield, *Selected Sermons of George Whitefield*

### Wheat and weeds

Not everything at Cambuslang was good. Many people cried out during the sermons, trembled, fainted or fell down. Some outsiders mocked the whole work. Others, including a strict group of ministers who had left the Church of Scotland, called it a delusion of the devil. Some who seemed to be converted later fell away.

McCulloch was honest about these things. He wrote that hearers should not give way to crying out if they could help it, and that ministers should do nothing on purpose to stir it up. They should preach Christ faithfully and leave the results to God. About nine years later he made a list of about four hundred people, awakened in 1742, whose lives had stayed changed, each one vouched for by people who knew them well. Reports from 1742 already told of drinkers who had become sober, quarrels mended, and money repaid to people who had been cheated. McCulloch told of wives who, when their husbands shouted at them, would "run into another room and engage in prayer," asking God to forgive their husbands and to give themselves patience. And the prayer meetings went on.

McCulloch also refused to take any credit. "The speakers," he wrote, "were but as the rams' horns" at Jericho. The walls fell because God was at work, not because of the trumpets.

Prayer at Cambuslang reached far beyond Scotland. In 1744 a group of Scottish ministers began a "concert of prayer," inviting believers everywhere to pray at the same set times for the spread of the gospel. As we saw in chapter 9, Jonathan Edwards took up the call in New England. In his farewell sermon to his church at Northampton in 1750 he gave this advice:

> "I would therefore advise you not only to be constant in secret and family prayer, and in the public worship of God in His house, but also often to assemble yourselves in private praying societies."
> Jonathan Edwards, *Selected Sermons of Jonathan Edwards*

### Three key insights

1. **Revival is usually born in small, faithful prayer meetings long before anyone notices.** The fellowship meetings of Cambuslang had prayed for years before February 1742. God's great works often have hidden roots in ordinary kitchens and barns.
2. **Hunger for God's word, spoken aloud by ordinary believers, is often where a work of God begins.** A shoemaker and a weaver asked their minister for one more sermon a week. They were hungry for God, and they said so. God answered beyond anything they asked.
3. **The truest sign of revival is not loud feeling but changed lives that last.** McCulloch did not count the people who cried out. He counted those whose lives were still changed years later. And he gave God all the glory: the preachers, he said, were only rams' horns.

### For us today

Many Christians long for revival: in a dry church in Europe, a struggling village congregation in Africa, a city in Asia where few know Christ, a country in Latin America torn by violence. Cambuslang suggests where to begin. Not with a famous preacher or a great event, but with a few people who meet faithfully to pray, and who ask for more of God's word.

You do not need a church building for this. The praying societies of Scotland met in homes and barns. Believers in places where churches are watched or closed often meet in small groups already. Such meetings may feel weak and small. But coals heaped together keep each other burning.

And when God does move, pray for wisdom too. Not every loud thing is from God, and not every quiet thing is dead. Ask Him for lives that are truly changed.

### Try this

This week, take one small step toward praying with others.

- **Keep a small meeting going.** If you belong to a small prayer group, keep meeting even when nothing seems to happen. If you do not, ask whether you may join one.
- **Pray Habakkuk's prayer.** Each day, pray these words for your church and your town: "LORD, renew Your work in our own time" (Habakkuk 3:2).
- **Ask for more of the word.** If your church has a Bible study or prayer meeting, go. If it has none, ask your pastor kindly whether one could begin.
- **Look for lasting fruit.** Thank God for one person you know whose life He has changed, and pray that they will keep going.

### A closing prayer

LORD, we have heard of Your fame and of the great things You have done, and we stand in awe. Thank You for the shoemaker and the weaver of Cambuslang, for their humble minister, and for the small groups who met to pray when no one was watching. Renew Your work in our own time. Begin in me, and in the few who pray with me. Keep our hearts warm together, like coals heaped on one fire. Guard Your work from pride and from what is false, and let the fruit be changed lives that last. In wrath, remember mercy, for we need it too. To You be all the glory, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- The people of Cambuslang asked for more of God's word. What would it look like for you to tell your pastor, or God, that you are hungry for more?
- Why do you think God so often begins His great works in small, hidden prayer meetings?
- Habakkuk prayed, "in wrath, remember mercy" (Habakkuk 3:2). Why is it important to pray for mercy, and not only for power, when we pray for revival?

## Résister

> "We must not tire of doing good. When the right time comes we will bring in the harvest, as long as we do not slacken." (Galatians 6:9)

On the flat salt marshes of southern France, near the Mediterranean Sea, stands the walled town of Aigues-Mortes. At one corner rises a great round tower of stone, built in the 1200s. It is called the Tour de Constance, the Tower of Constance. Its walls are several meters thick. Inside are two round rooms, one above the other, lit only by narrow slits in the stone.

For much of the 1700s the upper room was a prison for women. Their crime was that they were Protestants who had gone to worship God in a forbidden meeting. Almost any of them could have been set free. All a woman had to do was renounce her faith and promise to become a Catholic.

On 25 August 1730 a young woman named Marie Durand was brought into that room. She was about nineteen. (Older books say fifteen, but most historians now give 1711 as the year of her birth.) She did not come out until 14 April 1768. She spent thirty-eight years in the tower.

Visitors to the tower today are shown a word cut into the stone rim of a well in the upper room: RECISTER. It is the French word *résister*, "to resist," spelled with a *c*. The story is often told that Marie carved it herself, perhaps with a knitting needle. No one can be sure; another prisoner may have carved it. But whoever made it, the word speaks for all of them.

Life in the tower was hard. In winter the cold wind and rain came in through the openings. After one bad winter Marie wrote: "The place overflowed with water everywhere; we had no fuel but a little green wood; the snow was on our terrace." Some women grew sick. At least one lost her reason. Some, worn out, gave in and signed the paper to go free. Marie stayed. She wrote letters for women who could not write. She cared for the old and the sick. She read the Scriptures aloud and led the singing of the psalms.

### The church of the desert

To understand Marie's story, we need to go back a little. In 1598 a French king had given Protestants, often called Huguenots, the right to worship. In 1685 King Louis XIV took that right away. Pastors were banished. Protestant churches were pulled down. Many thousands of Huguenots fled to other lands. Those who stayed were ordered to become Catholics.

Many outwardly obeyed. But many kept meeting in secret, in caves, forests and lonely valleys, often at night. They called this "the church of the desert," remembering how God had cared for Israel in the wilderness. Men caught at these meetings could be sent to row in the king's war galleys for life. Pastors could be hanged. Women were sent to prisons like the Tour de Constance.

Marie's family belonged to this hidden church. Her brother Pierre was one of its pastors. In 1729 the authorities arrested her father, Étienne, and put him in a fortress on the coast. A year later they took Marie. Matthieu Serres, an older man she had married shortly before (the records are not clear whether the marriage was complete), was locked up with her father. In 1732 Pierre was caught and hanged at Montpellier.

From his prison Étienne wrote to his daughter. An old English translation of his letter gives these words: "We pray for all, even for our enemies; God give them grace to see the wrong they do to us and to themselves."

It is important to be fair. This persecution was the work of the French crown and the church leaders who supported it in that age. Christians of every church have persecuted others, as we saw in Volume 6, and a few years after Marie's death, in the French Revolution, it was Catholic priests and nuns who were imprisoned and killed for their faith. And Catholics helped to end this persecution. In 1752 a royal inspector, the Marquis de Paulmy, visited the tower. When he asked the women if they had been arrested for meeting, one answered: "we cannot believe the king considers it wrong that we should meet to pray to God." He wept, gave them money, and three times begged them to pray for him.

### Holding on in prayer

What kept Marie going for thirty-eight years? Her letters show a woman who prayed, who loved, and who held on.

The women prayed together. Another prisoner, Isabeau Menet, who had a little son with her in the tower, wrote in 1739: "Let us pray ... that the Lord will be pleased to shorten our sufferings; but we must imitate Jesus Christ, our divine leader." They sang the psalms in French, as Huguenots had done for nearly two hundred years. One story, told by an old historian, says that a prisoner's mother walked many miles, dressed as a beggar, to the foot of the tower. She began to sing a psalm, and voices from inside the tower sang back.

E. M. Bounds, writing about the widow in Jesus' parable who kept coming back to the judge, described this kind of prayer:

> "Importunity is made up of the ability to hold on, to press on, to wait with unrelaxed and unrelaxable grasp, restless desire and restful patience."
> E. M. Bounds, *Purpose in Prayer*

(*Importunity* means asking again and again without giving up.) That is what *résister* meant in the tower. It did not mean anger or violence. It meant refusing to let go of God.

Marie also kept doing good. She helped to raise a fellow prisoner's little girl. She wrote to her niece Anne, the daughter of her brother Pierre: "let the love of God and His fear be the rule of thy conduct." When gifts came from Protestants in England, she signed the receipt for the many women who could not write their names.

At last, in the 1760s, the mood in France began to change. In January 1767 the Prince de Beauvau, a Catholic nobleman who was the king's commander in that province, visited the tower. Moved by what he saw, he set women free, going further than his orders allowed. He wrote: "Justice and humanity pleaded for all the unfortunates alike." He was rebuked, but he did not back down. Marie walked out in April 1768. She went home to her village, poor and in weak health, and shared what little she had with a neighbor who had come back old and broken from the galleys. She died in 1776. Eleven years later, in 1787, French Protestants were at last given legal recognition, including the right to marry.

### Three key insights

1. **Holding on in prayer, day after day, is itself a way of resisting evil.** Marie did not fight her jailers or curse them. She resisted by refusing to let go of God. Faithful, patient prayer is a quiet form of courage.
2. **Doing good to those beside us is one of the ways we keep from growing weary.** Marie cared for the sick, taught a child and wrote letters for her friends. Love for others kept her own heart alive through long years of waiting.
3. **The harvest comes in God's time, and some who sow will see it only from heaven.** Marie was freed after thirty-eight years, but she did not live to see full freedom for her church. Her faithfulness still bore fruit, and God remembered every year of it.

### For us today

Today many believers are in prison for their faith, in parts of Asia, Africa, the Middle East and elsewhere. Some have waited for years. Hebrews tells us to "keep in mind those in prison, as if you were in chains alongside them" (Hebrews 13:3). Marie's story calls us to pray for them by name, to support those who visit and help them, and to speak up for them where we can.

Others face a different kind of tower: a long illness, a hard marriage, a child who has turned away, years of poverty or loneliness. The temptation is to stop praying, to grow tired of doing good. Paul's word is for you: do not slacken. The right time will come. *Résister*: hold on to God.

### Try this

This week, let Marie Durand teach you to hold on.

- **Write it down.** Write *résister*, or "hold on," on a card and put it where you will see it. Each time you see it, turn to God in a short prayer.
- **Pray for a prisoner.** Learn the name of one Christian in prison for their faith today, and pray for them every day this week.
- **Do one good thing while you wait.** Whatever you are waiting for, do one act of kindness each day for someone near you.
- **Sing a psalm.** Read or sing Psalm 46 or Psalm 121 aloud, as the women in the tower sang the psalms together.

### A closing prayer

Faithful God, You never grow tired of doing good to us. Thank You for Marie Durand and the women of the Tower of Constance, who prayed and sang and held on to You through long, cold years. Remember all who are in prison for Your name today. Give them courage, comfort and friends, and bring them out in Your time. When I grow weary of waiting and of doing good, hold me fast. Teach me to resist evil not with hatred but with patient prayer and love. And let me see the harvest You have promised, here or in heaven, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What do you think *résister* meant to the women in the tower? What would it mean for you to "resist" in your own situation?
- Marie kept doing good to others while she waited. How might serving others help you when you feel tired of praying?
- How could you or your church keep in mind those who are in prison for their faith today?

- Samuel Rutherford, *Letters of Samuel Rutherford*, especially "Letter LXX: To the Right Honourable and Christian Lady, my Lady Viscountess of Kenmure"

## Ask Large Petitions

> "Now to the one who is able to do all things, far beyond anything we ask or think, by the power that is at work in us, to Him be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus, to every generation, for ever and ever. Amen." (Ephesians 3:20-21)

Olney was a small market town in the English countryside, on the river Ouse in Buckinghamshire. Many of its people were poor. Many women made lace by hand, working long hours for very little money. In 1764 the parish church received a new curate, an assistant minister who did most of the work of the parish. His name was John Newton, and he had once been the captain of a slave ship.

Newton led a weekly prayer meeting, where ordinary working people sang, read the Bible and prayed together. Soon the room was too small. So in 1769 the meeting moved to a large house in the town owned by Lord Dartmouth, a friend of Newton's. People called it the Great House.

For the first meetings in the new place, Newton and his friend, the poet William Cowper, each wrote a hymn. Both are printed in the *Olney Hymns* under the heading "On Opening a Place for Social Prayer," that is, a place for praying together. Newton's hymn prayed: "As thou hast given a place for pray'r, So give us hearts to pray." Cowper's began: "Jesus, where'er thy people meet, There they behold thy mercy-seat." Thinking of the bigger room, he borrowed a picture from Isaiah 54:2, of a tent stretched wider: "Come thou, and fill this wider space ... Lord, we are few, but thou art near."

Among the hymns Newton wrote for his people is one that begins with the story of King Solomon. God appeared to him in a dream and said, "Ask for whatever you want me to give you" (1 Kings 3:5). Newton turned that invitation into a song for anyone about to pray. It begins:

"Come, my soul, thy suit prepare, Jesus loves to answer prayer; He himself has bid thee pray, Therefore will not say thee nay. Thou art coming to a King, Large petitions with thee bring; For his grace and pow'r are such, None can ever ask too much."

A *suit* or a *petition* is a formal request to a king. Newton was telling his people: you are not begging at a back door. You are coming before a great and generous King.

### Who he was

John Newton was born in London in 1725. His mother, a godly woman, taught him Bible verses and hymns, but she died before he was seven. At eleven he went to sea with his father, a ship's captain. As a young man he was forced into the navy, flogged for trying to desert, and then traded onto a ship that carried enslaved Africans. For about a year and a half he lived as the servant of a slave trader on an island off the coast of West Africa. He was so cruelly treated that he later wrote that he had been "in effect, though without the name, a Captive and a Slave myself."

In March 1748, sailing home on a ship called the *Greyhound*, he was caught in a terrible storm. As the ship filled with water, he began to pray, perhaps for the first time in years. He marked that day for the rest of his life as the day he turned to God.

But we must be honest. After this turning, Newton went on working in the slave trade for six more years, as a mate and then as a captain. Like many Christians of his time, he did not yet see how great an evil it was. He left the sea in 1754 because of illness, and in 1764 was ordained in the Church of England. He served Olney for sixteen years and then a church in London until his death in 1807.

### How they prayed

Many of the *Olney Hymns* were written for this praying, singing congregation. Newton said in his preface that hymns should be written "for the use of plain people," and he hoped his would be useful "to the weak and the poor of his flock," meaning the Lord's flock.

Cowper was meant to write half the hymns. But in 1773 a deep and terrible darkness of mind came upon him again, and he could write no more. For more than a year he lived in Newton's own house, and Newton cared for him. In the preface Newton spoke only of his friend's "long and affecting indisposition." Of the 348 hymns in the book, Cowper wrote 68. The prayer meeting went on.

"Thou art coming to a King, Large petitions with thee bring" became one of the best-loved lines on prayer in the English language. Nearly a century later Charles Spurgeon quoted it in a sermon and explained it like this:

> "We do not come, as it were, in prayer, only to God's almonry where He dispenses His favours to the poor, nor do we come to the back-door of the house of mercy to receive the broken scraps, though that were more than we deserve; to eat the crumbs that fall from the Master's table is more than we could claim; but, when we pray, we are standing in the palace, on the glittering floor of the great King's own reception room, and thus we are placed upon a vantage ground."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Mighty Power in Prayer*

(An *almonry* was a place where gifts were handed out to the poor.) Paul says the same in our key verse. God is able to do "far beyond anything we ask or think" (Ephesians 3:20).

But notice what Newton asked for in the rest of his hymn. The very next verse begins: "With my burden I begin, Lord, remove this load of sin!" Then he asks for rest, for guidance, for strength and for Christ's likeness. The largest petitions are not for money or fame. They are for grace.

### A late but large petition

As Newton grew older, he saw more and more clearly the wickedness of the trade he had once served. In 1788 he published a pamphlet, *Thoughts upon the African Slave Trade*. He wrote that silence, at such a time, "would, in me, be criminal," and he confessed: "I hope it will always be a subject of humiliating reflection to me, that I was, once, an active instrument, in a business at which my heart now shudders."

He described what he had seen on the ships, and supported William Wilberforce, who led the campaign in Parliament. Many others worked and prayed for the same cause, including Africans who had themselves been enslaved, such as Olaudah Equiano, whose life story was published in 1789. In 1807, a few months before Newton died, Parliament ended the British slave trade.

### Three key insights

1. **Because we come to a generous King, we may bring Him large requests.** God is able to do far beyond anything we ask or think. Small, timid prayers do not honor Him. We may ask boldly because of who He is, not because of who we are.
2. **The largest petitions are for grace: forgiveness, a changed heart and the likeness of Christ.** Newton's hymn begins with the burden of sin. Before we ask God for great things in the world, we ask Him to do great things in us.
3. **Prayer that draws us near to God will in time open our eyes to wrongs we once accepted.** Newton prayed for years before he saw the full evil of the slave trade. When he saw it, he confessed it publicly and worked to end it. Honest prayer leads to honest repentance.

### For us today

Some believers think large prayers are for great churches and famous preachers. Olney was a poor town of lace makers and laborers. Yet from that small room came hymns that are now sung in many languages, from Korea to Kenya, from Brazil to the Philippines.

Your small group can ask large things too: for your neighbors to know Christ, for an end to violence in your city, for justice for workers who are cheated, for the persecuted church, for the nations. And ask God to show you where you and your people may be blind, as Newton was. Millions of people are still trapped in forced labor and trafficking today. Pray large prayers for them, and then do what you can.

### Try this

This week, practice asking largely.

- **Sing before you pray.** Read or sing the first two verses of "Come, my soul, thy suit prepare" before your prayer time, and remember whose presence you are entering.
- **Make one large request.** Write down one request that seems too big to ask, for your family, your town or your nation, and bring it to God every day.
- **Begin with your burden.** Like Newton, start your prayer by asking God to remove your sin and change your heart.
- **Ask God to open your eyes.** Pray, "Lord, show me any wrong I have accepted without seeing it," and listen.

### A closing prayer

Great King and loving Father, You are able to do far beyond anything we ask or think. Thank You for John Newton, the slave trader You made a pastor, and for the plain people of Olney who met to pray. Forgive my small and timid prayers. With my burden I begin: remove the load of my sin, and make me like Christ. Then teach me to bring You large petitions for my family, my town and the nations. Open my eyes to wrongs I have accepted, and give me courage to turn from them. Set free all who are trapped and enslaved today. To You be glory in the church and in Christ Jesus, for ever and ever. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Newton pictured prayer as coming to a King. How does that picture change the way you ask, and what you ask for?
- What is the largest thing you have ever asked God for? Is there something larger you have been afraid to ask?
- Newton was slow to see the evil of the slave trade. What wrongs might Christians in your place and time be slow to see today?

## Pulled from Their Knees

> "There is no Jew or Greek, there is no slave or free person, there is no male and female, for all of you are one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28)

It was a Sunday morning in Philadelphia, at St George's Methodist Church on Fourth Street. Black and white Methodists worshiped there together. The Black members had given money toward the church's new gallery, an upper floor around the inside walls, and toward laying new floors.

As more Black worshipers came, the leaders grew uneasy and moved them from their usual seats to places around the walls. Then, on this Sunday, the sexton (the man who kept the building) met them at the door and told them to go up to the gallery. Richard Allen, a preacher who had once been a slave, tells what happened next:

> "We expected to take the seats over the ones we formerly occupied below, not knowing any better. We took those seats. Meeting had begun, and they were nearly done singing, and just as we got to the seats, the elder said, 'let us pray.' We had not been long upon our knees before I heard considerable scuffling and low talking."
> Richard Allen, *The Life, Experience, and Gospel Labours*

Allen raised his head. One of the trustees, the men who managed the church's property, had hold of his friend Absalom Jones and was "pulling him up off of his knees." "You must get up," the trustee said, "you must not kneel here." Jones did not fight. He asked only to finish praying: "wait until prayer is over, and I will get up and trouble you no more." The trustee refused and called another trustee, who went to pull up a third man, William White.

Allen writes: "By this time prayer was over, and we all went out of the church in a body, and they were no more plagued with us in the church."

We have this story in Allen's own account of his life, published in 1833, two years after his death. He gives no date. It was long placed in 1787; many historians now think it happened around 1792, when the gallery was built. The date is still debated.

### Who they were

Absalom Jones was born into slavery in Delaware in 1746. At sixteen he was brought to Philadelphia, where he worked in his owner's shop and was allowed to attend a night school run by the Quaker teacher Anthony Benezet. First he bought the freedom of his wife, Mary, so that their children would be born free. Then, in 1784, he gained his own freedom.

Richard Allen was born into slavery in Philadelphia in 1760 and grew up on a farm in Delaware. He came to faith through Methodist preachers, bought his freedom, and became a traveling preacher. In 1786 he came back to Philadelphia, preached at St George's, sometimes at five o'clock in the morning, and started prayer meetings among the city's Black people. In 1787 he and Jones founded the Free African Society, in which free Black people paid small amounts each month to help one another in sickness and to care for widows and orphans.

After the day at St George's, the two friends went different ways, but in peace. Most of the group joined the Episcopal Church and in July 1794 opened St Thomas's African Episcopal Church, with Jones as pastor. In 1802 he became the first Black priest of the Episcopal Church in America. Allen would not leave the Methodists, who had first brought him the gospel. He bought an old blacksmith's shop, hauled it to a lot he owned, and made it a church. It opened in July 1794, and it was named Bethel, "house of God," after the place where Jacob said, "This is God's own house, and here is the doorway into heaven" (Genesis 28:17).

For years white Methodist leaders tried to take control of Bethel. In 1816 the courts ruled for the congregation, and that same year Black Methodist churches from several cities formed the African Methodist Episcopal Church, with Allen as its first bishop. Mother Bethel still stands on the same ground, and the AME Church now has members on several continents.

### How they prayed

The trustees did not stop these men in the street. They stopped them in church, on their knees, in the middle of prayer, the one place where every believer should be welcome. Paul wrote that in Christ "there is no slave or free person ... for all of you are one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). James warned churches not to say to one person, "Take this good seat here," and to another, "Sit on the floor down by my footstool" (James 2:3). When a Methodist elder later threatened to put them out of the church, they answered him:

> "We told him we had no place of worship; and we did not mean to go to St. George's church any more, as we were so scandalously treated in the presence of all the congregation present; 'and if you deny us your name, you cannot seal up the scriptures from us, and deny us a name in heaven. We believe heaven is free for all who worship in spirit and truth.'"
> Richard Allen, *The Life, Experience, and Gospel Labours*

Their answer to injustice was prayer joined to action. On the day they began to dig the cellar for the new church, Allen writes, "I arose early in the morning and addressed the throne of grace, praying that the Lord would bless our endeavours."

In the late summer of 1793 yellow fever swept through Philadelphia. Thousands died, and all who could afford it fled. Doctors and city leaders asked Black Philadelphians for help, partly because people wrongly believed they could not catch the disease. Jones and Allen met to decide what to do, put their trust in God and agreed to go. In a pamphlet they wrote together in 1794 they said: "The Lord was pleased to strengthen us, and remove all fear from us, and disposed our hearts to be as useful as possible." For weeks they nursed the sick, buried the dead and gathered up orphaned children. They were not protected from the fever, and many Black helpers caught it.

When a printer named Mathew Carey (no relation of the William Carey of chapter 17) accused Black nurses of overcharging the sick, Jones and Allen answered him firmly, with facts. But in the same pamphlet they printed an address to slaveholders. It reminded them that Jesus commands us to love our enemies, and then it said:

> "I feel the obligations, I wish to impress them on the minds of our colored brethren, and that we may all forgive you, as we wish to be forgiven, we think it a great mercy to have all anger and bitterness removed from our minds; I appeal to your own feelings, if it is not very disquiting to feel yourselves under dominion of wrathful disposition."
> Richard Allen, *The Life, Experience, and Gospel Labours*

They asked God to remove their anger as a "great mercy" to themselves. That is not weakness. It is a heart set free from hatred.

### Three key insights

1. **Every believer has the same right to kneel before God, because all are one in Christ Jesus.** The trustees tried to decide who could pray where. But God had already decided. The ground at the foot of the cross is level, and no one may push another off it.
2. **Prayer can give the wronged the courage to act, not only the strength to endure.** Jones and Allen did not stay where they were not wanted. They prayed, walked out together, and built two churches. In the fever, prayer took away their fear so that they could serve.
3. **Forgiving those who wronged us does not mean pretending the wrong was small.** Allen named the injustice plainly and answered false charges with the truth. Yet he asked God to take all bitterness from his heart, and offered forgiveness to those who held his people in chains.

### For us today

Christians have treated other Christians this way in many lands, keeping them apart by race, caste, tribe or class, even at the Lord's Table. Some readers have been the ones pushed aside. Others belong to churches that did the pushing. The story of St George's asks all of us to look honestly at our own congregations. Who is quietly sent upstairs?

For those who have been wronged, Allen and Jones show a way between bitterness and silence. In Johannesburg or São Paulo, in Indian villages where Dalit believers are still kept apart, in a city church where migrants feel unwanted, believers can name the wrong, pray, and build something better without letting hatred rule their hearts. And the yellow fever teaches us all: the people a city despised were the ones who stayed to care for it.

### Try this

This week, let Allen and Jones teach you to pray for a church where all are one.

- **Pray Galatians 3:28.** Read it slowly each morning, and thank God for believers of other races, tribes and nations whom He has made your brothers and sisters.
- **Look around your church.** Ask God to show you who is left out or kept at the edges, and then go and sit with them.
- **Bring your anger to God.** If you have been wronged, tell God plainly what happened. Then ask Him, as Allen did, for the "great mercy" of a heart set free from bitterness.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, You welcome every sinner who comes to You, and You make us one family in Yourself. Thank You for Absalom Jones, who was pulled from his knees, and Richard Allen, who walked out beside him, and for how they still kept praying, who built houses of prayer and served a city that despised them. Forgive Your church for every time we have pushed others aside, even at prayer. When I am wronged, give me courage to speak the truth and grace to forgive. Take away my fear, so that I can serve the sick and the forgotten. Make Your church one, so that the world may believe. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Absalom Jones asked only to finish his prayer. Why do you think the trustees would not wait? What does that tell us about prejudice?
- How did Allen and Jones answer injustice without becoming bitter? Which part of their answer is hardest for you?
- Are there people who are kept at the edges of your church or community? What could you do this week to change that?

## All the Families of the Nations

> "People at the farthest ends of the earth will remember and come back to the LORD; every clan of every nation will bow low before You." (Psalm 22:27)

In July 1787 an old Mohegan minister named Samson Occom was traveling in the forests of central New York. He was about sixty-four. He had ridden and walked for weeks from his home in Connecticut to visit two small Native towns built on land given by the Oneida nation. One was Brothertown, where Christian families from several Native peoples of southern New England and Long Island had settled. The other was New Stockbridge, the new home of the Stockbridge people, who had moved there from Massachusetts.

At New Stockbridge he stayed with a leader named Captain Hendrick. On Sunday, 15 July, Occom preached twice, from "the only thing I would know was Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 2:2) and from Jesus' words to a weeping woman, "Your sins stand forgiven" (Luke 7:48). He preached in English. So just before sunset the people met again, and Captain Hendrick and another man repeated the sermons in their own language, as Occom wrote, because "many old People coud not readyly understand what I deliver'd in the English."

The next night, after everyone had gone to bed, someone knocked at the door. It was a widow named Quenney. A woman of the town, Cathrine Quenney, whom Occom calls Catey, had suddenly been taken ill. She could hardly breathe. Occom got up, dressed and went to her.

He sat down by her bed and asked if she was sick. She said no. "what then is the matter with you," he asked. Occom wrote down her answer: "with Tears, I want to Love God more, and serve him better." He told her that "if She really Desired and asked for it she shu'd have her desire granted, for it was a good Desire." Then she asked him to pray with her. He asked her what they should pray for. She said, "that she may have more Faith, that she might serve with her whole Heart." So they prayed together, and Occom went back to bed.

In the morning he went to see her again. She told him that she had slept very little and her body felt weak, but she "found more love and Peace." Once more she asked him to pray, this time for "Wisdom and more Faith."

We know this small scene because Occom kept a diary on his journeys. The volume for these years is held by the Connecticut Historical Society, and it was printed, with his own spelling, by the historian W. DeLoss Love in 1899.

### Who he was

Samson Occom was born in 1723 at Mohegan, near New London, Connecticut. In about 1740, when he was sixteen, the preachers of the Great Awakening came to his people. At seventeen he put his trust in Christ. The story of his youth is told in *They Were Young*, Book 1. In a short account of his life that he wrote in 1768, he remembered what he longed for as a new believer: "I used to wish I was Capable of Instructing my poor Kindred."

That wish shaped his whole life. He studied for four years with the minister Eleazar Wheelock, until his eyes failed. For about twelve years he was teacher, pastor and judge among the Montaukett people of Long Island, and he married a Montaukett woman, Mary Fowler. In 1759 he was ordained, one of the first Native Americans to become a minister in the English colonies. From 1766 to 1768 he preached across England and Scotland, perhaps three hundred times or more, and raised over twelve thousand pounds for Wheelock's school for Native students. But Wheelock used much of the money to found Dartmouth College, which educated mostly white young men. Occom felt betrayed, and he was right to.

He had other sorrows too. He was paid far less than white missionaries, and he was often poor. He saw his people lose land again and again to the colonists. Yet he did not give up on the gospel, or on his people. After the American Revolution he helped lead Christian families from several Native communities to their new home on Oneida land. In November 1785 they formed their town, which they called Brothertown, and which Occom wrote in their language as Eeyamquittoowauconnuck. He served them and the Stockbridge people as pastor until he died in July 1792.

### How he prayed

The Stockbridge people whom Occom visited in 1787 were not strangers to the gospel. In chapter 9 we met Jonathan Edwards, who called the church to a "concert of prayer." In 1751 Edwards had gone to the frontier town of Stockbridge, Massachusetts, as pastor and missionary to these same people. Now, a generation later, a Mohegan minister was preaching to them, and their own leaders were teaching his sermons and leading the prayers in their own tongue. On one evening that week, Occom notes, Captain Hendrick "made the last Prayer."

This is what Psalm 22 promised. The psalm begins with the cry Jesus prayed on the cross: "My God, my God, why have You abandoned me?" (Psalm 22:1). But it ends with a great turning. "Every clan of every nation will bow low before You" (Psalm 22:27). The Hebrew word for "clan" means a family or kindred, which is why some older English versions say "families" or "kindreds." God does not only save nations in general. He gathers families, clans and peoples, each with its own name and its own speech.

Occom knew that the English often confused the gospel with their own customs, and that many colonists despised Native people even while they prayed. But he never thought that Christ belonged to the English. He wanted his "Kindred" to worship God as Mohegans, Montauketts, Stockbridges and Oneidas, in towns they governed themselves. A small hymn for the morning that has long been credited to him (though no one can be sure he wrote it) begins: "Now the shades of night are gone, / Now the morning light is come: / Lord, we would be thine to-day."

His prayer with Catey shows the heart of his ministry. She did not ask to be healed or to be rich. She wanted to love God more. Occom did not make her wait or prove herself. He told her that God gives such a good desire to those who ask, and then he simply prayed with her. Like the disciples who said to Jesus, "Give us more faith!" (Luke 17:5), she asked for the one thing she most needed.

E. M. Bounds, the American Methodist writer, said that true prayer must be as wide as the human race:

> "In our praying hours, all men must have a place. The wants and woes of the entire race are to broaden and make tender our sympathies, and inflame our petitions."
> E. M. Bounds, *The Essentials of Prayer*

### Three key insights

1. **God's promise reaches every clan of every nation, so every people may pray to Him in its own tongue.** At New Stockbridge, Occom preached in English, but the people heard and prayed in their own language. The God of Psalm 22 is not the God of one nation only.
2. **A longing to love God more is a prayer He is glad to answer.** Catey Quenney could not explain her distress except to say that she wanted to love God more. Occom treated that longing as a gift from God, and they asked together for more faith.
3. **Those who are let down by God's people can still trust God and keep serving.** Occom was underpaid, betrayed by his teacher and grieved by the loss of his people's land. Yet he went on preaching and praying among the people he loved until the end of his life.

### For us today

Many Indigenous believers today, in the Americas, in Australia, in the Arctic and in the hill country of Asia, carry a painful memory. The gospel often came to their peoples together with the loss of land, language and freedom. Some have been told, openly or quietly, that to follow Christ they must stop being who they are. Occom's life says otherwise. He was a Mohegan and a minister of the gospel, and he saw no conflict between the two.

His story also speaks to every church that has sent missionaries or received them. Those who bring the gospel must never despise those who receive it, or use them for their own gain. And those who have been wronged by Christians can still find Christ Himself trustworthy. Wherever you live, look for the believers in your land whose language is not the language of power. Pray with them and for them, and learn from them.

### Try this

This week, let Psalm 22 widen your prayers.

- **Pray with someone of another people.** Find a believer near you from another tribe, nation or language, and pray together, each in your own tongue.
- **Ask for more love.** Pray Catey's prayer as your own: "Lord, I want to love You more and serve You better. Give me more faith."
- **Pray in your mother tongue.** If you usually pray in a second language, pray once a day in the language of your childhood, and thank God that He understands it perfectly.
- **Greet the morning with God.** Begin each day by giving it to Him, in the words of the old morning hymn: "Lord, we would be thine to-day."

### A closing prayer

Lord God, the whole earth is Yours, and every clan of every nation will bow before You. Thank You for Samson Occom, who loved his people and carried the gospel to them through poverty and betrayal. Thank You for Catey Quenney, who wanted to love You more. Forgive Your church for every time we have despised others or used them for our own gain. Give me a heart wide enough to pray for every people, and humble enough to learn from them. I want to love You more and serve You better: give me more faith and wisdom, through Jesus Christ, who died for all the families of the earth. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Occom longed to teach his "poor Kindred." Which people do you most long to see come to Christ?
- Catey Quenney asked for more faith, not for health or comfort. What would you ask for if you could ask for one thing?
- How can Christians share the gospel without asking people to give up their language and culture?

## The Prayer Call of 1784

> "Make room in your tent; let the curtains of your dwellings be spread wide, and hold nothing back. Let out your tent ropes and drive your pegs in firmly." (Isaiah 54:2)

In April 1784 a parcel of books arrived in the English Midlands from Edinburgh. It had been sent by John Erskine, a Scottish minister who loved to share good books with friends, to one of the circle around a young Baptist pastor named Andrew Fuller. Among the books was a small work by Jonathan Edwards, written in America nearly forty years before. Its long title began *An Humble Attempt*, and it called Christians everywhere to agree together in "extraordinary prayer" for the revival of religion and the spread of Christ's kingdom. In chapter 9 we saw Edwards write it.

Fuller and his friends read it, and it moved them deeply. In early June the Baptist churches of the Northamptonshire Association held their yearly meeting at Nottingham. One of the ministers, John Sutcliff of Olney, made a proposal. Let the churches set apart one hour on the first Monday evening of every month, he said, to pray together. The ministers agreed, and, according to Carey's biographer George Smith, John Ryland wrote the invitation that went out to all their churches. A Baptist history of 1842 gives its purpose: "for extraordinary prayer for the revival of religion, and for the extending of Christ's kingdom in the world."

The invitation asked the churches not to think only of themselves. As Smith reprints it, it said: "Let the whole interest of the Redeemer be affectionately remembered." Then, with special emphasis, it added: "Let the spread of the Gospel to the most distant parts of the habitable globe be the object of your most fervent requests." It even invited Christians of other churches "most cordially to join heart and hand in the attempt."

Fuller's diary for that summer lets us see the prayer call taking hold of one man's heart. On 9 July he read part of Edwards's book to his church at Kettering "to excite them to the like practice." On 12 July he read a poem about the cruelty of the English in India, who had caused famines there. His heart burned with longing for a kingdom of justice, and he wrote: "Lord Jesus, set up thy glorious, peaceful kingdom all over the world!" The next day he wrote: "Spent this day in fasting and prayer, in conjunction with several of my brethren in the ministry, for the revival of our churches and the spread of the gospel."

None of them knew that within ten years one of their own number would be in India.

### Who they were

These men were Particular Baptists, so called because they believed in "particular redemption": that Christ died to save a particular people, those whom God had chosen. Their churches were small and mostly poor, made up of farmers, weavers, shoemakers and servants in the villages and market towns of central England. Many of their pastors had to work at a trade to feed their families.

For years many of these churches had been cold. Some of their preachers taught that because God chooses whom He will save, ministers should not urge sinners to repent and believe. They feared that a warm invitation to everyone might take glory from God. Fuller came to see that the Bible itself invites all people to come to Christ. In 1785 he published *The Gospel Worthy of All Acceptation*, arguing that the gospel is worthy to be received by everyone, and that every hearer should be urged to believe it.

Andrew Fuller, born in 1754, was a farmer's son from the fen country of Cambridgeshire, and pastor at Kettering. John Sutcliff, born in 1752 in Yorkshire, had been pastor at Olney since 1775. This was the same small town where John Newton had led the prayer meeting we met in chapter 13. John Ryland, born in 1753, was a pastor in Northampton. In 1783 he had baptized a young shoemaker named William Carey in the river there. Sutcliff was known for his gentleness and good judgment, Fuller for his strength and boldness. Together they would be the first "rope holders" of a new mission.

### How they prayed

Their plan was simple. Once a month, on a Monday evening, believers met in chapels and homes for one hour to pray. The hour cost no money and needed no building. A farm worker could come after a day in the fields. And because the meetings were held on the same evening in many places, each little group knew that others were praying with it.

They prayed first for their own churches, that God would pour out His Spirit, wake up sleeping believers and bring sinners to faith. But the call stretched their prayers much wider: to "the whole interest of the Redeemer," and to "the most distant parts of the habitable globe." Isaiah's words fit them well: "Make room in your tent; let the curtains of your dwellings be spread wide" (Isaiah 54:2). Month by month, the prayer meetings were stretching the tent of their love.

For a long time little seemed to change. But the habit spread to other groups of churches. In 1789 Sutcliff reprinted Edwards's book so that more could read it. And the prayers began to change the people who prayed them. In 1792 William Carey published a book on the duty of taking the gospel to the nations. In it he looked back over the eight years of prayer: "I trust our monthly prayer-meetings for the success of the gospel have not been in vain." He saw them as the start of something larger, and he wrote: "Many can do nothing but pray, and prayer is perhaps the only thing in which Christians of all denominations can cordially, and unreservedly unite."

A century later the American teacher R. A. Torrey said the same about praying together:

> "Of course there is power in the prayer of an individual, but there is vastly increased power in united prayer. God delights in the unity of His people, and seeks to emphasize it in every way, and so He pronounces a special blessing upon united prayer."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Pray*

The answer came in 1792. At the end of May the ministers met again in Nottingham, the town where the prayer call had begun, and Carey preached the sermon we will hear in the next chapter. On 2 October 1792, in the back parlor of a widow's house in Kettering, about a dozen ministers formed a society to send the gospel overseas. Together they promised just over thirteen pounds. It became the Baptist Missionary Society, and the next year Carey sailed for India.

Fuller later described the moment with a picture. India, he said, was like a deep gold mine: "Who will venture to explore it?" As he told it, Carey answered that he would go down, but that his friends "must hold the ropes." For the rest of their lives Fuller, Sutcliff and Ryland held them, with letters, money, and above all with prayer.

### Three key insights

1. **Those who stay at home hold the ropes for those who go, and prayer is the strongest rope.** When the answer to eight years of prayer came, it came from inside their own circle. Carey went down into the mine, and Fuller, Sutcliff and Ryland held the ropes with letters, money and, above all, prayer.
2. **Prayer grows wider when we remember the whole church and the whole world, not only our own congregation.** The prayer call asked poor village churches to pray for "the most distant parts of the habitable globe." As they prayed for the world, God made room in their hearts for it.
3. **Praying together across churches is something every believer can do, whatever else divides us.** The Baptists invited other Christians to join them. Carey saw that many can do nothing but pray, and that in prayer believers of every church can be one.

### For us today

Few of us will ever preach a famous sermon or sail to another country. But almost anyone can keep a monthly hour of prayer. A small church in a village in Malawi, a house church in a crowded city in China, a group of mothers in a Lima neighborhood, or students in a dormitory in Manila can choose one evening a month to pray together for their own people and for the nations.

The story also warns us not to give up when nothing seems to happen. These believers prayed for eight years before they saw the answer. They did not know what God was preparing. Neither do we. Some prayers we offer may be answered after we are gone, in lands we never see.

And it reminds us that those who stay at home have a real part in the work. Not everyone can go down into the mine. But everyone can help hold the ropes.

### Try this

This week, begin a small prayer habit that can last for years.

- **Mark the first Monday.** Pick one evening each month, such as the first Monday, and set it apart for an hour of prayer for the spread of the gospel.
- **Pray with another church.** Ask a believer from a different church, or a few neighbors, to share your monthly hour of prayer.
- **Pray from near to far.** Begin with your own family and church, then your town, then your nation, and then one far country.
- **Hold someone's rope.** Choose one missionary or Christian worker, write to them, and pray for them by name each week.

### A closing prayer

Lord of the harvest, You promised that Your people would spread out to the right and to the left. Thank You for the poor village churches of England who met month after month to pray for Your kingdom, and for John Sutcliff, Andrew Fuller and John Ryland, who held the ropes for others. Make room in my heart for Your whole church and Your whole world. Keep me faithful in small and hidden prayers, even when I see no answer. Unite Your people of every church in prayer, and send out workers into Your harvest, until the gospel reaches the farthest ends of the earth, through Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- The prayer call began with one hour a month. What small habit of prayer could you keep faithfully for years?
- Why do you think praying for "the most distant parts" of the world changed the people who prayed?
- Who are the people "holding the ropes" for you, or for Christian workers you know? How could you help hold them?

## Expect Great Things

> "For you will burst out to the right and to the left; your descendants will take over the lands of nations and fill ruined cities with people again." (Isaiah 54:3)

On 30 May 1792, the Baptist ministers of the Northamptonshire Association met again in Nottingham. It was the same town where, eight years before, they had agreed to pray together on the first Monday of every month for the spread of the gospel. This time the preacher was a thirty-year-old pastor from Leicester named William Carey. He had once been a shoemaker. A few weeks earlier he had published a small book that asked whether Christians were still bound to take the gospel to every nation.

He chose his text from Isaiah 54, where God speaks to His people as to a woman who has been lonely and childless: "Make room in your tent; let the curtains of your dwellings be spread wide, and hold nothing back ... For you will burst out to the right and to the left" (Isaiah 54:2-3). God was about to give her more children than she could imagine. She must get ready for them.

The sermon was never printed, and no one wrote it down as it was preached. Our earliest account comes from John Ryland, who heard it. He wrote that Carey was "chiefly endeavouring to enforce our obligations to expect great things from God, and to attempt great things for God." Ryland was deeply moved. If all the people had wept aloud, he said, "as the children of Israel did at Bochim," the place in the book of Judges where God's people wept over their sin, "I should not have wondered at the effect."

Yet the next morning the ministers were ready to go home with nothing decided. The story is told that Carey took hold of Andrew Fuller's arm and cried, "And are you, after all, going again to do nothing?" They agreed to bring a plan to their next meeting. In October 1792, as we saw in the last chapter, the Baptist Missionary Society was born, and Carey offered to go himself.

Today his words are usually quoted as one saying: "Expect great things from God; attempt great things for God." But we know them only from later reports, and these differ. Some historians think Carey's own headings were shorter, without "from God" and "for God." The exact words are still debated. The meaning is not.

### Who he was

William Carey was born in 1761 in the village of Paulerspury, in Northamptonshire. He left school early and became a shoemaker. He came to faith through a fellow apprentice, taught himself several languages at his bench, and became the pastor of a tiny Baptist church. The story of his youth is told in *They Were Young*, Book 4. Reading about the voyages of Captain Cook, he began to think about the millions who had never heard of Christ. In 1792 he put his thoughts into *An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens*. ("Heathens" was the word then used for people who did not know the true God.) Many in his day said that if God wanted to save the far nations, He would do it in His own time, without them. Carey answered:

> "What openings of Providence do we wait for? We can neither expect to be transported into the heathen world without ordinary means, nor to be endowed with the gift of tongues, &c., when we arrive there. These would not be Providential interpositions, but miraculous ones."
> William Carey, quoted in John R. Mott, *The Evangelization of the World in This Generation*

The *Enquiry* called first of all for "fervent and united prayer." But it also said that prayer is not enough on its own: "We must not be contented however with praying, without exerting ourselves in the use of means for the obtaining of those things we pray for."

### How he prayed

Carey sailed for India in June 1793. It cost him dearly, and it cost his wife Dorothy even more. She had three small sons and was expecting another child. She had never been far from her village, and at first she refused to go. While his ship waited on the south coast of England, Carey wrote to her: "If I had all the world, I would freely give it all to have you and my dear children with me; but the sense of duty is so strong as to overpower all other considerations; I could not turn back without guilt on my soul." He added, "Tell my dear children I love them dearly, and pray for them constantly." At the last moment Dorothy agreed to come, with her newborn baby and her sister Kitty.

In India the family was poor and often ill. In 1794 their son Peter, aged five, died of dysentery. Dorothy's mind gave way under her grief and fear, and she never recovered. She lived in great suffering until her death in 1807. We know her mostly through the words of others, and some of them were not kind. She deserves to be remembered with tenderness, as one who paid a heavy price for a call she did not choose.

Carey's journal from these years is honest. On some evenings, he wrote, he was "much drawn out in prayer for my dear friends at Leicester, and for the Society that it may be prosperous; likewise for the ministers of my acquaintance, not only of the Baptist but other denominations." On other days prayer was hard: "I sometimes walk in my garden, and try to pray to God; and if I pray at all it is in the solitude of a walk."

In 1800 he moved to Serampore, a small Danish town near Calcutta, and joined two new colleagues, William Ward and Joshua Marshman. They did not forget the prayer call of 1784. "We meet two hours before breakfast on the first Monday in the month," Ward wrote, to pray for the people of Bengal. Carey had worked in India for seven years without seeing one Bengali come to faith. Then, on the last Sunday of 1800, a carpenter named Krishna Pal was baptized in the river. Over the next thirty years Carey and his Indian and English colleagues translated the whole Bible into several languages of India, and parts of it into many more.

He died at Serampore in June 1834. In his last weeks, remembering the many churches that had grown from the first small one, he would whisper, "What has God wrought!" Yet he wanted no praise for himself. Asked what text should be used at his funeral, he chose Psalm 51:1: "God, be gracious to me in keeping with Your loyal love." Charles Spurgeon later told this story, and added:

> "Only on the footing of free grace can the most experienced and most honoured of the saints approach their God. The best of men are conscious above all others that they are men at the best."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Morning by Morning*

### Three key insights

1. **Great expectation and great effort belong together in the life of prayer.** Carey expected great things from God, so he prayed boldly. He also attempted great things for God, so his prayers became plans and years of work.
2. **Those who answer God's call must keep praying for the people it costs.** Carey told his children that he prayed for them constantly. Every call of God touches others too, and those closest to us need our prayers, our tenderness and our care.
3. **When God blesses our work, the fitting response is wonder at His grace, not pride in ourselves.** Looking at the churches in India, Carey could only whisper, "What has God wrought!" At the end he asked for nothing but mercy.

### For us today

Christians read Isaiah's promise as a picture of the church growing among the nations, not by conquest but by the gospel. Carey worked in an age of empire, yet the East India Company, which ruled Bengal, tried to keep missionaries out. The gospel he carried was not the Company's gospel. Today the church in India has its own pastors, scholars and missionaries, and many of the world's missionaries now come from Africa, Asia and Latin America.

Carey's story also asks us to remember the families of those who serve. Behind many famous names are wives, husbands and children who suffered quietly. They need our prayers as much as the workers do.

And Carey reminds us that God uses plain people: a shoemaker who prayed and would not give up became a translator of the Bible for millions. In Volume 8, *Open Your Mouth Wide*, we will watch the fire of this age spread through the nineteenth century, from five students praying beside a haystack in the rain to George Müller, who fed thousands of orphans by prayer.

Great God, You promised that Your people would spread out to the right and to the left, and that the nations would hear Your name. Thank You for William Carey, who expected great things from You and attempted great things for You, and for Dorothy Carey and all who suffered with him. ## Conclusion: Passing the Torch

> "Never let your eagerness flag; be on fire in the Spirit; serve the Lord. Let your hope keep you rejoicing; hold out under trouble; keep at prayer." (Romans 12:11-12)

On a June evening in 1793, William Carey and his family sailed from England for India. He was thirty-one years old. He had no university degree, little money and no promise of success. Behind him, a small group of Baptist ministers who had been meeting to pray once a month for nine years promised to keep praying for him. Fuller later put it this way: Carey would go down into the mine, and they would hold the ropes.

That scene gathers up much of this book. A century and a half earlier, the churches of Europe had been tired and cold. Now a shoemaker was sailing to the other side of the world, sent out by prayer and held up by prayer. Between those two moments lay the stories we have read: small groups gathered around the Bible, an orphanage built on prayer, a prayer watch that ran night and day, preachers in the open fields, a young missionary praying in the woods, a woman praying for thirty-eight years in a prison tower, and a former slave trader teaching a country town to ask God for large things.

Paul's words to the Romans describe these believers well. They were "on fire in the Spirit," and they kept "at prayer."

### What the witnesses taught us

Here, in a few lines, is some of what the witnesses of this book taught us about prayer:

- Prayer is the heart turning to God, so even the simplest believer can pray (chapter 1, Jeanne Guyon).
- When believers who fear God talk together about Him, the Lord listens and remembers (chapter 2, Philipp Spener).
- God is a Father to orphans, and He loves to meet their needs through people who pray (chapter 3, August Hermann Francke).
- God often begins a work of prayer by first teaching His people to love one another (chapter 4, the Moravians at Herrnhut).
- Prayer for the nations begins with seeing what the Lamb paid for them (chapter 5, Zinzendorf and the Moravian missionaries).
- A crowded, noisy home can still hold a hidden place for meeting God (chapter 6, Susanna Wesley).
- God has chosen to do His work in answer to the prayers of His people (chapter 7, John Wesley).
- A true believer needs prayer as a living body needs breath (chapter 8, George Whitefield).
- When believers agree to pray together for God's kingdom, they take part in His plan for the world (chapter 9, Jonathan Edwards).
- Longing prayer for others is a kind of birth pain, and God uses it to bring new life (chapter 10, David Brainerd).
- Revival is usually born in small, faithful prayer meetings long before anyone notices (chapter 11, the praying societies of Cambuslang).
- Holding on in prayer, day after day, is itself a way of resisting evil (chapter 12, Marie Durand).
- Because we come to a generous King, we may bring Him large requests (chapter 13, John Newton).
- Every believer has the same right to kneel before God, because all are one in Christ Jesus (chapter 14, Richard Allen and Absalom Jones).
- God's promise reaches every clan of every nation, so every people may pray to Him in its own tongue (chapter 15, Samson Occom).
- Those who stay at home hold the ropes for those who go, and prayer is the strongest rope (chapter 16, the Northamptonshire Baptists).
- Great expectation and great effort belong together in the life of prayer (chapter 17, William Carey).

You will find every one of these lessons gathered by theme in Appendix 1, and every Scripture in this book listed in Bible order in Appendix 2.

### The fire on the altar

The title of this book comes from a command God gave to the priests of Israel: "Fire is to burn on the altar at all times, never going out" (Leviticus 6:13).

The Moravians at Herrnhut saw in that verse a picture of their own prayer watch, and many believers since have done the same. A fire like that does not keep itself burning. Someone must bring the wood, morning after morning. Someone must sit up in the night and watch the coals.

That is what the people in this book did. Their prayer was often unseen and ordinary: an hour at a set time, a monthly meeting, an apron over a mother's head, a walk in the woods, a weekly gathering in a large room in Olney. The great awakenings of the age grew out of such small and faithful fires.

They had their failures, and we have not hidden them. Some were proud or quarrelsome. Some, to our sorrow, accepted or even took part in slavery, while their brothers and sisters in Christ suffered under it. Yet God used their prayers. And through men like Absalom Jones, who was pulled from his knees in a Philadelphia church, and Richard Allen, who walked out beside him and went on with him to pray and found churches of their own, God also showed that no one can shut His children out of His presence.

### The torch is passed

By the year 1800, the gospel was spreading as never before. Missionary societies were forming in Britain, Europe and America. Bibles were being translated into the languages of India, Africa and the Pacific. And in all these places, new believers were learning to pray.

In **Volume 8, *Open Your Mouth Wide***, we will follow this "great century" of prayer and mission, from 1800 to 1900. We will kneel with five students sheltering from the rain beside a haystack in Massachusetts, and with a man named Daniel Nash, who is remembered for going ahead of the preacher Charles Finney to pray. We will watch George Müller feed thousands of orphans without asking anyone but God for money, and walk with a Russian pilgrim learning to pray without ceasing. We will join a noon prayer meeting in New York that spread across a nation, and look into the room under Spurgeon's chapel where, the story goes, people prayed while he preached. We will meet Hudson Taylor and Pastor Hsi in China, William and Catherine Booth in the slums of London, Amanda Berry Smith in Liberia and India, Samuel Ajayi Crowther on the Niger, Andrew Murray in South Africa and Mary Slessor in Calabar.

They were the heirs of the witnesses in this book. Müller learned from Francke. The missionaries learned from the Moravians and from Carey. Many of them prayed with the hymns of Wesley and Newton. The torch was passed.

### Your turn

The witnesses of this book now pass the torch to you.

So be still before God, and speak to Him simply, as a child to a father. Gather a few others to read the Bible and pray. Trust God for what you need, and help the poor and the orphan. Keep your own small fire burning by praying at set times, even when no one sees. Make a quiet place for prayer in a crowded home. Ask God boldly, because He does great things in answer to prayer. Pray with others for the spread of the gospel, and hold the ropes for those who go. When you are in prison, real or otherwise, resist and keep praying. Bring large petitions to a great King. And if anyone ever tries to shut you out of God's presence, kneel down and pray anyway. He hears you.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You set Your people's hearts on fire. Thank You for the believers of this age who kept the fire burning: for Jeanne Guyon and the Pietists, for Francke and the Moravians, for Susanna and John Wesley, George Whitefield and Jonathan Edwards, for David Brainerd and Marie Durand, for John Newton, Richard Allen and Absalom Jones, Samson Occom, and the ministers who prayed until William Carey was sent. Forgive us where we, like them, have been proud, divided or blind to the suffering of others. Stir up the flame in me. Keep me eager, on fire in the Spirit, rejoicing in hope, patient in trouble and faithful in prayer. Let the fire never go out. Amen.
