## Introduction: Where We Are in the Story

> "Not by might, nor by power, but by My Spirit, says the LORD of Armies." (Zechariah 4:6)

At the start of the twentieth century, many people in the West were sure that the world was getting better. Science and medicine were advancing quickly. New machines were changing how people worked and traveled. Some believed that education and progress would soon end war, poverty and ignorance. A few even thought that faith in God would slowly fade away, because people would no longer need it.

Within fifty years, the world had been through two terrible world wars, a worldwide influenza epidemic, revolutions in Russia and China, the death camps of Nazi Germany and the first atomic bombs. Progress had not saved anyone.

Yet in those same fifty years, the church saw some of the greatest revivals in its history. And they did not come through human might or power. They came, again and again, when ordinary believers gathered to pray and asked God to send His Spirit.

### Where we are in the series

This is the ninth volume of *A Cloud of Praying Witnesses*, a series that follows the people of God at prayer through the whole story of the church. The name comes from Hebrews 12:1, which pictures all the faithful who went before us as "so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us."

The first volumes followed prayer from the Old Testament to the age of revival. Volume 8, *Open Your Mouth Wide*, followed the great century of missions, from the students under the haystack to E. M. Bounds, rising before dawn to pray for the world.

This volume follows the first half of the twentieth century, from about 1900 to 1950.

### The world of these witnesses

In 1900 most of the world's Christians still lived in Europe and the Americas. By 1950 the center of the church was beginning to move south and east. Churches in Korea, China, India and Africa were growing fast, and many were led by their own people. Some of the most important witnesses in this book are Asian and African believers who never left their own countries.

It was also a time of suffering. Christians were imprisoned in Nazi Germany and in Japanese-occupied China, sent to concentration camps, forced to flee across mountains and driven underground. Many of the prayers in this book were prayed in prison cells, in hiding or on the road.

Some of the revivals in this book gave birth to new movements. The Azusa Street meetings in Los Angeles helped begin the Pentecostal movement, which has since grown to hundreds of millions of believers around the world. Churches still differ about some of the gifts of the Spirit that these revivals emphasized. We will tell these stories warmly and fairly, and keep our focus on what every believer can learn about prayer.

### What we will see

This volume has twenty chapters, in four movements:

- **Fire falls** (chapters 1 to 5). A young former miner prays, "Bend me," and revival sweeps through Wales. Pandita Ramabai's home for women in India is stirred by prayer. William Seymour leads a praying congregation on Azusa Street, and in Korea a whole church prays aloud together and begins to pray at the break of dawn.
- **Prayer that labors** (chapters 6 to 10). "Praying" John Hyde sows in tears in India. Sadhu Sundar Singh teaches that prayer is like breathing, and Amy Carmichael rescues children in South India. Smith Wigglesworth learns to "only believe," and Rees Howells stands in the gap for a world at war.
- **Prayer in China and in the camps** (chapters 11 to 16). Dora Yu answers God's call to preach. John Sung starts prayer bands across Asia, and Watchman Nee teaches the prayer ministry of the church. Dietrich Bonhoeffer prays in a Nazi prison, Corrie ten Boom learns to give thanks in a concentration camp, and Gladys Aylward leads a hundred children over the mountains.
- **Revival again** (chapters 17 to 20). Revival breaks out in East Africa, where a young deacon calls the sleeping church to wake up. Two old sisters on a Scottish island pray for years for an awakening. A group of businessmen pray for their city in a field on a dairy farm, and the farmer's teenage son becomes Billy Graham. And A. W. Tozer calls the church to the pursuit of God.

### How each chapter works

Every chapter in this series has the same shape:

- **A key verse** opens the chapter, the Scripture it rests on.
- **A story** sets the scene.
- **The teaching** looks at what the story shows us about prayer.
- **Voices from the past** quote men and women of prayer word for word from books in the Ochorus library.
- **Three key insights** sum up what the chapter teaches.
- **For us today** brings the lesson into ordinary life, wherever in the world you live.
- **Try this** gives one simple practice for the coming week.
- **A closing prayer** and **Questions for reflection** help you respond, alone or with others.
- **Read more on Ochorus** points you to books where you can go deeper.

At the end of the book, two appendices gather all the key insights by theme and list every Scripture quoted, in Bible order, with the chapters where it appears.

### A word about these stories

The people in this book lived recently enough that many of their own books, and many books about them, are still protected by copyright. Where we can, we quote their own words from books that are free to share. Where we cannot, we tell their stories in our own words and name the book the story comes from. Revival stories also tend to grow as they are retold, and some famous details were added later. In each chapter we say where a story comes from, and when a detail is uncertain, we say so.

### How to read this book

One chapter a day will carry you through the book in twenty days. With a small group, one or two chapters a week works well.

The witnesses in this book did not make revival happen. They could not. What they did was pray, confess their sins, forgive one another and wait for God. "Not by might, nor by power, but by My Spirit." As you read, ask Him to send His Spirit again: into your own heart, your church and your land.

## "Bend Me"

> "Do not get drunk on wine, for that is reckless, wasteful living. Instead, let the Spirit fill you." (Ephesians 5:18)

At six o'clock on the morning of Thursday, 29 September 1904, about twenty people set out from Newcastle Emlyn, a small market town in west Wales, for the village of Blaenannerch. One of them was a student of twenty-six named Evan Roberts. He had worked for years in the coal mines and for a time as a blacksmith. Three weeks earlier he had entered a small grammar school in Newcastle Emlyn, to prepare to become a minister.

That week an evangelist named Seth Joshua was holding meetings in the area, and the group was going to hear him. At the seven o'clock meeting Joshua closed with a prayer. Roberts later said he remembered none of the words except two. Joshua had asked God to do this, and this, and this, and then had added, "bend us." The meeting was in Welsh, and "bend" here means to bow, to yield, to give way. Joshua did not stress the words, Roberts said. But the Holy Spirit pressed them on his heart, and he seemed to hear the Spirit say, "That is what you stand in need of." As he went out of the door he prayed silently, "Lord, bend us."

At breakfast a troubling thought came to him: what if God offered His Spirit, and he was not ready to receive Him? At the nine o'clock meeting many people prayed aloud, one after another. Roberts felt he must pray too, but he waited. At last he fell on his knees, with his arms stretched over the seat in front of him. Sweat poured down his face, and he wept. Then he cried out, "Bend me, bend me, bend me." Afterwards a great peace filled him. The words that broke his heart that morning, he said, were from Romans: "God commendeth His love." In our Bible the verse reads, "God proves how much He loves us: Christ died for us when we were still sinners" (Romans 5:8). "It was God commending His love that bent me," he said, "and I not seeing anything in Him to commend."

Then a new burden came. He longed to go through the whole of Wales to tell people about the Savior.

### How we know the story

Roberts told this story in his own words to a friend, D. M. Phillips, who printed it in a long life of Roberts in 1906. Phillips admired Roberts greatly, and parts of his book are more praise than history, but this account is Roberts speaking about himself. The London journalist W. T. Stead also interviewed him, and his report was printed in *The Story of the Welsh Revival* (1905).

Roberts told Stead that some months before Blaenannerch he had been woken at one o'clock one morning and found himself in the presence of God. For four hours, he said, he was "privileged to speak face to face with Him as a man speaks face to face with a friend," and this happened every morning for three or four months. He had prayed for revival in Wales for years, long before anyone knew his name.

### From one chapel to a whole land

In late October Roberts felt that God was sending him home to Loughor, a small town near Swansea where he had grown up. On Monday, 31 October 1904, after the usual prayer meeting at Moriah Chapel, he asked the young people to stay behind. Phillips counts sixteen adults and one little girl. Roberts explained why he had come home and urged them to confess Christ openly. It was hard for them. After two hours, during which he prayed three times, all of them stood.

The meetings went on night after night, and every night there were more people. In a letter of that week Roberts set out his plain message in four points: confess to God every sin you have not confessed before; put away anything doubtful in your life; obey the Holy Spirit fully and at once; and confess Christ publicly.

Within two weeks the newspapers were reporting the meetings, and soon the revival had spread across the coal valleys of south Wales and beyond. Chapels were full until two or three in the morning. People confessed their sins, paid old debts and made peace with neighbors. Later writers often say that about a hundred thousand people came to faith in Wales within a few months, but no one counted them, so the number is only an estimate. Evan Hopkins, an English Bible teacher who wrote about the revival in 1905, gave its watchword as "Bend the Church, and save the world."

### The bending comes first

Paul's command in Ephesians 5:18 sets two things side by side. A drunk person is controlled by wine, and it leads him into "reckless, wasteful living." A believer is to be filled, and so controlled, by the Spirit of God, who fills the life that yields to Him. In Paul's Greek the verb points to something that goes on happening, day after day. Then Paul shows what the filling looks like: singing to the Lord, giving thanks "at all times and for everything," and serving one another (Ephesians 5:19-21).

Roberts's prayer, "Bend me," shows how that filling begins. We often ask God to fill us while we hold tightly to our own plans, our pride and our habits. The American evangelist D. L. Moody noticed this:

> "I believe many a man is praying to God to fill him when he is full already with something else. Before we pray that God would fill us, I believe we ought to pray Him to empty us."
> D. L. Moody, *Secret Power*

Notice also what Roberts did not ask. He did not ask God to bend the people of Wales, or his critics. He asked God to bend him. Revival in Wales began with believers inside the chapels, confessing their own sins and yielding their own wills.

### A clay pot

The story has a sad side, and it should be told honestly and gently. For more than a year Roberts traveled from town to town, sleeping little and speaking in meetings that lasted half the night. He was young and untrained. Crowds followed him, newspapers reported his every word, and some ministers attacked him in print. Many think he came to fear that people were looking at him and not at Christ. By 1906 his body and mind were worn out.

He went to rest in Leicester, England, in the home of Jessie Penn-Lewis, a Christian writer, and her husband, and he stayed with them for many years. He never returned to the public life he had led during the revival. Later he lived in Brighton and then in Cardiff, and those who knew him said he gave himself mainly to prayer. Some in Wales felt he had left them; others thought he was wise to step back. He died in Cardiff on 29 January 1951 and is buried behind Moriah Chapel in Loughor, where the meetings began.

Paul wrote that "we hold this treasure in pots made of clay, so that the power, great beyond measure, may be God's and may not come from us" (2 Corinthians 4:7). Evan Roberts was a clay pot. God poured great blessing through him, and the pot cracked. That does not make the blessing false. It reminds us that the power was always God's.

### Three key insights

1. **Being filled with the Spirit begins when we ask God to bend our own will.** Roberts did not ask for power or success. He asked God to bend him. A heart that yields to God has room for His Spirit.
2. **The Spirit's filling shows itself in plain obedience: confessing sin, putting away doubtful things and owning Christ in public.** Roberts's four points were not a way to produce feelings. They were simple steps that any believer can take, and the fire spread through people who took them.
3. **Even those whom God uses greatly are clay pots, and they need care, rest and our prayers.** Roberts broke under a weight no young man should carry alone. His story asks us to pray for those who lead, and to remember that the work is God's.

### For us today

Believers in many lands long for revival. In Nigeria and Ghana they keep all-night prayer meetings. In Brazil and the Philippines groups fast and pray for their cities. Christians in places of persecution pray quietly in homes. The story of Wales says to all of them: begin with yourself. Before we ask God to change our nation, we can ask Him to bend our own pride, our anger and our love of comfort.

Roberts's story also speaks to those who lead. Pastors and youth leaders in many lands carry heavy loads, often with little money and no help. A church that prays for revival must also care for the people God uses in it.

### Try this

This week, let Evan Roberts's short prayer shape your own.

- **Pray "Bend me."** Each morning, kneel or sit quietly and pray those two words. Then name one area of your life where you are holding on to your own way.
- **Use the four points.** Pray slowly through Roberts's four points: confess a sin, put away one doubtful thing, ask for grace to obey at once, and look for one chance to speak of Christ.
- **Sing your thanks.** The Spirit-filled life sings and gives thanks (Ephesians 5:19-20). Sing or say one hymn of praise each day.
- **Pray for a worn-out leader.** Think of a pastor or Christian worker who is carrying a heavy load. Pray for that person by name, and find one way to encourage them.

### A closing prayer

Holy Spirit of God, You fill those who yield to You. Thank You for Evan Roberts, who prayed "Bend me." I confess that I often ask You to fill me while I am full of myself. Empty me of pride, of fear and of my own plans. Bend me, Lord, and then fill me, so that my life may sing Your praise and give thanks at all times. Guard those who lead Your church, and when they are weary, carry them. Revive Your church, beginning with me, for the sake of Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What part of your life most needs to be bent toward God's will?
- Why do you think the revival in Wales began with believers confessing their own sins, and not with preaching to unbelievers?
- How can your church pray for and care for its leaders, so that they are not crushed by the work God gives them?

## Your Daughters Will Prophesy

> "And afterward I will pour out My Spirit upon all people. Your sons and your daughters will prophesy; your old men will have dreams, and your young men will see visions." (Joel 2:28)

At half past three on the morning of 30 June 1905, an American teacher named Minnie Abrams was woken in her room at Mukti, a large Christian home for women and girls at Kedgaon in western India. One of the older girls was at her door. Come and rejoice with us, the girl said. One of the young women had received the Holy Spirit. When Abrams reached the girls' building, everyone there was on her knees, weeping, praying and confessing her sins. In the middle sat the young woman, telling the others what God had done for her.

That evening Pandita Ramabai, the founder of Mukti, was teaching the Bible in the church to the members of a prayer circle. Then, as the story was told soon afterwards, many in the room began to pray aloud at once. Small children, older girls and young women wept and confessed their sins. Some prayed for hours. Abrams wrote: "From that time our Bible-school was turned into an enquiry room." Lessons stopped, and "we all, teachers and students," she said, "entered the school conducted by the Holy Spirit."

Many of these girls had come to Mukti as famine orphans or child widows. Soon they would go out across India to pray and to preach.

The fire had not come from nowhere. When news of the revival in Wales reached Mukti (chapter 1), Ramabai formed a new prayer circle in January 1905. In *A Testimony* (1907), a short account of her life, she wrote that about seventy of them "met together each morning and prayed for the true conversion of all the Indian Christians, including ourselves, and for a special outpouring of the Holy Spirit on all Christians of every land." Six months later, she wrote, "the Lord graciously sent a glorious Holy Ghost revival among us."

### Who she was

Ramabai was not simply a missionary's convert. She was one of the great Indian scholars and reformers of her century. She was born in 1858 in a forest home in the Western Ghats mountains, the daughter of a Brahmin scholar who broke the custom of his day by teaching Sanskrit, the language of the Hindu sacred books, to his wife and daughter. When famine struck, the family wandered as pilgrims. Her parents and sister died. With her brother she walked, she wrote, "more than four thousand miles on foot," from temple to temple, seeking God and finding no peace.

In Calcutta the learned men gave her the title *Pandita* (a learned teacher). She married, and was widowed with a baby daughter, Manorama, before she was twenty-four. She knew how India treated widows, and she gave her life to them. She was baptized in England in 1883, and in America she published *The High-Caste Hindu Woman* (1887), a strong book on the wrongs done to Indian women. In 1889 she opened a home and school for widows in Bombay, which soon moved to Poona.

Yet she later wrote that something was still missing. "I had found the Christian religion, which was good enough for me," she said, "but I had not found Christ." In about 1891, after reading the Bible for herself, she gave herself to Him without conditions. When famine came again in 1896 and 1897, she rescued hundreds of starving girls, and at Kedgaon she built the home she called Mukti, a Marathi word meaning salvation or freedom. By 1907 more than fifteen hundred people lived there. They had no endowment and no regular income. "The Lord is our Inexhaustible Treasure," she wrote.

### Praying by name

Ramabai guided the revival wisely. She did not want it to become a show. She sent out praying bands of girls and women to schools, orphanages and churches in other places. Their purpose, she said, was "prayer for the Christian people living in the places to which the bands go." She told them, "Our work will bear lasting fruit if we pray more and work less."

She also asked missionaries across India to send her the names of people who needed prayer. Before long, her biographer Helen Dyer reports, more than twenty-nine thousand people were being prayed for by name at Mukti. Some friends doubted that this was possible. Ramabai answered from her own past: "When we were Hindus we used to repeat one or two thousand names of the gods daily." "Why should we not, as Christians, be able to pray for many hundreds of people by name?"

### Daughters and servants

The prophet Joel promised a day when God would pour out His Spirit "upon all people." Then he named the people his hearers least expected: daughters as well as sons, old and young, and "even on servants, men and women alike" (Joel 2:29). On the day of Pentecost, Peter said that this promise had begun to come true (Acts 2:17-18). God's Spirit does not wait for the important, the learned or the male.

In the world Ramabai grew up in, many held that a woman could not read the sacred books, and a widow was often treated as a curse. At Mukti the Spirit came first on famine orphans and child widows, and they became the ones who prayed for India. Sixty years before, a Black American preacher, Zilpha Elaw, had defended her own right to preach the gospel from the same promise:

> "...the prophet Joel predicted that God would pour His Spirit on His handmaids, and that they should prophecy as well as His servants; and this prophecy, Peter, on the day of Pentecost, asserted was fulfilled; and if so, the Christian dispensation has for its main feature the inspirations of the holy prophetic Spirit, descending on the handmaids as well as on the servants of God; and thus qualifying both for the conversion of men, and spread of the Gospel."
> Zilpha Elaw, *Memoirs of Mrs. Zilpha Elaw*

The revival had its puzzles. There was loud crying, and some saw visions. In late 1906, after news came from Los Angeles (chapter 3), some at Mukti began to speak in tongues. Christians disagreed about these things, as they still do. Ramabai did not try to stop what she believed God was doing, and she did not make any of it a test. Later writers quote her paper, the *Mukti Prayer Bell*, from 1907: she taught, in effect, that perfect love is the one necessary sign that the Spirit has come. Even some who doubted the louder features agreed that the fruit was real: hundreds of women truly changed.

### A Bible for her sisters

Ramabai's last great work was a gift of words. She believed that the Marathi Bible then in use was full of high, learned words, some of them carrying Hindu meanings. So she learned Hebrew and Greek and translated the whole Bible into plain Marathi, the language of the villages. Her aim, Dyer wrote, was a Bible that women with little schooling "could easily understand." Mukti women set the type and printed it. She finished the translation shortly before she died, on 5 April 1922.

### Three key insights

1. **God pours out His Spirit on those whom the world overlooks, and He makes them messengers of His word.** Famine orphans and widows at Mukti became the praying bands of India. Joel's promise is for daughters and servants, the poor and the young.
2. **When we pray for revival, we must count ourselves among those who need it.** Ramabai's circle prayed for the conversion of Indian Christians "including ourselves." We cannot ask God to change others while refusing to let Him search our own hearts.
3. **Praying by name for many people is a work that ordinary believers can share.** Ramabai's household prayed for more than twenty-nine thousand people by name. A list of names, prayed through faithfully, can carry God's love far beyond our own walls.

### For us today

In many places today women and girls are still treated as less than men. Some cannot go to school, and some widows lose their homes. In parts of Africa, Asia and the Middle East, the church is growing fastest among women who are often poor and unseen. Joel's promise says to every one of them: God's Spirit is poured out on you too, and He has work for you to do.

Ramabai's story also speaks to churches that think they are too small or too poor to pray for the world. Mukti had no money in the bank, yet it prayed for tens of thousands of people by name. Women in a village in Malawi, students in Chennai or a family in a small flat in Lima can do the same. And her Bible reminds us that many peoples still wait for the Scriptures in their own language.

### Try this

This week, let the women of Mukti teach you to pray.

- **Pray "including ourselves."** When you pray for revival in your church or your country, add these words, and ask God to begin with you.
- **Start a list of names.** Write down ten people you know, and pray for one each day by name. Add to the list as God brings others to mind.
- **Pray for women and girls.** Pray for widows, orphans and girls who cannot go to school, in your own land and in others. Ask God to show you one way to help one of them.
- **Pray for Bible translators.** Find out about one people that does not yet have the Bible in its own language, and pray for those translating it.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You promised to pour out Your Spirit on all people, on sons and daughters, on the old and the young, and on servants, men and women alike. Thank You for Pandita Ramabai, who sought You through years of wandering and found You in Christ, and for the women and girls of Mukti who prayed for India. Pour out Your Spirit on Your church again, including me. Teach me to pray for others by name and to care for those whom the world forgets. Let Your daughters and Your sons speak of You in every land, for the sake of Jesus Christ our Lord. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Ramabai said she had found "the Christian religion" but "had not found Christ." What is the difference, and have you known it in your own life?
- Why do you think the revival at Mukti came first to orphans and widows?
- Whose names could you begin to pray for each day? What would it take to keep that list for a year?

## A Stable on Azusa Street

> "But when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, you will receive power, and you will bear witness to Me in Jerusalem, through all Judea and Samaria, and out to the farthest end of the earth." (Acts 1:8)

In the spring of 1906 a small group of believers met each evening in a wooden house on North Bonnie Brae Street in Los Angeles. Most were Black working people: cooks, maids, laborers and washerwomen. A few white believers came too. They had agreed to pray and fast for ten days, reading the second chapter of Acts and asking God to pour out His Spirit on them as He had at Pentecost.

Their leader was a quiet Black preacher of thirty-five named William J. Seymour. He had come from Houston, Texas, in February to lead a small holiness mission, but its leaders did not accept his teaching about the Holy Spirit, and, as he wrote later, "one night they locked the door against me." So he prayed with whoever would pray with him, in borrowed homes.

On the evening of Monday, 9 April 1906, the prayer was answered in a way they never forgot. Several in the meeting began to speak in tongues. They believed that God was doing again what He had done on the day of Pentecost (Acts 2:4). The news spread through the city. For several nights crowds of Black, white and Mexican American people filled the house and the street outside, and Seymour preached from the front porch.

The house was soon far too small. The group found an empty building at 312 Azusa Street, in a poor part of downtown Los Angeles. It had been built for a Black Methodist congregation, but after the congregation moved it had been used for other things. The mission's own newspaper, the *Apostolic Faith*, described it in its first issue in September 1906: "an old Methodist church that had been converted in part into a tenement house, leaving a large, unplastered, barn-like room on the ground floor." Two months later the paper said that it stood beside "a tombstone shop, stables and lumber yard." Later writers say that the building itself had been used to keep horses. Whether or not that is so, the paper drew the lesson: "You would hardly expect heavenly visitations there, unless you remember the stable at Bethlehem."

Frank Bartleman, a white preacher who prayed there from the early days, remembered sitting on planks laid across old nail kegs. Yet for the next three years people came from all over the world to pray there, often from morning until past midnight.

### Who he was

William Joseph Seymour was born on 2 May 1870 in Centerville, Louisiana. His parents, Simon and Phillis Seymour, had been born into slavery; his father served in the Union army in the American Civil War. Few records of his early years survive. He grew up poor, in a land where Black people were kept down by law and by violence. As a young man he moved north, to Indianapolis and then Cincinnati, where he worked as a waiter and was drawn into the holiness movement, a group of churches that taught a life of deep surrender to God. In Cincinnati, it seems, smallpox left him blind in one eye.

In Houston, early in 1906, he studied the Bible for a few weeks under Charles Parham, a white teacher who believed that the gift of speaking in tongues showed that a believer had been baptized with the Holy Spirit. Texas law kept Black and white students apart, and the story is told that Seymour listened to the lessons from his seat outside the classroom door. Then the call came from Los Angeles. "It was the divine call that brought me from Houston, Texas, to Los Angeles," he wrote.

### Power to bear witness

We began Volume 3 with this same verse, Acts 1:8, where it opened the story of the early church. Jesus spoke these words just before He returned to the Father. The disciples were to wait in Jerusalem until the Spirit came. So they waited, and they prayed: "All of these were devoted to prayer and of one mind in it" (Acts 1:14). The Spirit came to a praying people.

The believers on Bonnie Brae Street did the same. They had no building, no money and no famous leader, only a promise and a prayer meeting. The American Bible teacher R. A. Torrey, writing some years before, put it plainly:

> "Against all inferences is this clear teaching of the Word by precept and example that the Holy Spirit is given in answer to prayer. It was so at Pentecost; it has been so since."
> R. A. Torrey, *Baptism with the Holy Spirit*

Christians differ over what happened at Azusa Street, and over speaking in tongues and the other gifts that were sought there. Pentecostal and charismatic churches, now hundreds of millions of believers, trace much of their story to this building. Many other Christians understand the gifts differently. This book does not try to settle those questions. What all can learn is in the verse itself. The Spirit gives power, and the power has a purpose: "you will bear witness to Me." Within months, men and women from Azusa Street were sailing to Liberia, India, China and other lands, often with very little money, to tell people about Jesus.

### The color line

What amazed visitors most was who was praying together. In a city and a nation divided by race, Black and white believers knelt side by side, and a Black man led them. The *Apostolic Faith* reported that "the work began among the colored people," and it rejoiced: "God makes no difference in nationality, Ethiopians, Chinese, Indians, Mexicans, and other nationalities worship together." Bartleman wrote years later, in effect, that at Azusa the color line was washed away in the blood of Christ.

Some newspapers mocked the meetings for exactly this reason. Amanda Berry Smith, the Black evangelist we met in Volume 8, had written years before:

> "Thank God for real, practical, inright, outright, downright common sense; that is all I think people need on the color line. May the Lord give it to us quick. Amen."
> Amanda Smith, *Amanda Smith: An Autobiography*

Sadly, the unity did not last. In October 1906 Charles Parham came to Azusa Street and was disgusted by the mixing of the races. He denounced the meetings and set up a rival work. Other leaders left over teaching and over power. In 1908 two former helpers moved the newspaper to Portland, Oregon, and took its mailing list with them, so Seymour could no longer speak through it to the wider movement. By about 1914 his congregation had become a small Black church again. In the years that followed, many of the new Pentecostal churches in America divided along racial lines, as most other American churches already were. Seymour went on serving his small flock faithfully. He died on 28 September 1922.

Paul tells believers to "spare no effort to keep the unity the Spirit gives" (Ephesians 4:3). The story of Azusa Street shows both sides of that verse. The Spirit gave a unity that the world could not explain. Human pride and prejudice let it slip away.

### Three key insights

1. **The Spirit comes to people who wait for Him in prayer, and He gives power so that they can bear witness to Jesus.** The disciples in Jerusalem and the believers on Bonnie Brae Street both waited and prayed. In both places the power led straight to witness, near and far.
2. **God's presence does not wait for fine buildings, and a poor room can become a house of prayer for every nation.** A barn-like room near a stable, led by a poor Black preacher, became a place where people from many lands met God. He still chooses "what the world counts low and despises" (1 Corinthians 1:28).
3. **The unity the Spirit gives across race and nation is easily lost, so it must be guarded with humble love.** At Azusa Street believers of many races prayed as one family, but pride and prejudice divided them within a few years. Unity is a gift we must keep with care.

### For us today

Many churches today are divided by race, tribe, caste or language. In some cities, believers of different backgrounds live side by side but never pray together. In parts of Africa, old tribal wounds run through the church, and in India caste still divides some congregations. In America and Europe, Sunday morning can be among the most separated hours of the week. Azusa Street shows what the Spirit can do, and also how easily we can undo it.

The story also encourages believers who are poor or overlooked. Today many of the fastest-growing churches in the world meet in rented rooms, tin-roofed halls and homes, in Lagos, São Paulo, Manila and Nairobi. He comes to people who pray.

### Try this

This week, ask God for His power to be a witness.

- **Wait in prayer.** Each day, pray Luke 11:13, asking your Father in heaven for the Holy Spirit, and wait quietly before Him.
- **Pray across the lines.** Pray with a believer of a different race, tribe, nation or church from your own, even once.
- **Pray for the farthest places.** Choose one country where few people know Jesus, and pray each day for its people and for those who take the gospel there.
- **Bear witness nearby.** Ask the Spirit to show you one person in your own "Jerusalem" (your family, street or workplace) to whom you can speak about Jesus.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You promised that when the Holy Spirit came upon Your people, they would receive power to bear witness to You to the farthest end of the earth. Thank You for William Seymour and the humble believers of Azusa Street, who welcomed people of every race to Your altar. Father, You give the Holy Spirit to those who ask You, so I ask You now: fill me, and make me a faithful witness. Break down the walls of race and pride in my heart and in Your church. Help us to keep the unity Your Spirit gives, for Your name's sake. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think God so often begins His work among poor and overlooked people?
- What lines of race, tribe, class or language divide believers where you live? What would it look like to pray across them?
- Acts 1:8 begins in "Jerusalem," close to home. Who is in your Jerusalem?

## Everyone Praying Aloud Together

> "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous: He will forgive our sins and cleanse us from every kind of wrongdoing." (1 John 1:9)

Every January the Christian men of northern Korea came to the city of Pyongyang for the winter Bible class. In January 1907 they came in their hundreds. Many walked for days, carrying their own rice for the two weeks of study. By day they studied the Bible in classes. Each evening they crowded into the Central Church for a meeting, so many that women could not be admitted for lack of room. More than fifteen hundred men filled the large building.

For a week nothing unusual happened. On Sunday evening, 13 January, the meeting seemed dead, and the missionaries went home weary. At noon on Monday they met and cried out to God in earnest.

That Monday night, 14 January, each man who came in felt, Blair wrote, that the room was full of God's presence. After a short sermon, an American missionary named Graham Lee took charge and invited the men to pray. So many began praying at once that Lee said, "If you want to pray like that, all pray." Then the whole congregation began to pray out loud, all together.

His colleague William Blair never forgot the sound. "The effect was indescribable," he wrote. "Not confusion, but a vast harmony of sound and spirit, a mingling together of souls moved by an irresistible impulse of prayer." To him it sounded "like the falling of many waters, an ocean of prayer beating against God's throne."

As they prayed, a deep sorrow for sin came over the room. Someone began to weep, and soon the whole congregation was weeping. Lee wrote at the time that "man after man would rise, confess his sins, break down and weep." His own cook stood up to confess, broke down, and cried across the room, "Pastor, tell me, is there any hope for me, can I be forgiven?" The meeting went on until two in the morning, "with confession and weeping and praying."

### How we know the story

We have unusually good sources. Graham Lee wrote a short account at the time, "How the Spirit Came to Pyeng Yang" (the old English spelling of Pyongyang). William Blair quoted it and added his own memories in *The Korea Pentecost* (1910). The missionary James Gale heard the story from Korean leaders and told it in *Korea in Transition* (1909). The Canadian missionary Jonathan Goforth visited Korea soon afterwards and wrote about it in 1943, in a book we can describe here but not quote.

The way the men prayed that night became one of the marks of the Korean church. Today Koreans call it *tongsŏng kido*: everyone in the congregation praying aloud together, each in his or her own words, at the same time. Visitors are often surprised by it. But Blair's words describe what many Korean believers still experience: "It was not many, but one, born of one Spirit, lifted to one Father above."

### Hearts that needed healing

The revival came at a dark time for Korea. In 1905 Japan had defeated Russia in war and forced Korea to accept its control. Many Koreans were full of grief and anger, and some hated the Japanese bitterly. The missionaries saw that the church was in danger of losing its love.

In August 1906 the missionaries of Pyongyang, Presbyterian and Methodist together, met for a week of prayer and Bible study. They chose the First Letter of John as their study book. Its call to walk in the light, to confess our sins and to love one another searched their own hearts first. Blair wrote that before that week ended, God showed them "that the way of victory for us would be a way of confession, of broken hearts and bitter tears." All that autumn they kept praying for the January class.

On Tuesday night, the day after Lee's meeting, a church elder named Kim came forward. He confessed that he had hated another leader, Kang, and also Blair himself, because of a careless word Blair had once spoken to him in a hurry. Then he turned and asked, "Can you forgive me, can you pray for me?" Blair stood up to pray and got no further than "Apa-ge, Apa-ge" ("Father, Father"). Then, he wrote, it seemed as if the Spirit of God came down "in a mighty avalanche of power." He fell at Kim's side and "wept and prayed as I had never prayed before."

### Kil Sun-ju

Among the Korean leaders in Pyongyang that week was an elder of the Central Church named Kil Sun-ju, whose life we follow in the next chapter. Gale tells how, on the night after Lee's meeting, Kil (Gale spells it Keel) preached on John the Baptist, who called people to repent and confess their sins (Matthew 3:6). A Korean elder told Gale that "from the first it was not Keel's face," and Gale adds, "It was Jesus, it was not Keel." *Confess* was the word Kil was given to say, and confession was what the people had to do.

Later writers, Goforth among them, add the story of Kil's own confession. As they tell it, Kil stood and said that he was like Achan, the man whose hidden theft brought trouble on Israel (Joshua 7). A friend who was dying had asked him to look after his affairs for his widow. Kil had done so, but he had quietly kept a sum of the money for himself. He promised to give it all back the next morning. The earliest accounts we have checked do not include this story, so we tell it as it was later remembered, though it fits all that we know of those days.

### Confession and cleansing

John's promise is simple, and the verses around it warn us not to pretend we are free from sin (1 John 1:8-10). God already knows. Confession does not tell Him anything new. It agrees with Him. Augustine, preaching on this very letter long ago, said:

> "Tell men what thou art, tell God what thou art. Because if thou tell not God what thou art, God condemneth what He shall find in thee. Wouldest thou not that He condemn thee? Condemn thou."
> Augustine of Hippo, *Homilies on the First Epistle of John*

In Pyongyang, confession did not end in tears. Blair writes that all through the city men went from house to house, confessing to the people they had wronged and returning stolen money and goods. The revival spread across Korea, and the schools had to stop lessons for days while children "wept out their wrongdoings together."

Blair was also honest about the dangers. Not every confession was sincere, and the missionaries worried that some people might break down under the strain. Public confession of every sin is not required of every believer. Some sins are best confessed to God alone, or to the one we have wronged (James 5:16). But Blair came to a firm conclusion: "when the Spirit of God falls upon guilty souls, there will be confession, and no power on earth can stop it."

### Three key insights

1. **When believers pray aloud together with one heart, their many voices can rise to God as one prayer.** In Pyongyang more than a thousand men prayed at once, and it was not confusion but harmony. Praying together aloud can help shy and tired believers to pray, and it reminds us that we belong to one family.
2. **Hidden hatred blocks the prayers of a church, and confessing it can open the way for God to bless.** The missionaries saw that bitterness was eating away the love of the church. When Elder Kim confessed his hatred and asked for forgiveness, the whole meeting was changed.
3. **Real repentance goes beyond tears and sets right what it can.** The believers of Pyongyang paid back money, returned stolen goods and made peace with those they had hurt. God's forgiveness is free, and it makes us want to repair the harm we have done.

### For us today

Many believers today pray in the Korean way, all together and aloud, in Seoul, Lagos, São Paulo and Manila, and in house churches in China. Others pray silently or one at a time. Both are good. Praying aloud together can help those who are afraid to pray in front of others, because no one is listening to your words except God.

Pyongyang is now the capital of North Korea, where Christians suffer greatly for their faith. The city that once heard more than a thousand men praying aloud now has believers who must pray in whispers. Remember them when you pray. And remember that many of our own churches carry hidden bitterness, between neighbors, tribes, families or church leaders. The way of victory may still be a way of confession.

### Try this

This week, practice confession and prayer together.

- **Pray aloud with others.** With your family or a small group, agree on a topic and then all pray aloud together for a few minutes, each in your own words.
- **Use 1 John 1:9.** Each evening, ask God to show you any sin from the day. Name it to Him plainly, then read 1 John 1:9 and thank Him for His promise.
- **Ask forgiveness from one person.** If you have hated or hurt someone, go to that person, confess it, and ask, as Elder Kim did, "Can you forgive me?"
- **Set one thing right.** If you have taken or kept anything that is not yours, return it, or make it good in another way.

### A closing prayer

Faithful and righteous God, You promised that if we confess our sins, You will forgive us and cleanse us from every kind of wrongdoing. Thank You for the believers of Pyongyang, who prayed with one voice and confessed their sins with tears, and who went home to make things right. Search my heart, Lord. Show me any hatred, any hidden sin, any wrong I have not set right. Give me courage to confess it to You, and to those I have hurt. Make Your church one family in prayer again, and remember Your people in North Korea who pray in secret today. Through Jesus Christ, our Advocate. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Have you ever prayed aloud together with others, all at once? What helped you, or what made it hard?
- Why do you think the revival in Pyongyang came only after the believers began to confess hatred and hidden sin?
- Is there someone you need to ask, "Can you forgive me?" What is stopping you?

## At the Break of Dawn

> "God is in the middle of the city; nothing will topple it. God will come to its aid as the morning breaks." (Psalm 46:5)

It is still dark in Pyongyang. The city wall is a black line against the sky, and the lanes below it are quiet. Then a bell begins to ring from the hill where the Central Church stands, a wooden building with a tiled roof in the Korean style. Men in white coats and women with their hair pinned up come out into the cold, climb the hill and take off their shoes at the church door.

Inside, the floor is covered with mats, and the people sit on it, men on one side and women on the other. At the front is a man of about forty. His eyes are weak. For a time he was completely blind, and he still needs someone to lead him by the hand along the road. His name is Kil Sun-ju. He leads a hymn, reads a few verses of Scripture and speaks for a short time. Then everyone prays aloud together, each person pouring out his or her own needs to God, until the sound of prayer fills the room like rain on a roof. When the sky turns gray, they put on their shoes again and go down the hill to their fields, shops and kitchens.

This is how Korean Christians remember the beginning of *saebyeok kido*, "daybreak prayer." The story is usually told like this. Kil, an elder of the church, was troubled by the cold hearts he saw among believers, and by the dangers facing his country. He and another elder, Pak Chi-rok, agreed to meet in the church before dawn to pray. Others heard of it and asked to come. The church rang its bell and invited everyone, and before long, later accounts say, hundreds were climbing the hill in the dark each morning.

Most Korean books give the year as 1906. But the Korean historian Sung-Deuk Oak, who has studied the early records closely, has shown that this date comes from a short life of Kil written in the 1930s, and that the meetings at the Central Church were most likely first announced in 1909. They were begun, he thinks, when the great revival of 1907 was cooling and the leaders longed to see its fire burn again. Small groups had already been rising early to pray during Bible study weeks since the 1890s. What Kil did was to open the dawn to the whole church.

Today Korean churches all over the world still meet to pray before sunrise.

### Who he was

Kil Sun-ju was born in 1869 in Anju, a town in the north of Korea. He studied the Chinese classics as a boy and learned something of herbal medicine. As a young man he was hungry for spiritual power. He followed the cult of an ancient war hero, and then went into the mountains for about three years to practice a Daoist way of discipline. Daoism is an old Chinese teaching about living in harmony with the hidden "Way" of the universe. The missionary James Gale, who knew Kil well, wrote in 1909, "Our dear brother, S. J. Keel, was once a Taoist." ("Keel" was the old English spelling of his name.) Gale added that some in that teaching "pray the long night through to find God."

Kil did not find God in the mountains. Back home, a Christian friend, Kim Chong-sop, gave him Christian books to read. One that moved him deeply was John Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress*. Korean accounts say that after long nights of prayer he had an experience of God he never forgot, and he became sure that Jesus is the living Lord. In 1897 he was baptized by the American missionary Graham Lee. He became an elder of the Central Church and a traveling helper of the missionaries. In 1907 he was among the first seven Koreans ordained as Presbyterian ministers.

In the last chapter we followed the revival at Pyongyang in 1907. Kil was one of its leaders. Gale tells how a Korean Bible translator, Yi Chang-jik, watched him during meetings in Seoul that same year. Yi said Kil "seemed to pray all night, pray all night and then speak three or four times a day, led here and there by the hand, and never seemed to be tired."

### Prayer in a long night

The habits of Kil's old life did not disappear. They were turned toward the living God. The man who had once sat up all night chanting in the mountains now rose before dawn to pray to his Father in heaven. The loud, shared prayer of his church grew, some historians think, partly out of the same roots. This is a gift for believers in every land: God does not ask us to throw away everything in our past. He cleanses it and fills it with Christ.

Korea needed such prayer. In 1910 Japan took over the country completely. In 1911 many Christians were arrested in a case known as the "Incident of the 105." Kil's eldest son, Chin-hyeong, was among those arrested, and he died in 1917 of illness caused by the torture he suffered. On 1 March 1919 Korean leaders signed a declaration of independence and people across the land cried out peacefully for freedom. This is called the March First Movement. Kil was one of the thirty-three signers. He was arrested and held for nearly two years before his trial, and in the end he was found not guilty. Korean sources say that in prison he read the book of Revelation again and again.

Bunyan's pilgrims also knew prison. In *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Christian and his friend Hopeful are locked in the dungeon of Doubting Castle by Giant Despair. They have almost given up hope. Then:

> "Well, on Saturday, about midnight they began to pray, and continued in prayer till almost break of day. Now, a little before it was day, good Christian, as one half amazed, brake out into this passionate speech: What a fool, quoth he, am I, thus to lie in a stinking dungeon, when I may as well walk at liberty! I have a key in my bosom, called Promise, that will, I am persuaded, open any lock in Doubting Castle."
> John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*

Psalm 46 is a song for a city under attack. The mountains shake, the nations rage, kingdoms fall. Yet "God is a shelter and a strength for us, a help always close at hand in times of distress" (Psalm 46:1). And then comes the promise of the morning: "God will come to its aid as the morning breaks" (Psalm 46:5). In the old world, armies often attacked at night. The people inside the walls watched and waited for the dawn.

Kil lived through Korea's long night and kept watching for God's morning. After 1919 he preached more and more about the return of Christ, the final dawn when every wrong will be put right. His sight grew very poor, and in 1926 he stepped down as pastor of the Central Church. But he kept traveling and preaching. On 26 November 1935, while leading a week of Bible study at a country church, he suffered a stroke and died. He was sixty-six.

### Three key insights

1. **A church that meets God at daybreak learns to face the day's troubles together.** Kil did not only pray alone before dawn. He opened the church doors so that farmers, traders and mothers could begin each day side by side before the Lord.
2. **God can take the longings of our old life and turn them toward Himself.** The young man who chanted through the night in the mountains became a Christian who prayed through the night for his people. God cleansed his hunger for the unseen and gave it its true goal.
3. **When a nation's night is long, God's people can keep watch for His morning.** Kil lost a son, his freedom and his sight. Yet he went on praying and preaching, sure that God comes to the aid of His people as the morning breaks.

### For us today

Many believers rise before dawn anyway. A farmer in Malawi goes out before the heat, a market woman in Lagos sets up her stall in the dark, a factory worker in Dhaka catches the first bus. For them the early hour may be the only quiet one.

Daybreak prayer began among such people. It was never meant to be a rule that proves who is truly spiritual, and no one should feel guilty if illness, night work or small children make it impossible. God hears prayer at every hour. But the Korean believers teach us something precious: a day that begins with God, together with His people, is held in His hands before the troubles come.

They also teach us to pray for our nations. Some of us live where believers are watched, arrested or worse. Some live where the night is poverty, violence or corruption. Psalm 46 is for us too: God is in the middle of His people.

### Try this

This week, make room for God at the start of each day.

- **Set an early meeting with God.** Choose a time before your work begins, even fifteen minutes, and keep it every day this week.
- **Begin with a promise.** Read Psalm 46 slowly on the first morning, and pray one verse of it on each morning after.
- **Pray at the same hour.** Ask one other believer to pray at the same time, in the same place or in your own homes, as Kil and Pak Chi-rok did.
- **Pray for your land.** Each morning, name one need of your country or city, and ask God to bring His morning to it.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You are our shelter and our strength, a help always close at hand in times of trouble. Thank You for Your servant Kil Sun-ju, who sought You in the dark mountains and found You in Jesus Christ, and who opened the doors of Your house before dawn so that Your people could pray together. Teach me to give You the first moments of my day. When my nation's night is long, keep me watching for Your morning. Be in the middle of Your people, and let nothing topple us. In the name of Jesus, the bright Morning Star. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think daybreak prayer has lasted so long among Korean believers?
- What habits or longings from your life before Christ has God turned toward Himself?
- What "long night" in your community or country could you begin to pray about each morning?

## Sowing in Tears

> "People who sow their seed in tears will bring in the harvest with songs of joy. People may go out in tears as they carry the seed for sowing, but they will surely come home shouting for joy, their arms full of sheaves." (Psalm 126:5-6)

One day in the Punjab, in the north of India, an American missionary set out with two Indian evangelists for a distant village. Before they left, they had prayed together, and they believed God had promised them ten people that day who would turn to Christ.

They preached and sang in the village for hours, but no one showed any interest or offered them food. The evangelists wanted to go home. The missionary would not move.

At last they stopped at a small house to ask for a drink of water. The man there gave them milk as well, and as they talked, they found that he already knew a good deal about Jesus Christ. Would he and his family follow Jesus now? He called his wife and children. That evening nine people were baptized.

By now it was getting dark, and a dangerous road lay ahead. The evangelists hurried to the cart. But the missionary stood still. Nine was not ten. "What about that one?" he asked. The evangelists were tired and angry. They almost had to carry him to the cart. Then the father of the family came out to ask why the foreign teacher was waiting. When he heard, he said that his nephew, a boy he had adopted, had just come home. The boy was brought. He spoke clearly of his faith in Christ, and he became the tenth. Only then did the missionary climb into the cart and go home, with a sigh, as McGaw puts it, "of heart's ease and weary content."

The missionary was John Hyde. His friends came to call him "Praying Hyde." This story comes from a small book called *Praying Hyde*, written by his friend Francis McGaw and published in 1923. One of the evangelists told it to a friend of Hyde's, admitting with shame that he had lost his temper that day.

### Who he was

John Nelson Hyde was born in 1865 in Carthage, Illinois, in the United States. His father was a Presbyterian minister who often prayed, at home and from the pulpit, that the Lord of the harvest would send out workers into His harvest (Matthew 9:38). An old friend of the family, quoted in McGaw's book, often heard him pray it. John studied at college and then at McCormick Seminary in Chicago, and in the autumn of 1892 he sailed for India.

On the ship he opened a letter from a friend of his father's. It urged him to seek the fullness of the Holy Spirit as the great need for mission work. Hyde later wrote that he crumpled the letter in anger and threw it on the deck. But after a while he picked it up and read it again. "Possibly I did need something which I had not yet received," he admitted. He spent the rest of the voyage in prayer.

In India he was not at first a remarkable missionary. He was slow of speech and partly deaf, and the committee scolded him for spending more time on the Bible than on language study. "First things first," he answered. In time he spoke Urdu and Punjabi well. But his real gift was hidden.

In 1904 a group of missionaries and Indian Christians formed the Punjab Prayer Union. Each member promised to pray for half an hour every day, as soon after noon as possible, for a spiritual awakening, and to keep on "till the awakening comes." That same year the first convention was held at Sialkot, a town in the Punjab that is now in Pakistan. Each autumn after that, believers gathered there for teaching and prayer, and a prayer room was kept open day and night. McGaw writes that at the 1905 convention Hyde seemed almost to live in that room.

### How he prayed

His friends saw many tears in that prayer room. Hyde wept over the sins of the world and of the church, and over people in his own district who did not know Christ. But McGaw says again and again that the tears turned into praise. He held on to the promise of Jesus: "You will be full of sorrow, but your sorrow will become joy" (John 16:20). Psalm 126 describes this: the sower goes out weeping, with the seed in his hands, but he will surely come home shouting for joy, carrying the harvest.

In 1908 Hyde asked God for something very definite: one soul a day. He did not mean people who were only curious. He meant people who would openly confess Christ and be baptized. McGaw says that by the end of that year more than four hundred had been gathered in. The next year Hyde asked for two a day, and then for four a day. We cannot check these numbers, and McGaw tells them as a loving friend, not as a careful historian. But all who knew Hyde agree that his asking was costly.

He also learned that praise belongs in prayer. McGaw records that when a day passed without the souls he had asked for, Hyde would ask God what was wrong in himself. Again and again, he said, the answer was a lack of praise. Then he would confess it, ask for a spirit of praise, and take what Isaiah calls "a cloak of praise in place of a spirit burning low" (Isaiah 61:3).

Charles Spurgeon, writing about this psalm in a little book of daily promises, saw why tears and sowing go together:

> "Weeping times are suitable for sowing: we do not want the ground to be too dry. Seed steeped in the tears of earnest anxiety will come up all the sooner. The salt of prayerful tears will give the good seed a flavor which will preserve it from the worm: truth spoken in awful earnestness has a double life about it."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith*

One more lesson changed the way Hyde prayed for others. He was once praying for an Indian pastor whose church seemed cold. He began, "O Father, thou knowest how cold," and then stopped, as if a finger had been laid on his lips. He remembered that God says of His people, "whoever touches you touches the pupil of His eye" (Zechariah 2:8). Hyde asked forgiveness for being "an accuser of the brethren." Then he asked God to show him what was good in that pastor's life, and he spent the time thanking God for it. Soon afterward, McGaw says, he heard that the pastor had been wonderfully renewed.

In 1911, very ill, Hyde left India. In England the American evangelist J. Wilbur Chapman met him at small, struggling meetings in Shrewsbury. Chapman later wrote that Hyde came to his room, knelt, and waited five minutes in silence before he said, "Oh God!" Then he prayed for others as Chapman had never heard anyone pray. "I rose from my knees to know what real prayer was," Chapman wrote. Hyde died in Northampton, Massachusetts, on 17 February 1912, at the age of forty-six. A friend in India later heard that his last words were in Urdu: "Shout, the victory of Jesus Christ!"

### Three key insights

1. **Tears shed in prayer for others are seeds that God will one day turn into a harvest of joy.** Hyde wept in the prayer room over people far from God. His tears were not wasted. Like the sower in Psalm 126, he went out weeping and came home with sheaves.
2. **Praise belongs in intercession, because it lifts the heaviness that blocks our prayers.** When Hyde's prayers felt heavy and fruitless, he found that he had stopped thanking God. Praise did not replace his asking. It set his asking free.
3. **We should not accuse others before God, but thank Him for the good He is doing in them.** Hyde began to complain to God about a cold pastor and was stopped. When he gave thanks instead, his prayer became a blessing.

### For us today

Most of us will never pray for one soul a day. But all of us know people we love who do not know Christ: a husband or wife, a son who has wandered, a neighbor, a whole village. Many of us have cried over them. Psalm 126 tells us that those tears, given to God, are seed in the ground.

Hyde was shy, slow and partly deaf. That is good news for believers who feel they have no great gifts. In Pakistan and India today, where many Christians are poor and some face danger, the Church still remembers Sialkot. The prayers of ordinary people in a prayer room changed many lives.

And Hyde warns us against a quiet sin. It is easy to pray for others by listing their faults to God. Real intercession sees others as God sees them, and thanks Him for what He has begun.

### Try this

This week, sow some seed in prayer.

- **Name your harvest.** Write down the names of a few people who do not yet follow Christ, and pray for them by name each day.
- **Let your tears become prayer.** When you feel sorrow for someone, do not hide it. Tell God, and ask Him to use it.
- **Add praise.** Each time you pray for these people, thank God for at least one good thing in their lives.
- **Pray for a leader without accusing.** Choose a pastor or leader you find hard, and thank God for the good you see in him or her.

### A closing prayer

Lord of the harvest, You see every tear Your people shed for those who are far from You. Thank You for Your servant John Hyde, who wept and prayed and praised in the Punjab, and who would not go home while one was missing. Give me a heart that cares for the lost, and teach me to sow in tears without losing hope. Keep me from accusing my brothers and sisters before You, and fill my mouth with thanksgiving. Bring many home rejoicing, their arms full of sheaves, for the glory of Jesus Christ, who wept over Jerusalem and gave His life for the world. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Hyde would not leave the village while one person was still missing?
- Have you ever found that praise changed the way you prayed for someone? What happened?
- Who in your life do you find it easiest to "accuse" before God, and how could you pray for them differently?

## Prayer Is Breathing

> "You teach me the road that leads to life; in Your presence there is joy in full, and at Your right hand delights that never end." (Psalm 16:11)

In a village called Rampur, in the Punjab in north India, a boy of about fifteen sat alone in his room through a cold winter night. His name was Sundar Singh. Behind his father's house ran a railway line, and he knew that the express train to Ludhiana passed there at five o'clock each morning.

Sundar had decided that before that train came, he would find peace, or he would lie down on the track and die.

He was the son of a wealthy Sikh family, and his mother had been a deeply devout woman. She had taken him to sit with holy men and taught him passages from the sacred books of the Sikhs and the Hindus. He had read and searched and practiced meditation, but his heart had found no *shanti*, the Hindi word for a deep peace of soul. Then his mother died, when he was about fourteen. In his grief he turned against the Christians who ran the mission school in his village. He tore up his copy of the New Testament and burned the pages in the fire.

Now, in the night, he bathed in the Hindu way and began to pray. If there is a God, he asked, let Him show Himself. Parker says he held a New Testament in his hand. Hours passed. Then, as Sundar told it later, just before dawn a bright light filled the room, and in it he saw the face of Jesus Christ, full of love. His first friend and biographer, Rebecca Parker, wrote down the words Sundar remembered hearing: "Why do you oppose Me? I am your Saviour. I died on the cross for you."

His hatred was broken, and the peace he had longed for flooded his heart. He went to wake his father and told him that he was now a Christian. His father thought he was dreaming and told him to go back to sleep. But from that night, Sundar Singh belonged to Jesus. Accounts differ on the year: many give December 1904, others 1903.

### Who he was

His family begged him, then threatened him. Sundar believed that they tried to poison him. In 1905, on his sixteenth birthday, he was baptized in an Anglican church in Simla, in the hills of north India. Thirty-three days later he gave away his belongings and put on the saffron robe of a *sadhu*.

In India a sadhu is a holy man who gives up home, money and comfort to seek God, wandering from place to place and living on what people give. Sundar wanted to show his own people that Jesus is not a foreign God belonging to the British. So he went barefoot through the villages of the Punjab, Kashmir and the north-west in the dress of an Indian holy man, telling everyone he met about Christ. He began to cross the high passes of the Himalaya into Tibet, a land closed to Christian preachers. He was beaten, stoned and thrown out of towns. The Christians of the north called him "the apostle with the bleeding feet."

Many stories are told of his journeys: of a dry well where he was left to die, of a very old hermit in a mountain cave, of wild animals that did him no harm. These are his own accounts, or later tellings of them. Even in his lifetime some readers questioned them, among them a Swiss pastor, Oskar Pfister, who wrote against him in the 1920s, while others, such as the scholars B. H. Streeter and A. J. Appasamy, wrote of him with warm trust. We cannot settle every story now. What is clear is that he walked many hundreds of miles for Christ, owned almost nothing, and was loved and trusted by Christians of many churches.

In 1920 and 1922 he traveled to Britain, America, Australia and Europe. Crowds came to hear his simple parables drawn from nature. He was sad to find so much wealth and so little prayer in the West. Back in India, his health failed. In April 1929, against the advice of his friends, he set out once more for Tibet. He was never seen again, and no one knows how or where he died.

### Prayer is breathing

Sundar Singh wrote several small books. *At the Master's Feet* (1922) is written as a conversation between "the Master," Christ, and "the Disciple," a seeking believer. Sundar said that its truths had come to him in prayer, and that he could give them only in parables. In it the Master says:

> "Prayer is, as it were, a breathing in of the Holy Spirit, and God so pours His Holy Spirit into the life of the prayerful that they become "living souls" (Gen. ii.7; John xx.22). They will never die, for the Holy Spirit pours Himself by means of prayer into their spiritual lungs, and fills their spirits with health and vigour and everlasting life."
> Sadhu Sundar Singh, *At the Master's Feet*

Sundar is thinking of two moments in Scripture. In the beginning God "breathed the breath of life into his nostrils, and the man became a living being" (Genesis 2:7). On the evening of Easter, the risen Jesus "breathed on them and told them, 'Receive the Holy Spirit'" (John 20:22). Prayer, for Sundar, is how we keep breathing that life in.

He gave a picture from the sea. Some sea creatures live in the deepest water, but they come up from time to time to take in air, and so they can live in the depths. In the same way, he says, those who rise "by means of private prayer" breathe in the Spirit of God, and find life and safety even in a world where many are drowning.

We do not breathe only in emergencies. We breathe all day, without making a show of it, and when we stop, we die.

Sundar did not think of prayer as begging God for things. In the same book the Master says that prayer is "an effort to lay hold of God Himself, the Author of life." Sundar's own prayer, at the start of the book, shows what he wanted most:

> "From Thee I ask not for the world or its treasures, nor yet for heaven even make request, but Thee alone do I desire and long for, and where Thou art there is Heaven."
> Sadhu Sundar Singh, *At the Master's Feet*

This is what David sings in Psalm 16: "my good is found in You alone" (Psalm 16:2), and "in Your presence there is joy in full" (Psalm 16:11). Sundar walked through snow and stones and hunger, often alone. Yet those who met him said he was full of joy, because he carried the presence of Christ with him everywhere.

In a later book, *Reality and Religion* (1924), he gave another picture:

> "There are some plants whose leaves and flowers fold themselves when the Sun goes down, and unfold again when softly touched on the morrow by the Sun's light. In this way do they absorb the warmth and life of the Sun, so necessary for their growth and existence. Just so in prayer our hearts are open to the Sun of Righteousness, and we are safe from the dangers and difficulties of darkness and grow into the fullness of the stature of Christ."
> Sadhu Sundar Singh, *Reality and Religion*

### Three key insights

1. **Like a sea creature rising for air, we must keep coming up to God in prayer if we are to live in a drowning world.** Sundar did not leave the world. He walked through its towns and markets. But he kept rising to God in prayer, and so he could live in the depths without being lost in them.
2. **Christ meets those who honestly cry out to Him, even those who once fought against Him.** Sundar had burned the New Testament in anger. Yet when he prayed in despair, Jesus did not turn away. He came with love and said, "I died on the cross for you."
3. **Joy in God's presence can go with us on the hardest road.** Sundar was poor, homeless and often in danger, yet he was full of joy. The path of life that God shows us does not always avoid suffering, but it never leaves His presence.

### For us today

Many people today feel that they are drowning: in debt, in grief, in work that never ends, in the noise of phones and screens. We do not need to escape to a mountain cave. We need to keep coming up for air, again and again, through the day.

Sundar's story also speaks to believers who come to Christ from other faiths, as he did. Many in India, Nepal, the Middle East and Africa know what it costs to follow Jesus when one's family is angry. Sundar shows that it is possible to love Christ with all one's heart and still love one's own people and culture, and to pray for the family that rejects us.

### Try this

This week, practice prayer as breathing.

- **Come up for air.** Choose three times in your day, such as waking, midday and evening, and stop for one minute to turn to God.
- **Breathe a short prayer.** As you breathe slowly in and out, pray a short prayer such as "Lord Jesus, fill me with Your Spirit."
- **Ask for God Himself.** At least once this week, pray without asking for anything except more of God's presence.
- **Open like a flower.** Begin one morning outside, if you can, in the first light, and ask Christ to shine into your heart for the day.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, You breathed on Your disciples and gave them Your Spirit. Breathe on me today. Thank You for Your servant Sundar Singh, who sought You in the night and found You, who walked the roads of India and Tibet for You, and who taught us that prayer is the breath of the soul. When the world is like a deep sea around me, draw me up to You again and again. Teach me to seek You more than Your gifts, and show me the road that leads to life. In Your presence there is joy in full. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- In what ways is prayer like breathing for you, and in what ways not yet?
- Why do you think Jesus came to Sundar in love, when Sundar had fought against Him?
- Where in your daily life do you feel most in danger of "drowning," and when could you come up for air?

## Let the Children Come

> "When Jesus saw this, He was angry and told them, 'Allow the children to come to Me. Do not try to stop them, because it is to people like them that the kingdom of God belongs.'" (Mark 10:14)

It was getting dark in the village of Pannaivilai, in the far south of India, in March 1901. A Christian woman, whose name in English means Servant of Jesus, found a little girl standing alone in front of the church. The child was about seven years old. Her hair was tangled and her eyes were frightened. She was looking for someone, and she could not find her.

Servant of Jesus took her home for the night. The girl was hungry, but she would not eat the rice and curry she was offered. "Am I not a Vellala child?" she said proudly, naming her caste. Eating with a Christian would break the rules of that caste. So the kind woman gave her some sugar, and she ate it and fell asleep.

Next morning the woman brought the child to the mission bungalow. An Irish missionary was sitting on the verandah reading. As soon as the little girl saw her, she ran straight to her, climbed onto her knee without the least fear, and began to talk. "My name is Pearl-eyes," she said, "and I want to stay here always. I have come to stay."

Bit by bit her story came out. Her mother had given her as a baby to the temple women of a nearby village. Once, after being punished by having her fingers burned, she had run away and walked for three days to find her mother. But the temple woman followed her, and her mother gave her back. Then she heard the women whispering that she was soon to be "married to the god." She did not fully understand what that meant, but she was terrified. She ran into the temple, stood before the idol, and begged it, "Let me die! Oh, let me die!"

To frighten her, the temple women had pointed to a Christian woman of the mission and called her "the child-stealing Ammal." So the child had gone looking for her. Soon an angry crowd came to the verandah and demanded the child back. A cruel-faced woman ordered her to say aloud whether she would come. For a long moment the little girl stood shaking. Then she cried, "I won't! I won't!" and ran back to safety.

The missionary was Amy Carmichael. She told this story herself in *Things as They Are*, the book she wrote soon afterward; the date, March 1901, comes from her biographers. In her later books the child is called Preena. She was the first of the temple children, and the beginning of a family that would one day number many hundreds.

### Who she was

Amy Carmichael was born in 1867 in Millisle, a village on the coast of County Down in the north of Ireland, the eldest of seven children. As a young woman in Belfast she began a Sunday class for the "shawlies," poor mill girls who wore shawls because they could not afford hats. After hearing Hudson Taylor speak at the Keswick Convention, she became sure that God was calling her overseas. She went first to Japan in 1893, but her health broke down, and in 1895 she came to India. There she joined the Church of England Zenana Mission and worked in the Tinnevelly district (now Tirunelveli), in the south of what is now Tamil Nadu, alongside the missionary Thomas Walker and his wife.

With a small band of Indian Christian women, later called "the Starry Cluster," Amy traveled from village to village by bullock cart, telling the women about Jesus. She wore Indian dress and learned Tamil.

When she wrote *Things as They Are* (published in 1903), she did not want to give readers a happy, rosy picture of mission work. She wanted them to see the real darkness, so that they would pray.

### Praying for the children

Pearl-eyes opened Amy's eyes. She began to learn that in parts of south India girls were given to Hindu temples as small children and trained for a life of service that included forced prostitution. It was a hidden practice, protected by powerful people, and very hard to prove. Amy and her co-workers began to pray, to search, and to take in any child they could save.

Later in 1901 they moved to Dohnavur, a village at the foot of the mountains. Babies and little girls were brought there one by one, often after long journeys. The children called Amy *Amma*, which means "mother" in Tamil. The family wore Indian clothes and never asked anyone for money. They told God what they needed.

In her book *Lotus Buds* (1909), Amy wrote, "The story of the children is the story of answered prayer." The book is full of such stories. The Dohnavur family kept a regular Prayer-day. One morning their Indian sister Ponnammal, in charge of a branch nursery, felt she must go upstairs to pray for a little child in danger. She stayed in prayer until she felt sure of the answer. That evening a bullock cart drove up, and a child who had been in danger was laid in her arms. "We covet prayer for our children," she wrote. Elsewhere in the book she explained why she told such hard stories: "We believe there is some connection between knowing and caring and praying, and what happens afterwards."

She did not pretend she knew how to pray well:

> "This sort of thing throws us back upon God. Lord, teach us to pray! Teach us the real secret of fiery fervency in prayer. We know so little of it. Lord, teach us to pray!"
> Amy Carmichael, *Things as They Are*

And the children learned to pray too. Amy called Pearl-eyes "the Elf." She was a wilful, truthful, fascinating little girl, and every evening she prayed aloud, counting her sins and her blessings with great care. Her favorite request was for another child to be rescued:

> "O Lord, bring another little girl out of the lion's mouth, but, O Lord, please let her be a very little girl!"
> Amy Carmichael, *Things as They Are*

In Mark 10, when parents brought their children to Jesus, the disciples scolded them. Jesus was angry. He did not say, "Let the important people come first." He said, "Allow the children to come to Me." Then "He took the children in His arms, laid His hands on them, and blessed them" (Mark 10:16). Jesus also said, "Whoever welcomes a child like this one in My name is welcoming Me" (Mark 9:37). At Dohnavur, bathing a baby or teaching a little girl to read was a way of welcoming Christ.

The work was costly. Children fell ill and some died. Friends misunderstood her. In 1931 Amy fell into a pit at a building site and was badly injured. For the last twenty years of her life she seldom left her room at Dohnavur. But she prayed, and she wrote many books there. Ponnammal had once written to her, when Amy had been sent away to the hills during an epidemic, "God wanted to have one of us free to pray." Those words fit Amy's own last years. She died at Dohnavur on 18 January 1951, aged eighty-three, and asked that no stone be put over her grave. By then laws in India had forbidden the dedication of girls to temples.

### Three key insights

1. **When we pray for children at risk, we share both the anger and the welcome of Jesus.** Jesus was angry when the children were pushed away, and then He took them in His arms. Prayer for children in danger joins His anger at what hurts them and His love that gathers them in.
2. **Every child rescued from danger is a reason to trust God with the next one.** Amy called the story of the Dohnavur children "the story of answered prayer." Each answer gave the family courage to keep asking for the children they had not yet found.
3. **God sometimes takes us out of the battle so that we are free to pray.** Ponnammal saw this when Amy was sent to the hills, and Amy learned it again in her long years of illness. A room can become a place of mighty prayer.

### For us today

Children are still in danger in every land. They are trafficked, forced to work, recruited by armed groups, abused at home, or left alone on city streets from Manila to Kinshasa to Lima.

Amy's story tells us that the place to begin is prayer. But her prayer was never lazy. It led to search, to risk and to love. Some of us can open our homes, support a children's home, or speak up for a child in our village. All of us can pray, and pray by name.

The Bible gives us words for this. "Raise your hands to Him for the lives of your little ones" (Lamentations 2:19). God "acts as father to orphans" (Psalm 68:5), and our Father in heaven "does not wish to lose even one of these little ones" (Matthew 18:14).

### Try this

This week, let the children come to Jesus through your prayers.

- **Pray for the children nearest you.** Name each child in your family, your street and your church before God.
- **Learn one real story.** Find out about one group of children at risk in your country, so that your prayers are informed and caring.
- **Pray with a child.** Invite a child to pray with you, and let him or her ask God for other children, as Pearl-eyes did.
- **Do one thing.** Ask God to show you one practical step you can take for a child in need, and take it.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You were angry when the little ones were turned away, and You took them in Your arms and blessed them. Thank You for Your servant Amy Carmichael, for Ponnammal and the women who served with her, and for Pearl-eyes and all the children of Dohnavur. Teach me to pray, Lord. Give me eyes to see children in danger, a heart that cares, and courage to act. Bring many little ones out of the lion's mouth, and gather them into Your family. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Jesus was angry when His disciples sent the children away?
- Amy wrote that there is a connection between "knowing and caring and praying." How does knowing someone's story change the way you pray for them?
- Which children in your community most need someone to pray for them?

## Only Believe

> "Jesus answered them, 'Have faith in God.'" (Mark 11:22)

In the early 1900s a plumber named Smith Wigglesworth lay in bed in his house in Bradford, a mill town in the north of England. For six months he had suffered from appendicitis, a painful sickness of the bowel. One evening, at the little mission hall where he was pastor, the pain threw him to the floor. Friends carried him home.

All night he prayed and pleaded with God to set him free, but no relief came. His wife, Polly, was sure that he was dying, and she sent for a doctor. Wigglesworth later told what the doctor said: his body was too weak after so many months of sickness, and it was too late for an operation. The doctor went away and left Polly broken-hearted.

Then two visitors came to the door: a young man and an old woman whom Wigglesworth knew to be "a woman of real prayer." They came upstairs to his room. The young man jumped onto the bed and, in the name of Jesus, commanded the evil that was attacking Wigglesworth's body to leave him. Wigglesworth says that he was healed at once.

Then he got up, dressed and went downstairs. "I was still in the plumbing business," he said, and he asked his wife whether any work had come in. There was a job waiting, so he picked up his tools and went out to do it. Soon afterwards the doctor came back, put his tall hat down in the hall and climbed the stairs to the bedroom. The bed was empty. When Polly told him that her husband had gone out to work, the doctor answered that they would bring him back a corpse.

Years later, telling the story to a crowd, Wigglesworth gave his own reply: "Well, I'm the corpse." By then the "corpse" was a man in his sixties who had preached in many lands.

This is the story as Wigglesworth himself told it, in a sermon printed in his book *Ever-Increasing Faith* (1924). We have no doctor's report of it. But it shows us the man: plain, practical, full of humor, and sure that the God of the Bible still acts today.

### Who he was

Smith Wigglesworth was born in 1859 in Menston, a village in Yorkshire, into a very poor family. As a small child he worked in the fields and then in a woolen mill. He had almost no schooling and could not read. Yet he hungered for God, and he came to faith in Christ at about eight; his biographers say it was through the Methodists, to whom his grandmother took him.

As a young man he learned the plumbing trade, and in 1882 he married Mary Jane Featherstone, known as Polly. She was a gifted preacher who had worked with the Salvation Army. Polly taught her husband to read, and the book she taught him to read was the Bible. He often said later that it was almost the only book he ever read. Together they led a small mission in Bowland Street, Bradford. It is said that Polly usually did the preaching, while Smith, who wept whenever he tried to speak in public, prayed with those who came forward.

In 1907 he visited Sunderland, in the north-east of England. There, after Mary Boddy, the wife of the vicar, Alexander Boddy, laid hands on him and prayed, he received what he called the baptism of the Holy Spirit and spoke in tongues. Christians differ over how to understand such experiences, and this book does not try to settle that question. What is clear is that the shy plumber became a bold preacher. Polly died in 1913. For more than thirty years after that he traveled the world: to Europe, the United States, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, India and Ceylon (now Sri Lanka). He died in March 1947, at the age of eighty-seven, in a chapel in Wakefield, Yorkshire, where he had gone for the funeral of a friend.

### Only believe

When Jairus, a leader of the synagogue, heard that his little daughter had died, Jesus told him, "Do not be afraid. Just believe" (Mark 5:36). In the old King James Bible that Wigglesworth read, the words are "only believe." These two words became the heart of his message. Again and again his sermons come back to them. In a sermon on Mark 11, the passage of our key verse, he said:

> "Jesus here uses the figure of a mountain. Why does He say a mountain? Because, if faith can remove a mountain, it can remove anything. The plan of God is so marvelous, that if you will only believe, all things are possible."
> Smith Wigglesworth, *Ever-Increasing Faith*

Notice where Jesus begins. Before He speaks of mountains, He says, "Have faith in God." Faith is not a force we create by trying hard to feel certain. It is trust in a Person. Wigglesworth took the promises of the Bible as a child takes the word of a loving father.

He also noticed that straight after the promise about moving mountains, Jesus says, "And whenever you stand up to pray, if you are holding anything against anyone, forgive them, so that your Father in heaven may forgive you your wrongdoings as well" (Mark 11:25). Wigglesworth saw that a bitter heart blocks the way of faith:

> "I realize that God can never bless us on the lines of being hard-hearted, critical or unforgiving. This will hinder faith quicker than anything."
> Smith Wigglesworth, *Ever-Increasing Faith*

### Telling the story fairly

Wigglesworth's book is full of reports of healing, from his own meetings and from letters printed in Pentecostal papers of the 1920s. People said they were healed of deafness, tuberculosis, cancer and crippled limbs. We cannot test these reports today. Some may have been mistaken, and some may have grown with each telling. Others may be exactly what they claim to be: the kindness of the God who heals.

Christians today differ on how God heals. Some expect healing miracles often; others believe God usually works through doctors, medicine and time. Both can honor Wigglesworth's faith without copying everything he did, including his rough edges. He could be blunt, even harsh, with the sick. Reports say that he sometimes struck the place where a person felt pain, and when people objected, he said that he was fighting the devil, not the sick person. This troubled many, then and now.

There is another side, too. Later writers record that his daughter Alice was deaf all her life, and that in his later years he himself suffered great pain from kidney stones. The man who preached "only believe" knew weakness and unanswered prayer in his own home. His faith did not mean that every prayer was answered as he wished. It meant that he did not stop trusting God.

### Three key insights

1. **Faith that moves mountains is not confidence in our own faith but trust in God Himself.** Jesus did not say, "Have faith in your faith." He said, "Have faith in God." Wigglesworth's boldness rested on God's greatness, not on his own strength or learning.
2. **Faith keeps trusting God when the answer is slow or different from what we asked.** Wigglesworth preached "only believe," yet he knew deafness in his family and pain in his own body. Faith rests on God's goodness and wisdom, not on our demands.
3. **God can make a fearless servant out of someone who has no education and no fine words.** The boy from the mill could not read, and the young man could not speak without tears. God used a patient wife, an open Bible and the Holy Spirit to send him across the world.

### For us today

In villages in Nigeria and Kenya, in house churches in China, in the Pentecostal churches of Brazil and Guatemala, believers lay hands on the sick every week and ask Jesus to heal. Others pray quietly beside a hospital bed in Seoul or London while a doctor does her work. All of them can learn from Jesus' opening words: "Have faith in God." Look away from the size of the mountain and look at the size of your God.

His story also speaks to those who feel they have nothing to offer. Perhaps you never went to school, or you are shy and your words come out wrong. So was Wigglesworth. God did not wait for him to become clever.

And if you have prayed and the healing has not come, do not think that your faith has failed or that God does not love you. Even this man of faith knew sickness in his own family. Faith is not a key that forces God to act. It is a hand that holds on to Him. When the father in Mark 9 cried out, "I believe! Help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24), Jesus did not send him away.

### Try this

This week, practice trusting God for one "mountain."

- **Name your mountain.** Write down one situation that seems impossible, and under it write the words of Jesus: "Have faith in God."
- **Clear the way.** Before you pray about it, ask God whether you are holding anything against anyone. Forgive, and if you can, put it right.
- **Pray for someone who is sick.** Visit or call one sick person, and with their permission pray for them simply in the name of Jesus.
- **Read with a learner.** If you know someone who cannot read well, offer to read the Bible with them, as Polly did with her husband.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You told Your disciples, "Have faith in God." Thank You for Smith Wigglesworth, the plumber who learned to read Your Word and dared to believe it. Turn my eyes from the mountains that frighten me to You. Show me where I am holding anything against anyone, and give me grace to forgive. Heal the sick for whom I pray, according to Your will and Your kindness. And when the answer is slow, or not what I hoped, keep me trusting You. I believe; help my unbelief. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Jesus said, "Have faith in God," not "Have faith in your faith." What difference does this make when you pray?
- Why do you think Jesus put the command to forgive right beside the promise about moving mountains (Mark 11:23-25)?
- How can believers who differ about healing still learn together from Wigglesworth's trust in God?

## Standing in the Gap for a World at War

> "I looked for someone among them who would repair the wall and stand before Me in the gap on behalf of the land, so that I would not destroy it, but I found no one." (Ezekiel 22:30)

In the last days of May 1940, the news in Britain was very dark. The armies of Nazi Germany had swept through the Netherlands, Belgium and France in a few weeks. Hundreds of thousands of British and French soldiers were trapped around the port of Dunkirk, in northern France, with the sea behind them. Many feared that the whole army would be lost.

On a hill above Swansea Bay, in South Wales, about a hundred people were praying. They were the staff and students of the Bible College of Wales, and their leader was a former coal miner named Rees Howells. Since the war began in September 1939, they had met for prayer every evening, often from about seven o'clock until midnight. According to their friend Norman Grubb, who drew on the college's records, in the last week of May 1940 their prayer was given over to the soldiers at Dunkirk.

They were not the only ones praying. On Sunday 26 May, King George VI called the whole nation to a day of prayer, and churches across Britain were full. That same evening the rescue began. In the next nine days, warships and hundreds of small boats, many of them fishing boats and pleasure boats, some sailed by their own owners, carried more than 338,000 soldiers across the sea to England. Many in Britain called it a miracle.

We know about the prayer meetings at Swansea mainly from Grubb's book *Rees Howells, Intercessor* (1952). Grubb, a missionary leader, tells how the college prayed through each crisis of the war, and he was convinced that their prayers were part of God's answer. That is his reading of what happened, and many readers share it. But it is a conviction of faith, not something historians can measure or prove. What is certain is that, night after night, a company of believers stood before God on behalf of their land.

### Who he was

Rees Howells was born in 1879 in Brynamman, a Welsh-speaking mining village in Carmarthenshire. He was the sixth child of a family with little money. At twelve he left school to work in a tinplate mill, and later in the coal mine. In 1901 he went to America to work in the tinplate mills of Pennsylvania. There, through a Jewish Christian evangelist named Maurice Reuben, his faith in Christ came alive. He came home to Wales in time to see the great revival we met in chapter 1.

Grubb describes a turning point at a Christian convention at Llandrindod Wells in 1906. According to Grubb, Howells sensed the Holy Spirit asking him for an unconditional surrender: if God came in, He would come as God, and Howells must give up the right to run his own life. Howells said yes. From then on he took up burdens of intercession, often for people others ignored: tramps, the poor and the dying.

In 1910 he married Elizabeth Jones, and in 1915 they went as missionaries with the South Africa General Mission to Rusitu, in what is now eastern Zimbabwe. That year a revival began there that spread through the region. In 1920 they returned to Wales. In 1924, to train young people for missionary service, he opened the Bible College of Wales at Swansea. It is said that when he bought the first house he had only sixteen shillings, a very small sum. The college charged no fixed fees and made no public appeals for money. Its needs were brought to God in prayer.

His concern reached far beyond Wales: Grubb tells that he hoped to open a home for Jewish refugee children. He died on 13 February 1950, and his son Samuel led the college after him.

### Standing in the gap

In Volume 8, in the chapter on E. M. Bounds, we met this same verse from Ezekiel and heard God ask who will step in. Let us now look at its picture. In the ancient world a city's wall was its protection. When enemies broke a hole in it, someone had to stand in that broken place, the gap, with his body between the danger and the people inside. Through Ezekiel, God says that He looked for such a person among His people, and "found no one."

In Ezekiel's day the prophets, the priests, the officials and even the ordinary people of Jerusalem had all failed (Ezekiel 22:25-29). A psalm remembers how Moses once "stood in the gap" before God for Israel, "to turn His anger away" (Psalm 106:23), as we saw in Volume 1. To stand in the gap, then, is not to stand far off. It is to come close: close to God, and close to those who are in danger.

This was the center of Howells's teaching. In Grubb's account, he taught that a true intercessor first identifies with the people he prays for, letting their need become his own before he asks God to meet it. For Howells this was not only a feeling. He gave away money, comfort and time, and lived among those he prayed for.

Some years before, the South African pastor Andrew Murray had called every believer to this place:

> "Accept of the holy calling to be an intercessor, and to burn incense before the Lord continually. Love, work, pray, believe, as one whom God has sought and found to stand in the gap before Him."
> Andrew Murray, *The Two Covenants*

Murray wrote these words in a chapter on priesthood. Only Christ can truly stand between sinners and destruction, and He has done it at the cross. Our standing in the gap is a share in the love of the One who "is always alive to plead on their behalf" (Hebrews 7:25).

### Praying about history, humbly

Howells and his students believed that Nazism was a great evil that threatened the church, the Jewish people and the freedom to preach the gospel. Early in the war Howells published a small book, *God Challenges the Dictators*, which foretold the end of the war and Hitler's fall. They prayed with great boldness, and they believed God heard them.

We can honor that boldness and still be careful. Christians in many lands prayed during that war, including believers in Germany who suffered under Hitler. God is not the servant of any one nation. When we pray about wars and governments, we may ask boldly, as Scripture tells us to (1 Timothy 2:1-2). But we leave the final judgment on our prayers, and on the events of history, to God.

### Three key insights

1. **Standing in the gap means letting another's need become our own burden before God.** The intercessor does not pray from a safe distance. Like a guard in a broken wall, Howells placed himself where others were in danger, and it cost him something.
2. **A company that keeps praying night after night can carry a burden too heavy for one person.** The college at Swansea did not pray once and stop. They met every evening through the years of the war, and each one strengthened the others.
3. **We may pray boldly about the events of history while leaving the verdict on our prayers to God.** Howells's friends believed their prayers changed battles. We cannot prove such things, and we do not need to. God asks us to pray, and He alone knows how He answers.

### For us today

Wars have not ended. In recent years believers in Ukraine, Sudan, Myanmar, eastern Congo and many other places have lived with the sound of fighting, and many have fled their homes. For them, a broken wall is not a picture from long ago.

You may never pray until midnight, but you can stand in the gap. A mother in Goma can pray for her city each evening. A student in Manila can pray each week for a country at war, by name. A small church in Peru can give one prayer meeting a month to the persecuted church. Scripture tells us to remember those in prison "as if you were in chains alongside them" (Hebrews 13:3). That is identification: letting their pain come close to us in prayer.

And we pray for all sides. Jesus told us, "love your enemies, and pray for the people who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). We stand in the gap before the God who loves the whole world.

### Try this

This week, take one place into your heart in prayer.

- **Choose a land at war.** Find one country where there is war or great danger now, and pray for it by name every day this week.
- **Come close.** Learn the name of one town there, or the story of one family, so that your prayer is for real people.
- **Share the burden.** Ask one or two friends to pray with you for that land on one evening this week.
- **Pray for both sides.** Ask God for mercy, justice and peace for the people on every side of the fighting, and for His church among them.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You looked for someone to stand in the gap for the land. Thank You for Jesus, who stood in the gap for us all and still pleads for us. Thank You for Rees Howells and the praying company at Swansea, who stood before You night after night in a time of war. Give me a heart that lets the needs of others become my own. Teach me to pray for nations at war, for the frightened and the homeless, for soldiers and for leaders, and for my enemies too. Make wars cease to the ends of the earth. I leave the answers to You, for You rule over history. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What does the picture of a person standing in a broken wall teach you about prayer for others?
- Howells taught that an intercessor first identifies with those he prays for. What might that mean for you this week?
- How can we pray boldly about wars and nations while staying humble about what our prayers achieve?

## Here Am I, Send Me

> "Then I heard the voice of the Lord asking, 'Whom am I to send? Who will go on Our behalf?' I answered, 'I am ready. Send me.'" (Isaiah 6:8)

Early one morning, around the end of 1904, a young Chinese woman named Dora Yu rose at five o'clock to pray. That day a series of revival meetings was to begin in a church in China, and she had been asked to lead the meetings for women, every morning from nine until twelve. She had agreed only because two missionary women were to do most of the speaking. Then a message came: the missionaries could come only once or twice. Almost all the work would fall on her.

The burden felt very great. She had never done anything like this before. "As a child I used to lisp and could not speak plainly," she wrote later, and she always made mistakes when she tried to tell a story. She pleaded with God to send someone else. Now, in the early morning, she asked Him for a message to give the women. No message seemed to come.

Then the church bell began to ring. She took her Bible and went in "in fear and trembling." All the way, she says, God kept telling her, "I will put my own words into your lips when the time comes." She answered Him honestly: "Lord, the time has come, the bell has rung, and the people are gathering."

To her surprise, the difficulties she had feared did not come. There was blessing in the meeting. And yet, she admits, she went through the same fear for ten days in a row, and each day God gave her words.

That frightened beginning led to one of the great preaching ministries of modern China. We know this scene because she told it herself, in a short book of her life written in English, *God's Dealings with Dora Yü* (first printed about 1916, and again in London in 1928). Much of what we know about her comes from that little book, and some dates and details are uncertain.

### Who she was

Dora Yu (in Chinese, Yu Cidu) was born in 1873 in Hangzhou, in the mission compound where her father was training to be a preacher. He had once been a physician and had come to Christ as an adult. From childhood Dora prayed and loved the Lord. At fifteen she went to Suzhou to study Western medicine at a women's hospital and medical school. Within two years both her parents had died, and she was very lonely.

As a student she was engaged to be married, but she broke the engagement, fearing that her love for God would be divided. She never married. Then, about 1895, she went through weeks of terror, sure that her sins could not be forgiven, until one night, as she tells it, "God opened heaven to me, and filled me with His divine love."

In 1897 her friend Josephine Campbell, a missionary of the Southern Methodist church, invited her to Korea, where Campbell was beginning work among women in Seoul. Dora went, practiced medicine, learned Korean and taught in the mission school. She is often called the first Chinese woman missionary in Korea. Later she judged that she had gone too quickly. She had accepted "at once," she wrote, without asking her Heavenly Father, and so she left her own country "without permission from the King." She called those years in Korea her "Wanderings in the Wilderness." Her health broke down, and she struggled in spirit.

In the summer of 1903 she sensed God telling her to go home. With only twenty-six cents in the bank, she prayed, "Lord, if Thou dost want me to return to China, give me some money." Within a month she received five times the usual amount, "for no reason I ever discovered, except that God answered prayer." This time she went as one who was sent.

### Waiting to be sent

Isaiah's call, in our key verse, did not begin with Isaiah. It began with God. Isaiah saw the Lord high and lifted up, and he cried out, "My lips are unclean" (Isaiah 6:5). Then a seraph touched his mouth with a burning coal from the altar and said, "See, this has touched your lips. Your guilt is removed" (Isaiah 6:7). Only after that did Isaiah hear God's question, and only then did he answer. Older English Bibles put his answer in words many Christians still know: "Here am I; send me."

Dora Yu's life follows the same order. Her first journey to Korea was her own idea. Her later ministry was God's sending. Charles Spurgeon, teaching young preachers in London, made the same point about Isaiah:

> "Isaiah tells us that one of the seraphim touched his lips with a live coal from off the altar, and the voice of the Lord said, “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” Isa. vi. 8. Then said the prophet, “Here am I, send me.” He ran not before he had been thus especially visited of the Lord and qualified for his mission."
> Charles H. Spurgeon, *Lectures to My Students*

The woman who lisped as a child knew what it meant to have God touch her lips. Before every meeting she felt she had nothing to say. Again and again she found that God gave her His words when the time came.

She also learned that being sent requires a clean heart. In her book she tells how God brought small wrongs to her mind: losing her temper with a shopkeeper who cheated her, an old quarrel with a schoolmate. Each time she confessed it and, where she could, put it right. In the winter of 1907 she sensed God asking whether she was still working "simply for Love's sake or because I had now become known as a worker." She stopped her work for a time, confessed that she had left her first love, and waited on God with a few pastors who felt the same need.

### A house of prayer

In 1908, after prayer with two friends, she opened a "Bible Study and Prayer House" in Shanghai, where Chinese Christians could come apart to rest and pray. She took no salary and trusted God for each need. Invitations came from many parts of China, and in her meetings people confessed their sins and were reconciled to one another. In 1916 she opened a small Bible school near Shanghai. In 1927 she spoke at the Keswick Convention in England, a rare honor for a Chinese Christian at that time. She died in Shanghai in 1931.

In 1920 she preached in Fuzhou, and among those who came to Christ through her ministry at that time were a mother named Lin Heping and her seventeen-year-old son, who became known as Watchman Nee. We will meet him in chapter 13.

Dora Yu never wanted praise for herself. After seeing a church give all its praise to two missionaries, she wrote that it is our privilege "to forward all the praises to the Creator." She was only a messenger. The One who sent her deserved the glory.

### Three key insights

1. **It is better to be sent by God than to run ahead of Him, however good our reasons.** Dora Yu went to Korea in her own hurry and called it her wilderness. When she waited for God's sending, her ministry bore fruit. God's question "Whom am I to send?" comes before our answer.
2. **God gives His words to those who come to Him feeling they have none.** She lisped as a child and feared every meeting. Like Isaiah, whose lips God touched, she learned that God speaks through weak messengers who depend on Him.
3. **Before God sends a messenger, He cleanses the messenger, even of small sins.** God showed her a flash of temper and an old quarrel, as He had touched Isaiah's lips. She did not hide them, and her honesty before God and people opened the way for revival wherever she went.

### For us today

Some of us are eager to do great things for God. Others are frightened, sure that we have nothing to say. Dora Yu was both.

If you are eager, ask God first. Sometimes the most faithful prayer is "Lord, send me," followed by patient waiting. If you are frightened, remember the bell that rang before she felt ready. God did not give her a message hours early. He gave it when the time came.

She was a Chinese woman, a doctor and a preacher, at a time when many thought the gospel in China belonged to foreigners. Today the great mission force of the world comes from Korea, China, Nigeria, Brazil, India and many other lands. God still asks, "Who will go?" He is looking for answers from every nation, from women and men alike.

### Try this

This week, practice listening for God's sending.

- **Read Isaiah 6:1-8 slowly.** Notice what comes before Isaiah's "Send me": the vision of God, the confession, the cleansing.
- **Pray before you plan.** Before you begin something new for God this week, stop and ask Him, "Is this Your sending, or only my idea?"
- **Ask for words.** Before you speak, teach or visit someone, tell God honestly that you have nothing to say, and ask Him to fill your mouth.
- **Pass the praise on.** When someone thanks or praises you this week, quietly give the praise to God, as Dora Yu did.

### A closing prayer

Holy Lord, You asked, "Whom am I to send? Who will go?" Thank You for Your servant Dora Yu, who learned to wait for Your sending and to trust You for her words. Forgive me when I run ahead of You, and forgive me when fear keeps me silent. Touch my lips and take away my guilt. Show me the small sins I have hidden, and give me courage to make them right. When the bell rings and I feel unready, put Your words in my mouth. Here I am, Lord. Send me, and let all the praise go to You. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What is the difference between going for God and being sent by God? Have you ever experienced both?
- Isaiah answered God only after his lips were cleansed. Why do you think confession comes before being sent?
- Dora Yu felt she had nothing to say before almost every meeting. Where in your life do you need to trust God for His words?

## Prayer Bands Across Asia

> "Repent, then, and turn around, so that your sins may be erased, that seasons of relief may come from the presence of the Lord." (Acts 3:19-20)

At the end of August 1935, crowds began to fill the Chinese Methodist church on Telok Ayer Street in Singapore, a port city at the tip of the Malay Peninsula. For two weeks they came to meeting after meeting. On the platform was a thin Chinese preacher in a plain scholar's gown. His name was John Sung.

He preached in Mandarin, and a Singapore church worker, Miss Wu, turned his words into Hokkien, a Chinese language widely spoken in Singapore. His sermons lasted two hours, yet one listener remembered that they were never dull. He acted out the Bible stories, drew on a blackboard, and stopped in the middle of a message to teach a short chorus until everyone knew it by heart. Above all, he spoke about sin: not sin in general, but particular sins, named one by one. And he called people to repent, to turn from them, and to put right what they had done wrong.

Among the listeners was a boy of fifteen named Timothy Tow, who later became a pastor and wrote down his memories of those days. He remembered walking to the front with forty or fifty others to give his life to Christ. He remembered how, at the end of each sermon, the whole congregation stood and prayed aloud together, a great sound of praise and pleading rising at once. Between the meetings, he says, small groups of old and young people knelt together to pray. Before, he had only repeated a few set phrases; now he prayed to God as a Father.

Halfway through the campaign, Sung asked the new believers to join what he called preaching bands. Volunteers came to the pulpit and promised the Lord that they would tell others about Christ at least once a week. Sung prayed over them. Soon the bands could be seen on the street corners around Telok Ayer, singing and giving out gospel papers. One history counts about 1,300 people who came to faith in those two weeks, and more than a hundred bands formed to carry the work on.

When Sung sailed away, believers crowded the harbor to say goodbye, waving the small triangle-shaped flags of the preaching bands, each with a red cross.

### Who he was

John Sung (in Chinese, Song Shangjie) was born in 1901 in Hinghwa, now called Putian, in Fujian province on the south-east coast of China. His father was a Methodist pastor, and as a boy John helped him so often that church members called him "Little Pastor." In 1920 he sailed to the United States to study. He was brilliant. In six years he earned degrees from Ohio Wesleyan University and Ohio State University, ending with a doctorate in chemistry in 1926.

Then he entered Union Theological Seminary in New York. There, by his own account, his faith drifted, he read widely in other religions, and he felt that his soul was wandering in a desert. Around the turn of 1927 he heard a young evangelist preach and was deeply stirred. He later dated his true new birth to 10 February 1927, and he began to preach to everyone around him.

What happened next is told in two ways. In Sung's own telling, the seminary thought his zeal was madness and had him shut in a mental hospital for about six months, where he read the Bible through many times. Recent historians who have studied the hospital and seminary records believe that he also passed through a severe mental and emotional breakdown at that time. We do not need to choose between faith and honesty here. God meets His children even in the darkest valleys of the mind, and Sung came out of that hospital with the Bible in his heart.

Late in 1927 he returned to China. The story is told that on the voyage he threw his academic medals into the sea, keeping only his diploma for his father. For the next twelve years he traveled almost without rest, first through Fujian, then with a group called the Bethel Worldwide Evangelistic Band from 1931 to 1933, and then on his own. From 1935 to 1940 he made long journeys to the Chinese communities of Singapore, Malaya, Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand, Vietnam and Myanmar, and to Taiwan.

### Repent and turn around

Our key verse comes from a sermon of Peter. A man who had been lame from birth had just been healed at the temple gate in Jerusalem, and a crowd ran together. Peter told them plainly that they had rejected Jesus. Then he gave them hope: "Repent, then, and turn around, so that your sins may be erased." The promise that follows is beautiful: "seasons of relief" would come "from the presence of the Lord." Older Bibles call them "times of refreshing."

This was John Sung's message too. He believed that revival does not come by excitement but by repentance. And he taught that it is not enough to say in general, "Lord, forgive me, for I am a sinner." In his meetings people were led to confess particular sins, to turn from them, and where possible to put them right: to return what was stolen and to ask forgiveness from those they had wronged.

A century earlier, the American revival preacher Charles Finney urged believers to write down their sins and look at them honestly:

> "Unless you do take up your sins in this way, and consider them in detail, one by one, you can form no idea of the amount of your sins."
> Charles G. Finney, *Lectures on Revivals of Religion*

### How he prayed

Those who knew Sung remembered him as a man of long and earnest prayer. At his meetings, it is said, people filled in cards with their names and their prayer needs, and he carried these cards with him from place to place and prayed for the people by name. He was also known to confess his own faults in public, especially his quick temper.

He was not a perfect man. He could be harsh, even with pastors. He was away from his wife and five children for most of each year, and near the end of his life he grieved over this. He suffered for years from a painful illness that sometimes forced him to preach sitting or lying down. In his last years, too ill to travel, he urged others to give more time to prayer. He believed, one biographer writes, that he had leaned too much on his own strength. He died in Beijing in August 1944, at the age of forty-two.

His preaching bands, though, carried the work on. They were bands for preaching, not only for prayer, but they were born in prayer, sent out with prayer, and met to pray. In many towns of China and Southeast Asia, ordinary believers kept meeting, praying and telling their neighbors about Christ long after his visits had ended.

### Three key insights

1. **Repentance becomes real when we name our sins one by one and turn from each of them.** Sung would not let his hearers hide behind general words. Peter's call is to "turn around," and turning is always from something particular.
2. **New believers grow strong when they are gathered into small bands that pray and go out together.** The preaching bands kept the fire of 1935 burning long after the preacher had sailed away. No one had to follow Christ alone.
3. **The new birth turns set phrases into the prayer of a child speaking to a Father.** Before the campaign Timothy Tow repeated words he had learned. After it he prayed to God as his Father. Repentance clears the way for that kind of honest, simple prayer.

### For us today

Many churches today grow through small groups: cell groups in Seoul and Singapore, home fellowships in Lagos and Nairobi, Bible studies in Lima, house churches in China that must meet quietly. John Sung's bands remind us that such groups are not only for friendship but for prayer and for going out with the good news.

His message also speaks to churches that have grown comfortable. Revival is not a show. It begins when believers stop excusing their sins and turn from them, one by one. Then God gives "seasons of relief" from His own presence, like cool rain after long heat.

His weaknesses speak to us too. A great work for God does not excuse us from caring for our families, and even the most fruitful servant must learn to lean less on his own strength.

### Try this

This week, take one step of repentance and one step of fellowship.

- **Write it down.** Sit alone with God and, as Finney urged, write down the particular sins He brings to mind. Confess each one and turn from it. Afterwards you may destroy the paper.
- **Go out together.** With one or two others, find one neighbor this week to whom you can speak about Christ.
- **Form a small band.** Ask two or three believers to meet with you each week to pray together and to pray for people who do not yet know Christ.
- **Pray in your own words.** If you usually pray with set phrases, speak to God this week as a child speaks to a father: plainly, honestly and in your own words.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You call us to repent and turn around, so that our sins may be erased and seasons of relief may come from Your presence. Thank You for John Sung, who called the churches of Asia to turn from their sins and gathered new believers into bands that prayed and preached. Show me my sins one by one, and give me grace to turn from each of them and to put right what I can. Give me companions in prayer, and send us out together. Teach me to carry the names of others before You, and to lean on Your strength, not my own. Send times of refreshing to Your church in every land. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Why is it easier to confess sin in general than to name a particular sin? What does Acts 3:19 ask of us?
- What could a small band of believers in your church or town do together that one person alone could not?
- What can we learn from both John Sung's great fruitfulness and his honest regrets?

## The Prayer Ministry of the Church

> "Truly I tell you, what you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and what you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven." (Matthew 18:18)

On an April evening in 1920, in the city of Fuzhou on the south coast of China, a seventeen-year-old student sat alone in his room. His name was Nee Shu-tsu. He was a clever student at a mission college, and his grandfather had been a preacher. But he had never given his own life to Christ.

That spring a Chinese evangelist named Dora Yu had come to Fuzhou to hold meetings, and the boy's mother had been deeply changed by them (we met Dora Yu in chapter 11). Soon afterwards his mother did something that shook him. She asked her son to forgive her for having punished him unjustly. In those days it was very rare for a Chinese parent to ask a child's forgiveness. He knew that something real had happened to her.

On the evening of 28 April he was alone, struggling to decide whether he would believe. Years later he described that night. As he tried to pray, he said, he saw how great his sins were, and he saw Jesus as the Savior who had died for them. In his mind he pictured the Lord's hands stretched out on the cross, as if they were waiting to welcome him. He received Christ, and he knew that his life now belonged to Him. He could not believe in Him and go on living for himself.

The boy who prayed alone that night would spend the rest of his life teaching Christians to pray together. He took a new name, Ni To-sheng, which means "the sound of the watchman's rattle." In old Chinese towns the night watchman shook a rattle to wake sleepers when danger came. Nee wanted to wake the church. In English he became known as Watchman Nee.

### Who he was

Watchman Nee was born in 1903 in Shantou and grew up in Fuzhou. After his conversion he studied for a short time at Dora Yu's Bible school in Shanghai. Then a British missionary, Margaret Barber, became his teacher and lent him the books of many Christian writers.

In 1922 he and a few friends began to meet in a home in Fuzhou to break bread, pray and read the Bible together, without a foreign mission or a paid pastor. This was the beginning of what became the local churches, often called the "Little Flock" by others. Nee taught that all the believers in one city should meet as one church, and that every believer, not only the pastor, has a part to play. By the late 1940s there were hundreds of these churches across China.

Nee was often ill, yet he preached, trained workers and edited magazines. The best known in English, *The Normal Christian Life*, comes from talks he gave in Europe in 1938 and 1939, published in 1957.

### The church on its knees

Nee gave several talks in Chinese on Matthew 18, which were later gathered into a little book called *The Prayer Ministry of the Church*. The English translation appeared in 1973, the year after his death.

Jesus said, "what you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and what you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven." Nee noticed which comes first. Jesus does not say that heaven acts and then the earth follows. He says that when His people act on earth, heaven acts with them. Then He adds, "if two of you here on earth are of one mind about anything they ask for, it will be done for them by My Father in heaven" (Matthew 18:19).

From this Nee drew a large lesson. God has a will for this world, and He could do anything He pleases. But He has chosen to work through His people. Before He acts, He wants His church on earth to want what He wants and to ask Him for it. Nee pointed to Moses on the hill: "Whenever Moses held his hand high, Israel prevailed" (Exodus 17:11). We cannot make God do what He does not want, Nee said, but by failing to pray we can hold back what He does want.

So, in Nee's teaching, the prayer ministry of the church is not mainly asking God for our own needs. Its deeper work is to find out what God wants and then to stand with Him and say, "Lord, let this be done." He compared the church to a water tap. Behind it there may be water as great as the Yangtze River, but only as much flows out as the tap allows. And because Jesus spoke of "two of you," this is not the work of a lonely hero. It is the work of believers who agree together.

He made the same point in *The Normal Christian Life*: believers cannot do without one another, and that is why praying together matters so much. Seventeen centuries earlier, the North African bishop Cyprian had noticed the order of Jesus' words:

> "...most is given, not to the multitude, but to the unanimity of those that pray. “If,” He says, “two of you shall agree on earth:” He placed agreement first; He has made the concord of peace a prerequisite; He taught that we should agree firmly and faithfully."
> Cyprian of Carthage, *The Treatises of Cyprian*

*Unanimity* and *concord* both mean being of one mind and one heart.

Christians read Matthew 18:18 in different ways. In its setting, Jesus is speaking about a brother who sins and refuses to listen, and about the church's judgment in such a case. Many have seen in it the authority Christ gave His church to forgive and to declare what God has decided. Nee applied the verse more widely, to prayer for every part of God's purpose. Some readers have felt that his phrases went too far, as if God were waiting helplessly for us. Nee himself insisted that God is all-powerful and that our prayer is simply our "yes" to His will.

### Twenty years in prison

After the Communist victory in 1949, life grew hard for China's churches. Nee was arrested in 1952, at first on charges about the business he ran. In 1956 he was tried as an enemy of the state and sentenced to fifteen years. His sentence ended in 1967, but he was not released. He died on 30 May 1972, at a labor farm in Anhui province, after twenty years as a prisoner.

His family later reported that a note was found under his pillow, written in a shaking hand. In it he confessed that Christ is the Son of God, who died for sinners and rose on the third day, and that he was dying because of his faith in Christ.

Nee's life is still debated. Some of his teaching, about the parts of a person, about one church in each city, and about authority in the church, has been questioned by many faithful Christians. Movements that came after him have been both praised and sharply criticized. In 1956 the government also accused him of sexual misconduct. His supporters believe the charge was invented to destroy him, while some who knew him have written otherwise. We cannot settle these things here. We can say that what he taught about praying together has helped believers in many lands.

### Three key insights

1. **The church's greatest work is to ask God boldly for what He Himself wants done on earth.** Nee taught that God chooses to work through the prayers of His people. When we learn His will from Scripture and ask Him for it, we are not bending God to our wishes. We are saying "yes" to His.
2. **Believers need one another in prayer, because some answers come only when we pray together.** Jesus promised an answer when two agree on earth. A lonely Christian may pray, but the Lord gave a special promise to those who seek Him with one mind.
3. **Prayer that claims to agree with heaven must be tested by Scripture, not by the strength of our feelings.** Nee's teaching has been used wisely and unwisely. The safest way to pray with authority is to stay close to what God has clearly said, and to stay humble.

### For us today

Many believers in Asia still meet in homes, as the first local churches did. In Iran, in North Korea and in parts of Africa, small groups gather quietly to pray, often at great risk. Nee's teaching speaks to them with special force. A few believers, agreed in Christ, are not small in God's eyes.

His teaching also speaks to busy, comfortable churches, where many prayer meetings are small or have stopped. Nee would ask us: if God has chosen to work through the prayers of His people, what is He waiting to do among us?

### Try this

This week, practice praying with others for God's will.

- **Find one partner.** Ask a friend to pray with you once this week, even for ten minutes.
- **Agree first.** Before you pray, read Matthew 18:18-20 together and talk about what you both believe God wants in one situation.
- **Pray God's will back to Him.** Take a prayer from Scripture, such as "May Your will be done on earth, as it is done in heaven" (Matthew 6:10), and ask Him to do it in your street or city.
- **Pray for a house church.** Pray for believers who must meet secretly in homes today, in Iran, North Korea, China and elsewhere.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You promised that when two of us agree on earth about anything we ask, Your Father in heaven will do it. Thank You for Watchman Nee, who prayed alone as a boy and taught Your church to pray together. Forgive me for praying only for my own needs. Teach me to ask what You want, and to ask boldly. Give me brothers and sisters to agree with in prayer, and keep us humble, so that we test everything by Your word. Strengthen all who are in prison for Your name today, and let Your will be done on earth as it is in heaven. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Nee said that God chooses to work through the prayers of His church. How does that change the way you think about your church's prayer meeting?
- Why do you think Jesus gave a special promise to two or three who agree together?
- How can we learn from a gifted Christian teacher while still testing what he taught by Scripture?

## My Times Are in Your Hand

> "My whole life, every season of it, is in Your hand; rescue me from the power of my enemies and from those chasing me." (Psalm 31:15)

It was Sunday, 8 April 1945, the first Sunday after Easter. In the village of Schönberg, in the Bavarian Forest of southern Germany, a group of prisoners was locked in a schoolroom. Germany was losing the war, and the guards were moving their prisoners from place to place.

Among the prisoners were Germans, a Russian officer and a British intelligence officer named Sigismund Payne Best. One of the Germans was a pastor named Dietrich Bonhoeffer. He was thirty-nine and had been in prison for two years. The others loved him for his calm kindness.

That morning some of them asked him to lead a short service. At first he hesitated, because not everyone in the room shared his faith, and he did not want to force anything on them. But the others agreed, and so he prayed with them and spoke on the Bible texts for that day. Best later remembered that among them were the words of Isaiah, "His welts have brought us healing" (Isaiah 53:5), and the words of Peter about God's great mercy, by which "He has given us a new birth" and brought us into "a hope that is alive" (1 Peter 1:3).

He had hardly finished when the door opened. Two men in civilian clothes called for Prisoner Bonhoeffer to come with them. Everyone knew what such a summons meant. Bonhoeffer gathered his few things. He took Best aside and asked him, if he ever reached home, to give a message to his friend George Bell, the Bishop of Chichester in England. Best wrote down the message years later, so we have it as he remembered it: this was the end, Bonhoeffer said, but for him it was the beginning of life.

That night he was taken to the concentration camp at Flossenbürg. He was condemned in a hurried trial with no proper defense. At dawn on 9 April 1945 he was hanged with several other men of the resistance. Two weeks later American soldiers reached the camp. Within a month the war in Europe was over.

### Who he was

Dietrich Bonhoeffer was born in 1906 in Breslau, then in Germany, into a large and gifted family. At twenty-one he had finished a doctorate in theology. In 1930 and 1931 he studied in New York, where a fellow student took him to the Abyssinian Baptist Church in Harlem. There he heard the gospel preached with new warmth, and saw what racism did to his brothers and sisters in Christ.

When Hitler came to power in 1933, Bonhoeffer spoke against the Nazi idea of a leader who makes himself an idol. He stood with the Confessing Church, the pastors and congregations who refused to let the state rule the church. In 1935 that church asked him to lead a small seminary to train young pastors. It settled at Finkenwalde, in Pomerania, near the city of Stettin.

### A rule of prayer at Finkenwalde

Finkenwalde was unusual. The students did not only attend lectures. They lived together, and their whole day was shaped by prayer. Each morning began with worship before breakfast: a psalm, a hymn, readings from the Old and New Testaments, and prayer. The day ended the same way. And every morning each student spent half an hour in silent meditation on a short passage of Scripture, often the same passage for a whole week.

Some of the young men found this strange, but Bonhoeffer insisted. In meditation, he taught, we do not ask what a passage means for our next sermon or for other people. We ask what God is saying to us, today. We read slowly, stay with a word or phrase, and carry it with us into the day.

In 1937 the secret police closed the seminary. Bonhoeffer went on training pastors secretly in country villages. In 1938 he wrote a short book about their shared life, *Life Together*, which was published in 1939. In it he wrote about the day with others and the day alone. He taught that we must pray for one another by name, and he said, in effect, that we cannot go on despising or hating a brother or sister for whom we pray every day. As we bring them before God, we begin to see them as people for whom Christ died.

He also loved the Psalms. He called them the prayer book of the Bible, and he taught that when we pray them, we pray with Jesus Himself, who prayed the Psalms before us.

### Prayers from a prison cell

In 1939 friends brought Bonhoeffer to safety in America. Within weeks he knew he had made a mistake, and he sailed back to Germany to share the trials of his people. Through his brother-in-law he joined the military intelligence service, where some officers were secretly working against Hitler. He carried messages to church leaders abroad and helped a small group of Jews escape to Switzerland. Christians still discuss whether a believer may join a plot against a tyrant's life. Bonhoeffer himself knew that he was taking guilt upon himself, and he trusted only in God's grace.

He was arrested on 5 April 1943 and held at Tegel prison in Berlin. Some of his letters from there were later gathered into the book *Letters and Papers from Prison*. They show that the disciplines of Finkenwalde held him up in the cell. He wrote to his parents that he read the Psalms every day. He kept to the daily Bible texts of the Moravian Church. He prayed for his family, his friends and his fiancée, Maria von Wedemeyer.

In the summer of 1944 he wrote a poem called "Who Am I?" Other prisoners, he said, saw a man who was calm and cheerful, like a lord in his castle. But inside he knew a restless, frightened man who longed for freedom and for friends. Which one was the real Bonhoeffer? He could not tell. The poem ends with a turn to God: whoever he was, God knew him, and he was God's.

In December 1944, in the Gestapo prison in Berlin, he wrote another poem for his family, often called "By Gracious Powers." In it he spoke of being kept and sheltered by good powers, and of facing calmly whatever might come, because God is with us at night and in the morning, and surely on every new day. Today it is sung as a hymn in Germany and far beyond.

The words of Psalm 31:15 fit his life well. The title of this chapter follows the older English wording of that verse. The bishop J. C. Ryle, writing in Victorian England, used it to teach how a believer should pray about death:

> "We should pray to be able to say, 'My times are in Thy hands,' let it be when Thou wilt, where Thou wilt, and how Thou wilt"
> J. C. Ryle, *The Upper Room*

Notice that the psalmist speaks of his "times," every season of his life, and not only of the day of his death. The years of quiet at Finkenwalde and the months in a cell were all in God's hand.

### Three key insights

1. **Learning to pray in quiet days prepares us to pray in dark ones.** The morning psalms and silent meditation at Finkenwalde did not seem heroic. But when the cell door closed, those habits were already part of Bonhoeffer, and they held him up.
2. **When we cannot tell who we really are, we can still tell God that we belong to Him.** Bonhoeffer did not pretend to be stronger than he was. He brought his fear and confusion to God and rested in being known by Him.
3. **Our times are safe in God's hand, even when cruel people hold our days in theirs.** The psalmist prayed for rescue from his enemies, and he trusted that his life was in God's hand. Bonhoeffer's enemies decided the day of his death, but they could not take his life out of God's hand.

### For us today

Most of us will never stand before a court like the one at Flossenbürg. But many believers today are in prison for Christ, in Eritrea, in Iran, in North Korea and elsewhere. Some wait years without knowing when, or whether, they will be released. Psalm 31:15 is their prayer, and it can be our prayer for them.

For the rest of us, his life asks a simpler question: what are we doing with our quiet days? A mother in a crowded home in Lagos, a student in Seoul, a farmer in the hills of Peru: each can keep a short time each morning with a few verses and a psalm. The prayers we practice in peaceful times are the prayers we will have when trouble comes.

### Try this

This week, learn from the rule of Finkenwalde.

- **Choose one short passage.** Pick a few verses, such as Psalm 31:14-15, and read them slowly each morning for the whole week.
- **Pray a psalm aloud.** Each day, pray one psalm as your own prayer, even the parts that are hard to say.
- **Pray for those around you.** Each day, pray by name for the people you live or work with, including one you find hard to love.
- **Remember the prisoners.** Pray for one Christian who is in prison for their faith today.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, my whole life, every season of it, is in Your hand. Thank You for Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who learned to listen to Your word in quiet days and was held by it in prison and at his death. Teach me to begin my days with You. Give me patience to stay with Your word until it speaks to me. When I do not know who I really am, remind me that You know me and that I am Yours. Keep all who are in prison for Your name, and give them hope that is alive through the resurrection of Jesus Christ. In His name I pray. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Bonhoeffer's habits of prayer were formed long before he went to prison. What habits of prayer are you forming now?
- In "Who Am I?" Bonhoeffer admitted the gap between how others saw him and how he felt inside. Why is it good to bring that gap honestly to God?
- What would it mean for you to pray, "My whole life, every season of it, is in Your hand"?

## Give Thanks for the Fleas

> "Whatever happens, give thanks; this is what God wants for you in Christ Jesus." (1 Thessalonians 5:18)

In the autumn of 1944 two Dutch sisters were moved into Barracks 28 at Ravensbrück, a Nazi concentration camp for women north of Berlin. Corrie ten Boom was fifty-two. Her sister Betsie was fifty-nine, and already weak and ill.

The barracks had been built for a few hundred women. It held many more, and by the end, Corrie said, about fourteen hundred. The bunks were stacked three high and pushed close together. The straw on them was filthy and sour. The windows were broken. And the place was crawling with insects. As the women climbed into their bunks, the bites began.

The story of what happened next is told in *The Hiding Place*, the book Corrie wrote in 1971 with John and Elizabeth Sherrill. As she told it, Corrie cried out in disgust. How could they live in such a place? Betsie began to pray, asking God to show them how. Then she remembered the passage they had read together that very morning from the small Bible Corrie had smuggled into the camp. It was Paul's word to the Thessalonians: be joyful always, pray without ceasing, and give thanks in everything.

So Betsie began to give thanks. She thanked God that she and Corrie were together. She thanked Him for the Bible they still had. She thanked Him even for the crowding, because so many women would now be close enough to hear God's word. Then she thanked God for the fleas.

Corrie refused. She could not see how anyone could thank God for a flea. But Betsie said that Paul's word was for every circumstance, not only for pleasant ones. And so, unwillingly, Corrie joined her.

In the weeks that followed, the sisters held Bible readings in their barracks every day, sometimes twice. Women from many nations crowded around to hear: Dutch and French, Polish and Russian, Protestants and Catholics together. Corrie read aloud, others passed the words on in their own languages, and Betsie prayed. Strangely, the guards almost never came in to stop them. One day Betsie learned the reason. A supervisor had refused to enter the barracks because it was full of fleas.

Corrie's first book about the war, written in Dutch soon after her return, tells the camp story more briefly. Readers who have compared the two books report that the first speaks of lice rather than fleas, says that others kept away from the barracks because of them, and does not tell of Betsie's prayer of thanks. Perhaps Corrie simply left it out at first, or perhaps the story grew sharper in the telling over many years. But the spirit of it was certainly Betsie's. Everything we know of her in the camp shows a woman who gave thanks in the darkest place.

### Who they were

The ten Boom family lived above their watch shop in a narrow old house in Haarlem, in the Netherlands. The family called it "the Beje." Corrie was born in 1892, the youngest child. Her father, Casper, was a watchmaker, and Corrie became the first woman in the Netherlands licensed as a watchmaker.

Their faith was simple and steady. Every morning after breakfast, and again in the evening, Casper read the Bible aloud to the family and the workers and prayed. The home was always full of guests, foster children and people in need.

When the German army occupied the Netherlands in 1940, Jews were soon being hunted and sent away to die. The ten Booms joined the Dutch resistance and took Jews and others in danger into their home. A tiny hiding place was built behind a false wall in Corrie's bedroom. On 28 February 1944 the house was raided. The people in the hiding place were not found, but the family was arrested. Casper, who was eighty-four, died in prison about ten days later. Corrie and Betsie passed through a prison in Scheveningen and a camp at Vught before they were sent to Ravensbrück.

Betsie died there on 16 December 1944. Before she died, as Corrie later told it, she said that there is no pit so deep that God's love is not deeper still. Corrie was released at the end of December. She went home and opened houses for people broken by the war, and for more than thirty years she traveled to over sixty countries, telling what God had done. She died in 1983, on her ninety-first birthday.

### In everything, give thanks

Paul does not ask us to pretend that evil is good. Ravensbrück was evil, and Betsie never said otherwise. Paul says "Whatever happens, give thanks" (1 Thessalonians 5:18). We are not asked to thank God for the cruelty of sinful people. We are asked to thank Him in every situation, because He is still there and still good.

Hannah Whitall Smith, an American writer from a Quaker family, opened her chapter on this verse with a sharp contrast:

> "The soul that gives thanks can find comfort in everything; the soul that complains can find comfort in nothing."
> Hannah Whitall Smith, *The God of All Comfort*

Betsie's prayer of thanks did not change Barracks 28. The straw was still filthy and the guards were still cruel. But it changed how the sisters lived there. It turned their eyes from what was missing to what God was still giving. And in God's strange kindness, the very thing they hated became a shield for His word.

### The hand held out

After the war Corrie spoke often about forgiveness. In *The Hiding Place* she told of a meeting in a church in Munich in 1947. After she had spoken, a man came forward, and she knew him at once as one of the guards from Ravensbrück. He told her he had become a Christian and believed God had forgiven him, and he held out his hand and asked for her forgiveness too.

Corrie found that she could not lift her hand. She remembered Betsie and that terrible place. So she prayed silently, telling Jesus that she could not forgive this man and asking Him to give her His forgiveness. Then, as an act of the will, she put out her hand. She said that as their hands met, a warm love for this man flowed through her, a love she knew was not her own.

This story rests on her own account, first printed about twenty-five years after it happened. But it fits the whole pattern of her life. She learned that God, who commands us to love our enemies, also gives us the love to obey.

### Three key insights

1. **Giving thanks in a dark place is a way of confessing that God has not left it.** Betsie did not thank God for Ravensbrück's evil. She thanked Him for what He was still giving there, and in doing so she declared that He was present.
2. **God may hide His kindness inside the very things we complain about.** The fleas that tormented the women also kept the guards away from the Bible readings. We do not always see the purpose at once, but thanksgiving keeps our hearts open to it.
3. **The love to forgive an enemy is Christ's gift, given to those who honestly ask Him for it.** Corrie did not pretend to feel what she did not feel. She told Jesus the truth, obeyed with her hand, and found that He supplied the love.

### For us today

Many people live in hard places they did not choose: refugee camps in Uganda or Bangladesh, crowded rooms in the cities of Latin America, prisons where believers are held for their faith. Some face sickness, poverty or grief that will not go away soon. For them, Betsie's prayer is not a pretty idea. It is a way to survive.

Thanksgiving does not deny the pain. It looks for God in the middle of it. A widow in a village in Kenya can thank God for one friend who visits. A prisoner can thank Him for a verse remembered. A family with little food can thank Him for each meal and for one another.

And many of us carry old wounds. Someone hurt us deeply, and we cannot forget. Corrie's story does not tell us to pretend. It tells us to bring our hard hearts to Jesus and to ask Him for what we cannot produce.

### Try this

This week, practice giving thanks in everything.

- **Thank God for three things each night.** Before you sleep, name three gifts from the day, even small ones.
- **Thank Him in one hard thing.** Choose one situation you have been complaining about, and thank God that He is present in it.
- **Read Paul's words slowly.** Each morning, read 1 Thessalonians 5:16-18 and pray it back to God.
- **Bring one wound to Jesus.** If there is someone you cannot forgive, tell the Lord honestly, and ask Him for His love toward that person.

### A closing prayer

Father in heaven, You tell us to give thanks whatever happens, because this is Your will for us in Christ Jesus. Thank You for Betsie and Corrie ten Boom, who thanked You in a place of horror and shared Your word with women from many lands. Forgive me for complaining so quickly and thanking You so slowly. Open my eyes to Your gifts in hard places. Where there is someone I cannot forgive, give me Your love, because I have none of my own. Comfort all who suffer in camps and prisons today, and let them know that no darkness is too deep for You. In Jesus' name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- What is the difference between thanking God for evil and thanking God in every situation?
- What "fleas" in your life have you complained about? How might God be at work in them?
- Is there someone you find it hard to forgive? What would it mean to tell Jesus the truth about that, as Corrie did?

## Over the Mountains with a Hundred Children

> "Do not be afraid, for I am with you; do not glance about in alarm, for I am your God. I give you strength; yes, I bring you help; yes, I hold you up with My right hand, the hand of My righteousness." (Isaiah 41:10)

The war had come to the mountains of Shanxi, in northern China. Japanese armies had invaded China in 1937, and the little walled city of Yangcheng had been bombed and fought over. A small Englishwoman named Gladys Aylward had stayed with the people there through it all. She nursed the wounded, hid refugees and cared for children who had lost their parents. By now she was looking after about a hundred of them, from small children to teenagers.

She learned, as she later told it, that the Japanese had put a price on her head. If she stayed, she would be killed, and the children would have no one. There was an orphanage for refugee children far to the southwest, in the city of Xi'an, across the Yellow River in free China. So she decided to take the children there on foot.

The story of that journey was first told to the world by Alan Burgess, a BBC writer, in his book *The Small Woman* (1957), and later by Gladys herself in *The Little Woman* (1970). Both books are built on her own memories, and they do not always agree on dates and numbers. Many accounts put the journey in 1938; others place it in the spring of 1940. Most speak of about a hundred children; one count gives ninety-four.

They set out over the mountains with very little food. There were no roads, only steep paths. At night they slept on hillsides or in empty villages. Some days they met Chinese soldiers who shared their food. Other days there was almost nothing. The older children carried the younger ones. To keep them walking, Gladys led them in singing hymns and choruses, and they prayed together as they went.

After about twelve days they came down to the Yellow River, wide and swift. But there were no boats. The river had been closed because the Japanese were near, and the boats had been taken away. For several days they waited on the bank, hungry and afraid. As Burgess tells it, one of the girls asked Gladys whether God would open the river for them, as He had opened the Red Sea for Moses. The children knelt with her and prayed, and they sang.

In the same telling, a Chinese officer passing nearby heard them singing. He came to see who they were, and he arranged for a boat to carry them all across. On the other side the journey was still not over. There were more mountains, a train ride and more walking before they reached Xi'an. When they arrived, Gladys collapsed. She was seriously ill with fever, and for weeks she hardly knew where she was. But the children were safe.

### Who she was

Gladys Aylward was born in 1902 in Edmonton, in north London. She left school young and worked as a parlormaid, a servant who cleaned and served in a wealthy home. As a young woman she gave her life to Christ, and she became sure that God wanted her in China.

She applied to the China Inland Mission, the mission Hudson Taylor had founded, and was accepted for a three-month course. But her teachers thought she would never learn the Chinese language, and they did not send her. Gladys did not give up. She went back to work as a maid and saved her wages, coin by coin. In Burgess's telling, she once laid her Bible and her few coins on her bed and asked God to use them and her.

At last she heard of an elderly missionary in Yangcheng, Jeannie Lawson, who needed a helper. Gladys bought a ticket for the long train journey across Europe and Russia, and in October 1932 she set out alone (many accounts say 1930). After great danger in Siberia, she reached Japan by ship and then China.

In Yangcheng she and Mrs. Lawson opened an inn for the mule drivers who carried goods along the mountain roads. They called it the Inn of the Eight Happinesses. Travelers found a clean bed, good food and, in the evening, stories from the Bible, which they carried along the roads. After Mrs. Lawson died, Gladys went on alone. The local ruler made her his inspector of feet, to see that the new law against binding girls' feet was obeyed, and so she visited homes across the region and told the women about Jesus. She took in orphans, calmed a prison riot and became a Chinese citizen in 1936. The people called her Ai-weh-deh, "the virtuous one."

### A promise for the road

Isaiah 41:10 is one of the great promises of the Bible for people who are afraid: "Do not be afraid, for I am with you." A few verses later God says it again: "For I, the LORD your God, have hold of your right hand" (Isaiah 41:13).

Later retellings of Gladys's story, especially books for children, often pair it with this verse. We have not found it in the earliest accounts of the journey, so we do not claim that she prayed these words on the mountains. But few verses describe the journey better. A frightened woman with no money and a hundred children could not hold herself up. God held her up.

Gladys had been turned away by the mission Hudson Taylor founded. But she prayed on China's roads as he had. Taylor once spent a frightening night alone among strangers in China. He wrote of it:

> "As the night slowly passed on, I felt very weary; and to keep myself awake, as well as to cheer my mind, I sang several hymns, repeated aloud some portions of Scripture, and engaged in prayer in English"
> J. Hudson Taylor, *A Retrospect*

That is what Gladys did with her children. When they were tired, hungry and afraid, they sang, they remembered God's word, and they prayed.

### The film and the real woman

In 1958 her story became a famous film, *The Inn of the Sixth Happiness*, with the Swedish actress Ingrid Bergman. It made Gladys known around the world, but she was deeply unhappy with it. The tall Bergman looked nothing like the small, dark-haired Londoner. Names were changed, even the name of the inn. Worst of all, the film added a love story with a Chinese officer and ended with her leaving the children. Gladys had never had such a romance, and she felt the film damaged her good name.

After the war she returned to Britain for some years and spoke widely about China. Then, unable to go back to mainland China, she settled in Taiwan, where she founded a home for orphans. She died in Taipei in 1970.

### Three key insights

1. **Courage is not the absence of fear, but fear brought to God one day at a time.** Gladys was often afraid. She was hunted, hungry and ill. But each day she brought her fear to God and took the next step.
2. **God's promise to hold us up is meant to be prayed back to Him on the hardest days.** "I hold you up with My right hand" is not only a verse to admire. It is a word to repeat to God when our own strength is gone.
3. **The simple faith of a child in danger can call grown believers back to prayer.** On the riverbank one girl asked whether God would open the river, and the whole company knelt to pray. Praying with children is not only teaching them. It is learning with them.

### For us today

Millions of people today are refugees, forced from their homes by war: in Sudan, in Ukraine, in Myanmar, in the Democratic Republic of Congo and in many other places. Many of them are children. Some are walking long roads right now, as Gladys's children did. We can pray for them by name and country, and we can support those who care for them.

Gladys also speaks to those who feel too ordinary for God's work. She was a servant girl with little schooling. Experts said she could not learn Chinese. Yet God used her. You may not have education, money or position. You can still pray, offer God what you have, and trust Him to hold you up.

### Try this

This week, pray God's promises in your fears.

- **Learn the promise.** Memorize Isaiah 41:10, or just its first words, "Do not be afraid, for I am with you."
- **Name your fears.** Write down what you are afraid of, and pray the verse over each one.
- **Sing when you are afraid.** When fear comes this week, sing a hymn or chorus you know, as Gladys and the children did on the road.
- **Pray for refugees.** Choose one country where families are fleeing war, and pray for them every day this week.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You say to Your people, "Do not be afraid, for I am with you." Thank You for Gladys Aylward, the servant girl whom You sent to China and held up on the mountains with her children. When I am afraid, remind me that You hold my right hand. Teach me to bring my fears to You and to take the next step in faith. Protect the children who are fleeing war today, and send people to care for them. And let my prayers, like the children's songs by the river, reach You and bring help in time. In Jesus' name. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Gladys was turned away by a mission, but she did not give up her call. When have you felt "not good enough" for God's work?
- What fears are you carrying now? How might Isaiah 41:10 change the way you pray about them?
- What can children teach us about prayer, and how could you pray with a child this week?

## Sleeper, Wake Up

> "Sleeper, wake up! Get up from among the dead, and Christ will give you light." (Ephesians 5:14)

In September 1929 a tired English doctor climbed Namirembe Hill in Kampala, the capital of the kingdom of Buganda in Uganda. The doctor was Joe Church, thirty years old, a missionary of the Ruanda Mission. He ran a small hospital at Gahini, in the east of Rwanda. He had arrived in 1928, in a time of terrible famine, and had become exhausted caring for the starving. Now he had come to Kampala for a short rest, feeling empty.

On the hill he met a Ugandan man named Simeon Nsibambi. Nsibambi was a public health officer in the city, a respected churchman with a good post and a growing family. But he was not satisfied. He told the doctor that something was missing, in his own life and in the church around him, and he asked whether Church knew what it was.

Church shared what he had learned in England about the Holy Spirit and a life fully given to God, though he had not yet found it himself. Then the two men did a simple thing. For about two days, as both of them later remembered, they sat together with the Bible open between them. They looked up what the Scriptures say about the Holy Spirit, and they prayed. They confessed the sins they knew of, they asked to be filled, and they asked God to take everything.

Nothing visible happened that weekend, but both men had changed. Church went back to Gahini. Nsibambi soon gave up his government post and gave his days to prayer and to speaking with people about Christ. People remembered that he would ask almost anyone he met, with great earnestness, whether they were saved.

Historians of the East African Revival usually point to those two days on Namirembe Hill as its beginning. From that meeting an awakening spread through Rwanda, Uganda, Kenya, Tanzania and beyond, and it went on for most of the century. Its people came to be called the *balokole*, a Luganda word that means "the saved ones."

### Where the story comes from

Much of what we know comes from Joe Church himself: a small book about Blasio Kigozi written soon after his death, called *Awake!*, and much later an account of the whole movement, *Quest for the Highest* (1981). We retell them in our own words. Church saw the revival through a missionary's eyes; Ugandan and Rwandan believers told it too, mostly by word of mouth, and dates and details sometimes differ.

What is clear is that the revival was led by Africans. Nsibambi never held a church office, yet many called him a father of the revival. Yosiya Kinuka, the senior hospital assistant at Gahini, did not get along with Church. In 1931 the doctor sent him north to talk with Nsibambi. Kinuka came back changed, and he and Church learned to confess their faults to each other. At a convention at Gahini over Christmas and New Year 1933 to 1934, where Nsibambi and his younger brother Blasio Kigozi spoke, the awakening broke out.

Blasio Kigozi was born in 1909. His older brother had led him into a living faith, and he went south to Gahini, where he was ordained a deacon and ran the school for evangelists. He was known for long, burning prayer. In January 1936 he traveled to Kampala for the synod, the great meeting of the leaders of the Church of Uganda. He carried one message, in a single Luganda word: *Zukuka*, "Awake!" On the journey he caught tick fever. By one account he made his appeal to the synod and then died; another says he fell ill before he could speak. He died in hospital in Kampala on 25 or 26 January 1936 (the accounts differ), at about twenty-six. Nsibambi led his funeral on Namirembe Hill, and the word *Zukuka* was cut into his gravestone. His early death moved many more deeply than any speech.

### Walking in the light

The revival's people loved 1 John 1:7: "But if we walk in the light, just as He is in the light, we share fellowship with one another, and the blood of Jesus, His Son, goes on cleansing us from every sin." They called their way of life *walking in the light*. It meant living with nothing hidden, before God and before one another. When the Spirit showed a believer a sin, he confessed it plainly, by name. If he had wronged someone, he went to that person and put it right. Stolen goods were returned. African and European believers confessed to each other, which in colonial East Africa was rare and surprising.

In this the balokole stood in an old line. Catherine Booth of the Salvation Army had taught in London that honesty with God is a condition of answered prayer:

> "Walk in the light, and then you shall have fellowship with Him, and His blood will cleanse you from all sin, and the Spirit will teach you how to pray and what to pray for, which the great mass of professing Christians know nothing about."
> Catherine Booth, *Godliness*

The second mark of the revival was a song. Wherever they gathered, in churches, on the road, at graves and in prison, the balokole sang a short hymn in Luganda: *Tukutendereza Yesu, Yesu Omwana gw'endiga, Omusaayi gwo gunaazizza, Nkwebaza, Omulokozi*. In English: "We praise You, Jesus; Jesus, Lamb of God; Your blood has cleansed me; I thank You, Savior." It is a song about Jesus, not about the singers.

Kigozi's call matches our key verse. Paul wrote Ephesians 5:14 to a church, not to unbelievers. A church can be asleep while it is still busy. Two centuries earlier, Charles Wesley had preached on the same verse at Oxford, and he described the deepest sleeper of all:

> "If this sleeper be not outwardly vicious, his sleep is usually the deepest of all: whether he be of the Laodicean spirit, “neither cold nor hot,” but a quiet, rational, inoffensive, good-natured professor of the religion of his fathers; or whether he be zealous and orthodox, and, “after the most straitest sect of our religion,” live “a Pharisee;” that is, according to the scriptural account, one that justifies himself; one that labours to establish his own righteousness, as the ground of his acceptance with God."
> Charles Wesley, in John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

A *professor* means someone who professes the faith. The sleepers who most need waking may be quiet, polite church members. But the verse ends with a promise. When we wake, "Christ will give you light."

The revival had its shadows. Critics said that some confessions were too public and too detailed, and that some of the "saved ones" seemed proud and quick to judge. At times the movement quarreled with church leaders and divided within. Some of this criticism was fair. Yet the revival filled churches across East Africa with people who confessed their sins, forgave their enemies and sang about the blood of Jesus.

### Three key insights

1. **A church can be busy and still be asleep, and only the light of Christ can wake it.** Nsibambi had a good job and a place in the church, yet he said that something was missing. Kigozi's call to the synod was the same: wake up. Waking begins when we admit that we have been asleep.
2. **Walking in the light means putting things right with one another, and it can turn old rivals into a praying family.** Joe Church and Yosiya Kinuka learned to confess their faults to each other, and African and European believers did the same. The blood of Jesus that cleanses us also joins us together.
3. **Two believers who kneel as equals over God's word can become the seed of a movement that crosses every line.** A Ugandan health officer and an English doctor studied the Bible and prayed together as brothers. From that humble partnership came a movement that crossed tribe, nation and race.

### For us today

Many churches today are like the church Kigozi spoke to: busy, well organized and asleep. That is true in old churches of Europe and in churches of Africa, Asia and Latin America now in their second or third generation. The call *Zukuka* is for all of us. It starts with me, not with the people I want to wake up.

The revival also shows that God can begin something great with no money, no plan and no platform. Two nurses on a night shift in Manila, or a student and a farmer in a house church in China, can do what Church and Nsibambi did.

In many cultures it is shameful to admit a fault, yet the balokole did it and found freedom. Sometimes the best prayer is followed by a visit to say, "I was wrong. Please forgive me."

### Try this

This week, let the balokole teach you to walk in the light.

- **Ask the honest question.** Ask God a question like Nsibambi's: "Lord, what is missing in me?" Then wait quietly, and write down what comes to mind.
- **Study with one other person.** Spend an evening with a friend reading what the New Testament says about the Holy Spirit, then pray for each other.
- **Walk in the light with someone.** Meet with a believer you trust, tell each other honestly where you have failed, and pray for each other.
- **Learn the song.** Memorize the four lines of *Tukutendereza Yesu*, in Luganda or in your own language, and sing them when you are tempted or afraid.

### A closing prayer

Lord Jesus, You call to us, "Sleeper, wake up!" I confess that I am often asleep while I look busy. Thank You for Simeon Nsibambi and Joe Church, for Yosiya Kinuka and Blasio Kigozi, and for the saved ones of East Africa who walked in the light. Show me what is missing in me. Bring my hidden sins into Your light, and give me courage to confess them and to put right what I have done wrong. Lamb of God, Your blood has cleansed me, and I thank You, my Savior. Wake up Your church, and begin with me. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Nsibambi said that something was missing in himself and in his church. Is there anything you feel is missing in your own walk with God?
- Why do you think confession to one another, and not only to God, brought such power to the East African Revival? What are its dangers?
- Who could you invite to read the Bible and pray with you, as Church and Nsibambi did?

## Two Old Sisters on Lewis

> "For I will pour out water on parched ground and send streams across the dry land; I will pour out My Spirit on your children, and My blessing on those who come after you." (Isaiah 44:3)

The Isle of Lewis lies far out in the Atlantic Ocean, off the north-west coast of Scotland. It is the largest of the Outer Hebrides, a land of wind, rain, peat bogs and low stone houses. In 1949 most of its people lived in small villages along the coast. They kept sheep and cattle on little farms called *crofts*, cut peat for their fires, wove cloth and went to sea. Their first language was Gaelic, the old Celtic language of the Highlands. They were a churchgoing people, with a long and serious Presbyterian faith. But many of the young were drifting away from the churches, and the older believers grieved over it.

In the village of Barvas, on the west side of the island, lived two elderly sisters, Peggy and Christine Smith. As the story is usually told, Peggy was eighty-four and blind, and Christine was eighty-two and bent with arthritis. They were too frail to go to public worship. But they could pray, and they did. In their small cottage, the story says, they prayed through the night twice a week, from late evening until the early hours of the morning. They asked God to send revival to their parish. And they held on to one promise above all others, God's word in Isaiah 44:3: water on the dry land, and His Spirit poured out on the children.

Duncan Campbell, who told the story most often, said that one night Peggy received an assurance from God that revival was coming. She believed she saw the churches crowded again with young people. She sent for her minister, James Murray MacKay of the parish church, and urged him to gather the leaders of the church to pray.

MacKay did so. Some men of the congregation began to meet in a barn two or three nights a week to pray. For weeks, nothing seemed to change. Then, as Campbell told it, one night a young deacon stood up and read from Psalm 24: "Who has the right to climb the mountain of the LORD? Who may stand in the place of His holiness? Those whose hands are innocent and whose hearts are pure" (Psalm 24:3-4). He told the others that their prayers were empty unless they themselves were right with God. Then he knelt and asked God whether his own hands were clean and his own heart pure. The men came to a new humility before God, and they felt that the Lord was very near.

MacKay invited Duncan Campbell, a Gaelic-speaking evangelist of the Faith Mission, to come and preach. At first Campbell said he could not come. But his plans changed, and in December 1949 he arrived on Lewis. Within days, by his account, the meetings in Barvas were crowded, and they went on late into the night. Over the next two years, the awakening spread to other villages on Lewis and to Harris and to the small island of Berneray. Many people came to faith.

### Where the story comes from, honestly told

Almost everything in the famous version of this story comes from Duncan Campbell. He wrote a short booklet, *The Lewis Awakening 1949-1953*, and for twenty years he told the story in talks and sermons around the world. His booklet and talks are still under copyright, so we have told them here in our own words.

Historians who have studied the revival have raised careful questions about these accounts, and we should listen to them. Campbell gave many of his talks years after the events, and the details change from one telling to another. Other people were praying too. In 1949 the Free Church presbytery of Lewis called its congregations to consider the low state of religion on the island, and there was already a stirring in at least one Free Church congregation before Campbell came. Lewis had also known earlier revivals, including one in 1939. Some ministers, especially in the Free Church, were wary of the Faith Mission and of the strong feelings and fainting seen in some meetings. And many of the large numbers and dramatic details now repeated on the internet have no basis in the records. Even the ages of the sisters, and how much their prayers led to Campbell's coming, are told differently in different places.

So what can we say with confidence? There was a real and lasting revival in parts of Lewis and Harris between 1949 and 1952. People of the island remembered it for the rest of their lives. And there were believers, old and young, women and men, who prayed for it for a long time before it came, among them two old sisters in a cottage in Barvas. God does not need us to make the story larger than it was. The plain truth is enough.

### Praying from the promise

The sisters did not simply tell God their feelings. They prayed His word back to Him. Isaiah 44:3 was spoken to God's people in exile, a people who felt dry and forgotten. God promised to pour out water on parched ground, and to pour out His Spirit on their children. The sisters' Bible, in the older wording, spoke of water poured on the one who is thirsty. They knew they were thirsty. That was the beginning of their prayer.

John Calvin, whose teaching shaped the churches of Scotland, quoted this very promise, along with others like it, and then said who they are for:

> "These passages declare, that none are admitted to enjoy the blessings of God save those who are pining under a sense of their own poverty."
> John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*

Three hundred years before the Lewis revival, Samuel Rutherford, a Scottish minister of the 1600s, wrote a letter to a friend about the same verse. He longed to see God water Scotland, and he believed it would come. But he also knew that waiting is hard:

> "But when this shall be in Scotland (and it must be) is better to believe than prophesy; and quietly to hope and sit still (for that is yet our strength), than to quarrel with Him, that the wheels of this chariot move leisurely."
> Samuel Rutherford, *Letters of Samuel Rutherford*

*Leisurely* means slowly, without hurry. Rutherford's advice fits the sisters well. They did not try to set God's timetable. They believed, they hoped and they kept praying, night after night, for a long time. And in the barn, the men learned the other half of the lesson: the ones who pray for revival must first come to God with clean hands and a pure heart.

### Three key insights

1. **No one is too old, too weak or too hidden to pray for revival.** Two elderly sisters, one blind and one bent with pain, could not attend church. Yet their cottage became a place of intercession for the whole parish. Prayer is the one ministry that weakness cannot take away.
2. **Revival is God's gift in God's time, so we may keep believing and waiting without quarreling with Him.** The sisters prayed for a long time before anything changed. Rutherford knew that the wheels of God's chariot often seem to move slowly, and that quiet hope is our strength.
3. **God often answers many hidden prayers together, so no one who prays should claim the revival as their own.** The sisters prayed in their cottage, the men prayed in the barn, and others prayed in churches across the island. God heard them all, and the glory is His alone.

### For us today

Many churches in the world have members like Peggy and Christine: grandmothers and grandfathers who can no longer walk to the meetings, believers who are blind or sick in bed, people in hospitals, in prisons, in homes for the elderly. They may feel useless. The story of Lewis says to them: your prayers matter. You may be the hidden strength of your church.

It also speaks to those who long for revival in a land that seems spiritually dry: a city in Europe where few go to church, a village in Asia where believers are a tiny minority, a church in Africa or Latin America whose young people are leaving. God's promise has not changed. He still pours water on the thirsty.

And the honest history of Lewis teaches us something too. We do not need to make the stories of revival bigger or neater than they were. We can thank God for what He truly did, admit what we do not know, and keep praying.

### Try this

This week, learn from the praying sisters of Barvas.

- **Pray a promise.** Write out Isaiah 44:3, and pray it each day for your church, your town or your family, naming those you long to see come to faith.
- **Keep a night watch.** Choose one evening this week to stay up an extra hour for prayer, alone or with someone in your home.
- **Visit a hidden intercessor.** Go to an elderly or sick believer, ask them to pray for your church, and tell them what to pray for.
- **Read Psalm 24 slowly.** Ask God to search your own hands and heart before you pray for others.

### A closing prayer

Lord God, You promised to pour out water on parched ground and Your Spirit on our children. Thank You for Peggy and Christine Smith, who prayed in the night, for the men in the barn who let You search their hearts, and for all who prayed for the people of Lewis. We are thirsty, Lord. Our churches and our lands are dry. Search my heart and make my hands clean. Teach me to hold on to Your promises and to wait without quarreling with Your timing. Pour out Your Spirit on our young people, and let them come to You. In the name of Jesus Christ. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- Who are the "hidden intercessors" in your church or family? How could you honor and encourage them?
- Which promise of God could you pray back to Him for your church or your nation?
- Why do you think the men in the barn had to face their own hearts before revival came? What might God want to search in you?

- Charles H. Spurgeon, *Morning by Morning*, especially "November"

## The Praying Men of Charlotte

> "So it is with the word that leaves My mouth: it will not come back to Me with nothing done, but it will do what I want and succeed in the errand I sent it on." (Isaiah 55:11)

In May 1934 a group of Christian men walked out to a dairy farm on the edge of Charlotte, a city in North Carolina in the south-eastern United States. It was the time of the Great Depression. Banks had failed, many people had lost their jobs, and families everywhere were struggling. Most of them were businessmen, members of a Christian men's club in the city. They believed their city needed more than better times. It needed God.

They had decided to spend a whole day in prayer. The farmer, William Franklin Graham, known as Frank, let them use a quiet place on his land, a pasture with a grove of trees. There the men prayed for their city and for a coming series of gospel meetings. One of them, a businessman named Vernon Patterson, prayed a prayer that the Graham family never forgot. As Frank Graham remembered it, Patterson asked God to raise up from Charlotte someone who would preach the gospel to the ends of the earth.

Frank Graham's eldest son was fifteen that spring. He milked the cows, played baseball and loved adventure stories. His name was Billy, and he was not yet a believer. Years later he wrote, in effect, that at the time it was certainly not obvious that the someone might be him.

That autumn the gospel meetings they had prayed for began. An evangelist from Kentucky named Mordecai Ham came to Charlotte and preached for weeks in a large temporary tabernacle on the edge of town. Billy did not want to go. But a worker on the farm, Albert McMakin, persuaded him to come along. Night after night Billy went back. As he later told it, he even joined the choir, though he could not sing, so that the preacher's pointing finger would not seem aimed at him.

One night that autumn, shortly before or after his sixteenth birthday (the accounts differ on the exact date), Billy Graham went forward at the end of the meeting and gave his life to Jesus Christ.

### Who he was

Billy Graham was born on 7 November 1918 and grew up on that dairy farm. After his conversion he studied in Florida and then at Wheaton College in Illinois, where he met Ruth Bell, the daughter of missionaries to China. They married in 1943. He became a Baptist preacher and an evangelist with Youth for Christ.

In the summer of 1949 he faced a crisis. A close friend had begun to doubt that the Bible could be trusted, and his questions shook Billy. At a mountain conference center called Forest Home, in California, he went out alone into the woods one night, as he later told the story in his autobiography, *Just As I Am* (1997). He laid his Bible on a tree stump and prayed. He told God that he could not answer every question, but that he would accept the Bible by faith as God's word. From that night on, he preached with a new certainty. "The Bible says" became his familiar phrase.

A few weeks later, on 25 September 1949, his meetings began in Los Angeles in a huge tent at the corner of Washington Boulevard and Hill Street. They were planned for three weeks. But people kept coming, and they kept coming forward to receive Christ. A popular radio singer named Stuart Hamblen was converted. So was Louis Zamperini, an Olympic runner who had survived the war as a prisoner. Newspapers began to write about the young preacher. The meetings went on for about eight weeks. From that time on, Graham preached the gospel for more than sixty years, to crowds in every continent. He died on 21 February 2018, at the age of ninety-nine.

His own books are still under copyright, so we tell his story here in our own words.

### Prayer behind the preaching

Graham often spoke of how much his meetings depended on prayer. The Los Angeles campaign was prepared for months by many groups of praying people across the city. Some of the meetings had all-night prayer in a smaller tent beside the big one. A minister named Armin Gesswein led much of this prayer.

Two centuries earlier, George Whitefield had urged ordinary church members to pray for their ministers, and even for ministers not yet sent:

> "But how do they deserve good pastors, who will not earnestly pray to God for such? If we will not pray to the Lord of the harvest, can it be expected he will send forth laborers into his harvest?"
> George Whitefield, *Selected Sermons of George Whitefield*

The men of Charlotte did something even more surprising. They prayed for a preacher they had never met, who had not yet been converted. God answered them in a way none of them could have planned. The boy they needed was already living on the farm where they prayed.

Our key verse explains why prayer and preaching belong together. In Isaiah 55, God compares His word to rain and snow. They fall from the sky and do not go back up until they have watered the ground and made it bud and grow (Isaiah 55:10). In the same way, God's word "will not come back to Me with nothing done." It is sent on an errand, and it will succeed. So those who preach, and those who pray for them, can expect God to bless His word and can look for the people He has touched.

### An honest word

God used imperfect people in this story, as He always does. Mordecai Ham preached the gospel with great power, but in some of his preaching he also spread prejudice against Jewish people and Black Americans. Christians today rightly reject it. Graham himself grew up in a segregated South. In 1953 he removed the ropes that separated Black and white listeners at a meeting in Chattanooga, Tennessee, and from then on he insisted that his meetings be open to all. Later Christians have also debated some of his choices, such as his closeness to presidents. But to the end he saw himself as a simple messenger, and he gave the glory to God.

### Three key insights

1. **We can pray for workers God has not yet called, and He may already be preparing them.** The men of Charlotte asked God to raise up a preacher from their city. The answer was a farm boy who was not yet a believer, milking cows a short walk away. Jesus told us to ask the Lord of the harvest to send out workers (Matthew 9:38).
2. **God's word does not return empty, so we can pray and speak it with confidence.** Isaiah promises that God's word will succeed in its errand, like rain that waters the ground. Graham's preaching rested on that confidence, and so can our witness.
3. **Ordinary believers who pray for the preacher share in the harvest that follows.** The businessmen in the pasture, the farm worker who brought Billy to the tabernacle and the prayer groups of Los Angeles did not stand in the pulpit. Yet God's work was theirs too.

### For us today

Most of us will never preach to great crowds. But all of us can do what the men of Charlotte did. A group of mothers in Nairobi can pray for the children of their church. Workers in Seoul can meet before their shift to pray for their city. A small congregation in a village in Bolivia can ask God to raise up from among them someone who will carry the gospel to peoples who have not yet heard it.

Notice that the answer took years. The men prayed in 1934. Billy was converted that autumn, but the Los Angeles meetings came fifteen years later, and the worldwide preaching came after that. Many of those who prayed did not live to see the whole answer. We pray in faith, and we leave the timing to God.

Notice too that God's answer was close at hand. Perhaps the worker God will use is already in your own church, your family or your school, still young and not yet believing. Pray for the young people near you by name.

### Try this

This week, join the praying men of Charlotte.

- **Pray for a worker.** Ask God to raise up someone from your church or town to carry the gospel to the ends of the earth, and pray for the young people you know by name.
- **Pray for your pastor before he preaches.** Before the next service, spend ten minutes asking God to fill your pastor or Bible teacher with His Spirit.
- **Bring one friend.** Like Albert McMakin, invite one person to come with you to hear God's word preached.
- **Pray Isaiah 55:11.** When you share a Bible verse with someone, pray that God's word will succeed in the errand He sent it on.

### A closing prayer

Lord of the harvest, Your word will not come back to You with nothing done. Thank You for the men who prayed in a pasture in Charlotte, for Vernon Patterson's bold prayer, and for Billy Graham, whom You raised up to carry the gospel to the ends of the earth. Raise up workers from my city and my church, even from among those who do not know You yet. Bless those who preach Your word, and let it do what You want. Teach me to pray, to invite, and to trust You with the harvest. In the name of Jesus. Amen.

### Questions for reflection

- The men of Charlotte prayed for someone they had never met. Whom could you pray for in that way?
- Why do you think God chose to answer their prayer through a farm boy who was not yet a believer?
- How does Isaiah 55:11 give you confidence when you share God's word with others?

## The Pursuit of God

> "Like a doe that longs for running streams, I long for You, God, with all my being." (Psalm 42:1)

In 1948 a thin, plainly dressed pastor from Chicago boarded a train going south. He was A. W. Tozer, about fifty years old, the minister of a church on the South Side of Chicago. He was on his way to Texas. He did not own a car, and he liked trains. On a long train journey a man can read, think and pray without being disturbed.

The story is told that on this journey a book he had carried in his heart for a long time began to pour out. He wrote, so the story goes, through the night, as fast as his pen could move. By the time the train reached McAllen, in the far south of Texas near the Mexican border, the rough draft of a short book was finished.

We should treat this story with some caution. It comes to us from those who knew him and from later writers, not from Tozer's own book, and it may have grown in the telling. But the book is real. Tozer signed its preface in Chicago on 16 June 1948, and it was published that year. He called it *The Pursuit of God*.

It is a small book of ten chapters, and it is not about how to run a church or how to win an argument. It is about a soul that is thirsty for God. Its first chapter quotes Psalm 42, in the old words of the King James Version that Tozer loved. In the Open Word Bible the verse reads, "Like a doe that longs for running streams, I long for You, God, with all my being." Tozer believed that many Christians had stopped longing. They had accepted Christ, they believed the right things, and they were content to stay where they were. He wanted them to be thirsty again.

The book was soon read far beyond Chicago. In time it was translated into many languages, and many readers still count it among the most important Christian books of the twentieth century.

### Who he was

Aiden Wilson Tozer was born on 21 April 1897 in a small farming community in western Pennsylvania called La Jose. His family was poor, and he had little schooling. As a teenager he moved with his family to Akron, Ohio, and went to work in a rubber tire factory. One day, on his way home from work, he heard a street preacher tell his listeners that anyone who did not know how to be saved could simply call on God, as the tax collector did in the parable: "God, be merciful to me, the sinner!" (Luke 18:13). Tozer went home, climbed up into the attic of his house, and called on God. He was about seventeen.

He never went to college or seminary. Instead, he taught himself. He read the Bible, the great theologians, the poets and the old Christian mystics, often late into the night. In 1919 he became the pastor of a small church of the Christian and Missionary Alliance. From 1928 to 1959 he served Southside Alliance Church in Chicago, and in his last years he pastored a church in Toronto, Canada. He also edited his church's magazine. He died in Toronto in May 1963. His gravestone describes him simply as "A Man of God."

Tozer lived simply and gave much of his money away. But he was not perfect. His biographer notes that his long hours of study and his many journeys left his wife Ada and their seven children too often alone. Even a man who sought God so earnestly could fail those closest to him.

### A man who followed hard after God

Samuel Zwemer, the great missionary to the Muslim world, visited Tozer's church and wrote the introduction to *The Pursuit of God*. He described a self-made scholar who "seemed to burn the midnight oil in pursuit of God," and he said the book was "the result of long meditation and much prayer."

Tozer's preface explains why he wrote. Many churches, he said, taught the right doctrines, but there was little hunger for God Himself. Yet he saw a small sign of hope:

> "They are athirst for God, and they will not be satisfied till they have drunk deep at the Fountain of Living Water. This is the only real harbinger of revival which I have been able to detect anywhere on the religious horizon. It may be the cloud the size of a man's hand for which a few saints here and there have been looking."
> A. W. Tozer, *The Pursuit of God*

A *harbinger* is a sign that something is coming. The cloud "as small as a man's hand" comes from the story of Elijah, who prayed on Mount Carmel for rain after a long drought (1 Kings 18:44). For Tozer, the first sign of revival is not a crowd or a program. It is a hungry heart.

Each chapter of the book ends with a prayer, written, as Zwemer said, for the private room and not the pulpit. The prayer at the end of the first chapter has become one of the best loved in the book:

> "O God, I have tasted Thy goodness, and it has both satisfied me and made me thirsty for more. I am painfully conscious of my need of further grace. I am ashamed of my lack of desire. O God, the Triune God, I want to want Thee; I long to be filled with longing; I thirst to be made more thirsty still."
> A. W. Tozer, *The Pursuit of God*

This is a remarkable prayer. Tozer does not pretend to be full of longing. He confesses that his desire is small. Then he asks God for the desire itself. That is good news for anyone who feels cold in prayer. We do not have to make ourselves thirsty before we come. We can ask God to make us thirsty.

The psalmist of Psalm 42 knew this too. He was far from the temple, perhaps in exile, and he felt that God was far away. Twice he asks his own soul, "Why are you bent so low, my soul?" (Psalm 42:5). Yet he does not stop seeking. His thirst itself becomes his prayer: "I am thirsty for God, the living God" (Psalm 42:2).

### Three key insights

1. **The longing for God is itself a gift we may ask God to give.** Tozer prayed, "I want to want Thee." When our hearts feel cold, we do not have to pretend. We can confess our lack of desire and ask God to awaken it.
2. **A heart thirsty for God Himself is the first sign of revival, before any crowd or program.** Tozer looked for revival not in numbers but in people who would not be satisfied with less than God. Every revival in this book began with such people.
3. **The busiest life still needs unhurried time alone with God.** Tozer was a pastor in a great city, yet he made room to read, meditate and pray, and the book was born from those hours. Our pursuit of God needs time that we guard on purpose.

### For us today

Many believers today know a great deal about God. We can hear sermons on our phones, read the Bible in our own languages and join worship online. These are good gifts. Yet it is possible to know much and long for little. Tozer's question comes to us all: do we want God Himself, or only His gifts and the comfort of right opinions?

The answer does not depend on education. Tozer had almost none. A factory worker in Lagos, a market seller in Lima, a student in Seoul or a grandmother in a village in India can pursue God as truly as any scholar. All it takes is a thirsty heart and time given to God.

We have now come to the end of this book. From the Welsh valleys and the women of Mukti to the hills of East Africa, the island of Lewis and a pasture in Charlotte, the revivals of these fifty years began with people who were thirsty for God and would not stop praying. In **Volume 10, *Every Tribe and Tongue***, we will follow the praying church of our own times, from Jim and Elisabeth Elliot and Helen Roseveare to Festo Kivengere and Janani Luwum of East Africa, the prayer mountains of Korea and the all-night vigils of Africa.

## Conclusion: Passing the Torch

> "Let us know the LORD; let us press on to know Him. His coming is as certain as the sunrise; He will come to us like the showers, like the spring rain that waters the land." (Hosea 6:3)

The fifty years in this book began with great confidence and ended in great sorrow. In 1900 many people believed that progress would soon make the world safe, wise and kind. By 1950 the world had seen two world wars, the Holocaust and the atomic bomb. Empires were falling. Churches in Europe were half empty. In China, the new government was about to close the doors to foreign missionaries and put many Chinese believers in prison.

And yet the church was not weaker in 1950 than it had been in 1900. In many places it was stronger. There were more believers in Korea, in India, in East Africa and in China than ever before, and many of them had learned to pray in the hardest of places. The prophet Hosea's promise had proved true: God came to His people "like the showers, like the spring rain that waters the land."

### What the witnesses taught us

Here, in a few lines, is some of what the witnesses of this book taught us about prayer:

- Being filled with the Spirit begins when we ask God to bend our own will (chapter 1, Evan Roberts).
- God pours out His Spirit on those whom the world overlooks, and He makes them messengers of His word (chapter 2, Pandita Ramabai).
- The Spirit comes to people who wait for Him in prayer, and He gives power so that they can bear witness to Jesus (chapter 3, William J. Seymour).
- When believers pray aloud together with one heart, their many voices can rise to God as one prayer (chapter 4, the Pyongyang revival).
- A church that meets God at daybreak learns to face the day's troubles together (chapter 5, Kil Sun-ju).
- Tears shed in prayer for others are seeds that God will one day turn into a harvest of joy (chapter 6, John Hyde).
- Like a sea creature rising for air, we must keep coming up to God in prayer if we are to live in a drowning world (chapter 7, Sadhu Sundar Singh).
- When we pray for children at risk, we share both the anger and the welcome of Jesus (chapter 8, Amy Carmichael).
- Faith that moves mountains is not confidence in our own faith but trust in God Himself (chapter 9, Smith Wigglesworth).
- Standing in the gap means letting another's need become our own burden before God (chapter 10, Rees Howells).
- It is better to be sent by God than to run ahead of Him, however good our reasons (chapter 11, Dora Yu).
- Repentance becomes real when we name our sins one by one and turn from each of them (chapter 12, John Sung).
- The church's greatest work is to ask God boldly for what He Himself wants done on earth (chapter 13, Watchman Nee).
- Learning to pray in quiet days prepares us to pray in dark ones (chapter 14, Dietrich Bonhoeffer).
- Giving thanks in a dark place is a way of confessing that God has not left it (chapter 15, Corrie and Betsie ten Boom).
- Courage is not the absence of fear, but fear brought to God one day at a time (chapter 16, Gladys Aylward).
- A church can be busy and still be asleep, and only the light of Christ can wake it (chapter 17, the East African revival).
- No one is too old, too weak or too hidden to pray for revival (chapter 18, the Lewis revival).
- We can pray for workers God has not yet called, and He may already be preparing them (chapter 19, the praying men of Charlotte).
- The longing for God is itself a gift we may ask God to give (chapter 20, A. W. Tozer).

You will find every one of these lessons gathered by theme in Appendix 1, and every Scripture in this book listed in Bible order in Appendix 2.

### Revival fires

The title of this book comes from the revivals that run through it: in Wales and India, on Azusa Street and in Pyongyang, in East Africa and on the Isle of Lewis. They were not all alike. Some were loud and some were quiet. Some brought new movements to birth, and some ran their course within the old churches. Some were marked by excess, and some of the people who led them broke down under the strain.

But they had something in common. Each one began with people who prayed, often for a long time, and often without seeing any result. A young man in a Welsh coal town prayed for revival for years before it came. Two old sisters on Lewis prayed through the winter nights. Students in Korea prayed aloud together until they confessed their sins to one another. Businessmen in a field in North Carolina prayed for their city. And when the fire fell, the people who had prayed were the first to say that it was not their doing. "Not by might, nor by power, but by My Spirit."

This book also showed that God does not only work through revivals. Some of its witnesses never saw one. Bonhoeffer prayed in a prison cell and died before the war ended. Corrie ten Boom's sister Betsie died in a concentration camp. Watchman Nee spent twenty years in prison. Gladys Aylward walked over the mountains with a hundred children and did not know if they would live. Their prayers were not answered with crowds or headlines. They were answered with the presence of God in the darkest places on earth.

### The torch is passed

By 1950 the center of the church was moving south and east. In the decades that followed, the number of Christians in Africa, Asia and Latin America would grow beyond anything the missionaries of the great century had imagined.

In **Volume 10, *Every Tribe and Tongue***, we will follow the praying church around the world from 1950 to today. We will meet Jim and Elisabeth Elliot in Ecuador and Helen Roseveare in Congo, Brother Andrew carrying Bibles behind the Iron Curtain, and the brothers of Taizé praying for unity. We will climb Korea's prayer mountains and stand with Festo Kivengere and Janani Luwum in Uganda. We will join the house churches of China and Iran, the all-night prayer vigils of Africa and a prayer meeting in Brooklyn, and watch a 24-hour prayer room in England start a movement that spread around the world. And we will pray with a woman who prayed in a shipping container in an Eritrean prison.

They were the heirs of the witnesses in this book. Many of them read Tozer, Corrie ten Boom and Amy Carmichael. Some were sent out by the revivals we have read about. The torch was passed.

### Your turn

The witnesses of this book now pass the torch to you.

So ask God to bend you. Pray for the women and girls of your land. Pray with others, whoever they are, as one family. Confess your sins to God, and where it is right, to one another. Rise early to meet Him. Sow in tears for those you love. Breathe in prayer as you breathe in air. Pray for children. Believe. Stand in the gap. Say, "I am ready. Send me," when God asks whom He should send. Pray with others for your nation. Pray in Jesus' name with the authority He gives. Put your times in God's hands. Give thanks, even for the fleas. Trust God when the road is long. Wake up, and walk in the light. Pray for your town until God comes. Pray for the young people around you. And above all, pursue God Himself.

Lord God, You come to Your people like the spring rain. Thank You for the praying people of the twentieth century: for Evan Roberts and Pandita Ramabai, for William Seymour and the believers of Korea, for John Hyde, Sundar Singh and Amy Carmichael, for Smith Wigglesworth and Rees Howells, for Dora Yu, John Sung and Watchman Nee, for Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Corrie and Betsie ten Boom and Gladys Aylward, for the revival brothers and sisters of East Africa, the praying sisters of Lewis, the praying men of Charlotte and A. W. Tozer. Bend me. Send Your Spirit on my heart, my church and my land, not by might, nor by power, but by Your Spirit. And make me press on to know You. Amen.
