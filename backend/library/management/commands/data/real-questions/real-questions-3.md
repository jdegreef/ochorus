## Introduction: Pour It Out

Some questions are about the universe. Others are about Tuesday. Why can't I feel God? What if my friends think I'm stupid for believing? What if I'm being bullied, or I'm lonely, or a friend says something that scares me? What if I just want to give up?

The first two *Real Questions* books dealt with a lot of the big arguments: science, suffering, other religions, sex, the Trinity, free will. This third book keeps going, but many of its questions are closer to home. They are the ones you ask on the way home from school, or lying awake at night, or after a conversation that went badly.

All of them belong in the light. The Psalms, the Bible's own prayer book, are full of honest questions, complaints and fears brought straight to God. One of them says, "Trust in Him at all times, O people; pour out your hearts before Him. God is our refuge" (Psalm 62:8). Not tidy hearts. Poured-out ones.

### How each chapter works

As in the first two books, every chapter is one question, with four parts:

- **The short answer.** Two or three sentences, for when you only have a minute.
- **The longer answer.** What the Bible says, and real reasons, in a few pages.
- **Think about it.** One question to take away, alone or with friends.
- **Go deeper.** A chapter from a classic in the Ochorus library, written by someone who faced the same thing.

### Clear where the Bible is clear, fair where Christians differ

Some questions have one clear Christian answer, given by Christians across the world and through history. This book gives those answers plainly. Others are questions on which faithful Christians, who love Jesus and trust the Bible, have reached different conclusions: how Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Christians differ, whether a Christian may ever fight in a war, whether it is ever right to lie, what exactly heaven will be like. Here the book sets out the main views fairly, shows what they share, and says so plainly, so you can think it through with your church and family.

### Read it your way

You don't need to have read the first two books, and you don't need to read this one in order. Start wherever your question is. And if one of these chapters touches something that is hurting you right now, don't just read about it. Talk to someone.

### For parents, teachers and youth leaders

*Real Questions* is written for readers aged 13 to 17. It takes a mainstream evangelical view, and where faithful Christians genuinely differ it sets out the main views without taking sides.

Several chapters in this book touch painful subjects: loneliness, bullying, anger, and what to do when a friend talks about ending their life. They are written with compassion and without graphic detail, and every one of them points the reader to a parent or trusted adult, and, where anyone is in danger, to getting help straight away. You may want to read those chapters first. The "Think about it" questions work well for group discussion. All Scripture is quoted from the Berean Standard Bible.

# Part 1 · God, Faith and Me

## Why Can't I Feel God?

**The short answer:** Because faith and feelings are different things, and God doesn't promise that you will always feel Him. Dry seasons are normal, even for the strongest Christians in the Bible, and they are not a sign that God has left. He is as near on the empty days as on the bright ones.

Your friend says she felt God's presence so strongly at camp that she cried. The worship leader closes his eyes and seems to be somewhere else. You stand there, singing the same words, and feel... nothing. Maybe you never have. And a worrying thought follows: *If God were real, and if I really belonged to Him, wouldn't I feel something?*

It's a fair question. Here's an honest answer.

### Feelings are real, but they aren't a radar

Feelings are a gift from God. But they aren't a device for detecting Him. They rise and fall with sleep, hormones, music, stress, the weather and what you had for breakfast. A loud song in a dark room can produce goosebumps whether God is being worshipped or not. And a tired, anxious teenager can feel flat in the very room where God is most at work.

So "I can't feel God" tells you something true about your feelings. It doesn't tell you where God is. He has said, "Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you" (Hebrews 13:5). That promise doesn't come with a condition about how you feel.

### The Bible is full of dry seasons

If you think real believers always sense God, read the Bible's own prayer book. David wrote, "my soul thirsts for You. My body yearns for You in a dry and weary land without water" (Psalm 63:1). Another psalm-writer prayed all night and got nothing back: "my soul refused to be comforted" (Psalm 77:2). He even asked, "Has God forgotten to be gracious?" (Psalm 77:9).

Job, a man God Himself called blameless, said, "If I go east, He is not there, and if I go west, I cannot find Him" (Job 23:8). Then, in the same breath: "Yet He knows the way I have taken; when He has tested me, I will come forth as gold" (Job 23:10). Job couldn't find God. But God hadn't lost Job.

Notice that God put these prayers in Scripture. Feeling far from Him is so common that He gave you words for it.

### Why might God let it feel dry?

The Bible doesn't give one tidy reason, and it's wise not to pretend. But Christians through the centuries have noticed a few things. A dry season can show whether we love God or only the warm feeling of loving God. It can grow a deeper, steadier trust, the kind a friendship has after years, not just the rush of the first week. And sometimes dryness has a plain cause worth checking: a sin you keep hiding from God, exhaustion, or a life so crammed with noise that there is no space to listen.

Isaiah wrote to exactly this person: "Who among you walks in darkness and has no light? Let him trust in the name of the LORD; let him lean on his God" (Isaiah 50:10). Not "work up a feeling". Lean.

### What helps

- **Keep showing up.** Pray and read even when it feels like nothing. Faithfulness on the dry days is real faith, maybe the most real.
- **Remember.** The psalm-writer turned a corner when he said, "I will remember the works of the LORD" (Psalm 77:11). Write down what God has done, for you and in the Bible.
- **Look at Jesus, not your feelings.** The cross and the empty tomb don't change with your mood.
- **Get some rest.** Sleep, food, daylight and fewer screens late at night do more for your spiritual life than you'd think.
- **Stay with other Christians.** Let their faith carry you for a while.

Sometimes, though, the flatness is part of something heavier: a sadness that won't lift, no interest in anything, feeling worthless. That can be depression, which is an illness, not a failure of faith. (Book 1 answered whether depression means your faith is weak.) If that sounds like you, tell a trusted adult and see a doctor. And **if you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.**

**Think about it:** If you judged your friendship with God only by what He has said and done, and not by what you feel this week, what would you conclude?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Jeanne Guyon* — a seventeenth-century French woman who wrote a little book on prayer explains that God sometimes withdraws the feeling of His presence to draw us to seek Him for His own sake, while warning that some dryness comes from sin, exhaustion, grief or depression and needs a different kind of care. Start with chapter 7, "When the Beloved Hides".

## Is My Faith Just How I Was Raised?

**The short answer:** Your family may be how you first heard about Jesus, but that doesn't make Christianity true or false. Where a belief comes from and whether it's true are two separate questions. The real question is whether you will test the faith you were given and make it your own.

Someone at school says it like a checkmate: "You're only a Christian because your parents are. If you'd been born somewhere else, you'd believe something else." And you have to admit, there's something in it. You didn't pick your family. You've been going to church since before you could walk. How do you know you believe any of this for yourself?

That's a good question to ask. It's one God wants you to ask.

### Where a belief comes from vs whether it's true

Philosophers have a name for the mistake in that "checkmate". It's called the *genetic fallacy*: judging a belief by where it came from rather than by whether it's true. You learned that the earth goes round the sun from your teachers, and you'd have learned something different in another century. That doesn't make it false.

And notice that the argument cuts both ways. Someone raised by atheist parents who stays an atheist could be told, "You only think that because of how you were raised." Everyone was raised somewhere. The question isn't where you started. It's whether what you believe is true.

### God has no grandchildren

Still, the challenge contains a real warning. Being raised in a Christian home doesn't make you a Christian, any more than being born in a garage makes you a car. Faith can't be inherited like eye colour.

The Bible shows how a whole generation can lose it. After Joshua's time, "another generation rose up who did not know the LORD or the works that He had done for Israel" (Judges 2:10). They had grown up with the stories. They just never made them their own.

That's why Joshua put the choice in front of his people: "choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve" (Joshua 24:15). Then he gave his own answer: "As for me and my house, we will serve the LORD!" Even a family's faith has to be chosen person by person.

### Timothy's second-generation faith

The New Testament has a young leader who was raised exactly like you. Paul wrote to Timothy, "I am reminded of your sincere faith, which first dwelt in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice, and I am convinced is in you as well" (2 Timothy 1:5).

Notice the order. The faith was in his grandmother first, then his mother, and *now in him as well*. Paul doesn't say Timothy's faith is fake because it started at home. He says it has become Timothy's own. And he tells him to hold on to it, "since you know from whom you have learned them. From infancy you have known the Holy Scriptures" (2 Timothy 3:14–15). Learning faith from people you trust is not a weakness. It's how God often works.

### How to make it yours

**Test it.** The Bible says, "test all things. Hold fast to what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Read a Gospel for yourself, start to finish. Look at the evidence for the resurrection. Ask your hardest questions out loud.

**Meet Jesus, not just His fan club.** In John's Gospel, a woman told her town about Jesus. Later they said to her, "We now believe not only because of your words; we have heard for ourselves" (John 4:42). That's the move from second-hand faith to first-hand faith.

**Try it.** "Taste and see that the LORD is good" (Psalm 34:8). You can't taste food by reading the menu. Pray honestly. Obey Him in something specific. See what happens.

You may find that some of what you grew up with was more about your family's habits than about Jesus. That's fine to notice. Making faith your own may mean letting go of the extras and holding the centre more tightly.

**Think about it:** If your parents had never been Christians, what reasons would you have for following Jesus today?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of John Wesley* — Wesley grew up in a clergyman's home, was ordained and went abroad as a missionary, yet later told a university congregation that for years he had been only "almost a Christian", sincere and busy but without a living trust in Christ. Start with chapter 4, "The Almost Christian".

## Isn't Christianity a Western Religion?

**The short answer:** No. Christianity began in the Middle East, spread into Africa and Asia before it was strong in most of Europe, and today most Christians live in Africa, Asia and Latin America. It isn't any one culture's religion. It's been translated into thousands of languages because God speaks to every people in their own words.

On TV, Christianity can look like a white, Western thing: European cathedrals, American preachers, old paintings of a blond Jesus. Some people see it as part of the history of empire and say, "Why would anyone outside the West follow that?"

It's a fair challenge, and the history behind it isn't all comfortable. But the picture is badly out of focus.

### It started in the Middle East

Jesus was a Jewish man, born in Bethlehem, raised in Nazareth, who died and rose again in Jerusalem. He never visited Europe. The first churches met in Jerusalem, and the disciples "were first called Christians at Antioch" (Acts 11:26), in what is now Turkey, near the Syrian border.

On the day the church was born, the crowd included "Parthians, Medes, and Elamites; residents of Mesopotamia" (Acts 2:9), people from what are now Iran and Iraq, along with visitors from "Egypt and the parts of Libya near Cyrene" (Acts 2:10). Each of them heard the good news in his own language. One of the first non-Jewish believers was "an Ethiopian eunuch, a court official" (Acts 8:27). And the church at Antioch was led by a team that included "Simeon called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene" (Acts 13:1), very probably Africans.

### Africa and Asia came early

Some of the greatest thinkers of the early church were African. Augustine lived in what is now Algeria; Athanasius was a bishop in Egypt; Tertullian and Cyprian lived in Carthage, in modern Tunisia. Ethiopia had a Christian king in the 300s. Armenia became officially Christian around the same time, earlier than any other nation. Christians in India trace their church back to the apostle Thomas, and Christian missionaries from the Middle East had reached China by the 600s. Much of Northern Europe, by contrast, heard the gospel centuries later.

So when someone in Africa or Asia follows Jesus, they aren't borrowing a European religion. In many places they're returning to one of the oldest faiths their region has known.

### The church today

The centre of the Christian world has moved. Most of the world's Christians now live in the Global South, and researchers count more Christians in Africa than on any other continent. The Bible is now available in full in more than seven hundred languages, and at least part of it in thousands more.

That isn't an accident. From the start, Jesus sent His followers "to the ends of the earth" (Acts 1:8). In heaven the song is to the Lamb who "purchased for God those from every tribe and tongue and people and nation" (Revelation 5:9). Other faiths sometimes treat one sacred language as the only proper one for worship. Christianity has always translated. God doesn't ask you to become someone else's culture to belong to Him. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28).

### The honest part

It's also true that some Western Christians mixed the gospel up with their own culture and their own empires. Some missionaries treated local customs as worthless, and some stood by while colonial powers did great harm. That was wrong, and Christians should say so plainly. But others, often local believers, did the opposite: they put the Bible into people's own languages, built schools, and fought the slave trade. And the gospel itself judges every empire, Western ones included.

So if you are African, Asian, Latin American or anything else, Jesus isn't a foreigner to you. And if you are Western, the faith doesn't belong to you either. It belongs to Him.

**Think about it:** If God speaks every language, how might it change the way you pray to know He hears you in the words you actually think in?

**Go deeper:** *Samuel Ajayi Crowther (For Teens)* — the story of a Yoruba boy rescued from slavery who became a bishop and did the largest share of the work on the first Yoruba Bible, showing that the gospel never belonged to England and could speak Yoruba. Start with chapter 8, "In Our Own Language".

## Why Pray If God Already Knows Everything?

**The short answer:** Prayer isn't for giving God information. It's how children talk with their Father, and it's one of the real ways God has chosen to act in the world. Jesus knew God better than anyone, and He prayed more than anyone.

It sounds like a clever objection. If God already knows what I need, and already knows what He's going to do, why tell Him? Isn't prayer like texting a friend something they're already looking at?

Jesus actually answered this. He said, "your Father knows what you need before you ask Him" (Matthew 6:8). And then, in the very next breath: "So then, this is how you should pray" (Matthew 6:9). Notice the "so then". Because God already knows, *therefore* pray. For Jesus, God's knowledge isn't a reason not to pray. It's a reason to pray with confidence.

(Book 1 answered why God sometimes seems not to answer. This chapter asks a different question: why pray at all?)

### Prayer is relationship, not data

Imagine a family where the parents know their teenager is hungry, tired and worried about exams. Does that mean the teenager never needs to talk to them? Of course not. A parent who knows everything about you still wants to hear from you, because what they want isn't information. It's *you*.

That's what prayer is. Jesus called His followers friends: "I have called you friends" (John 15:15). Friends talk. Not because they're filling each other's knowledge gaps, but because talking is what love does.

So the Bible invites us to "approach the throne of grace with confidence" (Hebrews 4:16), and to bring Him everything: "in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God" (Philippians 4:6).

### God uses means

Here's the second half of the answer. God doesn't usually do things on His own when He could involve us. He could make bread appear on your table, but He normally feeds you through farmers, shops and someone who cooks. He could tell everyone the gospel by thunder from the sky, but He sends people. In the same way, He has chosen to do some things *in answer to prayer*.

James says it bluntly: "You do not have, because you do not ask" (James 4:2). And he points to an ordinary man: "Elijah was a man just like us. He prayed earnestly that it would not rain, and it did not rain on the land for three and a half years" (James 5:17). Prayer isn't a show we put on while God does what He was always going to do anyway. It's one of the ways His will gets done.

How that fits with God knowing everything ahead of time is a deep question, and Christians have thought hard about it. But notice: God knowing the future includes knowing your prayers, and the answers He has planned to give to them. Your prayers are part of the story, not an interruption to it. The thinker Blaise Pascal said that in prayer God gives us "the dignity of causality". You get to make a difference.

### Jesus prayed

If prayer were just informing God, the One who needed it least was Jesus. He was the Son of God. He knew the Father's mind perfectly. And yet "Early in the morning, while it was still dark, Jesus got up and slipped out to a solitary place to pray" (Mark 1:35). "He frequently withdrew to the wilderness to pray" (Luke 5:16). He prayed before choosing His disciples, at the grave of His friend, in the garden, on the cross.

He's still doing it. Hebrews says He "always lives to intercede" for His people (Hebrews 7:25). If Jesus, who knows everything the Father knows, keeps praying, prayer must be about far more than passing on information.

### Prayer changes you, too

One more thing. Prayer changes the person praying. When you bring your worries to God, you're promised that "the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:7). Over time, talking with God reshapes what you want. You start to care about what He cares about.

So pray. Not because God is in the dark, but because He isn't, and He wants you near.

**Think about it:** If prayer is more like talking with a friend than sending a request, what would you say to God today if you weren't asking for anything?

**Go deeper:** *Purpose in Prayer* by E. M. Bounds — a Civil War chaplain turned writer argues that God has made prayer one of the ways He shapes the world, and that "the prayers of God's saints strengthen the unborn generation." Start with chapter 1, "God Shapes the World by Prayer".

## What Is Sin, Really?

**The short answer:** Sin is more than breaking rules. It's missing the mark God made us for, turning away from Him to run our own lives, and failing to love Him and other people as we should. It's something we do, but also a power that holds us, and only Jesus can set us free from it.

"Sin" can sound like a church word for a list of banned things: swearing, lying, sneaking out. If that's all it is, the cure would be simple: try harder, keep the rules. But the Bible's picture is much bigger, and more serious. And it's more hopeful.

### Missing the mark

The most common words for sin in the Bible's original languages can be used for missing a target. The book of Judges describes soldiers who "could sling a stone at a hair without missing" (Judges 20:16); the Hebrew word for "missing" there is the ordinary word for sin.

So sin isn't only doing bad things. It's falling short of what we were made for. Paul puts it like this: "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23). You were made to reflect God's goodness, His love and His glory. Every time we fall short of that, even when nobody else notices, we miss the mark.

That means you can sin by *not* doing something too. "Anyone, then, who knows the right thing to do, yet fails to do it, is guilty of sin" (James 4:17).

### Rebellion

But sin isn't just a bad aim. It's also a choice of direction. "We all like sheep have gone astray, each one has turned to his own way" (Isaiah 53:6). At its root, sin is saying to God: *I'll run my own life, thanks.*

That's why David, after he had hurt other people terribly, prayed, "Against You, You only, have I sinned" (Psalm 51:4). He had wronged people, but deepest of all he had turned his back on God. The Bible even calls sin "lawlessness" (1 John 3:4): living as if God has no say.

### Broken love

Jesus summed up everything God asks in two commands: "Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind" and "Love your neighbor as yourself" (Matthew 22:37–39). So sin, at heart, is broken love. It's loving the wrong things, loving the right things too much or too little, or loving ourselves most of all.

God describes it through Jeremiah like this: "They have forsaken Me, the fountain of living water, and they have dug their own cisterns— broken cisterns that cannot hold water" (Jeremiah 2:13). Sin isn't just bad. It's a terrible trade. We swap the real thing for something that leaks.

### Not just acts, but a power

Here's what the rule-list idea misses. Sin isn't only things we do. It's a power that gets a grip on us. God warned Cain, "sin is crouching at your door; it desires you" (Genesis 4:7), like an animal waiting to pounce. Jesus said, "everyone who sins is a slave to sin" (John 8:34).

You've probably felt this. You promise yourself you won't do that thing again, and you do it anyway. Paul felt it too: "For I do not do the good I want to do. Instead, I keep on doing the evil I do not want to do" (Romans 7:19). Jesus said the trouble starts deep down: "For from within the hearts of men come evil thoughts" (Mark 7:21). Our behaviour is the fruit. The root is a heart turned away from God.

That's why trying harder is never enough. You can't free yourself from a power by being more determined. You need a rescuer.

### The cure

Paul's cry, "What a wretched man I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death?" is followed straight away by the answer: "Thanks be to God, through Jesus Christ our Lord!" (Romans 7:24–25).

Jesus deals with both sides of sin. On the cross He carried the guilt of it; Isaiah says "the LORD has laid upon Him the iniquity of us all" (Isaiah 53:6). And by His Spirit He breaks its power: "So if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed" (John 8:36). Christians still struggle, but "sin shall not be your master" (Romans 6:14).

Taking sin seriously isn't meant to crush you. A doctor tells you what's wrong so that you can be healed.

**Think about it:** Which picture of sin do you find most helpful: missing the mark, rebellion, broken love or slavery? Why?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Martyn Lloyd-Jones* — a London doctor who became a preacher argues that sin is not first a matter of things done but a condition of the heart, so moral reform can't cure it, and that the Bible's hard diagnosis is a mercy because it comes with a sure cure. Start with chapter 3, "What Is Really Wrong".

## What About People Who Lived Before Jesus?

**The short answer:** People in Old Testament times were saved the same way we are: by God's grace, through trusting Him. Abraham believed God's promise, and God counted him righteous. They looked forward to a Saviour they hadn't yet seen; we look back to the One who has come.

Here's a puzzle. If Jesus is the only way to God, what about Abraham, Moses, Ruth or David? They lived centuries before Jesus was born. They never heard His name, never read the New Testament, never heard of the cross. Were they saved? And if so, how?

(Book 1 answered what about people who never hear about Jesus. This is a different question: what about the believers who came *before* Him?)

### Abraham believed

Start with Abraham, about two thousand years before Jesus. God promised the childless old man that his family would be as many as the stars. And then comes one of the most important sentences in the whole Bible: "Abram believed the LORD, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Genesis 15:6).

Notice what it doesn't say. It doesn't say Abraham kept enough rules. The Law of Moses hadn't even been given yet. Abraham trusted God's promise, and God counted him as right with Him.

Paul builds his whole explanation of the gospel on that verse: "Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Romans 4:3). And he says those words "were written not only for Abraham, but also for us, to whom righteousness will be credited—for us who believe in Him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead" (Romans 4:23–24). Same way in, for him and for us.

### Looking forward

What did Abraham believe in? More than he could see. Paul says that God "foretold the gospel to Abraham" (Galatians 3:8). Jesus Himself said, "Your father Abraham rejoiced that he would see My day. He saw it and was glad" (John 8:56).

The believers of the Old Testament didn't know the name Jesus or the details of the cross. But they trusted the God who had promised to rescue them, and every promise He made was pointing to Christ. After the resurrection, Jesus walked with two disciples and, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, He explained to them what was written in all the Scriptures about Himself" (Luke 24:27).

Even the prophets didn't fully understand what they were writing. They "searched and investigated carefully" (1 Peter 1:10), trying to work out when and how God's promised Saviour would come.

### What about the sacrifices?

Weren't Old Testament people saved by sacrificing animals? Not really. Hebrews says plainly, "it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins" (Hebrews 10:4). The sacrifices were like a picture, or a signpost: something dies in someone else's place. They taught God's people to trust Him to deal with sin, and they pointed ahead to the real sacrifice.

So when Jesus died, His death reached backwards as well as forwards. Paul says that God presented Jesus as the atoning sacrifice "because in His forbearance He had passed over the sins committed beforehand" (Romans 3:25), waiting for the cross. Hebrews says Christ died "to redeem them from the transgressions committed under the first covenant" (Hebrews 9:15). The believers of the Old Testament were forgiven on credit, you might say, and the bill was paid at Calvary.

### The great crowd of Hebrews 11

Hebrews 11 lists a whole crowd of these people: Abel, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Moses, even Rahab, a Canaanite woman with a bad reputation, who "did not perish with those who were disobedient" (Hebrews 11:31). It says of them, "All these people died in faith, without having received the things they were promised. However, they saw them and welcomed them from afar" (Hebrews 11:13).

And then the chapter ends with something beautiful: "God had planned something better for us, so that together with us they would be made perfect" (Hebrews 11:40). They aren't a separate group with a separate deal. They're part of the same family as you, saved by the same Saviour, waiting for the same resurrection.

That also answers a quieter worry. If God has always saved people by grace through faith, then salvation has never been about being good enough, in any age. It has always been a gift.

**Think about it:** Abraham trusted God's promise before he could see how it would be kept. Is there a promise of God you're being asked to trust before you can see the ending?

**Go deeper:** *The Big Story* — this chapter walks through God's call and covenant promise to Abraham, showing that he was counted right with God by trusting the promise and that the same way in is open to anyone, from any family, who trusts Jesus. Start with chapter 5, "A Promise to Abraham".

## Why Did God Make Me?

**The short answer:** God didn't make you because He was lonely or needed anything. He made you out of love, to know Him, to reflect His goodness and to enjoy Him for ever. His glory and your deepest joy turn out to be the same thing.

It's a question that comes at strange moments. Lying awake at 2 a.m. Staring out of a bus window. Scrolling through other people's lives. *Why am I even here? What's the point of me?*

The world gives plenty of answers. You're here by accident. You're here to be happy. You're here to make your mark. The Bible gives a better one.

(Book 1 answered how to know God's will for the big decisions in your life. This chapter goes underneath that: why you exist at all.)

### Made by love

God didn't need you. The Father, Son and Holy Spirit had perfect love and joy together before anything was made. So He didn't create you to fill a gap. He made you the way an artist makes something beautiful, out of overflowing delight.

David wrote, "For You formed my inmost being; You knit me together in my mother's womb. I praise You, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made" (Psalm 139:13–14). Knit. Not mass-produced, not thrown together. And when God looked at the people He had made, along with everything else, He saw that "it was very good" (Genesis 1:31).

Whatever anyone has told you, you are not an accident.

### Made for His glory

God speaks of "everyone called by My name and created for My glory" (Isaiah 43:7). Paul says of Jesus, "All things were created through Him and for Him" (Colossians 1:16). In heaven they sing, "You created all things; by Your will they exist and came to be" (Revelation 4:11).

That can sound selfish at first. Doesn't God just want attention? But think about it. If God really is the best, most beautiful, most loving being there is, then the best thing He could possibly give you is *Himself*. A God who gave you anything less than Himself would be giving you less than the best. So when God makes you for His glory, He isn't competing with your happiness. He's offering it.

### Made for joy

That's why the Bible keeps putting glory and joy side by side. "You will fill me with joy in Your presence, with eternal pleasures at Your right hand" (Psalm 16:11). Jesus said eternal life is this: "that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom You have sent" (John 17:3).

Over 350 years ago, a group of Christians wrote a question-and-answer guide for young people. Its first question was, "What is the chief end of man?" and its answer: "Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever." Not one or the other. Both, together.

### Made to reflect Him

God made humans "in Our image, after Our likeness, to rule" over the earth (Genesis 1:26), and placed the first man in a garden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15). You were made to reflect what God is like into His world: creating, caring, building, making things grow, loving people. That means maths homework, music practice, football, cooking and helping a younger brother can all be part of what you're for. "So whether you eat or drink or whatever you do, do it all to the glory of God" (1 Corinthians 10:31).

And you were made for good work that God has in mind: "For we are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance as our way of life" (Ephesians 2:10).

### When the purpose got lost

Sin bent all this. We started living for ourselves, and like a phone used as a doorstop, we stopped working as designed. That's part of why life can feel empty even when it's full. Jesus came to bring you back to what you're for. In Him, you can know God again, and that is where the restlessness finally settles.

One last thing. God doesn't only love the person you'll become. Right now, as you are, "He will rejoice over you with gladness" (Zephaniah 3:17).

**Think about it:** If your deepest purpose is to know and enjoy God, how might that change the way you think about school, friends or your future?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Jonathan Edwards* — an eighteenth-century pastor asks why God made anything at all and argues that God's glory and our happiness are not rivals, because the best thing any creature can receive is God Himself. Start with chapter 3, "The End for Which God Created the World".

## What Will Heaven Actually Be Like?

**The short answer:** Not clouds, harps and endless church services. The Bible's final hope is a new creation: a real, physical world made new, where God's people live in resurrection bodies, with God Himself. Some details are hidden from us, and faithful Christians differ on a few of them, but what we are told is better than anything we can imagine.

Many people's picture of heaven comes from cartoons: people in white robes sitting on clouds, playing harps, forever. No wonder some people secretly think it sounds boring. "Won't I get bored singing for a billion years?" is a real question, and an honest one.

The good news is that the cartoon is wrong. (Book 1 answered what happens when we die. This chapter looks further ahead, to what life will be like at the very end.)

### A new creation

The Bible's story doesn't end with souls escaping the world. It ends with God renewing it. "For behold, I will create new heavens and a new earth" (Isaiah 65:17). "Behold, I make all things new" (Revelation 21:5). Not "all new things". All things, *new*.

Isaiah describes it in earthy terms: "They will build houses and dwell in them; they will plant vineyards and eat their fruit" (Isaiah 65:21). Weapons will be turned into farm tools: "They will beat their swords into plowshares" (Isaiah 2:4). Creation itself is waiting for the day when it "will be set free from its bondage to decay" (Romans 8:21).

### Real bodies

You won't be a ghost. When Jesus rose, He said, "Touch Me and see—for a spirit does not have flesh and bones, as you see I have" (Luke 24:39). Then He ate a piece of fish in front of His friends. His resurrection body is the pattern for ours: physical, recognisable, but no longer able to get sick, grow old or die.

That means real things to do. The Bible speaks of a banquet, where people "will come from the east and the west" to eat together (Matthew 8:11). It speaks of the nations bringing "the glory and honor" of their cultures into God's city (Revelation 21:26). It says God's people "will reign forever and ever" (Revelation 22:5), which suggests work, responsibility and creativity, not endless sitting.

### With God

But the best thing about heaven isn't the place. It's the Person. "They will see His face" (Revelation 22:4). There is no temple in the city, "because the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple" (Revelation 21:22).

That's why it won't be boring. Boredom comes from things running out. God never runs out. Worship there won't be one long service; it will be everything you do, done in the joy of His presence. Paul says, "Now we see but a dim reflection as in a mirror; then we shall see face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12).

### What we don't know

Here the Bible is humble, and we should be too. "No eye has seen, no ear has heard, no heart has imagined, what God has prepared for those who love Him" (1 Corinthians 2:9). Some details are open questions.

**Where Christians agree:** God will raise His people bodily; there will be no more sin, death or tears; God will live with His people for ever; Jesus will be at the centre of it all.

**Where Christians differ:**

- **Renewed or replaced?** Many Christians believe this present earth will be cleansed and renewed, like a body raised from the dead. Others read verses such as "the elements will be destroyed by fire" (2 Peter 3:10) as meaning God will start again with a wholly new creation.
- **Will we recognise each other?** Most Christians have believed we will, since the disciples recognised the risen Jesus. Exactly how much of this life we'll remember is less clear.
- **Marriage and family.** Jesus said that "people will neither marry nor be given in marriage" (Matthew 22:30). Christians differ on what that means for relationships we love now, though all agree nothing good will be lost that isn't replaced by something better.
- **Rewards.** Some think there will be different rewards or responsibilities; others stress that everyone will be fully happy.

This book won't settle those. What matters most is clear: you will be with God, and it will be good.

**Think about it:** If heaven is about being with God, what does that suggest about how to get ready for it now?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Augustine of Hippo* — Augustine, who began the *Confessions* with a restless heart, ends with the rest that has no evening and pictures heaven as seeing God face to face: "there we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise." Start with chapter 22, "The Sabbath Which Hath No Evening".

## Will My Pet Go to Heaven?

**The short answer:** The Bible doesn't say directly, so nobody can promise you either way. What it does say is that God cares for animals, that the whole creation will be made new, and that the new world will be full of life. Faithful Christians differ on whether our own pets will be there, but all agree that God is good and will not disappoint us.

If you've just lost a pet, you might be reading this with tears in your eyes. A dog that greeted you every day after school, a cat that slept on your bed, a rabbit you looked after for years: losing them really hurts. That isn't silly. You loved them, and they were part of your life. It's right to grieve. The Bible says, "weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15), and that includes this kind of grief.

So let's be honest and kind at the same time.

### What the Bible doesn't say

There is no verse that says, "Your pets will be in heaven," and no verse that says they won't. Anyone who tells you it's certain either way is going further than Scripture. Even the Bible admits some mystery about animals: "Who knows if the spirit of man rises upward and the spirit of the animal descends into the earth?" (Ecclesiastes 3:21).

That might feel disappointing. But it also means you don't have to believe anyone who says flatly, "Animals just disappear."

### What the Bible does say

**God cares about animals.** "The LORD is good to all; His compassion rests on all He has made" (Psalm 145:9). "O LORD, You preserve man and beast" (Psalm 36:6). Jesus said of sparrows, "not one of them is forgotten by God" (Luke 12:6). God even ended the book of Jonah by pointing out that He cared about a great city's people, "and many cattle as well" (Jonah 4:11). And He tells us to be kind to them: "A righteous man regards the life of his animal" (Proverbs 12:10).

**Animals are part of God's covenant and God's new creation.** After the flood, God made His promise "with every living creature" (Genesis 9:10). Paul says the whole creation is waiting for the day when it "will be set free from its bondage to decay and brought into the glorious freedom of the children of God" (Romans 8:21). Isaiah pictures a world where "The wolf will live with the lamb" (Isaiah 11:6). And John hears "every creature in heaven and on earth" praising God (Revelation 5:13).

So the new creation will not be empty of animals. That much is clear.

### Where Christians differ

**Some Christians** believe animals don't have souls that live on the way human souls do. They expect the new creation to be full of animals, but not necessarily the same individual animals we know now.

**Others** have hoped for more. John Wesley, the eighteenth-century preacher, believed that animals who suffered in this broken world would be restored in the new one. C. S. Lewis wondered whether animals deeply loved by people might share in some way in their resurrection.

**Many Christians** simply say: we don't know, and we can trust God with it.

All of them agree on what matters most. God made animals, loves them, and is renewing His whole creation. Whatever He does, it will be good, and nobody in the new creation will feel that something was stolen from them.

### If you're grieving

It's fine to pray about your pet and to thank God for the joy it gave you. Tell God you're sad. "Cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7). Talk to your family too, and let them help you remember. If the sadness grows into something that won't lift, tell a trusted adult.

And here's something worth holding on to. The God who noticed every sparrow noticed your pet. And He noticed you loving it. That love didn't come from nowhere. It came from Him.

**Think about it:** What does the way God cares for animals tell you about the way He cares for you?

**Go deeper:** *Sermons on Several Occasions* by John Wesley — in this sermon on Romans 8, Wesley argues from the Bible's teaching that God's "mercy is over all his works" and hopes that the animals who suffer now will be restored in the new creation, while urging his hearers to be tender toward animals today. Start with chapter 61, "The General Deliverance".

## What Is the Gospel in One Minute?

**The short answer:** God made us to know Him, but we turned away and broke our relationship with Him. Jesus, God the Son, lived the life we should have lived, died in our place for our sins, and rose again. Everyone who turns from sin and trusts Him is forgiven, made right with God and given new life for ever.

A friend asks, out of nowhere: "So what do Christians actually believe?" You have about sixty seconds before the bell goes. What do you say?

"Gospel" means "good news". It isn't advice about how to be a better person. It's news about something God has done. Paul summed it up "as of first importance: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).

### The one-minute version

Here's one way to say it. You don't have to memorise it word for word.

> God made you, and He loves you. You were made to know Him.
> But all of us have turned away from God to run our own lives. That's what the Bible calls sin, and it cuts us off from Him, now and for ever.
> God didn't leave us there. He came Himself in Jesus. Jesus lived a perfect life, died on the cross to take the punishment for our sin, and rose from the dead.
> Now He offers forgiveness and new life as a free gift. You can't earn it. You receive it by turning from your sin and trusting Him.

### Unpacking it

**God.** It starts with God, not with us. He made everything and He is good. You weren't an accident; you were made to know Him.

**Us.** The bad news makes sense of the good. "For the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 6:23). Sin isn't just a few mistakes. It's turning our backs on the God who made us, and it has a real cost.

**Jesus.** This is the heart of it. "For God so loved the world that He gave His one and only Son" (John 3:16). Jesus is God become a human being. He didn't come to show us how to save ourselves. He came to save us. "For Christ also suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring you to God" (1 Peter 3:18). Paul puts the exchange like this: "God made Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21). He took what we deserved; we receive what He deserves. And He didn't stay dead. The resurrection proves the payment was accepted and death is beaten.

**Response.** Jesus' own first message was, "Repent and believe in the gospel!" (Mark 1:15). Repent means turning around: from running your own life to following Him. Believe means trusting Him, resting your whole weight on what He has done. "For it is by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves; it is the gift of God, not by works, so that no one can boast" (Ephesians 2:8–9). And the promise is wide open: "believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead, you will be saved" (Romans 10:9).

### Saying it to a friend

- **Use your own words.** "Sin" and "repent" may mean nothing to your friend. Try "doing life without God" and "turning around".
- **Keep Jesus at the centre.** The good news isn't "come to my church" or "follow these rules". It's "meet Jesus".
- **Be honest about what it means to you.** One sentence about why you trust Him can say more than ten facts.
- **Don't force it.** "Always be prepared to give a defense to everyone who asks you the reason for the hope that is in you. But respond with gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15).
- **Invite, don't corner.** "Do you want to read one of the Gospels with me?" is a great next step.

And before you tell anyone else, it's worth asking: have *you* received this good news yourself? If not, you can, right now, in your own words. Tell God you've gone your own way, thank Jesus for dying and rising for you, and ask Him to be your Lord. Then tell a Christian you trust.

**Think about it:** Can you say the gospel in your own words, in under a minute? Try it out loud.

**Go deeper:** *Tukutendereza* — this chapter shows how the East African Revival carried its whole message in a four-line hymn ("We praise You, Jesus. Jesus, Lamb of God. Your blood has cleansed me. I thank You, Saviour."), showing the gospel can be short enough to sing at a graveside and still be about Jesus, not us. Start with chapter 13, "Tukutendereza Yesu — Four Lines as a Theology".

# Part 2 · Faith Out Loud

## What If My Friends Think Christianity Is Stupid?

**The short answer:** It hurts when people you like think what matters most to you is silly, and you're not the first Christian to feel it. You don't have to win every argument or prove you're the cleverest person in the room. Stay kind, stay honest, keep thinking, and let your friends see that faith and a working brain can live in the same person.

It might be a joke in the group chat. A friend rolls their eyes when you say you went to church. Someone in class says only gullible people believe in God, and everyone laughs, and you feel your face go hot. Nobody has actually argued with you. They've just made it clear that, in their eyes, believing is a bit embarrassing.

(Book 1 answered the big objections, like whether science has disproved God and whether the Bible was changed over time. This chapter is about something different: what it feels like when the people around you think it's all stupid, and what to do with that feeling.)

### You're in good company

The Bible expected this. Paul wrote, "For the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God" (1 Corinthians 1:18). To the clever people of his day, a God who died on a cross sounded ridiculous. Paul knew it, and he preached it anyway.

When Paul spoke to the philosophers in Athens about Jesus rising from the dead, "some began to mock him," while others said, "We want to hear you again on this topic" (Acts 17:32). Notice that the same talk got two reactions. Some laughed. Some got curious. That's often how it goes. Later, a Roman governor shouted at him, "You are insane, Paul!" and Paul answered calmly, "I am not insane, most excellent Festus; I am speaking words of truth and sobriety" (Acts 26:24–25). He didn't get angry. He didn't sulk. He just said, politely, that what he believed was true and sensible.

### Mockery isn't an argument

It helps to notice what is actually happening. A laugh or an eye-roll is not a reason. "Only idiots believe that" isn't evidence. It's a way of making a view look uncool without having to think about it. Plenty of brilliant people, scientists, writers, historians and philosophers, have been Christians, and many still are. You don't need to list them every time someone laughs. But you can quietly remember that being mocked and being wrong are not the same thing.

At the same time, be honest with yourself. Sometimes friends think Christianity is stupid because of things Christians have actually said or done. If a friend says, "Christians just hate people," that might be a reason to listen, not just defend.

### You don't have to win

Many Christians feel they have to answer every jab on the spot. You don't. "a servant of the Lord must not be quarrelsome, but must be kind to everyone, able to teach, and forbearing" (2 Timothy 2:24). Sometimes the best response is a light one: "Yeah, I do believe it. Happy to talk about why sometime." Then change the subject. You've been honest, and you haven't turned lunch into a debate.

"A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (Proverbs 15:1). When you stay calm and good-humoured, you show that your faith isn't fragile. People who are secure don't need to shout.

### Have some reasons ready

Peter wrote, "Always be prepared to give a defense to everyone who asks you the reason for the hope that is in you. But respond with gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15). Notice the word "asks". Mockery sometimes turns into a real question later, often one-on-one, when the group isn't watching. Be ready for that moment. Know why you believe Jesus rose from the dead. Read a Gospel for yourself. Ask your youth leader the hard questions you're embarrassed to ask anyone else. And if a friend asks something you can't answer, say, "Good question. I'll find out." That's not losing. That's being honest.

### Keep being their friend

Don't drop friends because they don't share your faith, and don't treat them as projects. The best answer to "Christianity is stupid" is often a Christian who is kind, thoughtful, funny and loyal for years. Some of the people who laugh now will remember you later, when life gets hard and they start asking real questions.

**Think about it:** When someone mocks your faith, what is your first instinct: to hide, to fight, or something else? What would a gentle, honest answer sound like?

**Go deeper:** *They Were Young – Book 3: Questions* — at seventeen C. S. Lewis wrote to his believing friend Arthur that all religions were made-up mythologies, and he became a confident atheist; years later Christian friends, including J. R. R. Tolkien, kept talking with him, and on a long walk one night helped him see that the story of Christ really happened. Start with chapter 6, "C. S. Lewis: The Boy Who Learned to Argue".

## What If My Faith Makes Me Unpopular?

**The short answer:** Jesus was honest that following Him would sometimes cost His followers friends and reputation. But He never asks you to go looking for rejection or to be awkward for the sake of it. Be the kindest, most normal version of yourself, hold firm where it matters, and remember that you are never alone.

Maybe you said no to a party everyone else went to. Maybe you won't join in when the group mocks someone, or share the video, or cheat on the test, and you've noticed the invitations drying up. Maybe someone found out you're a Christian and now you're "the religious one". It can feel lonely, and it can make you wonder whether faith is worth it.

### Jesus told us the cost

Jesus didn't hide this. "If the world hates you, understand that it hated Me first. If you were of the world, it would love you as its own" (John 15:18–19). Paul was blunter: "all who desire to live godly lives in Christ Jesus will be persecuted" (2 Timothy 3:12). For many Christians around the world today, that means prison, violence or losing their families. For most teenagers reading this, it's smaller, but it still hurts: being left out, laughed at, or misunderstood.

Peter described something very familiar. When Christians stopped joining in with their old crowd, "they consider it strange of you not to plunge with them into the same flood of reckless indiscretion, and they heap abuse on you" (1 Peter 4:4). People can feel judged simply because you don't do what they do, even when you haven't said a word.

And Jesus said something surprising about it: "Blessed are you when people insult you, persecute you, and falsely say all kinds of evil against you because of Me" (Matthew 5:11). Not just "it'll be all right". *Blessed*. God sees it, and it matters to Him.

### But don't confuse the two kinds of unpopular

There's an important catch. Peter also wrote, "none of you should suffer as a murderer or thief or wrongdoer, or even as a meddler. But if you suffer as a Christian, do not be ashamed" (1 Peter 4:15–16). Some unpopularity has nothing to do with Jesus. If people avoid you because you're preachy, judgemental, smug or constantly telling them what they're doing wrong, that isn't persecution. That's just being hard to be around.

So be honest with yourself. Am I being left out because I follow Jesus, or because I've been unkind, superior or weird in ways that have nothing to do with Him? Christians shouldn't go looking for opposition so that they can feel brave. Jesus Himself "grew in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and man" (Luke 2:52), and the early church was "enjoying the favor of all the people" (Acts 2:47). Being likeable isn't a sin. Being faithful sometimes costs you, but faithfulness and friendliness usually go together.

### Courage when it does cost

When it really is about Jesus, you will need courage. "The fear of man is a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD is set securely on high" (Proverbs 29:25). Fear of what people think can quietly steer everything: what you say, what you post, who you're seen with. Courage doesn't mean making speeches. Often it just means quietly doing the right thing, again and again, without apologising for it.

It also means not paying people back. If someone mocks you, you don't have to mock them. "If it is possible on your part, live at peace with everyone" (Romans 12:18). And if being left out turns into bullying, online or in person, that's different. It is never your fault. Tell a trusted adult, keep telling until someone helps, and keep the evidence if it's online. **If you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.**

### You need friends too

Following Jesus was never meant to be done alone. If you're the only Christian in your class, look for others: at church, in a youth group, at a Christian club at school, or even one friend who shares your faith. Ask God for one good friend. It's a prayer He loves to answer. And remember that being unpopular for a while is not the end of the story. Many people who keep their convictions quietly and kindly end up being the ones others trust most.

**Think about it:** Is there a place where your faith has cost you something? Is it really about Jesus, or is there something in how you've acted that you need to change?

**Go deeper:** *They Were Young – Book 4: Sent* — Eric Liddell, Scotland's fastest sprinter, quietly refused to run his best Olympic race because it was on a Sunday; some newspapers and officials thought he was letting his country down, but he didn't argue or sulk, he just trained for another race. Start with chapter 6, "Eric Liddell: The Race He Would Not Run".

## Should I Only Have Christian Friends?

**The short answer:** No. Jesus was famous for being a friend to people who didn't share His faith, and He sends His followers into the world, not away from it. But your closest friends shape you more than almost anything, so it's wise to have at least a few close friends who share your faith and help you follow Jesus.

Some Christian teenagers are told, or quietly feel, that they should keep away from non-Christians in case they're a bad influence. Others have almost no Christian friends at all and wonder whether that matters. Both questions deserve a good answer.

### Jesus, the friend of sinners

The religious people of Jesus' day were shocked by the company He kept. When some religious teachers saw Him eating with people everyone else avoided, they asked His disciples, "Why does He eat with tax collectors and sinners?" (Mark 2:16). Jesus answered, "It is not the healthy who need a doctor, but the sick" (Mark 2:17). His critics gave Him a nickname meant as an insult: "a friend of tax collectors and sinners" (Luke 7:34). Jesus wore it gladly.

Notice, too, what Jesus prayed for His followers: "I am not asking that You take them out of the world, but that You keep them from the evil one" (John 17:15). He doesn't want us hiding in a Christian bubble. He calls us "the salt of the earth" and "the light of the world" (Matthew 5:13–14), and salt only works when it's actually in the food.

Paul once had to correct Christians who thought they should cut off everyone outside the church. He said he wasn't talking about people "of this world". "In that case," he wrote, "you would have to leave this world" (1 Corinthians 5:10). Being friends with people who don't believe is normal Christian life.

### Influence goes both ways

Here's the honest part. Friendship changes people, and the change can go in either direction. "Bad company corrupts good character" (1 Corinthians 15:33). And, more positively, "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20).

That isn't about Christians versus everyone else. Some non-Christian friends are honest, loyal and kind, and good for you. Some Christian friends drag you down. The real question is: who is shaping me, and in which direction? If a friendship keeps pulling you towards things you know are wrong, or you always end up hiding your faith to fit in, that's a warning sign. You may need to step back, even if you still care about them.

### Why you need some close Christian friends

Being friends with lots of people doesn't mean every friendship is the same depth. Jesus was kind to crowds, but He had twelve disciples, and three of them, Peter, James and John, were closest of all.

Your closest friends are the ones you tell the truth to, the ones who notice when you're struggling. It makes a huge difference if at least some of them share your faith. "As iron sharpens iron, so one man sharpens another" (Proverbs 27:17). A Christian friend can pray with you, ask how you're really doing with God, and tell you when you're drifting. "Two are better than one... For if one falls down, his companion can lift him up" (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10). When following Jesus gets hard, you'll want someone beside you who understands why it matters.

The Bible's warning not to be "unequally yoked with unbelievers" (2 Corinthians 6:14) is about partnerships that tie your life closely to someone pulling a different way, like marriage. It isn't a ban on ordinary friendship. But it does remind you that the people you bind your life to most closely should be heading the same direction as you.

### Wisdom, not rules

So there's no rule like "only Christian friends" or "no Christian friends needed". There's wisdom:

- **Be a real friend to everyone.** Not to convert them, but because Jesus loves them and so do you.
- **Choose your closest friends carefully.** "A righteous man is cautious in friendship" (Proverbs 12:26).
- **Find your people.** If you have no Christian friends, ask God for one, and look for them at church or youth group.
- **Notice the pull.** If you're becoming someone you don't want to be, talk to a trusted adult about it.

**Think about it:** Who are your three closest friends? Which way is each of them pulling you, and which way are you pulling them?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Augustine of Hippo* — Augustine loved his friends deeply, read and laughed and argued with them, and found that friendship became something richer when friends shared their faith; his friend Alypius came to Christ in the same hour he did. Start with chapter 18, "His Friend in Thee".

## What If My Parents Aren't Christians?

**The short answer:** God still asks you to honour your parents, even if they don't share your faith, and the best way to show them Jesus is usually by how you live, not by preaching at them. Pray for them, be patient, and find other Christians to support you. And if believing ever leads to you being hurt at home, tell another trusted adult.

Maybe you came to faith through a friend, a school club or a camp, and you're the only Christian in your house. Maybe your parents are fine with it but don't understand. Maybe they think it's a phase. Or maybe they're actively against it, and church has become a battleground.

You're not as unusual as you might feel. Timothy, one of Paul's closest helpers, was "the son of a believing Jewish woman and a Greek father" (Acts 16:1). His family was split on faith. Even Jesus' own family struggled: "even His own brothers did not believe in Him" (John 7:5), and at one point His relatives tried to take Him home, convinced that "He is out of His mind" (Mark 3:21). Later, His brother James became a leader in the church. Families can change.

### Honour them

"Honor your father and mother" (Exodus 20:12) isn't only for Christian parents. Your parents have fed you, cared for you and given you more than you know, and God asks you to respect them. (Book 2 answered whether you have to obey your parents and where the limits are.) That means speaking respectfully, helping at home, being honest and not mocking their views, even when you disagree.

Honour also means not sneaking around. If your parents say you can't go to youth group on a school night, lying about where you are does more harm to your witness than missing a meeting. Ask respectfully, explain why it matters to you, and accept their answer when it's not about doing something wrong.

### Live it, don't preach it

Peter wrote to Christian wives whose husbands didn't believe, that they could be "won over without words" by their behaviour (1 Peter 3:1). The same principle works for teenagers. Your parents will learn more about Jesus from you doing the dishes without being asked, owning up when you're wrong and being kind to your siblings than from any sermon you give them at dinner.

That doesn't mean never talking about your faith. If they ask, tell them honestly. But don't lecture, don't leave tracts on their pillow, and don't tell them they're going to hell. Respect is far more persuasive than pressure.

### Pray, and be patient

Pray for your parents by name, regularly. Ask God to bless them, not just to convert them. And don't measure your faithfulness by how quickly they change. Some parents come to faith through their children years later. Some never do. Either way, your love for them is never wasted.

### When they oppose your faith

Some parents are deeply upset when a child becomes a Christian. It can feel like a rejection of the family or its religion. If that's your home, be gentle, keep honouring them, and don't make every conversation a fight. Jesus did warn that following Him could divide families (Matthew 10:37), and promised that those who lose family for His sake will find a new family among His people (Mark 10:29–30). "Though my father and mother forsake me, the LORD will receive me" (Psalm 27:10).

Disagreement is one thing. Being hurt is another. **If anyone at home hurts you, threatens you or makes you feel unsafe, because of your faith or for any other reason, it is not your fault. Tell another trusted adult right away: a teacher, a school counsellor, a relative or a youth leader.** Obeying God never means keeping abuse secret.

### Find Christian support

You need people who can help you grow: a youth leader, a Christian friend's family, an older Christian at church who can mentor you. Ask your parents if it's all right for you to spend time with them. If you can't get to church often, you can still read the Bible and pray every day. God is not limited by your circumstances.

**Think about it:** What is one way you could honour your parents this week that would show them, without words, what Jesus is like?

## How Should I Treat Friends of Other Religions?

**The short answer:** With love, respect and real friendship. You can be honest that you believe Jesus is the only way to God and still be a kind, interested, loyal friend to people who believe something different. Never mock what they hold sacred, and never treat them as a project.

At school you might sit next to a Muslim friend who fasts during Ramadan, a Hindu friend who celebrates Diwali, a Sikh friend who wears a turban, a Jewish friend or a Buddhist one. You might wonder: am I allowed to be close friends with them? Should I try to convert them? What if they ask me about my faith, or invite me to something at their place of worship?

(Book 1 answered whether all religions are basically the same. This chapter is about the relationships.)

### Love first, always

When an expert in the law asked Jesus what he must do, Jesus agreed with him that the heart of it all is to love God and to "Love your neighbor as yourself" (Luke 10:27). Then He told the story of the Good Samaritan, a man from a different and despised religious group who showed mercy to a stranger. Jesus ended, "Go and do likewise" (Luke 10:37).

Jesus Himself crossed religious lines. He asked a Samaritan woman for a drink, which surprised her because "Jews do not associate with Samaritans" (John 4:9). He talked with her honestly about God, and He treated her with dignity.

Peter puts it simply: "Treat everyone with high regard" (1 Peter 2:17). Everyone. Your friends of other faiths are made in the image of God, deeply loved by Him, and worth your time and respect.

### No contempt

Never mock someone's religion, their holy book, their prophet, their food laws, their clothing or their prayers. That's true even in jokes, even when they're not around, and even online. "Do to others as you would have them do to you" (Luke 6:31). You wouldn't want someone sneering at Jesus or at your church. Don't sneer at what they hold sacred.

When Paul visited Athens, a city full of idols that troubled him deeply, he didn't open with insults. He said, "Men of Athens, I see that in every way you are very religious" (Acts 17:22), and he started from something they already knew, an altar "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD" (Acts 17:23). He disagreed with them profoundly, and he still spoke respectfully.

### Be curious

Ask your friends what they believe and why. What does their faith mean to them? What do they pray for? What are their festivals about? Then listen properly, without just waiting for your turn to talk. You'll understand them better, and they'll feel respected. You may also find that they take their faith more seriously than many Christians do, and that may challenge you.

### Be honest too

Real friendship doesn't mean pretending you agree. You believe Jesus is God the Son, who died and rose again, and that He is the way to the Father. Your friends will usually respect you more for saying so kindly than for hiding it. Paul's advice works well here: "Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone" (Colossians 4:6). Gracious, but with some flavour.

If they ask about Jesus, tell them what He means to you. Invite them to read a Gospel or come to something at church, and accept a "no" without sulking. And if they want to tell you about their faith, listen respectfully. You can be secure enough in Jesus to hear another view without feeling threatened.

### Some practical wisdom

- **Visiting their festivals or homes.** Sharing a meal or celebrating a friend's family occasion is usually a kind thing to do. If you're unsure whether to join in an act of worship, talk to your parents or youth leader first. You can be present and respectful without praying to another god.
- **Don't be a salesperson.** If you're only friends with someone to convert them, they'll feel it. Be their friend because you like them.
- **Stand up for them.** If someone at school mocks or bullies them because of their religion, be the one who steps in or tells a teacher.

**Think about it:** Do you have a friend of another faith? What is one question you could ask them this week, and really listen to the answer?

Start with chapter 16, "Sympathy with All Classes".

## What's the Difference Between Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants?

**The short answer:** All three are Christian traditions that confess one God in three Persons, and Jesus as fully God and fully man, crucified and risen. They differ on serious questions: where final authority lies, how a person is made right with God, Mary and the saints, communion and the pope. Evangelicals themselves differ on how to regard Catholic and Orthodox Christians, and this chapter sets out the differences fairly without mocking anyone.

Maybe your best friend is Catholic and goes to Mass. Maybe you've walked into an Orthodox church full of icons and candles. Maybe you've heard someone say Catholics "aren't real Christians", and someone else say the differences don't matter at all. This is a question where facts matter and where Christians disagree, so here's both.

### Where they agree

Catholics (about half the world's Christians), Orthodox and Protestants all confess the ancient creeds, like the Nicene Creed. They agree that there is one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit; that Jesus is God the Son, born of the Virgin Mary; that He "died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4); that He will come again; and that the Bible is God's Word. That shared core is huge.

### How the split happened

For about a thousand years, Christians in East and West mostly belonged to one church, though with growing tensions. In 1054, the Eastern (Orthodox) and Western (Catholic) churches split, partly over the authority of the bishop of Rome. Then, in 1517, a German monk called Martin Luther challenged the sale of indulgences, and the Protestant Reformation began. It was mainly an argument about how a sinner is made right with God and about the authority of Scripture. The Catholic Church responded with reforms of its own, and the divisions remained.

### The main differences

- **Authority.** Catholics hold that God speaks through Scripture and Tradition, interpreted by the Church's teaching office under the pope. Orthodox Christians hold Scripture within Holy Tradition, especially the ancient councils. Protestants hold that Scripture is the final authority over every tradition, though they value the creeds.
- **Salvation.** All three say salvation is by God's grace in Christ. Catholics teach that God's grace, received through faith and the sacraments, makes a person righteous over a lifetime. Orthodox Christians often describe salvation as healing and growing union with God. Protestants teach that a person is "justified by faith in Christ and not by works of the law" (Galatians 2:16): declared right with God by trusting Christ alone, with good works following as the fruit.
- **Mary and the saints.** Catholics and Orthodox honour Mary highly and ask her and the saints to pray for them, insisting this is not worship. Protestants honour Mary as a model of faith, who said, "all generations will call me blessed" (Luke 1:48), but pray directly to God, stressing that "there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5).
- **Communion.** Catholics believe the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood; Orthodox believe in a real change without defining how; Protestants hold a range of views. (Book 2 answered what communion is for.)
- **The pope.** Catholics see the pope as Peter's successor and head of the Church on earth, pointing to Jesus' words, "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build My church" (Matthew 16:18). Orthodox and Protestants read that verse differently and don't accept his authority.

### How evangelicals regard them

Here evangelicals genuinely differ. Some warmly regard believing Catholics and Orthodox as brothers and sisters in Christ, while disagreeing on important points. Others are more cautious, believing that official teaching on salvation, Mary or the saints can obscure the gospel of grace. Almost all agree on two things: no one is saved by a church label, Protestant or otherwise, but by trusting Christ; and individual Catholics and Orthodox can certainly know and love Him. This book won't decide between those views.

### How to talk about it

Never mock someone's church. Learn what your Catholic or Orthodox friends actually believe, from them. Jesus prayed "that all of them may be one" (John 17:21). Talk about your differences honestly and kindly, and you may find you share more than you expected.

**Think about it:** Do you know what your own church believes about these questions, and why? What could you learn from a friend in another tradition?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of C. S. Lewis* — Lewis, an Anglican, pictured "mere Christianity" as a great hall with doors into many rooms: you must choose a room because you believe its teaching true, and then be kind to those who chose other doors. Before publishing one series of his famous talks, he checked the script with an Anglican, a Methodist, a Presbyterian and a Roman Catholic. Start with chapter 6, "The Faith Held in Common".

## What Does the Bible Say About Racism?

**The short answer:** The Bible teaches that every human being, of every colour and nation, is made in the image of God and belongs to one human family. Racism is a sin against God and against people He loves. Christians have sometimes failed terribly here, and the right response is honest repentance and active love.

You may have experienced racism yourself: a comment, a joke, being followed in a shop, being treated as if you don't belong. Or you may have heard racist things from people you know, even people at church, and wondered what God thinks. The Bible is clear.

### One human family

The Bible's story begins with all humanity made in God's image: "So God created man in His own image; in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them" (Genesis 1:27). Not one group. Everyone. Every person you will ever meet carries that image, and that is where their worth comes from.

Paul told the philosophers of Athens, "From one man He made every nation of men, that they should inhabit the whole earth" (Acts 17:26). The idea of separate, superior races has no place in the Bible's story. There's one human family, with many beautiful variations.

### God's plan has always included every nation

When God called Abraham, He promised that "all the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (Genesis 12:3). When Peter was sent to the house of a Roman soldier, he learned, "I now truly understand that God does not show favoritism, but welcomes those from every nation who fear Him and do what is right" (Acts 10:34–35). And the Bible's picture of the end is breathtaking: "a multitude too large to count, from every nation and tribe and people and tongue, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). Heaven is not one colour. Diversity isn't a problem God puts up with. It's part of the glory.

In Christ, the barriers that divide people come down. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). Jesus "has torn down the dividing wall of hostility" (Ephesians 2:14).

### Why racism is sin

James is direct: "if you show favoritism, you sin and are convicted by the law as transgressors" (James 2:9). Treating people as lesser because of their skin, their ethnicity or where their family is from is favouritism of the worst kind. John goes further: "anyone who does not love his brother, whom he has seen, cannot love God, whom he has not seen" (1 John 4:20). You can't love God and despise the people He made.

Even Bible heroes failed here. Peter, after all he had learned, drew back from eating with non-Jewish Christians "for fear of those in the circumcision group" (Galatians 2:12), and Paul confronted him publicly. Racism can creep in among believers. It has to be named.

### The church's failures

It would be dishonest to pretend Christians have always got this right. Some churches have kept Black worshippers in separate galleries or turned them away. Some Christians defended racial segregation, and some churches in South Africa supported apartheid, using twisted readings of the Bible. (Book 2 answered whether Christianity was used to defend slavery.) These were real sins, and many churches have since confessed them.

But it's also true that many who fought racism did so because of their faith, from Christians who opposed slavery to Black pastors who led the struggle for civil rights. They knew what the Bible said, and they called the church back to it.

### What to do

- **Check your own heart.** Do you think of some groups as less than others? Ask God to show you, and to change you.
- **Refuse racist jokes.** Don't laugh, don't share, and when you can, say kindly, "That's not okay."
- **Build friendships across lines.** Listen to friends whose experience is different from yours.
- **Stand with those targeted.** If someone is bullied because of their race, step in or tell a teacher.
- **If it's happening to you,** it is never your fault. Tell a trusted adult, and keep telling until someone helps. If it's online, keep the evidence. **If you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.**

God "has shown you, O man, what is good... to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8).

**Think about it:** Is there a joke, attitude or assumption about another group of people that you need to let go of?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Julia A. J. Foote* — Julia Foote, a nineteenth-century Black preacher, tells how her parents had to wait in the gallery until every white member had taken communion, and asks plainly whether a church that divides the Lord's table by colour is being led by the Holy Spirit. Start with chapter 10, "O Prejudice".

## Should Christians Ever Fight in a War?

**The short answer:** Faithful Christians genuinely disagree. Some believe Jesus calls His followers never to use deadly force, even in war; others believe a war can sometimes be just, to protect the innocent, though always as a last resort. Both sides agree that war is a terrible result of sin, that peace is God's goal, and that Christians must never hate their enemies.

Maybe someone in your family serves in the armed forces. Maybe you've seen war on the news and wondered how Christians can support it, or how they can refuse to defend people. Maybe you're thinking about a military career yourself. This is a question where sincere, Bible-loving Christians have reached different answers for nearly two thousand years.

### Where Christians agree

- **War comes from sin.** "What causes conflicts and quarrels among you? Don't they come from the passions at war within you?" (James 4:1). War is never good in itself.
- **Peace is God's goal.** "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called sons of God" (Matthew 5:9). One day God's people "will beat their swords into plowshares", and "Nation will no longer take up the sword against nation" (Isaiah 2:4).
- **No hatred, no revenge.** Jesus said, "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). "Do not avenge yourselves, beloved" (Romans 12:19). No Christian may delight in killing, commit atrocities or treat an enemy as less than human.
- **Respect for conscience.** Christians on both sides should respect those who serve in good conscience and those who refuse in good conscience.

### Pacifism

Many Christians, including Mennonites, Quakers and others, and many in the early church, hold that Christians should never kill, even in war. They point to Jesus' teaching: "I tell you not to resist an evil person. If someone slaps you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matthew 5:39). When Peter struck with a sword to defend Jesus, Jesus said, "Put your sword back in its place... For all who draw the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52), and healed the wounded man. Jesus told Pilate, "My kingdom is not of this world; if it were, My servants would fight" (John 18:36). Pacifists believe Christians are called to show the way of the cross: to overcome evil by suffering love, not by force. Many have served bravely as medics or relief workers instead.

### Just war

Many other Christians, following thinkers such as Augustine, believe that war can sometimes be right. They point to Paul's teaching that the governing authority "does not carry the sword in vain. He is God's servant, an agent of retribution to the wrongdoer" (Romans 13:4). They believe that loving your neighbour can mean protecting them from an attacker. They have set out strict tests for a just war: a just cause, such as defending the innocent; lawful authority; right intentions; a last resort, after peaceful means have failed; a reasonable hope of success; force no greater than needed; and never deliberately targeting civilians.

### Soldiers in the Bible

Both sides also have to think about soldiers in the New Testament. When some soldiers asked John the Baptist what they should do, he said, "Do not take money by force or false accusation... Be content with your wages" (Luke 3:14). He didn't tell them to quit. Jesus praised a Roman centurion: "I have not found anyone in Israel with such great faith" (Matthew 8:10). Cornelius, the first non-Jewish convert in Acts, was "a centurion" who was "devout and God-fearing" (Acts 10:1–2).

Just-war Christians see this as a sign that soldiering can be an honourable calling. Pacifists reply that these passages don't say whether the soldiers stayed in the army, and that the Bible meets people where they are before calling them further.

This book won't decide between these views. Both are held by faithful Christians who love Jesus and take the Bible seriously.

### What you can do now

Pray for peace, for those caught up in war, for soldiers and for leaders. Be a peacemaker in your own friendships and family. And if you're considering a military career, or wondering whether you could ever serve, talk it through carefully with your parents and a pastor you trust.

**Think about it:** Which arguments on each side do you find strongest? What would it mean for you to be a peacemaker this week?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Dietrich Bonhoeffer* — Bonhoeffer, who had come close to Christian pacifism, later supported a plot against Hitler; this chapter sets out honestly the case of those who think he was right and those who think he was wrong, and refuses to pretend the question is easy. Start with chapter 16, "Responsibility and Guilt".

## Is It Okay to Enjoy Music and Films That Aren't Christian?

**The short answer:** Many Christians believe yes: God is the source of all beauty and creativity, and good art can be enjoyed with thanks wherever it comes from. But Christians differ on where the lines are, and the Bible asks each of us to follow our conscience, respect others' and think honestly about what is shaping us.

Your playlist probably isn't all worship songs. You watch films, binge series, follow artists who never mention God. Some Christians you know might only listen to Christian music. Others never think about it. Is either right? This is a question where the Bible gives clear principles, but where faithful Christians apply them differently.

### Where Christians agree

**All good things come from God.** "Every good and perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of the heavenly lights" (James 1:17). Beauty, melody, a great story, a clever joke, a moving performance: these reflect a creative God, even when the artist doesn't know Him. Theologians call this "common grace": God gives gifts of skill and beauty to people whether or not they believe. The first person in the Bible described as "filled... with the Spirit of God" was a craftsman, given "skill, ability, and knowledge in all kinds of craftsmanship" (Exodus 31:3).

Paul quoted pagan Greek poets when he preached in Athens, borrowing their words to say of God, "in Him we live and move and have our being" (Acts 17:28). He knew their work, and he found truth in it.

**What we take in shapes us.** Paul gives a beautiful test: "whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is right, whatever is pure, whatever is lovely, whatever is admirable—if anything is excellent or praiseworthy—think on these things" (Philippians 4:8). Notice that this verse isn't limited to "Christian" things. Lots of non-Christian art is true, honourable and lovely. And some "Christian" art is shallow.

But the same verse warns us. Some songs celebrate cruelty or treat people as objects. Some films soak you in images you'll struggle to get out of your head. "Guard your heart with all diligence, for from it flow springs of life" (Proverbs 4:23).

### Where Christians differ

Some Christians choose to keep almost entirely to Christian music and films. They want their minds filled with things that point directly to God, and they find that other content pulls them away. Other Christians enjoy a wide range of art, thoughtfully, believing that good art from any source can be received with thanks, and that it helps them understand the world and the people in it. Most are somewhere in between, and draw lines in different places.

Paul addressed this kind of disagreement in Romans 14. Some Christians in Rome kept special days; others didn't. His answer: "Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind" (Romans 14:5). And to both sides: "let us stop judging one another" (Romans 14:13). He added a serious warning: "everything that is not from faith is sin" (Romans 14:23). If your conscience is troubled by something, don't push past it.

### Questions to ask

Rather than looking for a rule, ask yourself honest questions:

- **Can I thank God for this?** Paul says that "whatever you do, do it all to the glory of God" (1 Corinthians 10:31).
- **What is it doing to me?** Is it making me more cynical, more anxious, more lustful, more unkind? Or does it make me think, feel and see more deeply? "Everything is permissible," but not everything is beneficial (1 Corinthians 10:23).
- **Am I watching it, or is it watching me?** If something is taking over your time and attention, that's worth noticing.
- **What would my parents say?** Families have different rules, especially about age ratings. Honour yours.
- **Am I judging others?** A friend may enjoy something you avoid, or avoid something you enjoy. Leave them to God.

### Enjoy, with thanks

You don't need to feel guilty for loving a beautiful song just because the singer isn't a Christian. Enjoy it, thank God for it, and let it make you more grateful to the One who gave the gift. Just keep your eyes open, and keep your conscience awake.

**Think about it:** Think of a song or film you love. What is true, honourable or lovely in it? Is there anything in it you need to be careful about?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of George MacDonald* — the Scottish storyteller whose fairy tales helped bring C. S. Lewis to faith believed that the imagination is a gift of God made "to mirror truth", and that stories and beauty can carry truth into places a sermon cannot reach. Start with chapter 14, "The Servant Imagination".

## Does God Care About My Schoolwork?

**The short answer:** Yes. God cares about your schoolwork because He cares about you, and because learning is one of the ways you can love Him with your mind. Working hard and honestly can be worship. But your grades are not your identity, and God wants you to rest as well as work.

It's easy to think God only cares about the "spiritual" parts of life: church, prayer, Bible reading. School just feels like something to get through. Or the opposite: school feels so important that your results seem to decide your whole worth, and the pressure is crushing. The Bible speaks to both.

### Work as worship

Paul wrote to people doing ordinary, unglamorous work: "Whatever you do, work at it with your whole being, for the Lord and not for men" (Colossians 3:23). Whatever you do. That includes algebra, essays, revision and homework you'd rather not do. Doing it carefully and honestly, even when nobody is checking, is a way of serving Jesus. "It is the Lord Christ you are serving" (Colossians 3:24).

That changes the question from "What's the least I can get away with?" to "How can I do this well, for Him?" It doesn't mean you'll love every subject. It means even the boring ones can be offered to God.

It also means honesty. Cheating, copying, or getting AI to write the work you're meant to do yourself might get you a mark, but it isn't working "for the Lord". God cares more about who you're becoming than about your score.

### Loving God with your mind

Jesus said the greatest commandment is to "Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind and with all your strength" (Mark 12:30). Your mind is part of how you love God. Learning about how the world works, about history, language, numbers, music and science, is learning about the world He made and the people He loves.

Daniel and his friends were teenagers taken to a foreign country and put through years of study. The Bible says, "To these four young men God gave knowledge and understanding in every kind of literature and wisdom" (Daniel 1:17). God cared about their education, and used it. Even Jesus "grew in wisdom and stature" (Luke 2:52). Learning and growing is part of being fully human.

### But grades are not your identity

Here's the other side. For many students, school becomes the thing that decides whether they feel good enough. A good grade, and you're worth something. A bad one, and you're a failure.

That's not how God sees you. Before Jesus had done a single miracle or preached a single sermon, His Father said, "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased!" (Matthew 3:17). If you belong to Jesus, God's love for you doesn't rise and fall with your results. You can work hard because you're loved, not in order to be loved.

### Stress and rest

School stress is real: exams, deadlines, comparisons, worries about the future. The Bible doesn't tell you to stop caring. It tells you where to put the weight. "Cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7).

It also tells you to rest. "In vain you rise early and stay up late, toiling for bread to eat— for He gives sleep to His beloved" (Psalm 127:2). Pulling all-nighters week after week isn't holiness. Jesus once said to His exhausted disciples, "Come with Me privately to a solitary place, and let us rest for a while" (Mark 6:31). Sleep, exercise, time with friends and a day off are good gifts, not wasted time.

Some practical ideas:

- **Start with prayer.** Before you study, ask God to help you focus and learn.
- **Plan ahead.** Leaving everything to the last minute is the fastest route to panic.
- **Ask for help.** Talk to teachers when you're stuck. That's wisdom, not weakness.
- **Know when to stop.** Set a finishing time and keep it.

If the pressure starts to feel overwhelming, talk to a parent, teacher or school counsellor. **If you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.** No exam is worth more than you.

**Think about it:** Which is harder for you: caring about schoolwork at all, or caring about it too much? What would working "for the Lord" look like this week?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Martin Luther* — Luther taught that ordinary work done in faith is holy, that God serves the world through the farmer, the teacher and the parent, and that we work freely for our neighbour, not to earn God's favour; the chapter also notes that Luther was no workaholic, but believed in rest and feasting. Start with chapter 11, "Holy Work in Ordinary Places".

# Part 3 · Heart and Life

## What If I Feel Lonely?

**The short answer:** Feeling lonely is not a sin, and it doesn't mean God has forgotten you. Jesus Himself was left alone by His friends, so He knows exactly what it feels like. God's answer to loneliness is both Himself and other people, and there are real steps you can take, starting this week.

You can be lonely in an empty bedroom. You can also be lonely in a crowded lunch hall, in a group chat with forty people in it, or sitting next to your own family at dinner. Loneliness isn't really about how many people are around you. It's the ache of feeling that nobody truly knows you, or that nobody would notice if you weren't there.

Some Christians feel guilty about that ache. *If God is with me, I shouldn't feel like this. Maybe my faith isn't real.* Let's deal with that first.

### Loneliness is not a sin

Before sin ever entered the world, while everything was still "very good", God looked at Adam and said, "It is not good for the man to be alone" (Genesis 2:18). Adam wasn't doing anything wrong. He wasn't spiritually weak. He was made for other people, and so are you. Loneliness is the sign of a real need God built into you, a bit like hunger. Being hungry isn't a sin. It's a signal.

The Bible is honest about lonely people. Near the end of his life, the apostle Paul wrote from prison, "At my first defense, no one stood with me, but everyone deserted me" (2 Timothy 4:16). That was one of the greatest Christians who ever lived, and he felt abandoned. But he went on: "But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me" (2 Timothy 4:17). Both things were true at once. People had left. God had not.

### Jesus has been there

On the night before He died, Jesus took His three closest friends into a garden and told them, "My soul is consumed with sorrow to the point of death. Stay here and keep watch with Me" (Matthew 26:38). He asked for company. And they fell asleep. "Were you not able to keep watch with Me for one hour?" (Matthew 26:40). A few hours later, when the soldiers came, "everyone deserted Him and fled" (Mark 14:50). On the cross He cried out, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Matthew 27:46).

So when you pray in a lonely moment, you aren't praying to Someone who can't relate. You're praying to Someone who has been more alone than you will ever be, and who went through it so that you would never be finally abandoned. His promise is, "Never will I leave you, never will I forsake you" (Hebrews 13:5).

### God sets the lonely in families

But God doesn't only say, "You have Me, so you don't need anyone else." The Bible says, "God settles the lonely in families" (Psalm 68:6). One of the main ways God answers loneliness is through His people. The church is meant to be a family where there's room for the person on the edge.

That's why loneliness usually needs action as well as prayer. A few things that genuinely help:

- **Go where people are, and keep going.** A youth group, a club, a team, a choir. Friendships rarely happen in one visit. They grow from turning up again and again.
- **Look for someone else on the edge.** There is almost always another lonely person in the room. Sit with them. Ask them a real question. Often the cure for your loneliness is noticing someone else's.
- **Be the one who asks.** Waiting to be invited can mean waiting forever. Suggest the walk, the game, the coffee. Some people will say no. Some will say yes.
- **Be careful with screens.** Scrolling through other people's fun can make you feel lonelier, not less. A voice call or a real meeting does far more than likes.
- **Talk to God honestly.** "Turn to me and be gracious, for I am lonely" (Psalm 25:16) is a real prayer from the Bible. You can pray it too.

### When it gets heavy

Sometimes loneliness settles into something darker: a sadness that won't lift, a feeling that you don't matter, or thoughts of not wanting to be here. That is not something to carry alone. Tell a parent, a youth leader, a school counsellor or another trusted adult what you're feeling. **If you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.** You matter far more than loneliness tells you.

**Think about it:** Who is one person in your life who might be lonely too, and what could you do for them this week?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Elisabeth Elliot* — Elliot, twice widowed and often alone on the mission field, refused to treat loneliness as either a sin or a dead end; the chapter shows how she offered it to God, and urged lonely people to turn outward and set others "in families" around their own table. Start with chapter 10, "Loneliness".

## What If I'm Being Bullied?

**The short answer:** Being bullied is never your fault, and God sees every bit of it. Tell a trusted adult, and keep telling until something changes; if it's happening online, keep the evidence. You don't have to fight back in the same way, and forgiving someone never means staying somewhere unsafe.

If you're being bullied, you probably already know how it feels: the knot in your stomach on the way to school, the dread when your phone buzzes, the replaying of every cruel word at night. Maybe you've started to wonder whether you deserve it. Let's be completely clear.

**Being bullied is never the victim's fault.** Not because of how you look, how you talk, what you believe, how much money your family has, or anything else. The person choosing to be cruel is responsible for that choice.

### God sees, and God cares

The Bible has a lot to say to people who are being pushed around. One psalm says to God, "But You have regarded trouble and grief; You see to repay it by Your hand. The victim entrusts himself to You" (Psalm 10:14). Another says, "Put my tears in Your bottle— are they not in Your book?" (Psalm 56:8). Not one of your tears has gone unnoticed.

God is also just. Bullying is not a small thing to Him. The same psalm promises that God will "vindicate the fatherless and oppressed, that the men of the earth may strike terror no more" (Psalm 10:18). He is on the side of the one being crushed.

And Jesus knows what it is like from the inside. Isaiah said of Him, "He was despised and rejected by men" (Isaiah 53:3). He was mocked, spat on, laughed at in public and stripped in front of a crowd. Whatever is being said or done to you, you are not facing it with a God who doesn't understand.

### Telling is not snitching

Many people stay silent because they don't want to be a "snitch", or because they're afraid it'll get worse. But telling an adult about bullying isn't getting someone in trouble for fun. It's asking for protection, which is exactly what adults are there for. The Bible even tells us to "open your mouth for those with no voice" (Proverbs 31:8). Sometimes the person with no voice is you, and you're allowed to speak up for yourself.

- **Tell a trusted adult.** A parent, a teacher, a head of year, a youth leader. Be specific: who, what, when, where.
- **Keep telling.** If the first adult doesn't help, tell another. And if it carries on, go back and say so.
- **Keep the evidence.** If it's online or by message, screenshot it before you block or report. Save dates and times.
- **Stay near safe people.** Walk with friends; avoid being alone where it usually happens, if you can.

### Don't fight fire with fire

When you've been hurt, it's natural to want to hurt back: the clever insult, the screenshot shared to humiliate them, the fist. Paul writes, "Do not repay anyone evil for evil" and "Do not avenge yourselves, beloved, but leave room for God's wrath" (Romans 12:17, 19). That isn't because what they did doesn't matter. It's because God takes it so seriously that He has promised to deal with it Himself. When Jesus was abused, "He did not retaliate... but entrusted Himself to Him who judges justly" (1 Peter 2:23).

Refusing to retaliate isn't the same as doing nothing. It means telling, reporting and protecting yourself, rather than becoming the thing you hate. "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Romans 12:21).

### Forgiveness is not staying unsafe

You may hear that Christians should forgive. That's true, and in time God can help you do it (Book 2 answered how to forgive someone who really hurt you). But forgiving someone does not mean pretending it's fine, keeping quiet, or putting yourself back in harm's way. You can forgive and still report. You can forgive and still keep your distance.

### When it gets too much

Bullying can make people feel worthless, hopeless or desperate to escape. If that's you, you need more than a strategy. You need someone with you. **If you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.**

And if you see someone else being bullied, don't join in, don't laugh along, and tell an adult. You might be the friend they've been praying for.

**Think about it:** If you're being bullied, which adult will you tell first? If you've seen someone else being bullied, what will you do next time?

**Go deeper:** *The Imitation of Christ (For Teens)* by Thomas à Kempis — this short chapter on what people say about you insists that other people's opinions "do not make you different from what you are", while saying plainly that bullying and threats are not things to carry quietly: tell an adult. Start with chapter 7, "Words Are Just Words".

## Is It Wrong to Be Angry?

**The short answer:** Not always. God Himself is angry at evil, and Jesus was angry more than once, so anger in itself isn't a sin. But most of our anger is about ourselves, not about injustice, and even right anger can go wrong fast, which is why the Bible says, "Be angry, yet do not sin."

Maybe someone told you that good Christians don't get angry, so you squash it down until it explodes. Or maybe you're the opposite: you've got a temper, and you've hurt people with it, and you wonder whether God is fed up with you. Either way, the Bible says more about anger than you might expect.

### Two kinds of anger

Paul quotes an old psalm: "Be angry, yet do not sin." Then he adds, "Do not let the sun set upon your anger, and do not give the devil a foothold" (Ephesians 4:26–27). Notice that it doesn't say, "Never be angry." It assumes you sometimes will be, and tells you what to do with it.

So there are two kinds. **Righteous anger** is anger at what is truly wrong: cruelty, injustice, someone weak being trampled. It's a reflection of God's own heart. God is angry at evil because He loves the people evil destroys. If you feel angry when you see a younger child being bullied or someone being cheated, that anger may be a good thing.

**Sinful anger** is mostly about me: I didn't get my way, I was embarrassed, someone got in my way, my game was interrupted. James says, "man's anger does not bring about the righteousness that God desires" (James 1:20). And even righteous anger can turn sinful when it becomes cruelty, revenge or a grudge you nurse for weeks.

Here's a simple test: is my anger about God's honour and other people's good, or about my pride and my comfort? Be honest. Most of the time, for most of us, it's the second.

### Jesus was angry

When Jesus found the temple, the place of prayer, turned into a noisy market where poor worshippers were being exploited, "He made a whip out of cords and drove all from the temple courts" and "overturned their tables" (John 2:15). He said, "How dare you turn My Father's house into a marketplace!" (John 2:16). Another time, when religious leaders cared more about rules than about a man with a withered hand, "Jesus looked around at them with anger and sorrow at their hardness of heart" (Mark 3:5).

Notice two things. First, Jesus' anger was always for God and for others, never for His own wounded pride. When people insulted Him personally, He didn't lash out. Second, His anger came with sorrow. It wasn't a hot rush of hate. It was love, hurt by what it saw. That's the shape of good anger.

### Handling anger well

Whether your anger is right or wrong, here's how to stop it doing damage.

- **Slow down.** "Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger" (James 1:19). Walk away, breathe, wait before you reply, especially online.
- **Name it to God.** "Be angry, yet do not sin; on your bed, search your heart and be still" (Psalm 4:4). Tell Him exactly what you feel and why.
- **Deal with it before bed.** Don't let it sit and grow. Talk it out, sort it out, or hand it over.
- **Use it for good.** If something really is unjust, let anger push you to act well: speak up, report it, help the person being hurt.
- **Say sorry when you blow it.** If your temper has hurt someone, apologise without excuses. That's what changed people do.

"A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (Proverbs 15:1). That isn't weakness. It's strength under control: "he who controls his temper is greater than one who captures a city" (Proverbs 16:32).

### When anger frightens you

Sometimes anger gets bigger than this. If your anger scares you, if you break things, hurt people, or feel you can't control it, that's a sign to get help, not to hide. Talk to a parent, a youth leader, a school counsellor or a doctor. Anger often has other feelings underneath, like hurt, fear or sadness, and people can help you untangle them.

And if someone else's anger at home or at school frightens you, tell a trusted adult. You should never have to live afraid. **If you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.**

**Think about it:** Think about the last time you were really angry. Was it mostly about something wrong in the world, or something that bruised your pride?

**Go deeper:** *Absolute Surrender (For Teens)* by Andrew Murray — Murray compares temper to the hands of a clock, which show what's happening inside, and says the cure isn't trying harder to be nice but asking the Holy Spirit to fill you with love. Start with chapter 6, "What Your Temper Tells You".

## What If I Can't Stop Comparing Myself to Others?

**The short answer:** Almost everyone compares, but it steals your joy and your peace, and the Bible calls its bitter end envy. Your worth isn't measured against anyone else's, because it comes from God, who made you and loves you in Christ. Jesus' answer to comparison is short: "What is that to you? You follow Me!"

You open your phone and within ten seconds you've seen someone prettier, someone fitter, someone with better grades, more friends, a bigger house and a more exciting weekend. You close it feeling smaller than when you opened it. It's not just online, either. There's always someone in your class who seems to be better at everything.

Comparison feels like it's just noticing. But it rarely stays there.

### Where comparison goes

Comparing usually lands in one of two places. Either you come out on top and feel proud, or you come out below and feel worthless, jealous or resentful. Neither is good for you. The Bible's word for the second one is envy, and it doesn't call it a small thing: "A tranquil heart is life to the body, but envy rots the bones" (Proverbs 14:30). Envy eats you from the inside. It doesn't hurt the person you envy at all.

It also wrecks friendships. It's hard to be truly glad for a friend when part of you wishes their good news were yours. The Bible tells us to "Rejoice with those who rejoice" (Romans 12:15), and envy makes that almost impossible.

### "What is that to you?"

There's a moment at the end of John's Gospel that speaks straight to this. After His resurrection, Jesus told Peter some hard things about his own future. Peter turned round, saw John, and asked, "Lord, what about him?" (John 21:21). It's the most natural question in the world: *What's he getting? Is his path easier than mine?*

Jesus answered, "If I want him to remain until I return, what is that to you? You follow Me!" (John 21:22).

That isn't Jesus being harsh. It's Him setting Peter free. Peter didn't need to know how his life measured up against John's. He needed to follow Jesus on the road given to him. The same is true for you. Your story is not a competition with anyone else's.

### Your worth isn't on a scale

Comparison assumes that your value goes up or down depending on how you rank. But in Christ your value has already been settled. You were made by God on purpose: "I praise You, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made" (Psalm 139:14). And if you trust Jesus, you are loved and accepted because of what He has done, not because you beat anyone.

Paul uses the picture of a body. A foot that says, "Because I am not a hand, I do not belong to the body," is wrong, because "God has arranged the members of the body, every one of them, according to His design" (1 Corinthians 12:15, 18). You aren't meant to be someone else. You're meant to be you, doing what God gave you to do. So Paul says, "Each one should test his own work. Then he will have reason to boast in himself alone, and not in someone else" (Galatians 6:4).

### Feeds and highlight reels

It's worth remembering that what you see online is a highlight reel. People post the best photo out of thirty, the holiday not the argument in the car, the grade not the hours of panic. You're comparing your behind-the-scenes with everyone else's trailer. It isn't a fair contest, and it was never meant to be.

Some practical steps help:

- **Notice the trigger.** Which accounts, apps or situations leave you feeling worse? Mute, unfollow or limit them. That's not weakness. It's wisdom.
- **Practise gratitude.** "Give thanks in every circumstance" (1 Thessalonians 5:18). Each night, write down three things you're thankful for. Gratitude and envy struggle to live in the same heart.
- **Celebrate someone on purpose.** When a friend does well, tell them you're glad, even if it costs you a little. Feelings often follow actions.
- **Learn contentment.** Paul said, "I have learned to be content regardless of my circumstances" (Philippians 4:11). *Learned.* It's a skill, and it grows with practice.

If comparing yourself is linked to how you feel about your body or food, or it's dragging you down into real sadness, talk to a parent or trusted adult. You don't have to fight it alone.

**Think about it:** Who do you compare yourself with most? What would it look like to hear Jesus say to you, "What is that to you? You follow Me"?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Dietrich Bonhoeffer* — Bonhoeffer noticed that the moment any group forms, the comparing begins, just as the disciples argued about which of them was the greatest; this chapter sets out his answer, a set of humble everyday ministries (holding your tongue, listening, helping, bearing with others) by which Christians serve one another instead of competing. Start with chapter 12, "Listening and Bearing".

## Is It Wrong to Be Ambitious?

**The short answer:** No. Wanting to do something great with your life can be a very good thing, and Paul himself was ambitious. What the Bible warns against is *selfish* ambition: wanting to be great so that people will look at you. The question isn't whether you aim high, but who you're aiming for.

Maybe you want to be a surgeon, a footballer, a songwriter, an engineer, a business owner or the first in your family to go to university. Maybe someone has made you feel that wanting to be the best is somehow un-Christian, as if humble people should aim low. That isn't what the Bible teaches. (Book 1 answered whether it's wrong to be rich. This chapter is about something different: the drive to achieve.)

### Two kinds of ambition

The Bible is sharp about one kind of ambition. "Do nothing out of selfish ambition or empty pride, but in humility consider others more important than yourselves" (Philippians 2:3). James warns, "For where jealousy and selfish ambition exist, there will be disorder and every evil practice" (James 3:16).

The very first big building project in the Bible was driven by this. The people of Babel said, "let us build for ourselves a city with a tower that reaches to the heavens, that we may make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:4). *For ourselves. A name for ourselves.* That's selfish ambition: greatness as a way of making yourself the centre.

But there's another kind. Paul wrote, "In this way I have aspired to preach the gospel where Christ was not known" (Romans 15:20). He wanted something huge: to take the message of Jesus to places that had never heard it. He travelled thousands of miles, planted churches across an empire, and wrote letters that are still read every day. That's not a man who aimed low. And he summed up his deepest ambition like this: "So we aspire to please Him" (2 Corinthians 5:9).

Interestingly, Paul also told ordinary Christians "to aspire to live quietly, to attend to your own matters, and to work with your own hands" (1 Thessalonians 4:11). Doing ordinary work faithfully is a worthy ambition too. Greatness in God's eyes doesn't always look like greatness on a stage.

### Jesus redefined greatness

James and John once came to Jesus with a bold request: "Grant that one of us may sit at Your right hand and the other at Your left in Your glory" (Mark 10:37). Jesus didn't tell them to stop wanting greatness. He told them what greatness really is: "whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant, and whoever wants to be first must be the slave of all. For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve" (Mark 10:43–45).

So Jesus doesn't cancel ambition. He turns it round. Aim to be great at serving. Aim to be first at giving. Use every gift you have for God's glory and other people's good.

### Testing your ambition

Here are some honest questions to ask about your dreams:

- **Who is it for?** Would I still want this if nobody ever praised me for it? "Whatever you do, work at it with your whole being, for the Lord and not for men" (Colossians 3:23).
- **Who does it help?** Does this goal serve other people, or only me?
- **What will it cost?** Am I willing to cheat, trample friends, or neglect God and family to get there? If so, the ambition has become an idol.
- **Can I hold it loosely?** Could I trust God if this door closed? Ambition is good as a servant and terrible as a master.

### Aim high, stay humble

Hard work and skill are good gifts. "Do you see a man skilled in his work? He will be stationed in the presence of kings" (Proverbs 22:29). Practise. Study. Train. Paul even used athletes as a picture of the Christian life: "Run in such a way as to take the prize" (1 Corinthians 9:24).

But keep asking whose name you're trying to make great. Humility isn't thinking you're rubbish. It's forgetting about yourself because you're caught up in God and other people. The most ambitious thing you could ever want is the one Paul wanted: to hear, at the end, that you pleased the One who made you. "So whether you eat or drink or whatever you do, do it all to the glory of God" (1 Corinthians 10:31).

**Think about it:** What's one big dream you have? How could it become something you do for God and for others, not just for yourself?

**Go deeper:** *C. T. Studd (For Teens)* — Charlie Studd was one of the best cricketers in England and played in the famous 1882 Test that started the Ashes; the chapter tells that story and why he later said cricket had taken the place of Christ, while insisting games themselves were never sinful. Start with chapter 2, "Mr Studd at the Oval".

## What If a Friend Talks About Ending Their Life?

**The short answer:** Take it seriously, and tell a trusted adult straight away, even if your friend begs you to keep it secret. If they are in immediate danger, contact emergency services where you live, and stay with them if it's safe. It's not your job to fix them on your own, and telling isn't betrayal. It's love.

If you're reading this because a friend has said something that scared you, start here.

**If a friend talks about ending their life, never keep it secret, even if they ask you to. Tell a trusted adult straight away: a parent, a teacher, a school counsellor, a youth leader. If there is immediate danger, contact emergency services where you live. If it's safe, stay with them until help comes.**

Read that again if you need to. Everything else in this chapter comes after it.

### Take it seriously

Sometimes it's clear: "I want to die." Sometimes it's quieter: "Everyone would be better off without me." "I won't be around much longer." "What's the point?" A friend giving away their things, saying goodbye in strange ways, or suddenly seeming calm after a long time of despair can also be warning signs. You don't need to be sure. If you're worried, act as if it's real. It's far better to be wrong and safe than right and too late.

### It is not a betrayal to tell

The hardest part is often the promise. "Don't tell anyone. Promise me." You may feel that telling an adult would destroy their trust. But a promise to keep someone's danger secret is a promise you must break. Imagine a friend trapped in a burning building asking you not to call the fire brigade. Love calls anyway.

Your friend may be angry with you for a while. Many people who were helped later say they were glad someone told. "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity" (Proverbs 17:17). This is one of those times.

### What to say, and what not to say

You don't need perfect words. Being there matters more than being clever.

- **Do listen.** Let them talk. "Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak" (James 1:19).
- **Do say you care.** "I'm really glad you told me." "I don't want to lose you." "You're not a burden to me."
- **Do ask directly.** It's all right to ask, "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" Asking doesn't put the idea in their head. It shows you can handle the truth.
- **Do help them get help.** Offer to go with them to an adult, or to sit with them while they make the call.
- **Don't argue or lecture.** Telling them they're being selfish, or that they'd go to hell, or that other people have it worse, will only push them further away.
- **Don't promise secrecy.** Say, kindly, "I care about you too much to keep this to myself."
- **Don't leave them alone** if you think they're in danger right now.

### Not your job alone

You are not your friend's counsellor, doctor or rescuer. You're their friend. Your part is to care and to get them to people who can help. If, despite everything, your friend is hurt, that is not your fault. You are not responsible for someone else's choices.

The Bible says, "Carry one another's burdens" (Galatians 6:2), but notice it says *one another*. Nobody is meant to carry a burden this heavy by themselves. "Two are better than one... For if one falls down, his companion can lift him up" (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10). You lift your friend by bringing more people in.

### Looking after yourself

Being close to someone who is struggling like this is exhausting and frightening. Talk to an adult about how *you* are feeling as well. Keep sleeping, eating and doing normal things. Pray for your friend, and let other people pray for you. **If you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.** That line is for you too.

### There is hope

When the prophet Elijah was so exhausted that he "prayed that he might die," God didn't scold him. An angel touched him and said, "Get up and eat" (1 Kings 19:4–5). God cared for his body, gave him rest and company, and gave him a future. People who feel hopeless today can, and very often do, get better with the right help. Your friend's story isn't over, and by speaking up, you may be part of how it continues.

**Think about it:** Who are two adults you could go to straight away if a friend ever told you they wanted to end their life?

**Go deeper:** *The Key Teachings of Richard Baxter* — a seventeenth-century pastor who treated depression as a real illness needing medical help, Baxter tells those caring for a despairing person not to argue with every dark thought or add guilt, but to tend to the body, keep them in company, and sit with them while they cannot pray. Start with chapter 14, "Counsel for the Melancholy".

## Is It Ever Right to Lie?

**The short answer:** Ordinary lying is wrong. On that every Christian agrees, because God is truth and He calls His people to be truthful. But there are rare, extreme cases, like hiding refugees from people who want to kill them, where faithful Christians genuinely disagree about whether deceiving is right. This chapter sets out the views without picking one.

This is one of the questions where faithful Christians reach different conclusions. Before we get to the hard cases, though, let's be clear on the easy ones, because that's where most of our lies happen.

### Where Christians agree

God is completely truthful. "God is not a man, that He should lie" (Numbers 23:19). Paul calls Him the God "who cannot lie" (Titus 1:2). Jesus called Himself the truth, and called the devil "a liar and the father of lies" (John 8:44). So truthfulness isn't just a rule. It's part of being like God.

That's why the ninth commandment says, "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16), and why Paul writes, "Therefore each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to his neighbor, for we are all members of one another" (Ephesians 4:25). "Lying lips are detestable to the LORD, but those who deal faithfully are His delight" (Proverbs 12:22).

Every Christian tradition agrees that lies to get out of trouble, to look good, to hurt someone, or to cover up what you've done are wrong. That includes the "small" ones: the excuse to a teacher, the story to your parents about where you were, the exaggeration that makes you look better online.

### The hard cases

But the Bible itself contains some puzzling stories. When Pharaoh ordered the Hebrew midwives to kill every baby boy, they "feared God and did not do as the king of Egypt had instructed; they let the boys live" (Exodus 1:17). When Pharaoh asked why, they said the Hebrew women "give birth before a midwife arrives" (Exodus 1:19), which sounds like a cover story. Yet "God was good to the midwives" (Exodus 1:20).

When soldiers came looking for the Israelite spies, Rahab hid them on her roof and said, "the men went out, and I do not know which way they went" (Joshua 2:5). The New Testament praises her: "By faith the prostitute Rahab, because she welcomed the spies in peace, did not perish" (Hebrews 11:31).

Then there's a modern version. During the Second World War, Christians across Europe hid Jewish people from the Nazis. When soldiers knocked and asked, "Are there any Jews here?", what should they have said?

### Where Christians differ

**Never lie.** Some Christians, including Augustine long ago and many since, hold that lying is always wrong, even to save a life. They say God praised the midwives and Rahab for their faith and courage in protecting life, not for their words. They believe you should find another way: stay silent, refuse to answer, or tell the truth and trust God with the result. Corrie ten Boom's sister Nollie took this view during the war.

**Deception can be right to protect the innocent.** Other Christians believe that someone seeking to murder has no right to the truth, so deceiving them isn't "false witness against your neighbor" in the sense the commandment means. They point to the midwives and Rahab as examples to follow, and to Peter's words, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Many who hid Jews in the war, Corrie among them, acted this way.

**A tragic choice.** A third view says that in a broken world you can face a clash where every option involves some wrong. Lying is still not good, but saving a life is the greater duty, and the person humbly asks God's forgiveness for the lesser wrong.

These are serious believers who love truth and love life. This book won't decide between them.

### What this means for you

Notice what all three views share: lying is never casual, never convenient, never for your own benefit. The hard cases are about protecting someone innocent from deadly harm, not about protecting yourself from a detention or an awkward conversation. If you ever find yourself thinking, "Rahab lied, so I can lie to my mum," you've missed the point by a mile.

Most of us will never face a soldier at the door. We face a hundred small chances every week to be honest or not. That's where truthfulness is learned. "Do not lie to one another, since you have taken off the old self with its practices" (Colossians 3:9).

**Think about it:** What kind of lie are you most tempted to tell? What would it cost you to be honest instead?

**Go deeper:** *Portraits of Courage – Corrie ten Boom* — this chapter tells how the ten Boom family supplied ration cards and false papers to Jews in hiding, and how Corrie and her sister Nollie disagreed about whether lying was right; it sets out the midwives, Rahab, Augustine and Bonhoeffer fairly and doesn't claim to settle the question. Start with chapter 10, "Ration Cards and Lies".

## Is Gambling Wrong?

**The short answer:** The Bible never mentions gambling directly, so Christians differ on small, occasional stakes like a raffle. But Scripture speaks clearly about greed, the love of money and exploiting the poor, and modern gambling is built on all three. For teenagers it's also illegal in most places, and betting apps and loot boxes are designed to hook you.

If you've watched sport recently, you've probably seen more adverts for betting than for almost anything else. Maybe friends at school put money on matches, or spend real cash on loot boxes and card packs in games, hoping for a rare item. Is any of that a problem?

This is a question where Christians don't all agree on every detail, so let's start with what the Bible says clearly.

### What Scripture makes clear

**Greed is serious.** Jesus said, "Watch out! Guard yourselves against every form of greed, for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15). Gambling usually runs on the hope of getting something for nothing, of getting rich without work.

**Wanting to be rich is a trap.** Paul writes, "Those who want to be rich, however, fall into temptation and become ensnared by many foolish and harmful desires that plunge them into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is the root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:9–10). Proverbs warns, "one eager to be rich will not go unpunished" (Proverbs 28:20), and contrasts quick money with steady work: "what is earned through hard work will be multiplied" (Proverbs 13:11).

**Money is a trust.** Everything you have is God's, lent to you to use well, for your needs, for others and for His kingdom. Losing it on purpose for a thrill is hard to square with that.

**The poor are exploited.** Gambling companies make their profits from people who lose, and those who lose most are often those who can least afford it. The Bible warns against "Oppressing the poor to enrich oneself" (Proverbs 22:16). Even a "harmless" bet supports a system that ruins many families.

**Nothing should master you.** Paul said, "I will not be mastered by anything" (1 Corinthians 6:12). Gambling can become an addiction as powerful as drugs. People lie, steal and lose everything to chase the next win.

### Designed to hook you

Many gambling-style products are aimed straight at people your age. Loot boxes, spin-the-wheel rewards and card packs use the same tricks as slot machines: flashing lights, near-misses, random prizes, and the feeling that the next one could be the big one. Some sites let players bet game skins or items that can be turned into cash. Betting apps make it easy to place bets in seconds, and send offers designed to keep you playing.

In most countries, gambling for money under 18 is illegal. That alone settles it for now, because the Bible tells us to respect the laws of those in authority (Romans 13:1).

### Where Christians differ

**Some Christians** believe all gambling is wrong, even small stakes, because it feeds greed, wastes what God has entrusted, and props up an industry that harms the poor. Many churches have held this view for generations.

**Other Christians** believe that a small, occasional stake, like a raffle ticket for a charity or a friendly bet of a few coins, treated as entertainment money you'd happily spend on a film, isn't sinful in itself, though they still urge great caution. They note that the Bible uses lots to make decisions, "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Proverbs 16:33), though casting lots was about seeking God's will, not about winning money.

Both sides agree that greed, addiction and exploiting others are wrong, and that a Christian should never gamble with money they need, hope to get rich from it, or let it take hold of them.

### What to do

If you've been spending money on loot boxes or betting and find it hard to stop, you're not alone, and it's not too late. Tell a parent or trusted adult. Delete the apps, turn on spending limits, and ask someone to help you stay accountable. Gambling problems are real and treatable, and there is no shame in asking for help.

And remember where real security comes from. "Keep your lives free from the love of money and be content with what you have" (Hebrews 13:5), because the God who has promised never to leave you is worth more than any jackpot.

**Think about it:** Are there games or apps you use that work like gambling? How much have they cost you, in money and in time?

**Go deeper:** *Sermons on Several Occasions* by John Wesley — in "The Danger of Riches", Wesley preaches on 1 Timothy 6:9 and insists that the warning is not only about getting rich dishonestly but about the very desire to be rich, which he calls a snare even when the means are innocent. Start with chapter 88, "The Danger of Riches".

## What If I Feel Like a Fake Christian?

**The short answer:** If you're worried about being a fake, that worry is usually a sign you're not one. Real Christians still struggle, fail and feel cold sometimes; a hypocrite is someone who pretends and doesn't care, not someone who fights and falls. Your standing with God rests on Jesus, not on how well you feel or perform today.

You sing the songs on Sunday, then lose your temper on Monday. You pray at youth group, then do the thing you promised God you'd stop. You hear other Christians talk about how close they feel to God, and you feel nothing. And a voice whispers, *You're a fake. Everyone else is real. You're just pretending.*

(Book 2 answered why other Christians can be hypocrites. This chapter is about the fear that *you* are one.)

### Struggling isn't faking

The apostle Paul was one of the most devoted Christians in history. Yet he wrote, "I do not understand what I do. For what I want to do, I do not do. But what I hate, I do" (Romans 7:15). He even cried out, "What a wretched man I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death?" (Romans 7:24).

Was Paul a fake? No. A fake doesn't care about the gap between what he says and what he does. Paul cared so much that it hurt. And his answer wasn't to try harder or give up. It was to look at Jesus: "Thanks be to God, through Jesus Christ our Lord!" (Romans 7:25). Straight after that comes one of the best sentences in the Bible: "Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1).

Jesus was hard on hypocrites, people who were "on the outside... righteous, but on the inside... full of hypocrisy" (Matthew 23:28) and who didn't want to change. But He was gentle with struggling people. He never turned away anyone who came to Him honestly: "the one who comes to Me I will never drive away" (John 6:37).

### Feelings aren't the measure

Many people feel fake because they don't *feel* Christian enough. But faith isn't a feeling. It's trust in a Person. Some days you'll feel God's closeness; other days you'll feel nothing. Neither changes what Jesus has done. God "knows our frame; He is mindful that we are dust" (Psalm 103:14).

John wrote to Christians who had anxious hearts: "Even if our hearts condemn us, God is greater than our hearts, and He knows all things" (1 John 3:20). Your conscience can be a liar sometimes. God knows the truth about you better than your worst moods do.

### Assurance, not perfection

How can you know you belong to God? Not by being flawless, but by looking at where your trust rests. Are you relying on Jesus and what He did on the cross, rather than on how good you've been? Do you want to follow Him, even when you fail? Do you come back after you fall? Those are signs of real faith, however weak.

Paul does tell Christians, "Examine yourselves to see whether you are in the faith" (2 Corinthians 13:5). That's a healthy question to ask once in a while. But examining yourself isn't the same as endlessly picking at yourself. Look at your heart honestly, then look at Christ for longer.

### If there is pretending

Sometimes the feeling is pointing to something real: maybe you've been putting on a show for parents or church friends, while your real life is going another way. If that's true, the answer still isn't despair. It's honesty. Stop pretending, tell God the truth, and tell a Christian you trust. "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9). A hypocrite who comes clean is no longer a hypocrite.

And here's a promise to hold on to: "He who began a good work in you will carry it on to completion until the day of Christ Jesus" (Philippians 1:6). The work isn't finished yet. That's why you still struggle. But God doesn't abandon half-built people.

If feeling like a fraud is bound up with heavy sadness or anxiety that won't lift, tell a parent or trusted adult. That kind of weight needs company.

**Think about it:** What are you trusting in for your standing with God: how you feel and how well you're doing, or what Jesus has done?

**Go deeper:** *Grace Abounding (For Teens)* by John Bunyan — after years of fearing he was lost, Bunyan suddenly saw that "my righteousness was Jesus Christ Himself", not his good or bad moods; this chapter tells how his chains fell off and how he learned to face the Bible verses that had frightened him. Start with chapter 11, "Thy Righteousness Is in Heaven".

## What If I Want to Give Up on Faith?

**The short answer:** You're not the first to feel this, and it's better to say it honestly than to pretend. Before you walk away, ask why you want to, talk to someone you trust, and ask Peter's question: "Lord, to whom would we go?" Whatever you decide today, God doesn't stop holding on to you, and the door home stays open.

Maybe it isn't one big question. Maybe faith has just slowly faded: prayer feels like talking to the ceiling, church feels boring, and your friends who don't believe seem to be doing fine. Maybe something happened that made you angry with God. Or maybe part of you is tired of the rules. Whatever it is, you're thinking about quitting.

(Book 1 answered whether it's wrong to doubt. This chapter is for the moment when you're thinking of walking away altogether.)

### Why do you want to leave?

It helps to be honest about what's really going on, because "giving up on faith" can mean very different things.

- **A question you can't answer.** That's worth investigating, not running from. Many of the big questions have good answers, and some of them are in this series.
- **Drift.** Nothing dramatic happened. You just stopped praying, reading and showing up, and faith grew cold. "We must pay closer attention, therefore, to what we have heard, so that we do not drift away" (Hebrews 2:1). Drift is real, but it isn't the same as finding Christianity false.
- **Hurt.** Someone at church let you down, or God didn't stop something terrible. That pain is real and deserves to be taken seriously, and taken to God.
- **Something you want.** Sometimes we want to leave because faith is getting in the way of something we'd rather do. Be honest with yourself if that's part of it.

### "To whom would we go?"

There was a day when lots of people walked away from Jesus. His teaching was hard, and "many of His disciples turned back and no longer walked with Him" (John 6:66). Jesus turned to His closest friends and asked, "Do you want to leave too?" (John 6:67).

Peter's answer is one of the most honest in the Bible: "Lord, to whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life" (John 6:68). He didn't say, "Everything makes sense." He said, in effect, "Where else is there? Who else has what You have?" Before you leave, ask the same question. If not Jesus, then what? What else gives forgiveness, hope beyond death, a reason why you matter?

### The God who waits

Jesus told a story about a son who took his share of his father's money and left home to do what he wanted. It went badly. When he was starving, "he came to his senses" and decided to go back (Luke 15:17). Then comes the best line: "But while he was still in the distance, his father saw him and was filled with compassion. He ran to his son, embraced him, and kissed him" (Luke 15:20).

Notice: the father was watching the road. He never stopped looking. That's what God is like. Even if you walk away, He doesn't stop loving you, and the way back is always open.

And for those who belong to Him, there's a deeper comfort. Jesus said of His sheep, "No one can snatch them out of My hand" (John 10:28). Paul wrote, "if we are faithless, He remains faithful, for He cannot deny Himself" (2 Timothy 2:13). Your grip on God may be weak right now. His grip on you is not.

### What to do now

- **Talk to someone.** A parent, youth leader or older Christian you trust. Tell them honestly where you are. You'll probably find they've been there too.
- **Tell God the truth.** Even "I don't know if You're there, and I'm thinking of giving up" is a prayer. He can handle it.
- **Don't decide in a dark moment.** Feelings change. Give it time, and look for answers before you make it final.
- **Stay near.** Keep going to church or youth group if you can, even if you just sit at the back.

If wanting to give up on faith comes with wanting to give up on everything, that's urgent. **If you ever think about hurting yourself or not wanting to be alive, tell a parent or trusted adult right away.**

**Think about it:** If you left Jesus, where would you go? What would you be hoping to find there?

**Go deeper:** *C. S. Lewis (For Teens)* — between the ages of twelve and fourteen, Lewis quietly stopped believing, partly because he had made prayer about producing the right feelings; this chapter tells how it happened, and hints at the friendship through which he would one day tell the story of his return. Start with chapter 3, "A Faith Let Go".

## Conclusion: To Whom Would We Go?

There is a moment in John's Gospel when Jesus says something so hard that many of His followers walk away. He turns to the twelve who are left and asks, "Do you want to leave too?" And Peter answers with one of the most honest statements of faith in the Bible: "Lord, to whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life" (John 6:68).

Peter didn't understand everything Jesus had just said. He had questions, and probably some doubts. But he knew one thing: there was nobody else like Jesus, and nowhere else to find what He offered.

That might be exactly where you are after three books of questions. Some answers have helped. Some questions are still open. And you may have new ones that none of these books has touched. That's all right. The aim was never to give you an answer for everything. It was to show you that following Jesus is something you can do with your whole self, your mind and your questions included, and that He is worth following.

### The heart of it

Underneath every question in this book is the same good news. God made you, knows you and loves you. We have all turned away from Him, and that has broken things in us and around us. But God did not leave us there. Jesus, fully God and fully man, lived the life we could not live, died in our place and rose again. Everyone who turns from going their own way and trusts Him is forgiven, welcomed, and given a new start and a new family. Nothing can take that away: "neither death nor life, neither angels nor principalities, neither the present nor the future, nor any powers, neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 8:38–39).

If you have never trusted Jesus for yourself, you can do it today, honestly, in your own words. If you do, tell a Christian you trust.

### Keep asking, keep going

Keep bringing your questions to God. "Now if any of you lacks wisdom, he should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault, and it will be given to him" (James 1:5). Keep reading the Bible: if you'd like a map for that, try *The Big Story*. Find a church, and at least one adult you can ask anything. And look after each other. Several chapters in this book were about friends who are struggling. You may be the person God uses to help someone else.

If you're struggling yourself, please don't carry it alone. "Cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7). And tell someone today.

*Lord Jesus, thank You that I can bring You every question, every fear and every doubt. Thank You that You have the words of eternal life, and that nothing can separate me from Your love. When I can't feel You, hold on to me. When I'm tempted to give up, bring me back. Make me brave, honest and kind, and help me to follow You for real. Amen.*
