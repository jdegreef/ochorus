# They Were Young – Book 4: Sent

*Six who did something for God while they were young*

## Introduction: Too Young to Be Sent?

When God called Jeremiah to be a prophet, Jeremiah had the perfect excuse. "Ah, Lord GOD," he said, "I surely do not know how to speak, for I am only a child!" God did not agree. "Do not say, 'I am only a child.' For to everyone I send you, you must go, and all that I command you, you must speak. Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you" (Jeremiah 1:6–8).

Most of us have used Jeremiah's excuse at some point. I'm too young. I don't know enough. Someone older should do it. Somebody else will.

This book is about six people who heard God's call while they were still young and did something about it. None of them was famous when they started. Some were poor. One was a weaver's teenage daughter in a Welsh valley. Most of them were told, by somebody, that they were too young, too ordinary or too unrealistic. They went anyway. After His resurrection Jesus said to His friends, "As the Father has sent Me, so also I am sending you" (John 20:21). These six took Him at His word.

Here are the six people you will meet in *Sent*, the fourth book of *They Were Young*:

**David Brainerd**, an orphan who came to faith at twenty-one, was thrown out of college, and spent his short life travelling through the forests of colonial America to live among the Native American peoples there, while fighting a darkness in his own mind.

**William Carey**, a poor shoemaker's apprentice in an English village who came to faith as a teenager, taught himself languages at his workbench, and grew convinced that the good news of Jesus was for the whole world, India included.

**Mary Jones**, the daughter of a Welsh weaver who saved for years and walked a very long way to buy a Bible of her own, and whose story helped give the Bible to millions.

**Amy Carmichael**, a Belfast teenager who stopped in the rain to help an old woman, heard a verse that changed her life, and went on to rescue children in southern India.

**Eric Liddell**, a Scottish runner who would not race on a Sunday, won an Olympic gold medal anyway, and then left fame behind to teach in China.

**Jim Elliot**, an American college student who wrote in his journal about giving away what you cannot keep, and who went to Ecuador to tell an isolated people about Jesus.

### How each chapter works

As in the earlier books, each chapter begins with a moment, a decisive scene in that person's young life, and then pulls back to tell you how they got there and what came after. The rest of the life is told more briefly at the end.

After the story come three short sections. **In Their Own Words** gives you a few lines the person really said or wrote, with the source. **Think It Through** offers some questions to wrestle with, alone or with others. **Read More on Ochorus** points you to the person's own writing, or their biography, if you want to go further.

These stories are true, and we have tried hard not to add anything to them. We have not invented conversations or made up scenes. Where the records are silent, where historians disagree, or where a famous story has grown in the telling, we tell you.

### A word about being sent

It would be easy to read these stories and think that being sent means going somewhere far away and doing something dramatic. Some of these six did. But look closely and you will see that all of them started small, in a workshop, a village, a rainy street, a college room. They prayed, they learned, they said yes to the next thing in front of them. And every one of them paid a price. We have not hidden the cost, the mistakes, or the people they hurt along the way. Being sent is not a way to become a hero. It is a way of following Jesus. "Let no one despise your youth, but set an example for the believers in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity" (1 Timothy 4:12).

### For parents, teachers and youth leaders

These stories are written for readers aged about thirteen to seventeen. They include illness and early death, depression, a wartime internment camp and a violent death in Ecuador. All of this is handled honestly but without graphic detail, and wherever a chapter touches on despair it points the reader to a trusted adult.

We have tried to tell these lives without turning the people they went to into a backdrop for someone else's adventure. The Think It Through questions are meant to open conversation rather than settle it. You do not need to have read the first three books in the series to use this one. Each chapter stands on its own.

## David Brainerd: Light in a Dark Thick Grove

On a Sunday evening in July 1739, a young man was walking alone among the trees near his home in Connecticut, trying to pray and failing.

His name was David Brainerd. He was twenty-one. For months he had been in a kind of war with God, and he had lost. Two days earlier, on a Friday morning, walking in this same lonely place, he had finally seen that nothing he did could make him right with God: not his prayers, not his Bible reading, not his fasting, not his tears. He had felt, he wrote later, "brought quite to a stand." Since then he had felt strangely flat, as if "there was nothing in heaven or earth could make me happy."

For about half an hour he kept trying to pray, "very stupid and senseless," as he put it. And then, he wrote, "as I was walking in a dark thick grove, unspeakable glory seemed to open to the view and apprehension of my soul."

He was careful to say he saw no light with his eyes. It was, he said, "a new inward apprehension or view that I had of God, such as I never had before." "My soul rejoiced with joy unspeakable, to see such a God, such a glorious divine Being." For a while, he said, he hardly thought about himself at all. He stayed there, full of joy and peace, until it was nearly dark, and when he walked home, "I felt myself in a new world, and every thing about me appeared with a different aspect from what it was wont to do."

We know this because Brainerd kept a diary, which a famous pastor published after his death. It tells of a very short life, full of sickness, sadness and mistakes, given away to people most of the world had forgotten.

### Fatherless and Motherless

David Brainerd was born on 20 April 1718 in Haddam, a farming town on the Connecticut River in New England. Britain then ruled a string of colonies along the east coast of North America, on land that had been home to Native American nations for thousands of years.

He was the third son of nine children. His father, Hezekiah, a respected public man, died when David was nine. His mother, Dorothy, lived as a widow for about five years and died in March 1732, when David was thirteen. So, as the account of his life puts it, "in his youth he was left both fatherless and motherless."

He was not a carefree child even before that. Looking back, he described himself plainly: "I was from my youth somewhat sober, and inclined to melancholy." At seven or eight he became frightened of death, and found religion "a melancholy business that destroyed my eagerness for play." When a deadly sickness swept through Haddam in the winter of 1732, he prayed hard. Then his mother died, and he was, he said, "exceedingly distressed and melancholy."

In April 1733, a few days short of fifteen, he left his father's house and went to live in East Haddam, across the river, where he spent four years. He was not a wild teenager, but when he did join in with other young people, he said, "I never returned with so good a conscience as when I went."

### Trying Too Hard

At nineteen he moved to work a farm he owned in Durham, a few miles away. He wanted badly to go to college and become a minister. At twenty he began to study, and in April 1738 he went to live with the minister of Haddam, Phineas Fiske.

Now he became very, very religious. Mr. Fiske advised him to stay away from young company altogether and spend his time with "grave elderly people," and he did. He read the whole Bible more than twice in less than a year. He prayed every day for long stretches. "In short," he wrote, "I had a very good outside, and rested entirely on my duties, though I was not sensible of it."

You may know someone like that, or be someone like that: right on the outside, and quietly sure that all the effort must be earning something from God.

Then, one Sunday morning early in the winter of 1738, it all fell apart. He suddenly had "such a sense of my danger, and the wrath of God, that I stood amazed." For months he was in deep distress. He wrote that he "sometimes envied the birds and beasts their happiness," because they would never have to stand before God.

What followed was a long, exhausting struggle. He tried to make himself feel the right feelings and become humble enough. Each time he thought he had done enough, he told God so, and waited. When nothing happened, he grew angry. He admitted that at times he wished "there was no God, or to wish there was some other God that could control him."

What made him furious was that God's law demanded a good heart, which he could not give himself; that salvation came through faith, not effort ("I could not bear that all I had done should stand for mere nothing"); and that mercy was entirely up to God. He was even angry with a book he was reading, Solomon Stoddard's *Guide to Christ*, because it never told him something he could *do* to get to Christ.

Then, on that Friday in July, he stopped fighting. He saw that he could do nothing at all. He later said that his prayers had no more power to earn God's favour than "my paddling with my hand in the water."

### A New World

What he saw in the grove two days later was not, first of all, about himself. It was about God: his "excellency, loveliness, greatness." For the first time in his life, Brainerd was glad that God was God, and not just afraid of him.

And with that came something he had been unable to grasp for years. "At this time," he wrote, "the way of salvation opened to me with such infinite wisdom, suitableness, and excellency, that I wondered I should ever think of any other way of salvation." He wondered why the whole world did not see it and accept it: "this way of salvation, entirely by the righteousness of Christ."

That phrase is the heart of what Brainerd believed for the rest of his life. He could not be good enough for God. But Jesus Christ had lived the perfect life Brainerd could not live and died for the sins Brainerd could not undo. A person is made right with God by trusting Christ, not by piling up good deeds. As Paul wrote, "For it is by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves; it is the gift of God, not by works, so that no one can boast" (Ephesians 2:8–9).

For years he had treated faith as one more thing to achieve. That evening it was more like letting go.

### One Sentence at Yale

In September 1739, two months after the grove, Brainerd went to Yale College in New Haven to train for the ministry. He was nervous about it. He worried, he said, that he would not be able to live a life "of strict religion in the midst of so many temptations." He was right that it would be hard, though not in the way he expected.

In January 1740 measles spread through the college and he went home very ill. That summer he studied so hard that he began to cough up blood, and his tutor sent him home to rest. This was probably the first sign of the tuberculosis that would kill him.

Meanwhile the colonies were caught up in what historians call the Great Awakening, when huge crowds gathered to hear preachers like the English evangelist George Whitefield and Gilbert Tennent of New Jersey. In early 1741 it reached Yale, and Brainerd was, he wrote, "much quickened." But revivals can bring out pride as well as prayer. Some excited young converts began saying openly that this or that minister did not seem truly converted. The college authorities, led by the rector, Thomas Clap, were alarmed.

Brainerd's diary for those months has not survived; on his deathbed he asked for it to be destroyed. He knew he had been hot-headed. He later said he had since become more "refined from some imprudences and indecent heats."

The trouble came in one sentence. One day after a tutor named Chauncey Whittelsey had prayed with the students in the hall, Brainerd and a few close friends stayed behind. One of them asked what he thought of Mr. Whittelsey. Brainerd answered: "He has no more grace than this chair." He meant that, as far as he could tell, the man did not really know God.

But a first-year student outside the hall overheard, and told a woman in the town, who told the rector. The friends were made to say who had said it, and about whom. The college demanded a public confession. He refused, thinking it unfair to be shamed in public for something said in private. He had also gone, against the rector's orders, to a meeting of a "Separate" congregation that had split from the town's church. In the winter of 1742, in his third year, he was expelled.

Many people at the time thought the college had been far too severe. Jonathan Edwards, the pastor who later published his diary, politely declined to judge, but he plainly thought Brainerd had been harshly treated. But over time Brainerd came to see his own part clearly. In 1743 he offered the college a written apology, and it is a model of how to say sorry. "I humbly confess," it says, "that herein I have sinned against God, and acted contrary to the rules of his word, and have injured Mr. Whittelsey. I had no right to make thus free with his character." He did not add a "but."

Yale did not take him back. On 14 September 1743, the day his class graduated, he wrote: "This day I ought to have taken my degree; but God sees fit to deny it me." He had dreaded the day; instead, he said, God gave him calm.

In New England then, the normal road to the ministry ran through college. Brainerd, at twenty-three, had shut it with one careless remark. The closed door sent him somewhere he might never otherwise have gone.

### A Heap of Straw

In 1742 a group of ministers licensed him to preach anyway, and that autumn he was taken on by a Scottish missionary society. He was sent to Kaunaumeek, in the woods about twenty miles from both Stockbridge in Massachusetts and Albany in New York, to a small community of Mahican people.

He arrived on 1 April 1743. He was twenty-four. That first night he slept at the house of a poor Scottish settler, he wrote, "on a little heap of straw, in a log room without any floor." His interpreter was a young Mahican man named John Wauwaumpequunnaunt, who could read and write both languages. Brainerd rode twenty miles through the forest, again and again, to study the language with John Sergeant, the missionary at Stockbridge. He lived for a while in a wigwam, then built himself a small house. His bread came from ten or fifteen miles away, sometimes "mouldy and sour before I eat it."

And he was desperately low. A week after he arrived he wrote that he felt "exceedingly ignorant, weak, helpless, unworthy, and altogether unequal to my work." Then: "My soul was weary of my life; I longed for death, beyond measure." In other entries he called himself "the vilest, meanest creature upon earth."

We need to stop here and be honest. Jonathan Edwards, who knew Brainerd at the end of his life, wrote that he was "by his constitution and natural temper, so prone to melancholy and dejection of Spirit." Today we would probably say that Brainerd lived with depression, for much of his life. Loneliness, sickness, overwork and poor food made it far worse. He sometimes read his darkness as spiritual failure, and he was wrong about that. Depression is not a sign that God has given up on you, and it is not a lack of faith.

If you ever feel the way Brainerd felt, so low that you are weary of your life, please do not carry it alone the way he did. Tell a trusted adult: a parent, a teacher, a pastor, a doctor. And if you ever think about harming yourself, tell someone right away. Getting help is not weakness; it is one of the ways God looks after us.

What is remarkable is that Brainerd kept bringing his darkness to God, often on the same page, and that there were brighter days too. At Kaunaumeek he translated prayers and some psalms with his interpreter's help, learned to pray with the people in their own language, and set up a school, which John Wauwaumpequunnaunt taught. After a year he advised the Kaunaumeek families to move to Stockbridge, where they could have a settled minister and school, and they did.

### The People at the Forks

In June 1744 Brainerd was ordained, and moved to the Forks of the Delaware in Pennsylvania, where the Delaware River meets the Lehigh. The people there were Lenape, whom the English called the Delaware.

Something had just happened to them. In 1737 the colony's proprietors had used an old and doubtful deed to claim a huge stretch of Lenape land, by the distance a man could walk in a day and a half. They hired fast runners and cleared a path in advance. This Walking Purchase took the Forks from the people who lived there. Many Lenape had good reason to distrust white men who called themselves Christians.

Brainerd was a man of his time. His diary speaks of "poor Indians" and "pagans," and he saw no value in Lenape religion, which he called idolatry. When he heard the people were about to hold a great feast and dance, he went alone into the woods on 21 July 1744 and prayed so hard that, he wrote, "the sweat ran down my face and body." We can see what he could not: that he was a guest in someone else's homeland, and that much of what he called heathen was a whole way of life he never tried to understand. But he also cared about these particular people more than almost anyone in the colonies did, and gave up everything to be with them.

For a year at the Forks there was almost nothing to show for it, even after two hard journeys to the Susquehanna River, sleeping in the open woods. The one great exception was his own interpreter, a Lenape man usually named in the records as Moses Tatamy. In July 1745 he and his wife publicly professed their faith in Christ, the first Lenape converts of Brainerd's work.

### "I Never Saw Any Day Like It"

In June 1745, discouraged and ill, Brainerd rode about eighty miles south-east to Crossweeksung in New Jersey, where there were some scattered Lenape families. He found only a handful of people at home, "none but a few women and children." He preached to them. Then the women set off and travelled ten or fifteen miles to fetch their friends, so that others could hear too. Brainerd compared them to the woman of Samaria in John 4, who ran to tell her town about Jesus.

The numbers grew: seven or eight, then nearly thirty, then about forty. They asked him to preach twice a day. And on the afternoon of 8 August 1745, with about sixty-five men, women and children gathered, something happened that he struggled to describe. "The power of God seemed to descend upon the assembly 'like a mighty rushing wind,'" he wrote, borrowing words from the story of Pentecost in Acts 2, "and with an astonishing energy bore down all before it." People of every age were in tears, praying for mercy. He recorded what they were saying in their own language: "Guttummauhalummeh; Guttummauhalummeh," which he translated as "Have mercy upon me; mercy upon me." He wrote: "I never saw any day like it, in all respects."

What Brainerd preached at Crossweeksung was not mainly fear. He wrote that he spoke most of all of the love and compassion of Christ for sinners, and invited people to come to him freely. And the change was not just a feeling. People gave up heavy drinking, paid their debts and made peace with each other. Among them was a healer and conjurer Brainerd had all but given up on, who came slowly to faith.

The Crossweeksung believers are the real heroes here. They walked miles to hear, brought their friends, and went on following Christ long after Brainerd was gone. Old debts and settlers' claims threatened the little land they had, and money collected for the mission was used to clear the debts. In 1746 they moved together to land near Cranbury, New Jersey, to farm and to keep their community together.

### What Came After

Brainerd had little time left. In 1746 he made one more journey to the Susquehanna with six of the Christian Lenape. His tuberculosis grew worse. By spring 1747 he could no longer ride, and he left the people at Crossweeksung in the care of others.

At the end of May he came to Northampton, Massachusetts, to the home of the pastor and theologian Jonathan Edwards. There Edwards's daughter Jerusha, who was seventeen, nursed him through his last months. Some have said the two were engaged; there is no real evidence of that. A few days before he died he asked her, "Dear Jerusha, are you willing to part with me?" and told her he had committed everyone he loved to God. When someone asked how he was, he answered, "I am almost in eternity; I long to be there. My work is done." He died early on the morning of Friday, 9 October 1747. He was twenty-nine. Jerusha died the following February, aged seventeen, possibly of an illness caught while nursing him.

His younger brother John Brainerd took over the work among the Lenape believers in New Jersey and stayed with them for years. In 1749 Edwards published the diary, which became one of the most widely read Christian books in the English language. William Carey, whose story comes next, treasured it. Henry Martyn read it at Cambridge and went to India, and two centuries later Jim Elliot, whom you will also meet in this book, read it as a young man.

It would be wrong to tell Brainerd's story as if it were a pattern to copy in every way. He drove himself until his body broke, judged himself and sometimes others far too harshly, and spent long months alone when he needed friends. Edwards noticed that Brainerd himself slowly learned to tell which dark thoughts came from his "melancholy" rather than from God. But the centre of his life is worth copying: a young man who discovered that he could not save himself, found that Christ had done it for him, and then gave what time he had to telling other people so.

### Think It Through

1. Before the grove, Brainerd had "a very good outside" and was trying hard to earn God's favour. What is the difference between doing good things to earn God's love and doing them because you already have it?
2. One careless sentence got Brainerd expelled, and his apology did not undo the consequences. Was it still worth making? What makes an apology a real one?
3. Brainerd lived with deep sadness and mostly carried it alone. Why do you think people find it hard to tell anyone when they feel low? Who could you talk to?
4. Brainerd loved the Lenape people but also misunderstood much of their way of life. What can we learn from both sides of that when we go to serve people who are different from us?

## William Carey: The Apprentice Who Lost an Argument

The shop was small, and it smelled of leather, wax and tallow candles. Two young men sat at their benches, bent over shoes, with awls and thread and hammers in their hands. And they were arguing about God.

This was a village in Northamptonshire, in the middle of England, in the late 1770s. One of the apprentices was William Carey, about seventeen, quick, stubborn and proud of being right. He belonged to the Church of England, like his father, who was the parish clerk in his home village, and he looked down on the "Dissenters," the Christians who worshipped outside the national church in their plain little meeting houses. The other apprentice, John Warr, was a Dissenter, and a serious one. As they worked, he talked to William about sin, and about Jesus, and about what it meant to be truly changed by God.

William argued back. He was good at arguing. But years later, looking back, he admitted that Warr had something he did not have, and that the arguments he kept winning were not the point. John Warr lent him books. He kept talking. And slowly, in a shoemaker's shop, a boy who thought he had all the answers began to lose.

William Carey would become one of the most famous missionaries who ever lived. But his story starts here, at a cobbler's bench, with a fellow apprentice who would not stop being kind and would not stop telling the truth.

### A Weaver's Son

William Carey was born on 17 August 1761 in Paulerspury, a village in Northamptonshire. His father, Edmund, was a weaver who worked at a loom in their cottage, and later became the village schoolmaster and parish clerk. The family was poor, but there were books in the house, and William could read them.

He was a curious boy. He kept birds and insects in his room. He loved plants, and spent time with his uncle Peter, a gardener. Above all he loved stories of travel and faraway places, especially the voyage of Christopher Columbus, and other boys reportedly nicknamed him "Columbus." He also had a stubborn streak. A family story tells of him trying again and again to climb a tree to reach a bird's nest, falling, and going back up until he got it. Whether or not every detail is right, his sister remembered that whatever he started, he finished.

He wanted to be a gardener. But he had a painful skin condition that made it hard for him to work in the sun. So at fourteen his father apprenticed him to a shoemaker, Clarke Nichols, in the nearby village of Piddington. An apprentice in those days lived with his master, worked long hours for his keep, and learned the trade over several years. It was not a path that led anywhere grand. That is the point of the story.

### A Bad Shilling

The shoemaker's shop is where everything began to change, and not only because of John Warr.

William was, by his own later account, not a good boy, whatever he looked like on the outside. He went to church, and sometimes he swore and lied. One Christmas, when apprentices went round collecting small gifts of money from their master's customers, he was given a bad shilling, a fake coin worth nothing. He tried to get rid of it by swapping it for a good one from money that belonged to his master, and he told lies to cover it. He was found out. In a small village that was a real humiliation, and he was ashamed to show his face.

Carey later saw this as one of the ways God brought him low. He had thought of himself as a decent, church-going young man, better than the Dissenters he despised. Now he knew he was a thief and a liar, and he could not argue his way out of that. Meanwhile Warr kept talking with him about Christ, and William began to go, sometimes, to the prayer meeting at the Dissenters' chapel.

We should be honest about how much we do not know. Carey never gave a single date for his conversion. As far as we can tell from what he wrote later, it was not one dramatic night but a slow turning, over months, in which he stopped trusting in his church-going and his own goodness and began to trust in Jesus Christ alone. He came to believe that he was a sinner who could do nothing to save himself, that Christ had died in the place of sinners, and that anyone who trusted Christ would be forgiven and made new. By the time he was about seventeen or eighteen, he was a different person.

### Outside the Camp

One date we do have. On 10 February 1779, the government called for a national day of fasting and prayer, because Britain was at war with its American colonies. Carey went to the prayer meeting at the little Dissenting chapel in Hackleton. A visiting minister preached on a verse from Hebrews, which Carey heard in the words of the King James Bible: "Let us go forth therefore unto him without the camp, bearing his reproach" (Hebrews 13:13).

The verse is about Jesus, who was put to death outside the walls of Jerusalem, like an outcast. It calls Christians to follow him even when it means being looked down on. Carey took it personally. He decided he must leave the respectable church of his family and join the despised Dissenters he had once mocked. For a teenager whose father was the parish clerk, that was not a small step. It meant going "outside the camp" of everything he had grown up in.

Later that year his master, Clarke Nichols, died, and William went to work for another shoemaker, Thomas Old, in Hackleton. In June 1781, aged nineteen, he married Old's sister-in-law, Dorothy Plackett. She was some years older than him, from a Dissenting family, and, like most poor country women then, she could not read or write. Soon after, Thomas Old died, and William took over the business, along with the care of Old's widow and children. He was still only about twenty.

### The Cobbler's Bench

Here is the thing people remember most about the young William Carey: he taught himself languages while he mended shoes.

As a boy he had already worked his way through a Latin vocabulary book. In Clarke Nichols's shop, according to his own account, he found a commentary on the New Testament with some Greek words in it. He could not read them, so he copied them out carefully and took them to a weaver in his home village named Tom Jones, who had once had a good education and could tell him what they meant. That is how he started Greek. Later he learned Hebrew, and then Dutch and French, mostly by himself, often with a book propped open at his bench while his hands worked the leather.

He was not, he insisted, a genius. He was simply someone who did not stop. Years later he told his nephew that if anyone ever wrote his life and called him a plodder, that would be an accurate description. He meant it.

In October 1783, when he was twenty-two, he was baptised as a believer in the River Nene at Northampton, by a young Baptist minister named John Ryland. He began to preach in nearby villages, walking miles each Sunday, and in 1785 he moved to Moulton, where he was minister of a tiny Baptist church, ran a village school and still made shoes, because none of the three paid enough to live on.

### The Map on the Wall

It was during these years, in his early twenties, that something seized him and would not let go.

He read books about the world. He was especially gripped by the published journals of Captain James Cook, the English explorer who had sailed across the Pacific and died in Hawaii in 1779. Carey later said that reading Cook's last voyage first set him thinking about the peoples of the world who had never heard of Jesus. He began collecting every fact he could find: how many people lived in each country, what languages they spoke, what they believed.

There are stories, told by people who knew him and repeated ever since, that he made his own large map of the world and hung it on the wall by his bench, covered in his own notes about the population and religion of each land, and that for his school pupils he made a globe from scraps of leather. Some details may have grown in the telling. But the picture is true to him: a young shoemaker, glancing up from his work at a hand-made map of nations, and praying for them.

The question that kept him awake was simple. Jesus had told his followers, "Go and make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19). Did that command still apply? Many ministers of Carey's day thought it had been given to the first apostles only. If God wanted to save people in far countries, they said, he would do it himself, in his own time. There is a famous story that when Carey raised the question at a ministers' meeting in the mid-1780s, an older minister told him to sit down, because when God pleased to convert the heathen, he would do it without Carey's help. The story was told years later and the minister's son denied his father said it, so we cannot be sure of the words. But it captures what Carey was up against.

### Expect Great Things

Carey did not give up. In 1789 he became minister of a Baptist church in Leicester. And in May 1792, when he was thirty, he published a book with a very long title: *An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens*. Its opening sentence argued that since Christ told us to pray for his kingdom to come, "it becomes us not only to express our desires of that event by words, but to use every lawful method to spread the knowledge of his name."

That was his point. It was not enough to pray and wait. Christians should actually do something: raise money, send people, learn languages. The *Enquiry* answered the objections one by one: the distance, the danger, how a missionary would live, and the difficulty of strange languages. And in the middle he set out pages of tables listing every country he could find, with its size, its population and its religion. The cobbler had turned his wall map into an argument.

A few weeks later, at the end of May 1792, Carey preached to a meeting of Baptist ministers in Nottingham. His text was Isaiah 54:2–3, which he read in the King James wording: "Enlarge the place of thy tent, and let them stretch forth the curtains of thine habitations: spare not, lengthen thy cords, and strengthen thy stakes." As far as we can tell from those who heard it, his point was that God was telling his people to expect him to do far more than they imagined, and to make room for it.

The sermon itself has not survived. What has survived is its two-part message, which became the most famous thing Carey ever said. Today it is almost always quoted as "Expect great things from God; attempt great things for God." But the earliest written reports give it in a shorter form, simply "Expect great things; attempt great things." The words "from God" and "for God" seem to have been added later, as the saying was retold. Either way, the meaning is the same.

Even so, the ministers nearly went home without doing anything. According to a tradition, Carey pleaded with his friend Andrew Fuller not to let the moment pass. Whatever was said, it worked. On 2 October 1792, in a widow's house in Kettering, a dozen or so ministers formed a small society to send missionaries overseas, and pledged a little over thirteen pounds. It became the Baptist Missionary Society. A story handed down from those early days says that Carey compared the work to going down a deep mine: he would go down, if the others would hold the ropes. The exact words vary, but the arrangement was real. Fuller held the ropes in England for the rest of his life.

### Going, and the Cost

Carey offered to go himself, to India, with a doctor named John Thomas who had already worked there.

Not everyone was pleased. His father is said to have asked if he was mad. And his wife Dorothy said no. She had three young sons and was expecting a fourth child. She had never been far from her village. She did not want to take her children across the world on a voyage of five months, to a hot country full of illnesses, with no money and no promise of a return. It is easy to tell this story as if she simply lacked faith. That is not fair. She had good reasons, and she had not been the one who felt the call.

At first Carey planned to go without her, taking only their eldest son, Felix. That first attempt failed when the ship sailed without them, because they had no permission from the East India Company, which controlled British trade with India and did not want missionaries. Then, at the last minute, with her new baby only weeks old, Dorothy agreed to come, if her sister Kitty came too. On 13 June 1793 the whole family sailed from Dover on a Danish ship. William Carey was thirty-one. He never saw England again.

It would be dishonest to stop there, as if the story became an adventure. In India the family was poor and often ill. Their five-year-old son Peter died of dysentery in 1794. Dorothy, who had never wanted to come, grew seriously mentally ill, with delusions and terrible fears, and never recovered. For years Carey worked on with her suffering beside him, until she died in 1807. Some who have studied his life believe he neglected his family, and especially his sons, for the work. That is a fair question to ask, and there is no tidy answer to it.

### What Came After

Carey's first years in India were spent in Bengal, partly managing an indigo works to support his family while he learned Bengali with the help of an Indian teacher, Ram Basu. In 1800 he moved to Serampore, a small Danish settlement near Calcutta (now Kolkata), where the British could not expel missionaries. There he worked with Joshua Marshman, a schoolteacher, and William Ward, a printer.

For seven years there was not a single Indian convert. Then in December 1800 a Bengali carpenter named Krishna Pal was baptised in the river at Serampore, alongside Carey's son Felix. Krishna Pal became a preacher and wrote hymns in Bengali.

The plodder kept plodding. With Indian scholars working beside him, Carey and his colleagues translated the whole Bible into six languages, including Bengali and Sanskrit, and parts of it into dozens more. He became a professor at a college in Calcutta, wrote grammars and dictionaries, and founded Serampore College to train Indian students. He campaigned against the burning of widows on their husbands' funeral fires, which was finally banned in Bengal in 1829. In 1812 a fire destroyed the mission's printing works and years of manuscripts, and he began again.

He died at Serampore on 9 June 1834, aged seventy-two, after more than forty years in India. On his grave he asked for no titles, only two lines from a hymn by Isaac Watts: "A wretched, poor, and helpless worm, / On thy kind arms I fall." The boy who had argued with John Warr at the cobbler's bench had learned the lesson Warr taught him: the only one worth trusting is Christ.

## Mary Jones: The Long Road to Bala

In the spring of 1800, before it was light, a girl left a small stone cottage in a valley in North Wales and began to walk. She was fifteen. Her name was Mary Jones. In a purse or a bundle she carried every coin she had been able to earn and keep over six years, and she was going to spend all of it on one book.

The road ahead of her ran for about twenty-five miles over the hills of Merioneth to the little market town of Bala, where there lived a minister named Thomas Charles. She had never met him. She did not know for certain that he would have what she wanted. She only knew that if anyone in that part of Wales could sell a poor weaver's daughter a Bible in her own language, it was Mr Charles of Bala.

The story has always been told that she walked barefoot, carrying her shoes to save them for the town. That may well be true. Shoes were precious, and many country children in Wales went barefoot as a matter of course. But it is one of the details that has grown fonder with every retelling, and we cannot prove it. What we can say is that she went on foot, alone, a very long way, for something most people reading this book can pick up from a shelf without thinking.

Then she would have to walk home again.

This is a chapter about a girl who did one thing for God while she was young. It was not a sermon or a mission or a brave stand before a king. It was a long walk to buy a book. But it turned out to be one of the most famous walks in the history of the church, and it is worth knowing how much of the story is solid ground, how much is tradition, and why a teenage girl wanted a Bible badly enough to do it.

### A Weaver's Cottage Under Cadair Idris

Mary Jones was born on 16 December 1784 in the parish of Llanfihangel-y-Pennant, a scatter of farms and cottages in a green valley at the foot of Cadair Idris, one of the great mountains of Wales. Her parents, Jacob and Mary Jones, were weavers. They had married at the parish church the year before she was born, and when they did, neither of them could sign the register. They made their marks instead. Mary grew up in a home where nobody could read.

Her father died when she was very small, about four years old by most accounts. Her mother carried on weaving to keep them both, and Mary learned the work beside her. It was a poor life. A weaver's cottage was one room or two, with a loom taking up much of the space, a fire, a bed, and not much else.

To understand what came next, you need to know three things about Wales at the end of the eighteenth century.

The first is language. Almost everyone in Mary's valley spoke Welsh, and many spoke nothing else. English was the language of the law courts, the gentry and the government, but it was not the language of a weaver's kitchen or a shepherd's hillside. A Bible in English would have been almost no use to Mary. She needed one in Welsh.

The second is scarcity. Wales had had the whole Bible in Welsh since 1588, when Bishop William Morgan's translation was printed, and it was one of the great treasures of the nation. But printed copies were never plentiful, and they were expensive. A poor family might go its whole life without owning one. In many districts the only Bible was the big one chained or kept in the parish church, read aloud on Sundays.

The third is that Wales was in the middle of a spiritual awakening. For two generations, travelling preachers had been crossing the country, often preaching in the open air, calling people to repent and to trust in Christ. Those who responded were gathered into small societies that met in homes and simple chapels in the week to pray, sing and examine their lives together. They were called Methodists, and in Wales most of them were Calvinistic Methodists. Mary's parents were among them. When she was six, in 1791, a powerful revival broke out at Bala itself, and its effects were felt far beyond the town.

So Mary grew up hearing that God speaks in the Bible, that the Bible was the most important book in the world, and that every person needs to know it for themselves. And she could not read a word of it.

### Learning to Read

The man who changed that was Thomas Charles of Bala.

Charles was a clergyman who had thrown in his lot with the Welsh Methodists. He had seen that it was not enough to preach to people who could not read the Bible for themselves, so he set up what were called circulating schools. A teacher would come to a district and stay for some months, teaching children, and often adults too, to read Welsh from the Bible. Then the teacher moved on to the next district, and the next. Charles also threw his energy into Sunday schools, where old and young learned to read and studied Scripture together. Through these schools, thousands of ordinary Welsh people learned to read in a few years.

When one of these schools opened in the village of Abergynolwyn, a couple of miles from Mary's home, she went. The usual account says she was about ten. We do not know her teacher's name, or how long it took, but she learned. And as soon as she could read, she wanted to read the Bible.

There was a problem. Her family did not have one. According to the story as it has always been told, a farming family about two miles from her home owned a Bible and let her come and read it. So, week after week, she walked there and back to sit with it. Some versions say she learned whole chapters by heart so that she could carry them home in her head.

The story also says that Mary had already joined the Methodist society as a young child, professing faith at about eight. That would be very young by our standards, but in her world children were taken seriously in these matters. We cannot be sure of the age, and Mary herself left no account of how she came to faith. But what she did next shows what she believed. She believed that the Bible was the voice of God, and that it was worth more than anything she could earn.

### Six Years

At some point Mary decided she would not keep borrowing. She would have a Bible of her own.

It is worth pausing on how big a decision that was. For a girl in her position, a Bible was not a small purchase. It cost more money than she was likely to see in one place for years. She would have to earn it penny by penny, and keep every penny she earned instead of handing it over for food or clothes or fuel.

She did it the way poor children always have: with odd jobs, done over and over again. The traditional account says she kept hens and sold eggs, gathered sticks for firewood, helped neighbours with their work, mended and knitted and ran errands. We do not have her accounts, and the list may have been filled out by later tellers. But the main point is not in doubt. It took her about six years.

Think about what six years means at that age. If you started saving at nine, you would finish at fifteen. Every one of those years had its own temptations. A ribbon at the fair. A warmer shawl in winter. Sweets. It is not hard to imagine the voice in her head saying that it was too far away, that she would never get there, that God would understand if she spent a little. We do not know if she heard that voice. We know she did not stop.

There is something else hidden in this part of the story. Mary was not saving for an experience or an adventure. She was saving so that she could read, every day, for herself, the words she believed God had spoken. The writer of Psalm 119 said, "Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path," and "How sweet are Your words to my taste—sweeter than honey in my mouth!" (Psalm 119:105, 103). For most of us those are pleasant lines. For Mary they described something she had tasted at a neighbour's table and could not yet have at home.

### The Walk

By the spring of 1800 she had enough. There was only one place she could hope to buy a Welsh Bible, and that was from Thomas Charles in Bala.

A new edition of the Welsh Bible had just been printed. Ten thousand copies had come off the press at Oxford in 1799, paid for by an English church society, the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge, partly because men like Charles had begged for it. Charles was one of the people who distributed them in North Wales. But ten thousand copies did not go far in a nation hungry for Bibles, and they were being snapped up.

We do not know exactly which road Mary took. We do know the country she crossed: mountain tracks and valley roads, streams to ford, long climbs and longer descents, the great mass of Cadair Idris behind her and Bala, by its long lake, somewhere far ahead. Twenty-five miles is a long day's walk for a fit adult on a good road. It was not a good road, and she was a girl of fifteen walking alone. Most accounts say it took her the whole day and that she reached Bala as evening fell. The story says a local Methodist family took her in for the night, and that she went to Mr Charles in the morning.

Now we come to the heart of the story, and also to the part where the versions most disagree.

The best-known version goes like this. Mary found Thomas Charles and asked for a Bible. He told her, gently, that the copies he had were all sold or promised to other people. Mary, who had saved for six years and walked a day to get there, burst into tears. Charles was so moved by her tears and her story that he gave her one of the promised copies anyway, saying that the other person would have to wait.

Another version says she had to wait in Bala a day or two until a fresh supply arrived. And there is a third version, which comes from Mary herself. Many years later, as an old woman, she told the story to people who wrote it down, and a letter recording her account is kept today at the National Library of Wales. In her telling, she came home from Bala not with one book but with three.

How three? Some later writers said Charles let her have three for the price of one. Others suggest she was buying for neighbours too. We do not know. What we do know is that more than one Bible from that trip survives. One is in the National Library of Wales at Aberystwyth. The other, the one she kept as her own, is held today in the Bible Society's archive at Cambridge University Library. It is a copy of that 1799 Welsh edition. On the last page of the Apocrypha, the extra books printed between the Old and New Testaments in many Bibles of the time, Mary wrote a note in English, in her own spelling. It begins, "Mary Jones His the True Onour of this Bible", which is to say, "Mary Jones is the true owner of this Bible." And she noted that she bought it in 1800, in her sixteenth year.

That way of counting is why some books say she was sixteen and others fifteen. In the way people reckoned ages then, your sixteenth year was the year before your sixteenth birthday. Mary turned sixteen in December 1800, so for almost the whole of that year she was fifteen.

Then she walked home. Twenty-five miles back, over the same hills, with a Bible she had waited six years for. Nobody recorded what she thought on the way. You can probably guess.

### What the Walk Started

If the story ended there it would be a fine one: a poor girl, a long walk, a treasured book. But the reason the whole world has heard of Mary Jones is what happened next, and here we have to be especially careful about what is fact and what is tradition.

The facts are these. Thomas Charles was troubled by how many people in Wales still had no Bible, and how unreliable the supply was. On 7 December 1802 he was in London, at a meeting of the committee of the Religious Tract Society, a group that printed and spread Christian leaflets. He spoke about the need for Welsh Bibles and asked whether something could be done. One of the committee, a Baptist minister named Joseph Hughes, took the idea further than Charles had. If a society could be formed to provide Bibles for Wales, why not for the whole kingdom, and why not for the whole world? The Bible Society's own history remembers his question as: "If for Wales, why not for the kingdom? And if for the kingdom, why not for the world?" Older accounts word it a little differently, but they all agree on the shape of it.

Over the next year that question grew into a plan. On 7 March 1804, about three hundred people met at the London Tavern in the City of London and founded the British and Foreign Bible Society. Its aim was simple and enormous: to print the Bible, without notes or comment, in as many languages as possible, and sell or give it as cheaply as possible to anyone who needed it. Hughes became one of its secretaries. Within a couple of years it was printing Scripture in Welsh. One of its very first publications was the Gospel of John in Mohawk, for Native American readers in Canada.

The tradition is that Mary Jones was the spark. In this telling, Charles never forgot the weeping girl who had walked to Bala, and when he stood up in London it was her story he told.

Is that true? Honestly, we do not know. Charles did write about the hunger for Bibles in Wales, and about young people walking very long distances in the bare hope of getting one. Mary may well have been one of those he had in mind. But no record from 1802 or 1804 mentions her by name. Her story first appeared in print in 1878, in a family magazine, when she had been dead for fourteen years. A Welsh book about her followed in 1879, and then in 1882 an English writer, Mary Emily Ropes, published *The Story of Mary Jones and Her Bible*. That little book was read by children all over the English-speaking world for a century. It is a warm, vivid retelling, but it has whole conversations that its author could not possibly have known, and some of the details people remember best come from it.

So here is a fair way to put it. Mary Jones really lived, really saved, really walked to Bala, and really bought her Bible from Thomas Charles in 1800. The Bible Society really grew out of Charles's concern for Welsh Bibles. That Mary's walk was the very thing that moved him is a tradition, and a likely one, but a tradition. Her story became, in time, the story the Bible Society told about why it existed. That is not nothing. Stories like hers are how people come to care about a need they have never seen.

### What Came After

Mary did not become famous in her lifetime. She went on being a weaver.

In 1813 she married another weaver, Thomas Lewis, and some years later they moved to the village of Bryn-crug, near Tywyn on the coast of Cardigan Bay. Life was hard. By most accounts she had six children, and only one son, John, lived to grow up; several of the others died of tuberculosis, which killed many poor families in damp cottages in those years. John later emigrated to America. Her husband died before her, and she lived on as a widow for many years.

She kept her faith, and she kept her Bible. People who knew her in old age remembered her as a woman who loved the Scriptures and who gave what little she had to the work of spreading them. It is said that she kept bees and gave the money from her honey to the Bible Society and to missions. That detail is part of the tradition too, and we cannot confirm it, but it fits the woman everything else describes.

She died in December 1864, just after her eightieth birthday, and was buried in the graveyard of the Calvinistic Methodist chapel at Bryn-crug. Her gravestone describes her as the widow of Thomas Lewis, weaver. The ruins of the cottage where she grew up still stand in the valley under Cadair Idris, with a memorial to her beside them.

The society that her story is linked to has gone on to help translate, print and distribute the Bible in hundreds of languages, and many national Bible societies around the world trace their beginnings back to that meeting in 1804.

It is easy to make a story like this into a sugary lesson about being good. Mary's life was not sugary. She grew up poor, lost her father as a small child, buried most of her own children and her husband, and spent her life at a loom. Her one great deed, as far as the world could see, was done at fifteen and took two days. But she understood something at fifteen that many people never understand. Jesus said, "Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4). She believed it enough to go without for six years.

And she believed something else. The Bible she wanted so much is, from beginning to end, about Jesus. Jesus Himself said of the Scriptures, "These are the very words that testify about Me" (John 5:39). The people who taught Mary to read taught her that she was a sinner who could not save herself, that Christ had died for sinners and risen again, and that anyone who trusts Him is forgiven and made new. She did not want a Bible as an ornament for the shelf. She wanted to read for herself about the Saviour she had trusted, every day, in her own language.

## Amy Carmichael: What Will Last

It was a grey, wet Sunday morning in Belfast, in about 1885, and the Carmichael children were walking home from church.

Amy, the eldest, was about seventeen. With her were two of her younger brothers. As they went along the street they caught up with an old woman who was struggling with a heavy bundle. She was poor, and it showed: ragged clothes, the kind of person the respectable churchgoers of Belfast, in their good Sunday coats and hats, would have stepped around. The Carmichael children did not step around her. The boys took the bundle, Amy took the old woman's arm, and they helped her along.

And then, as Amy told it years later, she felt the eyes on her. The street was full of people coming out of church, people the Carmichaels knew. Here were three well-brought-up young people from a good family, seen in public helping a woman like that. Amy felt her face go hot. She was embarrassed, and ashamed of being embarrassed, and wishing they were anywhere else.

They passed an ornate public drinking fountain. And as they passed it, Amy said, words came to her with such force that she turned round to see who had spoken. There was nobody there. Only the fountain, the muddy street and the people with their surprised faces. The words were from the Bible, from Paul's first letter to the Corinthians, and she heard them in the King James Version she had grown up with:

"Now if any man build upon this foundation gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, stubble; every man's work shall be made manifest: for the day shall declare it, because it shall be revealed by fire; and the fire shall try every man's work of what sort it is. If any man's work abide..." (1 Corinthians 3:12–14, KJV).

She did not say anything to her brothers. They took the old woman where she was going and went home. But Amy said afterwards that she knew something had happened that changed the value of everything. That same afternoon, according to her own account, she shut herself in her room and settled with God, once and for all, what she was going to live for.

She was seventeen. The question she settled was not whether she was a Christian. That had happened already. It was whether she would care more about what people thought of her or about what would still be standing when God tested every life with fire.

### A Brown-Eyed Girl from Millisle

Amy Beatrice Carmichael was born on 16 December 1867 in Millisle, a village on the coast of County Down in the north of Ireland, the eldest of seven children. Her father, David, and his brother ran flour mills. The family were Presbyterians, devout and strict in the way of that time and place: family prayers, Sunday worship, the Bible and the catechism.

The most famous story of her childhood is a small one. When she was little she wanted blue eyes instead of brown, and she prayed for them, believing God could do it. In the morning she ran to the mirror and found her eyes still brown. She remembered being disappointed, and learning that "No" is an answer too. She told the story herself later in life, after she had discovered, in India, that brown eyes were a gift. It is the kind of story that people love to tidy up, but the bones of it come from her.

She was not a meek child. She was quick, bold, funny and wilful, the ringleader in her brothers' escapades, and more than once in trouble. When she was about twelve she was sent to a Methodist boarding school in Harrogate, in the north of England. It was there, at about fifteen, that her faith became her own. As the story is usually told, during a special mission for children at the school she understood for the first time that Jesus loved her personally and had died for her, and she trusted Him. She did not describe it as a dramatic experience. It was more like a door opening that she walked quietly through.

Then the family's world changed. The milling business ran into trouble, and the Carmichaels moved to Belfast, and in 1885 Amy's father died. Amy was seventeen. Her mother was left with seven children and much less money than before. As the eldest, Amy took on a great deal: helping her mother, keeping an eye on the younger ones, and trying to work out what her own life was for.

So the morning by the fountain did not happen to a carefree girl. It happened to a teenager who had just lost her father, whose family had come down in the world, and who was perhaps more aware than ever of what respectable people thought.

### The Foundation and the Gold

It is worth looking at the words Amy heard more closely, because they shaped the rest of her life.

Paul was writing to a church that was proud of its clever teachers and quarrelling over which one to follow. He reminded them that there is only one foundation for a church or a life. In a modern translation the verse just before Amy's words reads: "For no one can lay a foundation other than the one already laid, which is Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 3:11). The question is not whether you are building on Christ. A Christian is someone whose whole hope rests on Him and on what He did on the cross. The question is what you build on that foundation with the life He has given you.

Some building, Paul says, is like gold, silver and precious stones: it comes through fire unharmed. Some is like wood, hay and straw: it looks substantial until it burns. One day, "the Day", he says, every person's work will be tested. Paul is careful. He is not saying that anyone earns salvation by their work. In the next verse he says that even a person whose work burns up "will be saved, but only as if through the flames" (1 Corinthians 3:15). Salvation is a gift, built on Christ. But what we do with our lives matters, and some of it will last forever and some of it will not.

For Amy, that morning, the wood and hay and straw had a name. It was caring what the respectable people of Belfast thought of her. The gold had a name too. It was a poor old woman with a heavy bundle, whom Jesus loved.

### The Shawlies

What Amy did next was not glamorous, and it was not in India. It was in Belfast.

Belfast in the 1880s was a booming industrial city, and much of its wealth came from linen. Thousands of girls and young women worked long hours in its mills, in heat and noise and damp, for little pay. Many were about Amy's own age. They were too poor to afford hats, which every respectable woman wore to church, so they wore shawls over their heads instead, and people called them "shawlies". Most churchgoing Belfast did not expect to see them in its pews. The shawlies did not expect to be welcome.

Amy decided they would be. She started a Sunday class for mill girls in the hall of Rosemary Street Presbyterian Church. She taught them the Bible, prayed with them and got to know them. The respectable members of the congregation were not all delighted to have shawlies in their church hall. Amy did not much care. The fountain had settled that.

The class grew. Soon there were hundreds of girls coming, far too many for the hall. Amy saw an advertisement for a hall made of corrugated iron that could be put up for about five hundred pounds and would hold about five hundred people. She had no money. She prayed. A woman named Kate Mitchell gave five hundred pounds, and a mill owner gave a plot of land. The building went up on the corner of Cambrai Street, and it was called the Welcome Hall, because that was what Amy wanted the shawlies to be. Most accounts date the hall to 1887, when Amy was nineteen; some give the opening as January 1889. Either way, a teenager with no money had started a work that needed a building of its own. A church called the Welcome still meets in that part of Belfast today.

At the same time, Amy was growing in her own understanding of God. In September 1886, at eighteen, she went to a meeting in Glasgow that followed the pattern of the Keswick Convention, a yearly gathering in the English Lake District where Christians met to pray and to hear teaching about living a holy life by depending on the Holy Spirit rather than on their own effort. She came away sure that the God who had saved her was also able to keep her and to make her holy, and that she could trust Him to do it. The next year she went to Keswick itself, and there she heard Hudson Taylor, the founder of the China Inland Mission, speak about the millions of people in China who had never heard of Jesus.

### Choosing to Go

The call to go abroad did not come all at once.

In 1889 the family moved again, to Manchester in England, and Amy threw herself into work among the mill girls there too, living for a while in a cramped lodging in a poor district. It wore her out. Her health had never been strong. She suffered all her life from neuralgia, a painful condition of the nerves that could keep her in bed for weeks.

Then a new friendship changed her life. Robert Wilson, one of the founders of the Keswick Convention, a Quaker by background, had lost his wife and his only daughter. He came to love Amy almost as a daughter, and he invited her to live with his family at their home in Cumberland. She called him "the Dear Old Man". When her first book about India was published, its preface by the mission leader Eugene Stock described her as "the adopted daughter of Mr. Robert Wilson, the much-respected chairman of the Keswick Convention." It was not a legal adoption, but it was a real one in every other way.

On 13 January 1892, as she told it, Amy became sure that God was telling her to go. The words she heard were two of the plainest in the Bible, Jesus' command in the King James Version she knew: "Go ye" (Mark 16:15). It was not easy. Robert Wilson was elderly and leaned on her. Her mother was a widow. Amy wrote to her mother, and her mother, though it cost her, gave her blessing.

She applied first to Hudson Taylor's China Inland Mission. She went to London to train and was turned down on medical grounds; the doctors did not think she was strong enough. So she found another way. In March 1893, aged twenty-five, she sailed for Japan, one of the first missionaries supported by the Keswick Convention.

### What Came After

Japan was hard. Amy worked there for about fifteen months, studying the language, living as simply as she could, and learning that her own ideas of how things should be done were not always the best. Her health broke down. She went on to China and then to Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, for a short time, before returning to England when Robert Wilson fell ill. Some people decided she was flighty. She was learning.

In 1895 she sailed for India, arriving in November. Apart from that arrival, she never left it. For fifty-five years she did not go home on leave even once.

She went to the Tirunelveli district (then spelled Tinnevelly) in the far south, a Tamil-speaking region, and worked with an English missionary couple, Thomas Walker and his wife. Amy learned Tamil, wore Indian dress and travelled from village to village by bullock cart with a band of Indian Christian women, who taught and prayed alongside her. One of them, Ponnammal, became one of her closest friends and co-workers. The work was slow. Many listened politely and went back to their lives. Some who wanted to follow Christ faced being cut off by their families and their whole community, and Amy saw the real cost of that choice to people she loved.

She wrote about it honestly, too honestly for some readers at home. Her book *Things as They Are* (1903) refused to tell the cheerful success stories that missionary supporters expected. Some people in England said she must be exaggerating. Some missionaries in India wrote to say she was not. Readers today will also notice that she wrote about Hindu religion as a missionary of her time, and that Hindus would describe their own faith very differently. But nobody could say she had made mission sound easy. In one chapter, which she called "Humdrum", she said she had held on to the text "It is required in stewards that a man be found faithful", and added, "Praise God, it does not say 'successful.'"

Then, on 7 March 1901, a seven-year-old girl named Preena escaped from a Hindu temple where she was being kept and found her way to Amy. She had run away before and been caught and punished. Through her, Amy learned about the practice of dedicating young girls to temple service, which for many of them meant a life of sexual exploitation. Amy could not unknow it. She began, with her Indian colleagues, to take in and protect children in danger, one by one, at a place called Dohnavur. The children called her Amma, which means "mother" in Tamil. The work grew into the Dohnavur Fellowship, a large family with nurseries, schools and in time a hospital, which took in boys too from 1918. It was never safe or simple. Amy was accused of kidnapping, faced court cases, and buried children she had fought to save. She held a strict rule never to ask anyone but God for money, and the Fellowship believed He provided.

In 1931 Amy had a bad fall and was badly injured. She was largely confined to her room for the last twenty years of her life, in pain, and from there she wrote much of her large shelf of books and went on caring for her Dohnavur family. She died at Dohnavur on 18 January 1951, aged eighty-three. She had asked for no gravestone. The children put a birdbath over her grave, marked with one word: Amma.

It is easy to tell Amy's story as if it began in India. It began in a wet street in Belfast, when a seventeen-year-old who cared too much about her reputation decided that she would rather build with gold. Everything after, the shawlies, Japan, Dohnavur, the long years of pain, was the same choice made again and again. She did not think the choice made her a better kind of Christian than anyone else. She thought it was simply what it meant to follow a Saviour who had given Himself for her, and who did not mind being seen with the poor.

1. Amy was embarrassed to be seen helping a poor woman, and then ashamed of her embarrassment. When have you cared more about how something looked than about what was right? What would "gold, silver, precious stones" look like in your week?
2. The Bible passage Amy heard says the foundation is Christ, and that even someone whose work burns up "will be saved." So why does it matter what we build, if salvation is a gift?

## Eric Liddell: The Race He Would Not Run

On Sunday 6 July 1924, the heats of the men's 100 metres were run at the Olympic Games in Paris. Crowds filled the stadium at Colombes, on the edge of the city. Athletes from all over the world crouched at the start, waiting for the gun, and the fastest of them went through to the next round.

The man many people in Britain had expected to win the race was not there. Eric Liddell, a twenty-two-year-old science student from Edinburgh, held the British record for the 100 yards. That Sunday, by the accounts that have come down to us, he was across the city at the Scots Kirk, the Scottish church in Paris, where he had been asked to speak.

He had not pulled out at the last minute. He had decided months before, as soon as he learned the heats would be on a Sunday, told the people in charge quietly, and started training for a longer race nobody expected him to win.

To many people at the time it looked foolish, even unpatriotic. To Eric it was simple. Sunday was the Lord's Day. He had kept it all his life. He was not going to stop for a gold medal.

How a shy boy, raised mostly without his parents, came to hold that line in front of the whole country is a story that starts not in Paris or Edinburgh but in China.

### Born in Tientsin

Eric Henry Liddell was born on 16 January 1902 in Tientsin (now usually written Tianjin), a busy port city in northern China. His parents, James and Mary Liddell, were Scottish missionaries with the London Missionary Society. James had come to China to preach and teach; Mary had come out to marry him. Eric was their second son. His brother Rob was two years older, and a sister, Jenny, and a younger brother, Ernest, came later.

It was a dangerous time to be a foreigner in China. Two years before Eric's birth, in the uprising foreigners called the Boxer Rebellion, many missionaries and thousands of Chinese Christians had been killed, caught up in anger at the foreign powers that had forced their way into China. The Liddells stayed. They believed the good news of Jesus was for the Chinese people as much as for anyone in Scotland.

Eric's first years were spent in China, among Chinese neighbours and Chinese voices. But missionary families of that time faced a hard choice. There were few schools for foreign children in China, and the custom was to send them home to Britain to be educated, often for years at a time.

In 1907 the family went home to Scotland on leave, and in 1908, when Eric was six and Rob eight, the two boys were enrolled at a boarding school in London called the School for the Sons of Missionaries. Then their parents went back to China.

Try to imagine it: a six-year-old in a strange city, waving goodbye to his mother and father. For long stretches of his childhood Eric saw his parents only when they came back to Britain on leave. They believed they were obeying God, as did thousands of missionary families. But the cost fell on the children, and it is right to say so. Eric did not complain about it later. He had Rob, and the two brothers stayed close. Still, the boy who grew up to be famous for his quiet, steady kindness had learned very early what it was to be far from home.

### The School for the Sons of Missionaries

In 1912 the school moved to new grounds at Mottingham, on the south-eastern edge of London, and took the name Eltham College. It was a school full of boys like Eric: sons of missionaries from China, India and Africa, whose parents were on the other side of the world.

Eric was shy. Teachers and classmates who remembered him later spoke of a quiet, modest boy, not especially bookish, not a leader who pushed himself forward, and kind to younger boys. What changed things was sport. Eric turned out to be fast, and strong for his size, and very good at games. By his later years at the school, by most accounts, he was captain of both the rugby and the cricket teams, and he was winning races on the school sports days. Rob was a fine athlete too. The two Liddell boys were well known around the school.

What about his faith? There is no dramatic conversion story for Eric Liddell, no night in a storm or sermon that turned his life around, and it would be wrong to invent one. He grew up in a Christian family and a Christian school, with daily prayers, chapel and Bible teaching. Somewhere in those years, quietly, what he had been taught became his own. The people who knew him as a young man describe someone who already prayed and read his Bible every day because he wanted to, not because a teacher was watching.

Many teenagers raised in Christian homes worry that their faith is not "real" because they cannot point to a dramatic moment. Eric could not point to one either. What he could point to was a life: he kept choosing, again and again, to take Jesus at his word.

He was not a loud Christian, either. Those who knew him said he rarely talked about himself and found speaking in public very hard.

### The Fastest Man in Scotland

In 1920, at eighteen, Eric followed Rob to the University of Edinburgh to study science. For a while his parents were back in Scotland on leave, and the family lived together in Edinburgh, something Eric had hardly known since he was six. Then they went back to China again.

At first Eric did not take running seriously. In 1921, by the usual account, friends persuaded him to enter the university sports, and his speed astonished everyone, possibly including himself. Soon he was being trained by Tom McKerchar, a well-known Edinburgh athletics coach, who taught him to start properly and to look after his body. He also played rugby, as a fast winger, and in 1922 and 1923 he won seven caps for Scotland, which meant he played seven times for his country in international matches. Before he had finished his degree, he was a national sporting hero.

His running style was famous, and not because it was elegant. In the final stretch Eric would throw his head back, chin to the sky, arms flailing, mouth open, as if he had forgotten technique altogether and was simply pouring everything into going faster. Coaches winced. Crowds loved it.

In July 1923, at the national championships in London, he won the 100 yards in 9.7 seconds, a British record that lasted for decades, and won the 220 yards too.

A week or so later came the race that people who saw it never forgot. At an international meeting at Stoke-on-Trent, in the English Midlands, Eric was running the 440 yards, a lap of the track, when early on he was bumped by another runner and stumbled off the track onto the grass. By the time he got back on, the others were far ahead, by some accounts around twenty yards. He set off after them, head back, arms going, and caught and passed them all to win. Then he collapsed. The details vary from telling to telling, but the main fact is not in doubt: he won a race he had already lost.

He was twenty-one. People began to talk about the Olympic Games in Paris the next summer.

### Armadale

That spring, something else happened to Eric that he found far more frightening than any race.

In April 1923, a young Scottish evangelist named D. P. Thomson came to see Eric in Edinburgh. Thomson was organising meetings for the Glasgow Students' Evangelistic Union, a group of students who went out to towns and villages to tell people about Jesus, and he had an idea. He was planning a meeting for men in Armadale, a mining town between Edinburgh and Glasgow, and he wanted to bring in a speaker the men would actually come to hear. Who better than the famous young rugby player and sprinter? Would Eric come and speak?

Eric said yes.

That may not sound like much. But Eric hated speaking in public, and rarely talked about his faith even to his friends. Saying yes meant standing in front of a hall full of working men, many of whom had come only to look at a sportsman, and telling them what Jesus meant to him. In later years he spoke about how hard the decision had been for him, and he seems to have seen it as one of the most important he ever made. It seems to have been the moment he stopped keeping his faith to himself.

The meeting was held on 6 April 1923. By Thomson's account, far more men came than usual. Eric was not a polished speaker. He spoke simply and without drama about his faith in Christ, and the men listened. Newspapers reported it. And from then on he became one of the most sought-after speakers in Scotland, going with Thomson and the students to town after town. Huge crowds came, at first to see the athlete. Many stayed to hear what he had to say.

What did he say? We do not have word-for-word reports of most of those talks, and it would be dishonest to invent them. But we know what he believed, because he lived it and later wrote about it. He believed that Jesus Christ was the Son of God, who had died for the sins of the world, his own included, and had risen again. He believed that knowing God's forgiveness through Christ was the most important thing in any life, more important than records or medals. And he believed that following Jesus meant handing over the whole of your life to God: your time, your ambitions, your talents, your will. The word he came back to again and again, all his life, was surrender.

That is what made the decision about Paris possible. A young man who had already decided that his life belonged to God would not find a Sunday race much of a question.

### The Timetable

Sometime in late 1923 or early 1924 (the exact date is not certain), the timetable for the Paris Olympics reached the British athletics officials. Eric learned that the heats of the 100 metres, his best event, would be held on a Sunday. So would some of the relay heats.

The film *Chariots of Fire*, made in 1981, tells this part of the story differently. In the film, Eric learns about the Sunday heats only as he is boarding the boat to France, and there is a tense scene in which officials and even the Prince of Wales try to persuade him to change his mind, until a teammate offers to give up his own place in the 400 metres. It makes great drama, but it did not happen that way. Eric knew months in advance, told the officials early and calmly, and was entered for the 200 and 400 metres instead. The film also invented scenes, rearranged races, and gave his sister Jenny a disapproval of his running that, by the family's accounts, she did not really have. It is a fine film. It is not a documentary.

Why would Eric give up his best race? He had grown up keeping Sunday as a day set apart for God: for worship and rest, not for work or competition. Many Scottish Christians of his time did the same. He took seriously God's command in the Ten Commandments: "Remember the Sabbath day by keeping it holy" (Exodus 20:8). For him, as for most Christians, the Lord's Day was Sunday, the day Jesus rose from the dead.

Christians today hold different views about Sunday, and plenty of good Christians have competed on it. Eric never made a speech about it or criticised anyone else. What is striking is not the rule but the man. He had a conviction, and he would not trade it, even when the reward was a gold medal and the price was being called a traitor to his country. Some newspapers and some sports officials thought he was letting Britain down. He did not argue with them. He simply did not run.

Nor did he sulk. He spent the months before Paris training for the 400 metres, which asks a sprinter to run nearly flat out for a whole lap while his legs fill with pain. Most experts did not think he could win it.

### Paris

The Paris Games opened in July 1924. On Sunday 6 July, while the 100 metres heats were run, Eric was at church. The next day the 100 metres final was won by his British teammate Harold Abrahams.

On Wednesday 9 July, Eric ran in the final of the 200 metres and came third, winning a bronze medal behind two Americans. It was a good result. But the 400 metres was still to come, and in the heats and semi-finals he had been running faster than anyone expected.

On the morning of the final, Friday 11 July, Eric was handed a small piece of paper. Most accounts say it came from one of the team masseurs, the men who rubbed down the athletes' muscles; in the film it is handed over by an American runner. The note quoted, in the old King James words, a promise God made in the Old Testament: "them that honour me I will honour" (1 Samuel 2:30, KJV). We do not know exactly what Eric thought when he read it. But it must have meant a great deal to a young man who had been told for months that honouring God was costing him his chance.

The final was run later that day. Eric had drawn the outside lane, the worst place to run from, because with the staggered start he could not see the other runners behind him. He would have to run the whole race blind, not knowing whether he was winning.

When the gun fired he went off as if the race were 200 metres instead of 400, and he did not slow down. He was ahead at halfway. Observers expected him to fade, as runners who go out too fast usually do. He did not. Head back, arms flailing, he held on and crossed the line first, in 47.6 seconds, an Olympic record. The American Horatio Fitch was second and the Briton Guy Butler third.

About a week later, back in Edinburgh, Eric graduated from the university with his science degree. When he went up to receive it, the university's principal placed a crown of olive leaves on his head, the prize of the ancient Greek games, and his fellow students carried him out on their shoulders.

He was twenty-two, a champion, and could have had almost any career he wanted.

He chose China.

### What Came After

Eric had decided long before that he would follow his parents back to the land where he was born. After a year studying theology in Edinburgh, he sailed for China in 1925 to teach science and sport at the Anglo-Chinese College in Tientsin, a school run by his parents' mission for Chinese boys. He spoke Chinese, coached the boys, helped to run a Sunday school at a church in the city, and was known for his patience and his laughter. He still raced now and then, and he still won. But he never went back to the Olympics. He believed he was where God wanted him.

In 1932, home on leave, he was ordained as a minister. In 1934 he married Florence Mackenzie, the daughter of Canadian missionaries in Tientsin, and they had two daughters, Patricia and Heather.

China in those years was being torn apart. In 1937 Japan launched a full-scale invasion, and much of northern China became a war zone. That same year Eric left the comfortable college to work in the countryside around Siaochang (Xiaozhang), on the plain south-west of Tientsin, where his brother Rob, now a doctor, ran a mission hospital. It was dangerous, exhausting work: travelling between villages to preach and encourage Chinese Christians, often between Chinese and Japanese soldiers, and helping to bring the sick and wounded to the hospital. Those years cost him his health and much of his strength. They also show the shape of the man. He did not go to China to be a famous foreigner. He went to serve.

By 1941 the danger was so great that Eric sent Florence and the girls to safety in Canada. Florence was expecting their third child. He planned to join them later. He never did. In December 1941 Japan went to war with Britain and America, and in 1943 Eric was taken, with about eighteen hundred other foreign civilians, many of them children, to an internment camp at Weihsien (now Weifang), in Shandong province. His third daughter, Maureen, was born in Canada. He never met her.

Life in the camp was crowded and hungry, and many of the children there were separated from their parents, as Eric had once been. He became, by every account, one of the people who held the camp together. He taught science to the teenagers, organised games and races, refereed matches, helped carry coal and water for older people, and spent time with anyone who was struggling. Survivors remembered him as "Uncle Eric." One story they told later is that he at first refused to referee games on Sundays, but when the teenagers organised their own Sunday game without him and it ended in a fight, he came the next week and refereed. Whether or not every detail is right, it is the kind of story told about a man who held his convictions firmly but cared more about people than about winning arguments. Some who were young in the camp also remembered him teaching them to pray for their Japanese guards, because Jesus said, "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44).

In early 1945 he became ill. He was exhausted, and he suffered headaches and strange weakness. On 21 February 1945 he died, at forty-three. After his death the doctors found he had had a brain tumour that could not have been treated even outside the camp, though overwork and hunger had worn him down too. The camp was freed by American soldiers six months later.

A nurse in the camp, Annie Buchan, was with him near the end. She remembered his last words, and they sum up what he had believed since he was a young man in Edinburgh: that the whole of life belongs to God, and the best thing to do with it is to give it back.

Many people who know nothing else about Eric Liddell know a line from *Chariots of Fire* in which he says that God made him fast, and that when he runs he feels God's pleasure. It is a beautiful line. But it was written for the film; there is no record that Eric ever said it. The real words he left behind were plainer, and they cost more.

### In Their Own Words

> "It's complete surrender."
> — Eric Liddell, his last words, as remembered by the camp nurse Annie Buchan (Weihsien, 1945)

### Think It Through

1. Eric had no dramatic conversion story. His faith grew quietly in a Christian home and school until it became his own. Does a faith need a dramatic beginning to be real? How would you know whether your faith is your own?
2. Eric decided about the Sunday heats months ahead, calmly, without making speeches or criticising anyone. What is the difference between holding a conviction firmly and using it to look down on other people?
3. For Eric, saying yes to speak at Armadale was harder than any race. What is the thing you would find hardest to do for God? What might happen if you said yes?
4. Eric was famous at twenty-two and chose to spend his life as a teacher in China, away from the crowds. What does that suggest about what success really is?

## Jim Elliot: The Man Who Would Not Keep His Life

On 28 October 1949, a twenty-two-year-old named Jim Elliot sat down with his journal and wrote one sentence that millions of people have since quoted.

He was not anywhere special. He was not on a mission field or a battlefield. He had graduated from college that summer and gone home to his parents' house in Portland, Oregon, on the west coast of the United States. He was working, studying his Bible, preaching now and then in small churches, and waiting. He knew he wanted to take the good news of Jesus to people who had never heard it. He did not yet know where. Some of the older Christians around him thought a young man with his gifts would do more good staying in America. He was restless, impatient and, by his own journals, sometimes hard to live with.

That day he had been thinking, as he often did, about what it means to give your life to God. Earlier Christians had written about it; one seventeenth-century English minister, Philip Henry, had said something very like it. But the sentence Jim wrote was his own. It said that a person is not a fool if he gives away what he cannot keep in order to gain what he cannot lose.

We do not know exactly what he was doing that day, or how long he sat over the page. We know only that he was twenty-two and no one was watching. He was not writing an epitaph. He had lost nothing, faced nothing, proved nothing. He was working out, privately and in advance, what he believed his life was for.

Six years later, he would be dead at twenty-eight, killed on a river sandbar in the rainforest of Ecuador, by the people he had gone to reach.

### Portland

Philip James Elliot, always called Jim, was born on 8 October 1927 in Portland, Oregon, the third of four children of Fred and Clara Elliot. His father preached in the small Christian congregations of the Plymouth Brethren, a movement that met simply, without professional clergy, and took the Bible with great seriousness. His mother was a chiropractor. The house was busy, plain and full of Scripture. Missionaries and travelling preachers often stayed with them, and the children grew up hearing stories of faraway places where people had never heard of Jesus.

Jim professed faith in Christ as a small boy, at about six. A faith that starts so early has to be taken over by the person as they grow, and Jim's was. By the time he was a teenager he was not a boy repeating what his parents believed. He was a young man who had decided, for himself, that Jesus Christ was Lord, and that a Lord is someone you obey.

He went to Benson Polytechnic High School in Portland, a large public school where boys learned practical trades as well as ordinary lessons. Jim studied architectural drawing. He was also a gifted and confident speaker, active in public speaking at the school, and he acted in school plays. Those who knew him remembered that he made no secret of being a Christian. He is said to have carried his Bible to school with his schoolbooks, and he was not embarrassed to talk about it. These were the years of the Second World War, and the world his classmates were growing up into was full of fear and loss. Jim had settled the question of where his loyalty lay.

There is something worth noticing here. Jim was talented: a good speaker, good at drawing, physically strong, well liked. Plenty of doors were open to him. Many of the people who make great sacrifices for God have not been people with nothing to lose. Jim had a lot to lose, and he knew it.

### Wheaton

In the autumn of 1945, a month before he turned eighteen, Jim enrolled at Wheaton College, a Christian college near Chicago, half a continent away from home.

He threw himself into it. He joined the wrestling team, not mainly to win, but because he believed the discipline would make his body fit to be used by God. He studied hard, chose to major in Greek so that he could read the New Testament in its original language, and did well enough to graduate in 1949 with honours. He was a leader in the college's missionary fellowship, where students prayed for the nations and for one another's callings.

He was also intense, and he could be hard on people who were not. He had little patience with what he saw as wasted time: endless socialising, entertainment, campus gossip. Some students found him inspiring. Others found him exhausting. Both reactions were probably fair. Jim's later journals show that he knew it too, and was often hard on himself.

What fed him most were books about other young Christians who had given everything. He read the diary of David Brainerd, the young missionary to Native Americans who had died in 1747 at twenty-nine, worn out by his work and his illness, and Brainerd's single-minded devotion became something of a pattern for him. He read Amy Carmichael, the missionary in India. He was not looking for an easy faith. He was looking for one that would cost something.

In the summer of 1947, Jim and a friend went to Mexico for several weeks and stayed with a missionary family. It was his first real taste of life in another culture. He worked at Spanish, met Mexican Christians, and saw for himself what it was like to live among people of another language for the sake of the gospel. He came back sure that God was calling him to Latin America.

He also began, from early 1948, to keep the journal that would later be published. It is a surprising document. It is full of prayers, Bible verses, plans and self-criticism. It is also, as Elisabeth Elliot later said when she let it be printed as he wrote it, earnest and sometimes immature, the private notebook of a young man still growing up.

### Holding Things Loosely

At Wheaton, Jim's closest friend was a fellow wrestler named Dave Howard. At Christmas 1947 Dave brought Jim home to his family in New Jersey, and Jim met Dave's older sister, Betty, a quiet, serious Greek student a year ahead of him at Wheaton. The next term the two of them began studying together. By the spring of 1948 they were in love.

That should have been simple. It was not. Jim had come to believe that God might be calling him to stay single, so that he could go to the hardest and most dangerous places without anyone depending on him. He was not sure. And he was not willing to let his feelings decide. On 9 June 1948, just before Betty's graduation, they told each other how they felt, and agreed to give their love back to God, with no promises, and to wait.

They waited five years. That story belongs to Betty, who became Elisabeth Elliot, and it is told in full in her own life story. Here it matters for one reason. Jim's famous sentence was not only about dying. It was about every good thing a person might clutch. He was learning, in the most personal part of his life, what it meant to hold something precious with an open hand. Jesus said, "For whoever wants to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for My sake will find it" (Matthew 16:25). Jim believed him, and was finding out how hard that was.

It is fair to say that not everyone admires how Jim handled those years. Betty waited a long time, often painfully, and some readers think Jim's certainty about God's leading was hard on the people around him. That is part of the honest picture too. Young people who are on fire for God can still hurt others, and still have a lot to learn.

### The Line in the Journal

After graduation in June 1949, Jim went home to Portland. For most of the next two years he lived there, working, studying, preaching and praying about where to go. It was during that waiting that he wrote the sentence on 28 October 1949.

It is usually quoted as "He is no fool who gives what he cannot keep to gain what he cannot lose." In the journal, as it was later published, it reads slightly differently: "that which he cannot lose." The meaning is the same.

What was he saying? Think about the things you cannot keep. Your time is passing whether you like it or not. Your health, your looks, your strength, your reputation, even your life: you will lose every one of them in the end. You cannot hold on to them however hard you grip. Jim's point was that it is not foolish to give them to God now. It is the only sensible thing to do. And what you gain in return, knowing God and being known by him, being forgiven and made his child through Jesus, is the one thing that can never be taken away.

What did Jim believe about Jesus? That he was the Son of God, who had given his own life on the cross for sinners and risen again; that he was not just a Saviour to be thanked but a Lord to be obeyed; and that people everywhere needed to hear of him. Jim took Paul's questions seriously: "How can they believe in the One of whom they have not heard? And how can they hear without someone to preach?" (Romans 10:14). If there were people in the world who had never heard the name of Jesus, someone had to go. He could not see why it should not be him.

### Ecuador

In the summer of 1950 Jim went to a linguistics course in Oklahoma run by Wycliffe Bible Translators, to learn how to study and write down languages that had never been written. There, by the usual account, he met a missionary who had worked in Ecuador, a small country on the equator in South America. He heard about the Quichua people of the eastern rainforest, and about another people beyond them, called by outsiders the "Aucas," a Quichua word meaning "savages." Their own name was, and is, Waorani, "the people." They lived deep in the forest between two rivers, they had killed outsiders who came into their land, and they had suffered greatly at outsiders' hands too, from rubber collectors and oil prospectors. No outsider had ever reached them in friendship.

Within months Jim was sure God was sending him to Ecuador. A friend from Seattle, Pete Fleming, agreed to go with him. In February 1952 they arrived in Ecuador. Jim was twenty-four.

He began not with the Waorani but with the Quichua, at a mission station called Shandia, on the edge of the forest. He learned Spanish and then Quichua, preached, taught, built, and ran a small school. In 1953 a flood swept away much of the station, and he rebuilt it. On 8 October 1953, his twenty-sixth birthday, he and Betty were married in Quito, the capital. Their daughter, Valerie, was born in February 1955. He was a husband and a father now, with everything a young man might wish to keep.

But he had never stopped thinking about the Waorani.

### What Came After

In the autumn of 1955, Jim and four other young missionaries began a plan to make friendly contact with the Waorani. The others were Pete Fleming; Ed McCully, a friend from Wheaton; Roger Youderian; and Nate Saint, the mission pilot. All five were married, and four had young children. They called it Operation Auca, using the insulting outsiders' name. For weeks Nate flew over a Waorani settlement while the men lowered gifts on a line from the plane, and the Waorani began to send gifts back. Jim learned a few Waorani phrases from Dayuma, a Waorani woman who had fled her people years before; it turned out later that he had learned them imperfectly. The men kept the plan almost entirely secret. Historians have since asked whether they moved too fast.

On 3 January 1956 they landed on a sandbar on the Curaray River and called it Palm Beach. On 6 January three Waorani came out of the forest and spent a friendly day with them. Two days later, on Sunday 8 January, a group of Waorani men attacked the camp and killed all five with spears. The men had guns, but they had agreed beforehand that they would not use them against the Waorani, because the Waorani were not ready to die and they believed that they themselves were. Jim was twenty-eight.

Years later, the Waorani who were there explained what had happened. A young man who had been at the friendly meeting had told his relatives, to cover up trouble of his own, that the foreigners had attacked them. The Waorani already believed that outsiders were dangerous, and some believed they ate people. They were not monsters. They were frightened people acting on a lie, within a long history of violence, both among themselves and from outsiders. Their side of the story matters as much as the missionaries' does.

The story did not end on the sandbar. Within weeks it was known around the world. And then Jim's widow, Elisabeth, with their small daughter, and Rachel Saint, the pilot's sister, went in 1958 to live among the very people who had killed the men, at their invitation. Elisabeth stayed about three years. Over time many Waorani came to trust Christ, among them some of the men who had been on the beach, and the cycle of killing that had shaped their lives began to slow. Elisabeth spent the rest of her life telling the story honestly, including the parts that did not fit a neat ending: the mistakes, the conflicts among missionaries, and the questions without answers.

Jim did not go to Ecuador looking to die. The five men went in hope. Jesus once said that "unless a kernel of wheat falls to the ground and dies, it remains only a seed; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). On the evening of 8 January 1956, by the silent radio at the mission base, nobody could see any fruit at all. It took years to grow, and it grew in ways none of the five could have planned.

Jim's sentence has been printed on posters and bookmarks ever since, often by people who know nothing else about him. It is worth remembering that he wrote it long before it cost him anything. He wrote it at twenty-two, at home, in an ordinary week, deciding in advance what he would do with his life. That is the part any young person can copy.

### In Their Own Words

> "He is no fool who gives what he cannot keep to gain that which he cannot lose."

### Think It Through

1. Jim wrote his famous sentence at twenty-two, before it had cost him anything. Why might it matter to decide what you believe about your life before the hard moments come?
2. Make a list of things you cannot keep, however hard you try. Which of them do you grip most tightly? What would it look like to hold it with an open hand?
3. Jim was intense, and some people found him inspiring while others found him hard to be around. Can a person be very devoted to God and still need to grow in kindness and patience? What does that look like?
4. The Waorani have their own account of what happened at Palm Beach. Why is it important to hear the story from the side of the people missionaries went to, and not only from the missionaries?
