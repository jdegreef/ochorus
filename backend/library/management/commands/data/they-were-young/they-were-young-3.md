# They Were Young – Book 3: Questions

*Six who wrestled with doubt while they were young*

## Introduction: When Faith Asks Questions

Once, a desperate father brought his son to Jesus. The boy had been suffering for years, and the father had nearly given up hope. "If You can do anything," he begged, "have compassion on us and help us." "If You can?" echoed Jesus. "All things are possible to him who believes!" And the father said something so honest that it has comforted doubting people ever since: "I do believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:22–24).

Jesus did not send him away for doubting. He healed his son.

This book is about six people who had questions as teenagers: about God, about the Bible, about suffering, about whether any of it was true. Some of them believed as children and then lost their faith. Some never had faith to lose. Some believed all along but could not make their minds and their hearts agree. They were not lazy thinkers. Several of them were among the cleverest people of their age. And every one of them found, sooner or later, that following Jesus did not mean switching off their minds. Jesus Himself said the greatest commandment was to "love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind" (Matthew 22:37).

Here are the six people you will meet in *Questions*, the third book of *They Were Young*:

**Augustine**, a brilliant, restless teenager in Roman North Africa who stole pears just for the thrill of it, read a book that set his mind on fire, and spent years chasing answers everywhere except where his mother prayed he would find them.

**Blaise Pascal**, a French boy so clever that he worked out geometry for himself, built a calculating machine as a teenager, and discovered that the God of the philosophers was not enough.

**Isaac Watts**, a sharp-witted boy from a family persecuted for their faith, who came to trust Christ at about fifteen and went on to write some of the best-loved hymns in the English language.

**Jonathan Edwards**, who went to college at thirteen, argued with God's control over everything until one day a single verse changed the way he saw the world, and who then wrote himself seventy rules for life.

**C. S. Lewis**, who lost his mother as a boy, lost his faith at school, and became a confident teenage atheist, before the long road that brought him to Jesus.

**Dietrich Bonhoeffer**, who lost his brother in a war, told his scientific family at fourteen that he was going to study theology, and kept asking what it really means to follow Jesus, all the way to a Nazi prison.

### How each chapter works

As in the first two books, each chapter begins with a moment, a decisive scene in that person's young life, and then pulls back to tell you how they got there and what came after. The rest of the life is told more briefly at the end.

After the story come three short sections. **In Their Own Words** gives you a few lines the person really said or wrote, with the source. **Think It Through** offers some questions to wrestle with, alone or with others. **Read More on Ochorus** points you to the person's own writing, or their biography, if you want to go further.

These stories are true, and we have tried hard not to add anything to them. We have not invented conversations or made up scenes. Where the records are silent, or where historians disagree, we tell you. Where a famous story is a tradition rather than a fact, we say so.

### A word about doubt

You might expect a Christian book to treat doubt as the enemy. These six people would not let us. Their questions were often the very road God used to bring them home. But you will also notice that none of them stayed in doubt as if it were a place to live. They kept asking, and they kept looking for the truth, even when the answer cost them their pride. The Bible says God made people "that they would seek Him and perhaps reach out for Him and find Him, though He is not far from each one of us" (Acts 17:27). That is what these six did.

### For parents, teachers and youth leaders

These stories are written for readers aged about thirteen to seventeen. They take young people's questions seriously, including doubt, the loss of faith, grief and suffering, and they show faith that thinks rather than faith that is afraid to. Some chapters touch on war, the death of a parent and execution, and one mentions a young man's sexual relationships; all of this is handled honestly but briefly and without detail.

The Think It Through questions are meant to open conversation rather than settle it. You do not need to have read *Called* or *Tested*, the first two books in the series, to use this one. Each chapter stands on its own.

## Augustine: The Restless Heart

Late one night, in a small town in Roman North Africa, a gang of teenage boys finished their games in the street and went looking for something else to do. Near the vineyard that belonged to one of their families stood a pear tree, heavy with fruit. The pears were not especially good. They were not a pretty colour and they did not taste of much. None of the boys was hungry. Every one of them had better fruit at home.

They shook the tree anyway. They stripped it, and carried off armfuls of pears, more than they could possibly eat. They took a bite or two. Then they threw the rest to the pigs.

That was all. It is the kind of thing that happens in every town in every century, and most of the people who do it have forgotten it by twenty.

One of those boys never forgot it. His name was Aurelius Augustinus, and we know him as Augustine. Nearly thirty years later, when he was a bishop and one of the most respected thinkers in the Christian world, he sat down to write the story of his life, and he spent page after page on that stupid night with the pears. He could not leave it alone. He kept asking the question every honest teenager has asked at some point: *Why did I do that?*

"I lusted to thieve, and did it," he wrote, "compelled by no hunger, nor poverty." He had not wanted the pears. "Nor cared I to enjoy what I stole, but joyed in the theft and sin itself." And then the line that still makes readers stop: "It was foul, and I loved it."

He was, by his own count, in his "sixteenth year", which in the way he reckoned ages means he was fifteen or sixteen. The book he wrote is called the *Confessions*, and it is the reason we know anything at all about a provincial teenager who lived more than sixteen hundred years ago. It is also one of the most honest books ever written about what goes on inside a restless young person who is clever enough to ask hard questions and not yet ready to hear the answers.

### Born in Thagaste

Augustine was born on 13 November 354 in Thagaste, an inland market town in the Roman province of Numidia. Today it is Souk Ahras, in Algeria. North Africa was then one of the richest parts of the Roman Empire, full of farms and olive groves and busy cities, and its people spoke Latin, read Latin books, and dreamed of getting on in the Roman world.

His father, Patricius, owned a little land and was always short of money. He was not a Christian, and he had a quick temper, though Augustine says he was also warm-hearted. His mother, Monica, was a Christian, and a determined one. She made sure her son learned the name of Jesus as a small child, and that name, Augustine later said, went into him "with my mother's milk" and never quite came out again. When he fell dangerously ill as a boy, he begged to be baptized. Then he got better, and the baptism was put off. In those days many families delayed baptism, thinking it better to wait until the wild years were over.

He was brilliant, and everyone could see it. He hated Greek, which he was beaten for not learning, but he loved Latin literature, especially the poet Virgil, and he could make an audience cry with a speech. His parents decided he would be an orator: a professional speaker and teacher of speaking, which in the Roman world was the path to a good career, perhaps even to a government post. They sent him to school in Madauros, a larger town about thirty kilometres away. And then the money ran out.

### A Year with Nothing to Do

So, at about fifteen or sixteen, Augustine came home to Thagaste and waited while his father scraped together the money to send him on to the great city of Carthage. It was a year with no school, no work and no supervision, and Augustine later looked back on it as the year everything began to slide.

He describes it plainly. His body was changing, and his desires with it. He says he wanted "to love, and be loved", but could no longer tell the difference between love and lust. His father noticed at the public baths that his son was becoming a man, and went home pleased, already imagining grandchildren. His mother was alarmed. She took him aside and warned him privately not to sleep around, and above all never to go near another man's wife. Augustine's reaction will sound familiar to anyone whose parent has tried to have that conversation: he thought her advice was embarrassing, "womanish", and he was ashamed to take it.

What shocked him most, looking back, was not what he did but why he did it. He wanted to impress his friends. When they boasted about what they had been up to, he felt small if he had done less, so he made things up. "I would say that I had done what I had not done," he admitted, "that I might not seem contemptible." He was lying about sins he had not committed, to be accepted by people he did not even admire.

That is the background to the pears. When the older Augustine finally asked himself what he had loved in that theft, he realised it was not the fruit, and not even really the stealing. It was the gang. He wrote that "alone I had never done it," and he repeated it several times, as if he could hardly believe it. He described the moment we have all seen happen in a group, when someone says, "Let's go, let's do it," and suddenly everyone is "ashamed not to be shameless."

The older Augustine did not think this was a small thing. For him, the pears were a window onto a much bigger question: where does evil come from? If the boys were not hungry and did not want the pears, then the evil was not in the fruit, or in their stomachs. It was in their hearts, in a will that wanted to do wrong simply because it was wrong. He would spend years trying to find an answer that let him off the hook. He never found one, and in the end that honesty was part of what saved him.

### To Carthage

At about seventeen he finally reached Carthage, the second city of the Latin-speaking West, on the coast near modern Tunis. His father had borrowed and stretched to pay for it, and a wealthy family friend called Romanianus helped too. Around this time, Patricius died. He had been baptized shortly before the end, after years of Monica's prayers.

Carthage was huge, loud and exciting: a harbour full of ships, theatres, chariot races, schools of rhetoric, and thousands of young men from the provinces trying to make their name. Augustine's first sentence about it is one of the most famous in the whole *Confessions*: "To Carthage I came, where there sang all around me in my ears a cauldron of unholy loves." He went on: "I loved not yet, yet I loved to love." He was not looking for anyone in particular. He was in love with the idea of being in love.

He worked hard, too. He quickly became the top student in the school of rhetoric. Some of the students called themselves the "Subverters" and amused themselves by bullying new arrivals. Augustine ran with them, but says he never joined in their cruelty, and felt a strange shame at not being as bad as they were.

Around this time, probably when he was seventeen or eighteen, he began to live with a woman. He never tells us her name. They were not married; in Roman society a young man with ambitions often lived with a partner of lower social class until he could make a "good" marriage. Augustine stayed faithful to her for many years, and she bore him a son. They named him Adeodatus, which means "given by God", a strangely hopeful name from a young man who was running from God as fast as he could. Augustine never pretends this part of his life was right, and he never pretends he did not love them.

### A Book Without a Name in It

Then, in what he calls his "nineteenth year", so when he was eighteen, something happened that he had not planned at all. As part of the ordinary course of study, he was given a book by Cicero, the greatest Roman orator, called *Hortensius*. It has since been lost, but it was an appeal to young people to stop chasing money, fame and pleasure and to give themselves to the search for wisdom. The Greek word for "love of wisdom" is *philosophy*.

Augustine had picked the book up to improve his style. Instead, it changed what he wanted. The book, he wrote, "altered my affections, and turned my prayers to Thyself O Lord." Suddenly his ambitions looked small. "Every vain hope at once became worthless to me; and I longed with an incredibly burning desire for an immortality of wisdom." He was eighteen, and for the first time in his life he wanted truth more than applause.

It is worth noticing that the thing that first stirred Augustine towards God was not a sermon or a church. It was a hard book by a pagan writer, read for a school assignment. God, it seems, is not limited to the obvious channels.

But there was one thing wrong with *Hortensius*: "the name of Christ was not in it." The boy who had heard that name from his mother could not settle for any wisdom without it, however learned and polished it was. So he did what seemed obvious. He opened the Bible.

And he was disappointed. The Latin Bibles of North Africa in his day were rough, word-for-word translations, and to a young man trained in the music of Cicero, they sounded crude. The stories of the Old Testament seemed primitive, even shocking. Beside what he called "the stateliness of Tully" ("Tully" is Cicero), they seemed unworthy of a serious mind. Looking back, he saw the real problem: the Scriptures were written to be entered by the humble, "such as would grow up in a little one". But, he confessed, "I disdained to be a little one." He was too proud to become a beginner. So he closed the book.

### The Religion That Promised Reasons

It was at this moment that he met the Manichees.

The Manichees followed a third-century Persian teacher called Mani, who taught that the universe was a battle between two equal powers, Light and Darkness, Good and Evil. They used the name of Jesus and talked a great deal about "Truth", but they rejected the Old Testament, cut out the parts of the New Testament they disliked, and taught a complicated mythology about particles of light trapped in matter. They had strict rules about food. Augustine later laughed at himself for believing that a fig wept when it was picked.

Why would a brilliant eighteen-year-old fall for that? Partly because the Manichees promised exactly what he was hungry for. They said they did not ask anyone to believe on authority; they would give reasons. And they asked the questions that were troubling him. They asked "whence is evil?" and "is God bounded by a bodily shape, and has hairs and nails?" They made fun of the Old Testament heroes, with their many wives and their wars. These were real questions, and the Church people Augustine knew did not seem to have good answers.

The Manichees also offered something more comfortable. If evil was a separate power, a kind of dark substance fighting against the light in us, then the evil was not really *me*. It just happened in me. For a young man who did not want to change his life, that was a very convenient answer to the question of the pears.

He stayed with them, as one of their "Hearers", for about nine years. He argued for them, brought friends into the sect, and enjoyed winning debates against ordinary Christians who could not keep up with him.

At home, Monica was devastated. For a time she would not even let him eat at her table. Then she had a dream. She was standing on a wooden rule, weeping, and a shining young man asked her why. When she told him she was mourning her son's ruin, he told her to look: where she was, there her son was also. She looked, and saw Augustine standing beside her. When she told him the dream, Augustine, ever the debater, tried to twist it: perhaps it meant *she* would one day be where *he* was. Without a pause she answered that the dream had not said that he was where she was, but that she was where he was. He admitted later that her quick reply moved him more than the dream itself. But he did not change.

She went to a bishop, whose name Augustine does not give, and begged him to argue her son out of his errors. The bishop refused. Augustine was too puffed up for argument, he said; let him alone, and pray; he would find out the truth by reading. When Monica kept begging and weeping, the bishop finally said, a little impatiently: "Go thy ways and God bless thee, for it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish." She held on to those words for years.

Around the age of twenty, Augustine went back to Thagaste to teach. There he had a close friend, someone he had grown up with, whom he had drawn into the Manichees too. The friend fell ill with a fever and, while he was unconscious, was baptized. When he came round, Augustine made a joke about the baptism, expecting him to laugh. Instead his friend turned on him, and told him that if he wanted to stay his friend, he must never speak like that again. Augustine was startled, and decided to wait until he was well to win him back. A few days later the fever returned, and his friend died.

Augustine was shattered. "I became a great riddle to myself," he wrote. Everything in his home town reminded him of his friend. His Manichaean beliefs gave him nothing to hold on to, and when he told his soul to trust in God, it would not listen, because the "God" he had been taught to imagine was less real than the friend he had lost. He fled back to Carthage.

### What Came After

The rest of the journey took more than ten years, and the *Confessions* tells it in full. Here it is in brief.

The Manichees kept telling Augustine that his hardest questions would be answered when their famous bishop Faustus came to Carthage. When Faustus finally came, Augustine was then twenty-eight or twenty-nine. He found him charming and fluent, and unable to answer anything. The spell began to break. In 383 Augustine sailed for Rome, slipping away by night after lying to Monica at the harbour so that she would not come with him. The next year he won a prestigious post as professor of rhetoric in Milan, where the emperor's court then lived.

In Milan he went to hear the bishop, Ambrose, at first only to study his preaching style. But Ambrose showed him that the Old Testament he had sneered at was deeper than he had thought. Then some books of the Platonist philosophers helped him imagine, for the first time, a God who was spirit and not some vast shining substance, and showed him that evil is not a thing at all but a twisting of something good. In those books he found much that agreed with the opening of John's Gospel. But one thing, he said, he did not find there: "that the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us." The philosophers could point to God from a distance. They could not tell him that God had come down. He found no way home, he wrote, "until I embraced that Mediator betwixt God and men, the Man Christ Jesus."

His mind was coming round. His will was not. Meanwhile Monica, who had followed him to Milan, arranged a respectable marriage for him, and the woman he had lived with for about fifteen years was sent back to Africa. She vowed never to be with another man and left their son with him. Augustine wrote that his heart was "torn and wounded and bleeding". He knew what he ought to do, and could not make himself do it. As a young man he had prayed, "Give me chastity and continency, only not yet."

In August 386, when he was thirty-one, in a garden in Milan, he finally broke. He threw himself down under a fig tree and wept, asking how long he would go on saying "tomorrow". Then he heard a child's voice from a nearby house chanting, "Take up and read; Take up and read." He picked up the letters of Paul and read the first lines his eyes fell on, from Romans 13: "Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying; but put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, in concupiscence." (That is the old English translation of his *Confessions*; a modern Bible reads, "clothe yourselves with the Lord Jesus Christ.") "No further would I read; nor needed I," he wrote, for at once "all the darkness of doubt vanished away." His years of arguing were over. What he needed was not one more answer but a Saviour, and he took him.

At Easter 387 he was baptized by Ambrose, together with his friend Alypius and his son Adeodatus, then not quite fifteen. A few months later, on the way home to Africa, Monica died at the port of Ostia, content that she had seen what she had prayed for. Adeodatus died a couple of years later, still in his teens. Back in Africa, Augustine was pressed, in tears, into becoming a priest in 391 in the port city of Hippo, and a few years later its bishop. He served there for about thirty-five years, preaching, judging disputes, writing letters and books that shaped Christian thinking for centuries, including the *Confessions* and *The City of God*. He died on 28 August 430, aged seventy-five, while an invading army besieged the city.

He had spent his teenage years asking where evil comes from, and his answer, in the end, was not an argument but a confession: it comes from me, and only grace can heal it. The questions were real, and he never stopped asking them. But he found that they came to rest in a person. He put it on the very first page of his book: "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee."

### Think It Through

1. Augustine says that alone he would never have stolen the pears. Why do people do things in a group that they would never do on their own? Have you seen it happen?
2. The Manichees attracted Augustine partly by asking good questions, and partly by giving him answers that let him off the hook. How can you tell the difference between an answer that is true and an answer that is merely comfortable?
3. A pagan book written for a school assignment first turned Augustine's heart towards God, but he was too proud to read the Bible as a beginner. What would it look like for a clever person to become "a little one" without switching off their mind?
4. Monica prayed for her son for years while nothing seemed to change. What do you think kept her going, and what does her story say to someone praying for a friend who wants nothing to do with God?

## Blaise Pascal: The Boy Who Wanted Reasons

A boy of about twelve is alone in the room in Paris where he is allowed to play. He has a piece of charcoal in his hand, and he is drawing on the floor tiles.

He is not drawing pictures. He is drawing shapes: circles, triangles, straight lines, and then more lines between them. He is trying to work out how to make a circle that is perfectly round, a triangle whose sides and angles are all equal, and how one shape relates to another. He does not know the proper names for any of these things, because nobody has ever taught him. So he has made up his own. A circle he calls a "round". A line he calls a "bar". From these he has built his own definitions, then his own rules, and from the rules his own proofs, each one leading to the next.

He is so absorbed that he does not hear the door open. His father walks in and stands behind him for a long while before the boy notices.

The boy's name is Blaise Pascal, and he is not supposed to be doing this. His father has strictly forbidden him to study mathematics, has hidden all the books on it, and has told him not even to think about it. Both of them are startled. When his father asks what he is doing, Blaise explains that he is looking for a way to prove a particular thing. His father realises, with a shock, that it is the thirty-second proposition of the first book of Euclid, the famous ancient textbook of geometry: the proof that the three angles of any triangle add up to two right angles. When his father asks how he got there, the boy explains the step before it, and the step before that, all the way back to his "rounds" and "bars".

His father leaves the room without a word. He goes straight to the house of a friend, a learned man named Le Pailleur, and stands there so stunned that the friend, seeing tears in his eyes, is alarmed. "I am not weeping for sorrow, but for joy," his father tells him.

We know this story because Blaise's older sister, Gilberte, wrote it down in a short life of her brother after he died. Some historians think it may have grown a little in the telling; a big sister proud of a famous brother is not the most neutral witness. But no one doubts the heart of it. Blaise Pascal had a mind that would not stop asking why, and would not be satisfied with anything less than a real answer.

That mind would one day make him famous across Europe. It would also bring him, by a long road, to a night when he found that the most important answers were not the kind you can prove on a floor in charcoal.

### A Father's Plan

Blaise was born on 19 June 1623 in Clermont, a town in the volcanic hills of the Auvergne in central France. His father, Étienne Pascal, was a senior royal official in the tax courts and a gifted amateur mathematician. His mother, Antoinette Begon, died when Blaise was three. He had two sisters: Gilberte, three years older, and Jacqueline, two years younger, who was nearly as brilliant as he was.

Étienne never remarried. Instead, he decided to educate his son himself. In 1631, when Blaise was eight, he moved the family to Paris so that he could give himself to the task. Blaise never went to school or college. Everything he learned came from his father, from books, and from his own restless head.

Étienne's plan was careful. He did not want to overload the boy, so Latin would wait until Blaise was twelve. Meanwhile, he talked with him about everything: how languages work, why grammar has rules, the strange effects of nature, like gunpowder. And here, according to Gilberte, the boy showed what kind of mind he had. She wrote that "he wanted to know the reason for everything." When his father did not know, or gave the usual answers that were really just ways of avoiding the question, Blaise was not satisfied. He went looking for himself, and would not let go until he found a reason that held. In all things, she said, "truth was the sole object of his mind."

Once, at dinner, someone struck an earthenware dish with a knife. It rang loudly, and then went silent the moment a hand was laid on it. Most people would not have noticed. Blaise wanted to know why. He began experimenting with sounds, and at about eleven or twelve he wrote a short treatise about them.

Mathematics was the one thing his father kept from him, because he feared it would swallow everything else. Blaise kept begging. His father told him only that geometry was the way of making figures correctly and finding the proportions between them, and then forbade him to think about it. It was a bit like telling a hungry person what food is and then locking the kitchen. That is how the charcoal came out.

### Among the Mathematicians

After the day on the playroom floor, Étienne gave up. He handed his son a copy of Euclid's *Elements* to read in his free time. Blaise worked through it, Gilberte says, without needing anyone to explain it.

Soon he was going with his father to a weekly gathering of the best scientific minds in Paris, which met around a friar named Marin Mersenne. Mathematicians and scientists brought their latest work there to be examined, and problems arrived by letter from Italy, Germany and further afield. In that room of grown men, the teenage Blaise held his own. He brought new work of his own, and sometimes spotted mistakes that the others had missed.

Picture what that must have been like. Most people your age are not asked for their opinion by the leading experts of their country. Blaise was. It is easy to see how a young person in that position might become proud, and it would be a long time before he understood how proud he was.

At sixteen he wrote an essay on conic sections, the curves you get when you slice through a cone at different angles, such as circles, ellipses and parabolas. A short summary was printed in 1640, and one of its results is still taught as "Pascal's theorem". The older mathematicians were astonished. Gilberte says some of them said nothing so powerful had been seen since Archimedes, the greatest mathematician of the ancient world. That may be a sister's pride again, but the essay is real, and it was genuinely new.

All this time he was still doing his Latin and Greek, and talking with his father about logic and physics over meals.

And what about God? This is the surprising part. In a book about young people who wrestled with doubt, Blaise Pascal's teenage years contain very little of it. Gilberte says he never went through a phase of mocking religion, as many clever young men of his day did. His father had given him a rule: whatever is an object of faith cannot be an object of reason. Faith was one room; science was another; and the boy who demanded reasons for everything in the second room did not ask many questions in the first. He went to Mass, believed what the Church taught, and gave his mind to mathematics.

It was a respectful faith, and a sincere one. But it was kept in a box. Blaise had not yet asked whether the God he believed in had any claim on the rest of his life: on his ambition, his pride, his brilliant, hungry mind. That question was still waiting for him.

### Trouble and a Machine

When Blaise was about fifteen, the family's comfortable life was suddenly shaken. In 1638 the government of Cardinal Richelieu, the most powerful man in France, cut the interest it paid on government bonds, where Étienne had invested much of his money. Étienne joined a protest, and when the authorities began arresting protesters, he went into hiding. For a time the children were left in Paris without him.

The family's rescue came from Jacqueline, then about thirteen. She was a talented young poet and performer, and in 1639 she acted in a children's play in front of Richelieu himself. Afterwards she recited verses asking him to pardon her father. The cardinal was charmed, and he did. More than that, he gave Étienne a new job: overseeing the collection of taxes in Normandy, based in the city of Rouen. Around the start of 1640, the family moved there.

Tax collection meant endless sums, done by hand with pen and counters, often late into the night. Blaise, now about eighteen, watched his father labouring over columns of figures and asked another question: could a machine do this?

He set out to build one. It was a box of gears and wheels in which numbers were dialled in and the machine carried the tens by itself, so that it could add and subtract without its user needing to know any arithmetic. Gilberte called it "something new in nature": a science that had lived only in the mind, now done by a machine. The idea came to him easily. The hard part, she says, was making the craftsmen understand what he wanted. It took him around two years to get it working, and he went on improving it for years afterwards, through dozens of versions. A handful of his calculating machines still survive in museums today.

But that labour came at a cost. From around this time, his health began to break down: headaches, stomach pain, and later times when he could barely walk. He told his family that "since the age of eighteen he had not passed a day without pain." For the rest of his life, his brilliant mind would have to work inside a body that was always hurting.

### The Winter of the Broken Hip

The question that had been waiting for him arrived in January 1646, in an unlikely way. Étienne slipped on the ice in Rouen and badly injured his hip. Two brothers from a local noble family, the Deschamps brothers, who were skilled at setting bones, came to treat him, and stayed in the house for about three months while he recovered.

They were devout men, part of a renewal movement in the French Catholic Church centred on the convent of Port-Royal near Paris. Its enemies later called it "Jansenism". The movement taught, much as Augustine had taught twelve centuries before, that human beings are helpless to save themselves and depend completely on the grace of God, and it called Christians to a serious, wholehearted life rather than a respectable Sunday religion. The brothers had brought books, and they talked about them. The Pascals began to read.

For Blaise, who turned twenty-three that summer, it was like a door opening in a wall he had not known was there. For the first time he saw that Christianity was not one room among many, but a claim on the whole house. Gilberte describes it this way: he understood "that the Christian religion obliges us to live for God alone and to have no other object but him." He began to turn away from what she called his "researches". Soon the whole family had been drawn in. Even Étienne, the father who had taught him everything, began to follow his son's lead.

Historians often call this Pascal's "first conversion", because something even deeper was to happen eight years later. But it was real. A young man whose whole identity rested on his mind had been confronted with the possibility that his mind was not the most important thing about him.

### Certain and Young

It would be nice to say that Blaise immediately became humble and gentle. He did not. Like many people who have just discovered something important, he became very sure of himself.

In 1647, not long after his awakening, he and two friends went to hear a man in Rouen who was teaching a new philosophy. When the man drew conclusions about Christian doctrine that they believed were false, the three young men warned him, then reported him to the church authorities, and kept pressing until he was made to withdraw his views. Gilberte tells the story with approval. Readers today may feel less comfortable. Pascal was right to care about truth, but there is something hard about three clever young men hunting down an older one's mistakes. Zeal and humility do not always arrive at the same time.

Nor did he give up science as completely as his sister suggests. Those same years, 1646 to 1648, were the years of his most famous experiments. An Italian scientist, Torricelli, had shown that a glass tube of mercury, turned upside down in a dish, leaves an empty space at the top. Almost everyone at the time believed, following the ancient philosopher Aristotle, that "nature abhors a vacuum" and that truly empty space could not exist. Pascal tested the idea again and again, with tubes of water and wine and mercury, and then in 1648 had his brother-in-law, Florin Périer, carry a tube of mercury up the Puy de Dôme, a mountain near Clermont. As Périer climbed, the mercury fell. The weight of the air was pressing it up, and there was less air above it at the top. The ancient idea had to go.

That is the young Pascal in a single picture: a man who would not accept an idea, however ancient and respected, just because everyone believed it. He wanted to test it. Later, writing about faith, he would turn that same honesty on reason itself. "We must know where to doubt, where to feel certain, where to submit," he wrote. A person who doubts everything, he thought, is as foolish as a person who doubts nothing. The skill is knowing which is which.

### What Came After

In 1651 Étienne died. Jacqueline, who had longed for years to join Port-Royal, entered the convent soon afterwards. Blaise, at first, opposed her, partly from loneliness and partly over money, and it caused real pain between them. His doctors told him to rest and amuse himself, and he did. For about three years he moved in fashionable Paris society, made friends with a young duke, and studied games of chance; out of that came his work with Pierre de Fermat that founded the mathematics of probability. Later he looked back on these years as a time when he had drifted from God. They also left him empty, and by 1654 he was visiting Jacqueline in her convent and telling her so.

On the night of Monday 23 November 1654, when he was thirty-one, something happened to him alone in his room, from about half past ten until about half past midnight. He never spoke of it. After he died, a servant found a piece of parchment sewn into the lining of his coat, with a paper inside it, both in his handwriting. He had carried it with him for eight years, sewing it into each new coat. It is known as the *Memorial*. It is not in our library, and it was written in French, but in one English translation it begins with a single word in capitals, "FIRE", and then: "God of Abraham, God of Isaac, God of Jacob, not of the philosophers and the learned." Further down come the words "Joy, joy, joy, tears of joy." The Memorial also copies out Jesus' prayer from John's Gospel, which a modern Bible renders, "Now this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom You have sent" (John 17:3).

That was the answer to the question his teenage years had left unasked. The God he had believed in, at a respectful distance, was not a conclusion to be reached by clever people, like a theorem on a floor. He was a living person who could be met, and he was known through Jesus Christ. "We know God only by Jesus Christ," Pascal later wrote.

The rest of his life was short, and full. He used his brilliance in *The Provincial Letters* (1656–57), a series of witty anonymous letters defending his friends at Port-Royal and attacking what he saw as a dishonest, easy-going morality. He began a great book to persuade doubters and the indifferent that Christianity was true, starting not with proofs but with the puzzle of human beings: so great and so wretched, so clever and so lost. He never finished it. His illnesses grew worse; he gave away much of what he had to the poor; and he died in Paris on 19 August 1662, aged thirty-nine. Gilberte says almost his last words were, "May God never forsake me!" His friends gathered the scraps of his unfinished book and published them as the *Pensées*, or "Thoughts". They have been read by doubters ever since.

The boy who wanted reasons for everything never stopped wanting them. He simply discovered that reason itself has limits, and that the last thing a really honest mind does is to admit it. "The last proceeding of reason," he wrote, "is to recognise that there is an infinity of things which are beyond it."

2. As a teenager, Pascal kept faith and reason in separate rooms. What are the advantages and dangers of doing that? Do you tend to do it?
3. Pascal was right to care about truth, but the way he and his friends pursued the teacher in Rouen seems hard. How can someone hold strong convictions without becoming a hunter of other people's mistakes?
4. "We must know where to doubt, where to feel certain, where to submit." What is one thing you think it is right to doubt, one thing you feel certain of, and one thing you might need to submit to?

## Isaac Watts: Something Better to Sing

In the winter of 1688, England turned upside down. On 5 November a Dutch prince, William of Orange, landed with an army on the coast of Devon. Within weeks the Catholic king, James II, had lost his nerve and his friends, and before the year was out he had fled to France. Bells rang. Bonfires were lit. In towns all along the south coast of England, people argued in the streets about what would happen next.

In the port town of Southampton, a very clever boy of fourteen was having an upheaval of his own. His name was Isaac Watts. Years later, in a private list he kept of the chief events of his life, he set down what happened to him that year in a single line. In 1688, he wrote, he "fell under considerable convictions of sin."

That is all he tells us. He does not say what he had done, or what he was reading, or who he was listening to. He describes no sermon and no scene. The next year's entry is just as short. In 1689, when he turned fifteen, he "was taught to trust in Christ, I hope."

Read that line again, slowly. Not "I was saved." Not "I gave my life to God on such-and-such a night." *I was taught to trust in Christ, I hope.* Someone taught him. What he was taught was to trust, not to achieve. And he finished with two small words that some would call a lack of faith, and others would recognise as the voice of a thinking person who knew how easily the human heart fools itself.

This is a chapter about a boy who asked questions: about his family's suffering, about whether his own faith was real, and about why the church sang what it sang. His answers are still being sung all over the world.

### Born Beside a Prison

To understand the fourteen-year-old, you have to know the world he was born into.

Isaac Watts was born in Southampton on 17 July 1674, the eldest of what became a large family. His father, also called Isaac, was a clothier, a dealer in cloth, and a leading member, by most accounts a deacon, of the town's Independent church. The family were what the English called Dissenters or Nonconformists: Protestants who would not "conform" to the Church of England, its prayer book and its bishops, because they believed a church should be a gathered company of believers ordering its worship by the Bible alone.

In the 1670s and 1680s that belief was against the law. Dissenters could be fined for holding their own services, banned from living near the towns where they had been ministers, and thrown into gaol. Isaac's father was imprisoned for his convictions more than once, including in the year his son was born. There is a family story, told by the earliest writers about Watts, that while his father was in prison, his mother used to sit on a stone near the gaol door with her baby in her arms. Nobody can now prove it, and stories like that can grow in the telling. But the world it describes is real. The boy was born into a church that met in secret, in fear of the knock on the door.

When Isaac was nine, in 1683, his father was locked up again, for about six months. According to Isaac's own notes, after his release he had to leave his family and live away from home, in hiding, for about two years. Think about that from the point of view of a boy of nine or ten. Your father is gone, not because he has done anything wicked, but because of the way he worships God.

And in 1685, when Isaac was eleven, things grew darker still. A rebellion against the new king broke out in the West Country and was crushed. The judge sent to punish the rebels, George Jeffreys, became infamous for the number of people he sentenced to death in what were called the Bloody Assizes. At Winchester, about twelve miles from Southampton, an elderly widow named Alice Lisle, who had sheltered two men fleeing the battle, was condemned and beheaded in the town's market place. For a Dissenting boy in Southampton, that was not history. It was the news.

It would be natural for a boy like that to ask: is it worth it? Would it not be easier to go to the parish church like everyone else and keep quiet? We do not know if Isaac asked that out loud. We do know what he decided, because a few years later he had to decide it for himself.

### The Cleverest Boy in Southampton

Whatever was happening at home, at school Isaac was astonishing.

From the age of about six he went to the free grammar school in Southampton, where the master was a Church of England clergyman named John Pinhorne. By the usual account, which comes from Watts's own notes as his early biographers reported them, Isaac had begun Latin with his father at four, Greek at about nine, French at about eleven, and Hebrew at about thirteen. Years later, as a grown man, he wrote a poem in Latin in honour of Pinhorne. A Dissenter's son praising a Church of England schoolmaster may not sound like much, but in that bitterly divided town it says something good about both of them.

He was also a rhymer. One early piece has come down with his name on it: an acrostic, a poem in which the first letters of the lines spell out a name, said to have been written when he was a small child, with the lines beginning with the letters of ISAAC WATTS. Its first line is "I am a vile polluted lump of earth." We cannot be certain how old he was when he wrote it, or whether it is entirely his. But if it is, this was a boy who, very young, was already turning his questions about himself and God into verse.

### Convictions of Sin

Now we can come back to 1688, and the line in his notebook.

What does it mean to fall under "convictions of sin"? It is an old phrase, but the experience is not old-fashioned. It means that the things you have always heard about God stop being things you know and become things you feel. You do not just know that God is holy; you realise that he is holy and you are not. You do not just know that sin is wrong; you begin to see your own pride, selfishness, lying and coldness, and you find you cannot shrug them off. For some people it comes with terror; for others, as a slow weight that will not lift.

Isaac does not tell us which it was for him, or whether a sermon started it, or a book, or nothing in particular. He had grown up with Bible teaching his whole life. He knew the right answers. Like many young people who grow up in church, he may have discovered at fourteen that knowing the answers is not the same as having peace with God.

We can see something of how he thought about it from the little songs he wrote for children many years later, which you can read for yourself on Ochorus. In one of them, called "Seeing God," a child prays, "Lord, at thy feet ashamed I lie, / Upward I dare not look; / Pardon my sins before I die, / And blot them from thy book." That was written by a grown man in 1715, not by a fourteen-year-old. But a man does not usually write like that about something he has never felt.

### "I Hope"

Then came 1689. That year the whole country changed again. Under the new king and queen, William and Mary, Parliament passed a law that came to be called the Toleration Act. For the first time, Dissenters could meet openly for worship without being fined or imprisoned, as long as their meeting houses were registered. The knock on the door was over. Isaac's father, who had paid for his convictions with years of his life, could now worship freely in his own town.

And that same year, his son "was taught to trust in Christ, I hope."

Who taught him? Isaac does not say. Perhaps it was the minister of the Southampton church. Perhaps his father. Perhaps it was a book. What we do know is what he came to believe, because he spent the rest of his life putting it into words that ordinary people could sing. He believed that he could never make himself right with God by being good enough, clever enough or religious enough. He believed that Jesus Christ, the eternal Son of God, had taken human flesh and died on the cross for sinners, bearing the punishment they deserved, and had risen again. And he believed that the way a guilty person is forgiven is simply by trusting Christ: leaning the whole weight of your soul on what he has done, instead of on what you have done.

In another of his children's songs, "Praise to God for our Redemption," he put it like this: "He honour'd all his Father's laws, / Which we have disobey'd; / He bore our sins upon the cross, / And our full ransom paid."

So why the "I hope"?

Some readers find it unsettling. Should a Christian not be sure? But listen to it again. Isaac is not saying, "I hope Christ is able to save." He is saying, in effect, "I hope that what happened to me was the real thing." He was a teenager who knew how good he was at words, how easily feelings come and go, and how possible it is to fool yourself about your own heart. He did not want to claim more than he could see. His confidence was in Christ; his caution was about himself. That is not the opposite of faith. It is what faith looks like in an honest mind.

That is freeing for anyone who has worried that their own conversion was not dramatic enough to count. As far as we can tell, Isaac's had no thunderclap. It had a boy, a gradual weight of guilt, a slow learning to trust, and an honest "I hope." Out of that quiet beginning came one of the most confident voices in the history of Christian worship.

### A Door He Would Not Walk Through

At about sixteen, Isaac faced a real choice.

His brilliance at school had been noticed by people with money. A Southampton physician, Dr John Speed, and others are said to have offered to pay for him to go to university. In England that meant Oxford or Cambridge, and the road to a comfortable career. There was one condition, and it was not written by Dr Speed but by the law: to study at the universities, a young man had to conform to the Church of England.

Isaac turned the offer down. The writer Samuel Johnson, who wrote a short life of Watts later in the eighteenth century and was no friend of Dissenters, recorded simply that the young man declared his resolution to take his lot with the Dissenters.

Think about what that cost. The Toleration Act had made Dissenters safe, but it had not made them equal. They still could not take degrees at Oxford or Cambridge, or hold many public offices. To stay a Dissenter was to accept, at sixteen, that some doors would always be shut. We do not know exactly how he reasoned it through. But having seen what his father had suffered for his conscience, he was not going to trade his own conscience for a degree.

So in 1690 he went instead to a Dissenting academy at Stoke Newington, then a village just north of London, run by an Independent minister named Thomas Rowe. These academies were the Dissenters' answer to being shut out of the universities. Isaac threw himself into study so hard, by most accounts, that he damaged his health, which was never good. He read philosophy, logic, history, languages and theology. He filled notebooks. He wrote Latin verse. By his own notes, in 1693, when he was eighteen, he joined Thomas Rowe's church as a member, which meant making a public profession of his faith. The "I hope" of fifteen was becoming something he was willing to stand up and own.

### Something Better to Sing

In 1694, at twenty, Isaac came home to Southampton for about two years. It was then, according to a tradition told ever since, that the most famous thing he ever did began with a complaint.

In the Independent chapel at Above Bar in Southampton, as in most Dissenting churches of the time, the congregation sang only the psalms of the Old Testament, turned into rhyming verse so that they could be sung to a handful of tunes. Many of these versions were clumsy, and the way they were sung was often dreary: often a clerk would read out a line, the congregation would drag it back to him, and then he would read out the next. The story goes that Isaac grumbled to his father about how dull the singing was, and that his father, perhaps tired of hearing it, told him that if he could do better, he should. And so, the story says, Isaac went away and wrote a hymn, and the church sang it, and liked it, and asked for another.

We should be careful here. This story is a tradition, passed down in the family and among Watts's admirers, not something he wrote down himself, and the details vary from teller to teller. It may have been tidied up over the years, as good stories usually are. But two things in it are certainly true. The hymn usually named as his first, "Behold the glories of the Lamb," is built on Revelation 5, where heaven sings to the Lamb who was slain. And Watts really was troubled by the singing of the churches, and he said so in print.

His question went deeper than "This is boring." When his first great collection, *Hymns and Spiritual Songs*, came out in 1707, he explained in its preface that he had watched whole congregations singing with dull, thoughtless faces, and wondered how much the words meant to them. Part of the problem, he believed, was the words themselves. The psalms were God's word, and he loved them. But Christians were singing them exactly as an Israelite would have sung them, centuries before Jesus came, as though the cross and the empty tomb had never happened. His question, in effect, was this: why should the church pray to Christ, preach Christ and read about Christ, but only sing as if it were still waiting for him?

Plenty of older Christians thought that question dangerous. To them, singing words written by a mere human being in church, instead of the inspired words of the Bible, was close to arrogance. Watts did not throw the psalms away. He did two things. He wrote hymns of his own, drawn from the whole of Scripture, in which a Christian could speak to God in his or her own voice. And later he rewrote the psalms themselves as a Christian who had seen the cross might sing them.

In Revelation 5:9, the scene behind that first hymn, the living creatures and elders around the throne "sang a new song: 'Worthy are You to take the scroll and open its seals, because You were slain, and by Your blood You purchased for God those from every tribe and tongue and people and nation.'" That was what Watts wanted: a new song about the Lamb who was slain. Not because the old songs were wrong, but because the church now knew how the story turned out.

### What Came After

In 1696 Watts went back to Stoke Newington as tutor to the son of Sir John Hartopp, a leading Dissenter. He preached his first sermon on his twenty-fourth birthday, in 1698. In 1702 he became pastor of the Independent church at Mark Lane in London, one of the most respected Dissenting congregations in the city, and he stayed its pastor, as it moved to new buildings, for the rest of his life.

In 1707 he published *Hymns and Spiritual Songs*. Among them was a hymn written for the Lord's Supper, headed "Crucifixion to the World by the Cross of Christ," based on Galatians 6:14. It began, "When I survey the wondrous cross." Its second line first read "Where the young Prince of Glory died"; a few years later he changed it to the line most people sing today, "On which the Prince of glory died." It ends with a Christian standing before the cross and answering in his own words: "Love so amazing, so divine, / Demands my soul, my life, my all." In 1719 came *The Psalms of David Imitated in the Language of the New Testament*, in which Psalm 90 became "Our God, our help in ages past" and Psalm 98 became "Joy to the World," which Watts wrote about Christ's reign over the earth, not about Christmas at all.

His health broke down badly in 1712, and for about four years he could hardly preach. Sir Thomas Abney, a former Lord Mayor of London, and his wife invited him to stay at their house in Hertfordshire to recover. He came intending to stay a week, and stayed with the family for the rest of his life, about thirty-six years. He never married. There he wrote *Divine Songs for Children* (1715) and a long line of other books, including a *Logick* (1725), an introduction to clear thinking that was used for generations, even at the universities whose doors had been closed to him as a Dissenter. The boy who asked hard questions became the man who taught others how to reason.

He kept asking questions as an old man, and not all of them came out neatly. For years he struggled to find a way to explain the doctrine of the Trinity, one God in three persons, that satisfied both his reason and his Bible, and some of his ideas troubled his friends. Near the end of his life he wrote a prayer, published after his death, in which he brought those hard questions to God himself, on his knees. It is a good picture of a thinking believer: not a man who stopped asking, but a man who learned to do his asking in the presence of God rather than away from him.

He died at Stoke Newington on 25 November 1748, aged seventy-four. Friends remembered him saying in his last years that he believed the promises of God enough to venture an eternity on them. He is usually credited with about seven hundred and fifty hymns and psalm versions, and people all over the world still sing them, often without knowing whose they are.

### Think It Through

1. Isaac described his conversion in two short lines, ending with "I hope." Is that a sign of weak faith or of honesty? Can a person be sure of Christ and still be careful about claiming too much for themselves?
2. At sixteen, Isaac turned down a university place because it would have meant going against his conscience. What doors might you be tempted to walk through by quietly giving up something you believe?
3. Watts questioned the way his church sang, and some older Christians thought he was arrogant. When is it right for a young person to question how things have always been done in church, and how can they do it well?
4. Watts's faith began quietly, with no dramatic scene. Why do you think so many people feel their own story "doesn't count" unless it is dramatic?

## Jonathan Edwards: The Doctrine He Hated

One day, probably in 1721, a seventeen-year-old student in New England was reading his Bible. He had read it all his life. He was the son of a minister and the grandson of a famous one. He could read the Bible in Latin, Greek and Hebrew. He was, by any measure, one of the cleverest young men in the American colonies. And he had come to these particular words in Paul's first letter to Timothy many, many times before.

They were a single sentence of praise, which in the King James Version he used read: "Now unto the King eternal, immortal, invisible, the only wise God, be honour and glory for ever and ever. Amen" (1 Timothy 1:17).

This time, something happened. Years later, he tried to describe it as carefully as he could. "As I read the words," he wrote, "there came into my soul, and was as it were diffused through it, a sense of the glory of the Divine Being; a new sense, quite different from any thing I ever experienced before." He said that no words of Scripture had ever seemed to him the way those words did then. He thought to himself how excellent a Being God was, and how happy he would be if he might enjoy that God, and be with him for ever. He kept repeating the words, and sort of singing them over to himself, and he went to pray, in a way he had never prayed before.

He did not know, at first, what had happened to him. He did not think, "This is my conversion." He thought it was a lovely experience, and wondered at it. Only later did he see that it was the first taste of something he would spend his whole life trying to describe: the difference between knowing about God and seeing that God is beautiful.

The student's name was Jonathan Edwards. And the reason that moment mattered so much is that, until then, there was one thing about God he had found almost impossible to accept.

### A House Full of Daughters

Jonathan Edwards was born on 5 October 1703 in East Windsor, a farming parish on the Connecticut River in New England. New England was then a set of British colonies on the east coast of North America, first settled about eighty years earlier by English Puritans who wanted to build communities ordered by the Bible. His father, Timothy Edwards, was the minister of East Windsor. His mother, Esther, was the daughter of Solomon Stoddard, the powerful minister of Northampton, Massachusetts, a man so influential in the Connecticut Valley that people half-jokingly called him its pope.

Jonathan was the fifth of eleven children and the only boy. He grew up with ten sisters. His father, who prepared boys for college to earn extra money, taught him Latin early, and he learned fast.

He was also, from very young, a boy who looked hard at things. As a teenager he wrote careful notes about the "flying" spiders of the New England woods, which float through the air on long silk threads, working out how they did it. A story often told says he wrote this at eleven or twelve. Modern scholars think his notes on spiders and insects more likely come from his later teens, and the polished letter he wrote about them from when he was about twenty. Either way, it shows the kind of mind he had. He did not just accept that spiders flew. He wanted to know how, and he watched them until he found out. And what struck him most, in the end, was not the mechanics but the generosity of a Creator who gave even spiders pleasure.

### Religious, but Not Changed

Jonathan grew up religious. That is not the same as saying he grew up a Christian, and later he was careful to say the difference.

When he was a boy, "some years before I went to college," as he put it, there was a time of religious awakening in his father's church. Many people became serious about God, and so did young Jonathan. He prayed five times a day in secret. He talked about religion with other boys. He and some of his schoolmates built a booth, a little shelter, in a swamp in a hidden place, and went there to pray. He felt moved and delighted. He was, for a while, a very religious child.

And then it wore off. The feelings faded. He stopped praying in secret. He went back to his old ways, which he later described with a blunt old Bible phrase, saying that he returned "like a dog to his vomit" (the image comes from Proverbs 26:11). Looking back, he believed that those childhood feelings, however sincere, had not been the real thing. He had enjoyed religion. He had not yet known God.

This is worth stopping on, because it is the experience of a great many people who grow up in Christian homes. You can have strong religious feelings at a camp, or at a moving service, and then find them gone a few weeks later. Edwards did not conclude that his childhood feelings had been fake, exactly. He concluded that feelings are not enough. Something deeper has to change.

### Yale at Twelve

In September 1716, a month before his thirteenth birthday, Jonathan went to college. It was the young Collegiate School of Connecticut, which in 1718 was named Yale College after a wealthy donor. At the time it was small, poor and divided: for part of Jonathan's time there, quarrels about where the college should be located meant that some students, including him, studied at Wethersfield, near his home, instead of at New Haven. He moved to New Haven in 1719.

Imagine being twelve and living among young men of sixteen and seventeen, studying logic, Latin, Greek, Hebrew, mathematics and natural philosophy, which is what people then called science. Jonathan was not just keeping up. He was racing ahead.

His first biographer, Samuel Hopkins, who knew him well later in life, said that when Edwards was about fourteen he read John Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, a new and difficult book by an English philosopher about how the human mind knows things, and enjoyed it more than a greedy miser enjoys gathering up handfuls of silver and gold. Some modern scholars think he may have read it a little later than that. But whatever his exact age, he plainly loved hard questions. In his college years and just after, he began notebooks with titles like "Of Being" and "The Mind," where a teenager worked through questions that philosophers still argue about: What does it mean for something to exist? What is space? How do we know that anything is real?

He graduated in 1720, top of his class, at sixteen, and gave the valedictory speech, in Latin. Then he stayed on at New Haven for two more years to study theology.

### The Question He Could Not Swallow

So here was a brilliant teenager who loved questions. And there was one question that kept snagging.

Edwards had been brought up in the Reformed, or Calvinist, tradition of the Puritans. One of its central teachings is the sovereignty of God: that God rules over everything, including salvation, and that it is God, not human beings, who decides whom he will save. Many Christians believe it in some form, and many find it hard. Edwards found it very hard indeed.

He described his objection with startling honesty. "From my childhood up," he wrote, "my mind had been full of objections against the doctrine of God's sovereignty," in choosing whom he would to eternal life and leaving others to perish. "It used to appear like a horrible doctrine to me."

It is important to see what kind of question this was. It was not lazy doubt, or doubt as an excuse to do as he liked. It was the honest protest of a mind that wanted God to be fair, and could not see how this was fair. It is the same protest Paul imagines someone making in Romans 9, where he anticipates the question: if God chooses, why does he still find fault with us? Edwards was not the first young believer to struggle with that chapter, and he will not be the last.

What is striking is how he told the story of what happened next. He did not say he found a brilliant argument that knocked down his objections. He said that there came a time when he seemed to be convinced and fully satisfied about it, but that he could never give an account of how, or by what means, he was convinced. He was a person who could argue about anything, and he admitted that in this case it was not argument that settled him. His reason was engaged, and stayed engaged. But something else changed too.

### The Pleurisy and the Struggle

Before that change came, there was a hard stretch.

Towards the latter part of his time at college, Edwards fell seriously ill with pleurisy, a painful inflammation of the lining of the lungs, which in those days could easily kill. He wrote that God "brought me nigh to the grave, and shook me over the pit of hell." He was frightened into seriousness. And then, as soon as he got better, he slipped back into his old ways again.

But he could not settle. He described "great and violent inward struggles," and making vows and resolutions to God, and breaking off from things he knew were wrong. He became very religious again, seeking salvation with great effort. Yet he said afterwards that he sought it "in a miserable manner." He was trying harder, but he was still doing it all himself, out of fear. He was running hard in a circle.

It is the kind of honesty that makes Edwards worth reading. He did not pretend that his teenage years were a straight line from doubt to faith. They were a tangle of illness and fear, effort and relapse, sharp questions and stubborn objections. If your own faith has looked like that, you are in good company.

### A New Sense

And then came the day with 1 Timothy 1:17.

What changed? Not his information. He knew exactly what the verse said, and had known for years. He could have explained its grammar in Greek. What changed was what he would later call a "sense of the heart." For the first time, God did not just seem true or powerful or frightening. He seemed glorious, lovely, someone to be enjoyed.

Soon afterwards, Edwards talked with his father about what had been happening to him, and was much affected by the conversation. Then he went out walking alone in his father's pasture, looking up at the sky and the clouds. As he walked, he said, there came into his mind such a sweet sense of the glorious majesty and grace of God that he did not know how to express it. It seemed to him that God's majesty and his gentleness belonged together: an awesome sweetness, a high and great and holy gentleness.

After that, he said, everything looked different. He saw something of God's glory in the sun and moon and stars, in the clouds and the blue sky, in the grass and flowers and trees. He used to sit and look at the moon for a long time. He had always been terrified of thunderstorms. Now, he said, a thunderstorm made him glad, because it made him think of the majestic voice of God. He would walk out in the woods and sing his thoughts.

And the doctrine he had hated? This is the most surprising part. It did not simply become bearable. Looking back, he wrote that the sovereignty of God had very often appeared to him "exceeding pleasant, bright, and sweet." A God who is truly God, who does not need us and owes us nothing, and who nevertheless gives himself freely to sinners, had become the God he wanted.

What did Edwards actually come to believe about Jesus? He soon found himself reading the Song of Solomon and applying its words to Christ, especially "I am the rose of Sharon, and the lily of the valleys" (Song of Solomon 2:1, in the King James wording he read). He longed, he said, to be alone with Christ and to be "swallowed up" in him. He had come to see Jesus not as a rule-giver he had to satisfy, but as the beautiful Saviour, full of grace, in whom God's glory shines. Years later he would preach that sinners are justified, made right with God, by faith in Christ alone, not by anything good in themselves. The young man who had been running in circles trying to save himself had found that salvation was a gift, and that the Giver was lovely.

### Seventy Resolutions

In August 1722, aged eighteen, Edwards went to New York City, then a small port town, to preach for about eight months to a little Presbyterian congregation. He lodged with a family there and often walked out alone along the banks of the Hudson River to pray and think. And that winter, at nineteen, he began to write what became the most famous thing he did as a young man: his Resolutions.

There were seventy in the end, written between late 1722 and August 1723. He meant to read them over once a week, and for years he did. Some are about time and death: "Resolved, never to lose one moment of time; but improve it the most profitable way I possibly can," and "Resolved, to live with all my might, while I do live." One is a question he resolved to ask himself before doing anything: whether he would be afraid to do it if it were the last hour of his life. One shows exactly the kind of mind he had: he resolved that whenever he thought of a hard question in theology, he would immediately do what he could towards solving it, if nothing prevented him. For Edwards, faith and thinking were not enemies. A hard question about God was not something to run away from. It was something to work on, prayerfully, until it gave up its light.

Read on their own, the Resolutions can sound like a crushing self-improvement plan. But Edwards wrote a short sentence at the top before he wrote a single "Resolved," and it changes everything. In it he says that he knows he is unable to do anything without God's help, and he asks God, by his grace, to enable him to keep these resolutions, as far as they agree with God's will, for Christ's sake. They were not a ladder to climb up to God. They were the plans of a young man who had already been found by God, and who knew he could not keep a single one of them on his own.

He also kept a diary in those years, and it is full of failures: days of dullness, times of feeling far from God, disappointment with himself. The Resolutions did not make him perfect. They gave him something to come back to.

### What Came After

In 1723 Edwards met Sarah Pierpont of New Haven, and wrote a famous short piece about her as a young woman who seemed to be always full of joy, and who walked in the fields as though someone invisible were always with her. They married in 1727 and had eleven children. That same year he became assistant to his grandfather Stoddard at Northampton, and on Stoddard's death in 1729 he became its minister.

In 1734 and 1735 Northampton was shaken by a revival, and a few years later Edwards was at the centre of the Great Awakening that swept the American colonies. In 1741 he preached "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God," the sermon he is most remembered for, reading it in a level voice from his notes. He spent years thinking hard about which religious experiences were real and which were counterfeit, and wrote *A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections* (1746) to explain the difference. In 1750 his own congregation, after a bitter dispute over who should be admitted to the Lord's Supper, voted to dismiss him. He went to Stockbridge, a frontier mission, to serve a small English congregation and the Mahican and Mohawk people, and there wrote some of his greatest books, including *Freedom of the Will*. In 1758 he became president of the College of New Jersey, later Princeton. Smallpox was in the town; he was inoculated against it, the inoculation went wrong, and he died on 22 March 1758, aged fifty-four.

Honesty requires saying something hard here. Jonathan Edwards owned enslaved people. In 1731 he bought a girl of about fourteen named Venus, and his household included other enslaved people over the years. He criticised the cruelty of the slave trade, but defended slaveholding itself. A man who could examine the human heart better than almost anyone of his time did not examine his own blind spot here, and the people who served in his house paid for it. His son, Jonathan Edwards Jr., later became an outspoken opponent of slavery. The father's failure should be faced, not explained away.

Yet the heart of his story is still that teenage discovery. In one of his best-known sermons, "A Divine and Supernatural Light," preached in his early thirties, he put it this way: "There is a difference between having a rational judgment that honey is sweet, and having a sense of its sweetness." That was what had happened to him at seventeen. He had always known about God. Then, reading a verse he had read a hundred times, he tasted.

### Think It Through

1. Edwards had strong religious feelings as a boy that faded away. Why do you think feelings alone were not enough? Have you ever had an experience like that?
2. Edwards found one teaching about God "horrible," and said so honestly. Is it wrong for a Christian to find parts of the Bible hard to accept? What did Edwards do with his question instead of hiding it?
3. He said there is a difference between knowing honey is sweet and tasting it. What would it mean for you to "taste" something about God that you already know?
4. Edwards resolved to work at hard questions instead of avoiding them, but he began his Resolutions by admitting he could not keep them without God's help. Why does that opening sentence matter so much?

## C. S. Lewis: The Boy Who Learned to Argue

In September 1914, a month after the First World War broke out, a fifteen-year-old boy from Belfast stepped off a train at Great Bookham, a village in Surrey, south of London. He had been sent there to be taught by a private tutor, because his school had made him so miserable that even his father could see something had to change. The tutor was waiting on the platform: a tall, lean old man with side whiskers, shabby clothes and a voice that carried.

The boy's name was Clive Staples Lewis, though he had refused to answer to Clive since he was tiny, and everyone who loved him called him Jack. The tutor was William Kirkpatrick, who had once been headmaster to Jack's father. As they walked from the station, Jack tried to make polite conversation, the kind of thing a nervous boy says to fill a silence. He remarked that the Surrey countryside was wilder than he had expected.

Kirkpatrick stopped him. What, he wanted to know, did Jack mean by "wild"? And what grounds did he have for expecting anything else? Had he studied maps of the area? Read about it? Seen photographs? Jack had to admit he had done none of those things. Then, said Kirkpatrick in effect, he had no right to an opinion on the subject at all.

That is how Lewis told the story forty years later, and he told it with delight. Many boys would have been crushed. Jack was thrilled: for the first time in his life a grown man was taking his words seriously enough to test them.

Kirkpatrick was an atheist who simply assumed that no clear-thinking person could believe in God. Jack, by the time he got off that train, was an atheist too. He had quietly stopped believing a year or two before, at school, and had told almost no one. Now he would spend two and a half years with a man who taught him to argue better than almost anyone alive. He thought the argument about God was over.

It was not. It would take seventeen more years, and it would end in a direction neither of them expected.

### A Prayer That Did Not Work

Jack Lewis was born on 29 November 1898 in Belfast, in the north of Ireland, a city of shipyards and linen mills. His father, Albert, was a solicitor in the police courts: clever, emotional, fond of long words, and anxious. His mother, Flora, was quiet, funny and unusually well educated; she had a university degree in mathematics and logic at a time when very few women anywhere went to university. His best friend was his older brother, Warren, called Warnie, and the family lived in a big, draughty house called Little Lea, crowded with books.

The family went to church, and Jack said his prayers, but he took God for granted, the way he took the house for granted.

Then, early in 1908, his mother was found to have cancer. She was operated on at home, as was common then, and for a while seemed to recover. In the summer the illness came back. The two boys were kept out of the sickroom, frightened and mostly uninformed.

Jack prayed. He had been told that God answers prayer, and he believed it. He prayed that his mother would get well. She died on 23 August 1908, aged forty-six. Then he prayed that she would come back to life. Nothing happened.

It would be easy to say that this was the moment Jack lost his faith, and plenty of people have said it. Lewis himself was more careful. Looking back nearly fifty years later, he thought that as a boy he had been treating God less as someone to love and more as a kind of magician: someone who would perform the trick, and then could be thanked and forgotten. When the trick did not happen, he was not so much shattered as left with nothing. But he believed the disappointment laid a foundation for what came later.

His father, who lost his own father and a brother in the same year as his wife, became for a time almost a stranger, given to outbursts of grief and temper. Less than a month after the funeral, Albert sent both boys across the Irish Sea to a boarding school in England. Jack and Warnie drew close to each other and away from their father, and began a habit of keeping their real thoughts hidden that lasted for years.

### How a Faith Slips Away

The school, Wynyard, in the town of Watford, was a terrible one. Its headmaster beat the boys freely and taught them almost nothing, and he was later declared insane. And yet it was while he was at Wynyard, of all places, that Jack was taken to a church where the faith was preached as if it really mattered. For a while he prayed seriously and read his Bible.

Wynyard closed in 1910 for lack of pupils. After a term at a school in Belfast, Jack went in 1911 to Cherbourg House, a much better preparatory school in the spa town of Malvern, in the west of England. He was happier there. He worked hard and, in 1913, won a scholarship. And somewhere between the ages of twelve and fourteen, there, he quietly stopped being a Christian.

There was no single dramatic moment. Looking back, Lewis named several causes, and they are not the ones people usually expect.

The first was a kind adult. The school matron, whom the boys liked, had drifted away from ordinary Christianity into a vague mixture of spiritualism and Eastern ideas, and she talked about them. She meant no harm. But to a clever, lonely boy, her talk made the plain faith he had been taught seem less solid, just one opinion among many.

The second was the way he had been trying to pray. At Wynyard he had got it into his head that a prayer did not count unless he felt the right things while saying it. So night after night he would lie awake repeating his prayers, trying to produce the proper feelings, and failing, and starting again. It became a torment. When he finally let the whole thing go, the relief was enormous. Notice that the "faith" he dropped was not really trust in God at all. It was trust in his own feelings.

The third reason went deeper and was more like a real question. At school he read the classics, the poets of ancient Greece and Rome, and his teachers treated the old gods of those poems as fables, beautiful stories that nobody had believed for two thousand years. Why, Jack asked himself, should the Christian God be any different? Nobody gave him an answer that satisfied him. And he had begun to notice how much waste and pain there is in the world. He later said that one argument weighed more with him than any other: if a good God had made the world, surely it would not be so frail and faulty. He found it put most powerfully in the Roman poet Lucretius.

That is a serious question, and Lewis never pretended it was not. Much later he wrote a whole book, *The Problem of Pain*, wrestling with it. But he was also honest enough to admit something uncomfortable about his teenage self. Not all of his reasons were reasons. Part of him, he said, simply wanted to be left alone: not to be interfered with, not to have anyone, even God, with a claim on his life. Atheism suited that part of him very well.

So he stopped believing, and told nobody. He kept going to school chapel because it was expected. Inside, he was done.

### Malvern and a Friend

In 1913 Jack followed Warnie to Malvern College, an expensive boarding school. Warnie had loved it; Jack hated it. The senior boys used the younger ones as servants, and everything revolved around games, at which Jack was hopeless, and around popularity and power. Lewis later wrote about it bitterly; Warnie thought his account unfair. But Jack was miserable, and his lifelong distrust of what he called the "inner ring" (the in-crowd that everyone longs to belong to) started there.

One thing never left him through all of this, and it matters for the rest of the story. Ever since he was small, Jack had been caught now and then by a sudden, piercing longing: at the sight of far-off hills, in a story, in a line of poetry. It always seemed to point beyond whatever had set it off. He later called it "Joy." In these years it came to him most strongly through the old myths of northern Europe, the Norse stories of gods who would one day die. He did not believe in them, any more than he believed in God. But they moved him in a way nothing else did, and he could not explain why.

In the Easter holidays of 1914, back in Belfast, he went to visit a boy across the road who was ill in bed. His name was Arthur Greeves. On the bed lay a book of Norse mythology. Arthur asked whether Jack liked that kind of thing, and in a moment each discovered that the other loved exactly the same stories. They wrote to each other for nearly fifty years.

That September, Jack's father finally gave in to his pleading letters, took him out of Malvern, and sent him to Great Bookham.

### The Great Knock

The family called Kirkpatrick "the Great Knock." He taught languages the same way he argued, and before long Jack was reading Homer's Greek at speed.

But what Kirkpatrick really taught him was how to think. Define your words. Ask what your evidence is. Notice when someone, including you, is talking nonsense. Never say anything you cannot defend. Lewis later said those years were among the happiest of his life, and the clear, sharp way he wrote for the rest of it was learned in that house.

It also made Jack a more confident atheist. In the autumn of 1916, when he was seventeen, he wrote to Arthur, who was a believer, explaining that he believed in no religion at all. All religions, he said, were simply mythologies that human beings had made up, and Christianity was one more of them. He wrote it with all the confidence of a clever teenager who has just learned to win arguments.

But there was a crack in it, and Lewis saw it clearly later. He admitted that in these years he was living in a whirl of contradictions. He insisted that God did not exist, and at the same time he was angry with God for not existing. He was angry, too, with God for making a world like this one. You cannot really be angry at someone you are sure is not there. Part of him was still arguing with Someone.

Then there was a thing he did that he regretted for the rest of his life. In December 1914, just after he turned sixteen, he was confirmed at his family's church in Belfast and took his first Communion. Confirmation is the service in which a young person declares in front of the church that they believe the Christian faith. Jack did not believe a word of it. He went through with it because he did not want a scene with his father, and because he could not face the argument if he refused. Later, after he became a Christian, he counted it among the worst things he had ever done: a solemn act before God, made out of cowardice.

It is worth stopping on that. Jack prided himself on honesty. And he stood in front of a congregation and said things he thought were false, to keep the peace at home. Being clever is not the same as being honest. Doubt, for him, was never only a matter of the mind. It was tangled up with fear, pride, loneliness, and a father he could not talk to.

### A Book from a Station Bookstall

In March 1916, waiting for a train at Leatherhead, near Bookham, Jack bought a cheap book from the bookstall. It was *Phantastes*, a fairy-tale romance by George MacDonald, a Scottish writer who had once been a minister. He read it that evening.

He had no idea MacDonald was a Christian, and nothing in the book tried to argue him into anything. But it changed something. The old longing, the "Joy" that the northern myths had given him, was there in *Phantastes* too, but with a different feel to it: something bright, clean and good. Lewis later said that the quality he met in that book was something he could only call holiness, though at the time he did not know that was its name. He said that MacDonald had begun to work on his imagination. The rest of him, his reason and his will, would take years to follow.

It is worth knowing that the first step back toward God, for one of the great Christian thinkers of the twentieth century, was not an argument. It was a story he picked up to pass the time on a railway platform.

### Nineteen in the Trenches

In December 1916 Jack won a scholarship to University College, Oxford. He arrived there in April 1917, but the war had emptied the university. As an Irishman he could not be conscripted; he chose to serve anyway. In June he joined a cadet battalion housed in Keble College, where his roommate was another young Irishman, Paddy Moore. Paddy's mother, Janie Moore, had come to Oxford to be near her son, and Jack spent many hours at her table.

At some point Jack and Paddy made a promise. If one of them was killed, the survivor would look after the other's parent. We know about it from Paddy's sister, Maureen, who remembered it years later, and from the way Jack lived afterwards.

Jack reached the front line in France on his nineteenth birthday, 29 November 1917. He said very little about the war later, and never pretended he had been a hero. He tried, without complete success, to put it out of his mind. In February 1918, in hospital with trench fever, he read G. K. Chesterton for the first time and loved him, without in the least agreeing with his Christianity. Looking back, he joked that a boy determined to stay an atheist should have kept away from such books.

In March 1918 Paddy Moore was posted missing in the great German offensive. Months later, his family learned he had been killed. In April Jack was back with his battalion near the village of Mont-Bernenchon, in northern France. On 15 April 1918 a shell burst close to him, very probably a British one falling short. He was wounded in the hand, the leg and the chest, and the sergeant beside him, Harry Ayres, a steady, kind man who had looked out for his young officer, was killed. A small fragment of metal stayed in his chest for many years.

He wrote from London to his father, asking him to come. Albert did not come, that summer or that autumn. Janie Moore did.

The war did not make Jack a believer. In March 1919 he published a small book of poems, *Spirits in Bondage*, under a made-up name; many of them argue with a God the poet says is absent or cruel. Even as an atheist, notice, he could not stop addressing God.

### What Came After

Jack went back to Oxford in January 1919, and kept his promise to Paddy. For more than thirty years he made a home with Janie Moore and her daughter, supporting them and looking after them, though for about ten of those years he hid much of it from his father. In 1925 he became a Fellow of Magdalen College, Oxford, teaching English literature.

The argument he thought had ended at Great Bookham started up again. His new friends were among the cleverest people he knew, and several of them were Christians, including a quiet professor named J. R. R. Tolkien. His reading kept betraying him too: Chesterton's *The Everlasting Man* made Christian history make sense to him in a way he could not shake off. And Kirkpatrick's own lessons turned on him. If our thoughts are nothing but the side effects of atoms moving in the brain, Lewis came to see, then we have no reason to trust them, including the thought that atoms are all there is. A mind trained to test every claim began to test its own atheism, and found it would not hold.

One night in his college room, Lewis gave in. He knelt and admitted that God was God. He later called himself "the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England." He dated it to 1929, the year his father died; biographers who have read his letters closely think it was more likely 1930. Either way, he now believed in God. He did not yet believe in Christ.

That came more slowly. He could not see how the death of one man in Palestine nineteen centuries ago could have anything to do with him. On the night of 19 September 1931, he walked and talked for hours along a riverside path at Magdalen with Tolkien and another friend, Hugo Dyson. They talked about the old myths of a god who dies and lives again, which Jack had loved since boyhood. The story of Christ, they said, was like the myths in its power, with one immense difference: it really happened, at a real time, in a real place. A few days later, on 28 September, his brother drove him to Whipsnade Zoo on a motorcycle, Jack riding in the sidecar. Somewhere on that ride, without any new argument, he found that he believed Jesus Christ was the Son of God.

What did he believe about Jesus? That he was not a great teacher or a beautiful legend but God himself, come in person. As John put it: "The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us" (John 1:14). He believed that Christ's death and rising again were real events, and that through them God forgives and remakes people who could not remake themselves. The longing he had called Joy, he decided, had been a signpost all along, pointing to the God who made him.

The rest of his life is famous: the wartime radio talks that became *Mere Christianity*, *The Screwtape Letters*, the seven Chronicles of Narnia, and from 1954 a professorship at Cambridge. Late in life he married an American writer, Joy Davidman, and lost her to cancer in 1960. He died at his home near Oxford on 22 November 1963, a week before his sixty-fifth birthday.

He never stopped arguing. But he argued now as someone who knew from the inside what it was to want reasons, to be hurt, and to want to be left alone.

## Dietrich Bonhoeffer: The Boy Who Chose the Church

Imagine a big, comfortable house in Grunewald, a leafy suburb on the western edge of Berlin, around the year 1920. It is full of people: a large family of brothers and sisters and their friends, a father who is one of the most respected doctors in Germany, a mother who runs the household like a small school. In the evenings there is music. Someone is always at the piano. At dinner the talk is quick, clever and sometimes sharp, because in this family you are expected to say what you mean and to be able to defend it.

The youngest boy, Dietrich, is about fourteen. He has fair hair, a serious mind, and a gift for the piano so great that some people have wondered aloud whether he will become a professional musician. Everyone expects great things of him. And he has just told his family that he is going to be a theologian: to study God, and serve the church.

His brothers thought he was throwing himself away. The family remembered the scene for years afterwards, and the story they told runs something like this. The older brothers told Dietrich that the church was a poor, feeble, dull institution, small-minded and dying, and not worth a gifted young man's life. And Dietrich answered that, in that case, he would reform it.

We do not know exactly how the conversation went, or whether it happened all at once or over many evenings. It comes to us through the family's memories, retold by Dietrich's friend and biographer, Eberhard Bethge. But it fits everything else we know about him. He did not argue his brothers down. He simply did what he said he would. And in the end the church he chose would cost him his life.

### Eight Children in Berlin

Dietrich Bonhoeffer was born on 4 February 1906 in Breslau, then a city in eastern Germany, now Wrocław in Poland. He had a twin sister, Sabine. They were the sixth and seventh of eight children. When Dietrich was six, his father, Karl Bonhoeffer, became professor of psychiatry (the medical study of the mind) at the university in Berlin, and the whole family moved to the capital.

It was a privileged family, and a remarkable one. Karl was a scientist through and through: careful, reserved, distrustful of anything he could not observe and test. He was not hostile to religion, but he had no use for it in his own life, and the family hardly ever went to church. Dietrich's mother, Paula, was different. She was a trained teacher, the granddaughter of a well-known church historian, and she taught her younger children at home in their early years. She read them Bible stories, sang hymns with them, and wanted them to know the faith, even though the household did not practise it in public.

The older brothers followed their father's path. Karl-Friedrich became a scientist, Klaus a lawyer. The house was full of argument, music and books, and the children were expected to think for themselves. Sloppy reasoning was laughed at. Showing off was laughed at too.

Sabine later remembered that she and Dietrich, as small children sharing a room, would lie awake at night talking about death and eternity, trying to imagine what "for ever" could possibly mean. It is a small detail, but it tells you something. Long before he studied theology, Dietrich was a child who asked the biggest questions there are.

### Walter

In 1914 the First World War began. At first it seemed far away. The younger Bonhoeffer children played at soldiers and followed the battles on maps. Then the older brothers came of age.

In the spring of 1918 the second son, Walter, eighteen years old, went to the front in France as an officer cadet. Within weeks he was badly wounded, and at the end of April he died of his wounds. Dietrich was twelve.

The family was devastated. Paula Bonhoeffer was so overwhelmed by grief that for weeks she could hardly function, and for a long time afterwards she was not her old self. Karl, the calm scientist, was shaken in a way his children had never seen. The quick, confident household had met something that no amount of clever argument could fix.

We do not know exactly what went on in Dietrich's mind that year; he did not write about it. But it is hard to believe it had nothing to do with what came next. Walter's death put death and eternity, the questions he and Sabine had whispered about in the dark, right at the centre of the family's life. When Dietrich was confirmed in 1921, his mother gave him Walter's Bible. According to his family and friends, it was the Bible he used for his daily reading for the rest of his life.

### A Waste of a Good Mind

It was not long after this, at around fourteen, that Dietrich made his announcement. He seems to have decided quietly first and told his family later. At school he chose to learn Hebrew, the language of the Old Testament, so that he could read the Bible in its original words.

Why theology? We do not have a neat answer from him at that age, and it would be dishonest to invent one. Some of it may have been a teenager's wish to go his own way in a family of high achievers: his brothers had science and law, and he wanted something of his own. Some of it was certainly intellectual. Theology, for him, was the hardest and most important subject there was: questions about God, about truth, about what a human being is for. Some of it, perhaps, was Walter.

What is clear is that his family thought it was a mistake. His brothers saw the church as an institution in decline, on the wrong side of modern science and modern life. His father was disappointed but did not stand in his way. Years later, after Dietrich had become caught up in the church's struggle against the Nazis, Karl wrote to him admitting that when Dietrich first chose theology, he had privately thought it a pity that such a gifted son should settle for the quiet, uneventful life of a minister. He had been wrong about the quiet, he said.

What makes Dietrich's choice interesting is that he did not make it in spite of his family's way of thinking. He made it with their way of thinking. He did not become a Christian by turning his mind off. He chose the subject precisely because it demanded the whole of his mind. He would spend his life insisting that faith has to be thought through, honestly, and then has to be lived.

But there is one more thing to be honest about. At fourteen, Dietrich wanted to study God. That is not quite the same as wanting to follow him. It would take him more than ten years to discover the difference.

### Questions in Tübingen, Rome and Berlin

Dietrich finished school in 1923, at seventeen, and went to the university of Tübingen, in southern Germany, as his father had done. Germany was in chaos: the country had lost the war, and that year its money became almost worthless, so that prices rose by the hour. He studied hard anyway.

In the spring of 1924, aged eighteen, he travelled to Rome with his brother Klaus. It was his first real encounter with the church outside the quiet Lutheran Germany he knew. In St Peter's on Palm Sunday he watched people from every nation worshipping together, and he wrote in his diary that he was beginning to understand what the word "church" meant. It was not a building, or a German institution, or a fading club for the old-fashioned. It was a worldwide people. That idea would stay at the centre of everything he wrote.

That summer he moved to the university in Berlin, one of the great centres of theology in the world. Some of its professors were famous "liberal" theologians: they treated the Bible mainly as a human book and Christianity mainly as a moral and cultural tradition. The most famous of them, Adolf von Harnack, was an elderly neighbour of the Bonhoeffers in Grunewald, and Dietrich joined his seminar. Harnack liked him. But Dietrich was also reading, with growing excitement, the work of a Swiss theologian named Karl Barth, who insisted that God is not a human idea at all: that God speaks, and we listen; that the Bible is where God's word reaches us, not just a record of human religious feeling.

So here was a young man in his late teens sitting between two great arguments, taking both seriously, and working out for himself which one was true. He did not simply take sides with the teachers he liked. He tested them.

In 1927, at only twenty-one, he was awarded his doctorate. His dissertation was called *Sanctorum Communio*, Latin for "the communion of saints," a study of the church as a real community of people bound together in Christ. It was an astonishing piece of work for someone so young. Karl Barth, reading it many years later, called it a theological miracle. Dietrich then spent a year as an assistant pastor to a German congregation in Barcelona, Spain, and came back to Berlin to write a second thesis that qualified him to teach at the university.

By any measure, he was a success. But by his own later account, something was missing.

### Harlem

In 1930 he was twenty-four and too young to be ordained as a pastor in his church, which required candidates to be twenty-five. So he crossed the Atlantic to spend a year studying at Union Theological Seminary in New York.

He was not impressed by the lectures. He thought the American students talked a great deal about society and very little about God. But in New York he met a fellow student named Frank Fisher, an African American, and Fisher took him to the Abyssinian Baptist Church in Harlem, one of the largest Black churches in America.

Dietrich went back again and again. For months he worshipped there most Sundays and helped teach a Sunday school class. He heard preaching that spoke plainly of sin and grace and the love of God, with a passion he had not met in the lecture halls of Berlin. He heard the spirituals, songs born out of slavery, and he bought records of them to take home to Germany. And he saw, close up, what racism did to people: the insults, the segregation, the constant humiliations that his friends lived with. He was horrified, and he never forgot it. When, a few years later, his own country began to single out a whole people as inferior, he had already seen where that led.

In New York he also became friends with a French student, Jean Lasserre, a pacifist who took Jesus' Sermon on the Mount with complete seriousness. Jesus told his followers to "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). Lasserre believed Jesus meant it, literally, and expected his followers to do it. Dietrich had studied the Sermon on the Mount as a text. Lasserre made him ask whether he had ever obeyed it.

Scholars are careful not to call the New York year a sudden conversion. But something shifted in Dietrich in the early 1930s, and he described it himself a few years later in a private letter to a friend. Looking back, he said that he had thrown himself into his work in a very unchristian way, driven by ambition. He had preached and written about the church, he said, but he had not yet become a Christian. He had turned the teaching of Jesus into something for his own advantage. Then the Bible, and especially the Sermon on the Mount, set him free. He began to read it every day, to pray, and to see that following Christ was not an idea but a life.

That is what a theologian at fourteen became in his mid-twenties: a disciple. Jesus Christ was no longer, for him, a subject to master but the living Lord, who had died for him and now called him to follow. He had spent years asking questions about God. Now he heard God asking a question of him.

### What Came After

He returned to Berlin in 1931, was ordained, and began teaching at the university and working with young people. On 30 January 1933 Adolf Hitler came to power. Two days later Bonhoeffer gave a radio talk warning that a leader who makes himself into an idol becomes a misleader; the broadcast was cut off before he finished, though exactly why is not certain. When the new government barred people of Jewish descent from public jobs, and a powerful pro-Nazi party in the church wanted to do the same to its own pastors, Bonhoeffer opposed it at once. He wrote that the church must not only help the victims crushed under the wheel of the state, but might one day have to jam the wheel itself.

He joined the Confessing Church, the Christians who refused to let the Nazis take over their faith. From 1935 he led one of its seminaries for training pastors, at Finkenwalde, in what is now Poland; it was illegal, and the secret police closed it in 1937. Out of those years came his two best-known books, *The Cost of Discipleship* and *Life Together*. In the first he attacked what he called "cheap grace," forgiveness that asks for no change, and set against it "costly grace": grace that cost God the life of his Son, and that calls a person to follow Jesus with everything. Jesus said, "If anyone wants to come after Me, he must deny himself and take up his cross and follow Me" (Matthew 16:24). Bonhoeffer believed him.

In 1939 friends arranged for him to go to New York, where he would have been safe. Within weeks he decided he had made a mistake, and sailed home before war broke out, saying he could have no part in rebuilding the church in Germany afterwards if he did not share its trials now. Through his brother-in-law Hans von Dohnanyi he was drawn into the circle in German military intelligence that was secretly working against Hitler, and he came to support a conspiracy that included plans to kill Hitler. He was not a gunman or a bomb-maker. He was a pastor who believed that standing by while the innocent were murdered was a greater guilt than acting, and who did not pretend the choice was clean. He also helped smuggle a small group of Jews to safety in Switzerland.

In January 1943 he became engaged to Maria von Wedemeyer. On 5 April 1943 he was arrested. For eighteen months he was held in Tegel military prison in Berlin, where he wrote letters, prayers and poems, later published as *Letters and Papers from Prison*. One of the poems is called "Who Am I?" In it he asks whether he is really the calm, cheerful man the guards see, or the restless, frightened prisoner he feels like inside. He does not answer the question. He ends by telling God that whoever he is, God knows him, and he belongs to God. The boy who lay awake asking about eternity was still asking honest questions at thirty-eight, and still bringing them to God.

After the failed attempt on Hitler's life in July 1944, documents were found that exposed the conspiracy. Bonhoeffer was moved from prison to prison, and at last to Flossenbürg concentration camp in Bavaria. He was condemned by a brief court with no real defence, and at dawn on 9 April 1945 he was hanged, at thirty-nine, about two weeks before American soldiers reached the camp and a month before the war in Europe ended. His brother Klaus and two of his brothers-in-law were also killed by the regime that month. A British prisoner who was with him in the last days remembered that, as he was led away, Bonhoeffer asked him to carry a message to a friend in England: that this was the end, but for him the beginning of life.

The boy whose brothers said he was wasting his mind on a dying church had followed Christ all the way. He did not find the church easy, or comfortable, or always right; he spent his life trying to call it back to Jesus. But he never decided it was a waste.

### Think It Through

1. Dietrich chose theology in a family that thought it was a waste of a good mind. Have you ever been made to feel that taking faith seriously was not very intelligent? How did you respond?
2. Walter's death brought the biggest questions into the Bonhoeffer house. How can grief make questions about God more urgent rather than less?
3. Bonhoeffer later said he had studied and preached about the church for years before he really became a Christian. What is the difference between knowing about God and following him?
