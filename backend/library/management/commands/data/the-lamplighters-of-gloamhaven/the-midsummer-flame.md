# The Midsummer Flame

## Waiting

Of all the hard things Wren Ashby had done in her life — and she had done a good many, from climbing the Beacon stair in the dark to asking Tobin Harrowgate to forgive her — the very hardest, she decided, was waiting.

"I could have been to Emberwick and back," she said, "three times."

"Four," said Tobin, from the roof. "Actually. If you walked as fast as you did coming here, and didn't stop to eat, which you would. But four, roughly."

"Then four." Wren lay on her back in the grass at the top of Cinder Hill with her hands behind her head and scowled at the sky. "Four times, and here I am. Lying about. Like a cat."

Cinder, who was lying about beside her exactly like a cat, opened one eye and shut it again.

It was grass, now, that Wren was lying in. That was the thing that still surprised her every morning when she woke. When she had first climbed the Hill, alone, with the dark coming down and her lamp burning low inside her coat, there had been nothing at the top but ash — soft grey ash, warm underfoot, and the long ruined workshop, and the cold. Now there was grass. Not much of it, and not very tall, but real grass, green as a pea-pod, coming up out of the ash in tufts and patches and spreading a little further every day, the way warmth spreads through a cold room from a fire. Down the slopes it ran in long green fingers. In the hollows there were small white flowers whose name nobody knew, so the Remnant's children had named them themselves, and they were called, depending on which child you asked, *stars*, *snowdrops*, *Posy's buttons* and *the little white ones*.

"Day forty-one," called Tobin from the roof. "Midsummer in five."

"You said that yesterday."

"Yesterday I said six. It's a calendar, Wren. That's how they work."

He had built it on the workshop roof the second week, when it had become clear that the Lampwright had not said *which* Midsummer and somebody needed to know exactly when it was. It was a stick, set upright in a pot of stones, with a ring of chalk marks round it on the slates and a great deal of string. Tobin went up every noon to see where the stick's shadow fell — which was difficult, because the pale sun of Gloamhaven threw a very poor sort of shadow, more of a smudge — and then he climbed down and told everyone, whether they had asked or not. He had worked out Midsummer three different ways and got the same answer each time, and he was so pleased about this that nobody had the heart to tell him that Sister Hale had known it all along, because the Remnant had kept Midsummer in secret every year for three hundred years.

Wren sat up and looked round at the Hill.

It was not empty any more, either. When the Lampwright walked away down the eastern slope, there had been perhaps twenty of the Remnant on the Hill, counting Sister Hale and counting the fox. Now there were hundreds. They had been coming every day since. Nobody had sent for them; or rather, nobody could quite say who had. Word had gone out, the way word does in the poor parts of a country — from a farm to a mill, from a mill to a cellar, from a cellar to a barn — that something had happened on Cinder Hill. That the old songs were true. That colour had come back to the ash. And so they came, in twos and threes and families, walking across the Burned Country with their bundles on their backs: farmers, a cobbler, two washerwomen, a retired Guild clerk who apologised to everybody, a family of tinkers with a cart, old men who had to be helped up the last of the slope, and children who ran up it.

They camped all round the workshop, in tents and lean-tos and under carts, and in the evenings there were so many cooking-fires on the Hill that from the plain below, Mira said, it must look like a second Ring.

"It's a very small Ring," said Tobin. "Technically. And it isn't on a wall."

"It's a Ring if I say so," said Mira. "I'm the one who's seen both."

---

Wren got up and wandered down towards the workshop, with Cinder at her heels.

Sister Hale was sitting on an upturned bucket in the doorway, with her crutch across her knees and her bad arm still in its sling, shelling peas into a bowl with her good hand and her teeth. She was singing, which she nearly always was, in her cracked strong voice that could be heard, on a still day, at the bottom of the Hill.

"Morning, duck," she said, round a pea-pod. "Five days, I hear."

"Everybody's heard," said Wren. "He tells the *sheep*."

"He's excited," said Sister Hale. "So am I. I've been excited for seventy years, duck. It's very tiring." She spat a pod into the bucket. "Sit down. Shell some peas. It's good for waiting."

"Nothing's good for waiting."

"Peas are," said Sister Hale. "Try them."

Wren sat down on the step and shelled peas. It was, she had to admit, slightly better than lying in the grass. Her fingers had always liked having something to do; they had picked pockets and scraped flues and mended boots and climbed walls since she was six. Now they split pods and ran their thumbs along the inside and the little green peas rolled into the bowl, and her mind, which had been going round and round like a dog after its tail, began very slowly to lie down.

"Sister Hale."

"Duck."

"What *is* it? The gift. What's he going to give us?"

"I don't know," said Sister Hale.

Wren stared at her. In forty-one days she had never once heard Sister Hale say she didn't know something. She knew which mushrooms you could eat and which ones made you see purple goats. She knew forty-three verses of the Lamplighters' Psalm and the tunes of all of them. She knew how to set a broken finger and how to get a mule across a river and how to make a Guild clerk go red to the ears with one look out of her good eye.

"You don't know?"

"There's an old song," said Sister Hale, "that says he'll send his fire on them that wait for it. That's all. *On them that wait.* My grandmother used to say it meant the Flame would come back to the Rings one day. My mother said it meant something else, but she never would tell me what." She shelled another pea. "I used to lie awake and wonder. When I was a girl. What it'd be like. Whether it'd be fire falling out of the sky, like rain. Whether it'd burn." She smiled, and her face went into a hundred wrinkles. "I've stopped wondering. He said wait, and he said you'll know. That'll do for me. When you've waited seventy years, duck, five days is nothing. It's a sneeze."

"It's not nothing to *me*," said Wren.

"No," said Sister Hale. "I can see that."

---

The trouble was, Wren thought — though she would not have said it out loud, not even to Cinder — that she did not really know what she was waiting *for*.

She had the lamp.

She had had it since the day she found it in the sealed flue at Thornbury House, burning away with no oil in a niche in the brick, warm as a loaf. It had been given to her. It had come back to her out of the Soot Canal. It had lit the Great Beacon of Emberwick and all one hundred and forty-four lamps of its Ring. It had healed Nettle and Mrs Fenn and Tavi and Pim and Posy and half of Soot Lane. It was not hers the way a coat is yours, or a pair of boots. But it was hers the way a name is yours. It had been *given*.

And the Lampwright — whose own lamp it was, the very one he had carried down the Mountain — had stood in his workshop and folded his scarred hands round her hands and said *Keep it. You'll want it. You've a long way to carry it yet.*

So what was the gift for? What could he give her that she had not already got?

She did not like the question. It made her feel small and greedy and ungrateful, all at once, which were three things she had felt a great deal of in Soot Lane and had hoped to be done with. So she did what she always did with a feeling she did not like, which was to go and do something instead.

That afternoon she went into the workshop and stood in front of the racks.

The hundred lamps were still there. They had not moved since the Lampwright had stood and looked at them and smiled. Brass bodies, polished and finished, every one, with the little hook on top for carrying — but no chimney, no wick, and no flame. They stood in their rows on the long wooden shelves in the grey light from the door, and Wren thought again what she had thought the first night: that they looked like a choir waiting for somebody to give it the note.

"Well," she said to them, "I've got the note. Haven't I?"

She took out her lamp.

It burned up at once, the way it always did now — a small steady gold flame inside its cracked glass chimney, leaning a little, very slightly, to the east, the way the Lampwright had gone. The light went over the brass bodies of the hundred lamps, and every one of them shone back at her, gold on gold.

Wren picked up the nearest one. It was lighter than hers, and newer, and not dented anywhere. She held it next to her own, so that the little gold flame was right up against the empty top of it where a wick ought to be.

Nothing happened.

She tried again. She tipped her lamp so the flame touched the brass. She held it there while she counted to twenty. She breathed on it, gently, the way Ash used to breathe on a spark. She said, "Go on. *Go on*. It's given. It's — I'm giving it you. Look."

The brass got warm in her hand from her own lamp. But there was nothing for the flame to take hold of. You cannot light a lamp that has no wick, any more than you can light a stone.

"Rot," said Wren, and put it back on the shelf, rather harder than she meant to.

"They're not ready," said a voice behind her.

It was Mira, in the doorway, with the Chronicle in her arms. She was always with the Chronicle in her arms, these days; the Lampwright had told her to look after it, and Mira had taken him at his word. She slept with it. She ate with it on her knees. She had made a bag for it out of an old flour sack, with a strap, and Tobin had said she looked like a very small travelling scholar, and Mira had said thank you, she would take that as a compliment, and Tobin had said it *was* one, and gone red.

"I know they're not ready," said Wren. "I've got eyes."

"I know you have. So have I. It's still very exciting." Mira came in and stood beside her and looked at the racks, and then — out of habit, the old habit from the Deeps — clicked her tongue softly, twice, and listened to the click come back. "A hundred," she said. "I counted them by sound, the first night. Before I counted them with my eyes. I wanted to be sure the eyes were telling me the truth."

"Were they?"

"They were. It was a great relief." She looked sideways at Wren. "You wanted to light one."

"I wanted to see if I *could*."

"And you can't."

"Nobody can. There's no wick. It's just brass." Wren shoved her own lamp back inside her coat, against her ribs, where it belonged. "I don't know why he left them like that. Half done. It's not like him. He finished the toast."

"He burned the toast," said Mira.

"He finished it, though. He didn't leave it half done on a shelf for three hundred years."

Mira did not answer that at once. She stood with her head a little on one side in the way she had, as if she were listening for something a long way off.

"Perhaps," she said, "they're not half done. Perhaps they're waiting. The way we are."

"For what?"

"I don't know," said Mira, and smiled. "That's two of us who don't know, then. That makes it better."

"It does *not*," said Wren. But she found that it did, a bit.

---

That evening, as on every evening, Mira read from the Chronicle.

She sat on the workbench at the back of the workshop with the great book open on her knees, and the Remnant's children sat on the floor in front of her in a heap, with the bigger ones at the back and the smallest ones in front, sucking their thumbs, and Cinder in the very middle of them, being leaned on. The grown-ups stood round the walls and in the doorway, pretending they were only listening for the children's sake.

Mira read well. She had only learned her letters on the road, from Tobin, in the evenings in Vaelport and on Cinder Hill; for twelve years she had read only with her fingers, from the carved stories of the Delvers' walls. But she read the way she did everything — carefully, all the way through, giving every word its full weight — and the children loved her for it.

She read them the Making of the world, in the first clear strong hand, and how the sun was golden then, and how the Maker was glad. She read them Ardric's bargain, which made the smallest ones cry, and the Lampwright's walking, which made them laugh, because of the bit where he lit a lamp for a goat. She did not read them the last night on the Hill. Sister Hale had said, gently, that they were too small for that, and Mira had agreed.

And then, every evening, when she had finished reading, Mira turned to the end of the book.

There was the last written page, in the second hand, the cramped and shaking one that had got steadier as it went: *On the third morning, the fire woke.* And after it, page after page of nothing. Thick, creamy, empty pages, the edges a little brown with age, all the way to the back cover.

Mira did not read them, because there was nothing to read. She only turned them, one at a time, slowly, and ran her fingers over each one, the way she would once have run her fingers over a carved wall to find the story in it; and the children watched her in perfect silence, as if she might find something there that they could not see.

"Why do you do that?" Wren asked her that night, when the children had gone to their beds.

"I don't know," said Mira. "It seems polite." She closed the book and held it against her chest. "Somebody is going to write in them, I think. One day. I like to say good evening to the pages first."

---

Four days.

Three.

On the second-to-last day before Midsummer, a drover came up the Hill.

He was a lean grey man with a lean grey dog, and he had come all the way from the Emberwick road with a handful of sheep that had not survived the Burned Country and a great deal of news that had. He sat by Sister Hale's fire and drank three bowls of soup and told it, and the Remnant crowded round to hear.

"Prowle's back," he said.

Wren, who had been half asleep against the workshop wall, sat up as if somebody had poured cold water down her neck.

"Prowle? *Guild Master* Prowle?"

"*Lord Steward* Prowle, he calls himself now." The drover spat into the fire. "Came back through the South Gate in the spring with a paper and a seal and a dozen grey men in armour at his back, and nobody to say him no. Warden's gone, see. Nobody knows where. Hall's shut up. So Prowle sits in it." He wiped his mouth. "He's had the Beacon stair bricked up."

"*What?*" said Tobin.

"Bricked up. Top to bottom of the doorway. Says the fire in the Beacon's Guild property, being as it's in a Guild tower, and anybody that wants a light from it can go through the Steward's gate and pay the Steward's fee. A silver penny the light." He looked round at them. "The Ring still burns, mind. Gold. Nobody's ever seen the like. But down in the Low Quarter, where folk can't pay, it's dark again in the houses."

Wren had got to her feet without knowing she had done it. She was thinking of Soot Lane. Of Mrs Fenn's bakery and Nettle and the door-to-door morning when she had gone along the whole lane with the lamp in her hands, saying *it's given, it's given*, and every window lit.

"He can't," she said. "He *can't*. It was free. I gave it them. It was *given*—"

"He's calling you lot the Cinder sect," the drover went on, not unkindly. "Witches. Folk that light a lamp without paying get took up. There's a reward." He scratched his dog's ears. "And it's not only Prowle. There's the grey captain."

The fire went quiet.

"On the roads," said the drover. "Him with the scar on his hand. He's been up and down the country since the spring, riding hard. Burning barns. Any barn where the old songs were sung. Taking folk off with him, roped. Nobody knows where to." He looked into the fire. "He was always a cold one. But folk that've seen him lately say he's gone worse. Like a man with a wasp in his head. Like he's trying to outride something."

Tobin had gone very still. Wren looked at him, and then at Mira, and she knew they were all three seeing the same thing: a tall grey man on a black hill at dawn, staring at a stranger's scarred hands, and then at his own; and then dropping his sword and running.

"He's frightened," said Mira softly.

"He's *wicked*," said Wren.

"He can be both," said Mira. "Most people who are wicked are."

---

Wren did not sleep much that night, or the next.

She lay by the fire with Cinder curled against her stomach and the lamp inside her coat and thought about Prowle and his bricks, and the dark houses in Soot Lane, and a lean grey dog. She thought about all the people on the roads, roped. She thought: *we should be going. Now. Tonight. What's the use of sitting on a hill shelling peas, when—*

And then, every time, she heard the Lampwright saying it. *At Midsummer there will be a gift. Wait for it.*

She was not good at doing what she was told. She had never been. She had climbed the Beacon when Ash told her to stay put, and gone into the Glass Palace when Ash told her to keep out, and run away from three different sweep-masters before Mott. But she had said *thank you* to him on the slope of this Hill, and she had meant it; and you cannot very well say thank you to somebody and then do the exact opposite of the one thing he asked.

So she waited. It was the worst thing she had ever done.

---

On Midsummer Eve, nobody on Cinder Hill went to bed.

It was not planned. Nobody said *let's stay up*. But when the dark came down — quickly, as it always did in Gloamhaven, like a lid going on a pot — the fires were built up, and nobody put them out, and nobody went to their tents. Little by little, as the night went on, they all drifted to the same place, the way water finds the lowest part of a field. They came in from the camp and up to the top of the Hill, and into the long low workshop, and when the workshop was full they stood outside it, at the doors and windows, and on the grass, hundreds of them, wrapped in shawls and blankets, with the little children asleep on their parents' shoulders.

Sister Hale sat on her bucket by the cold forge, singing very quietly. Mira sat on the workbench with the Chronicle in her lap. Tobin was up a ladder by the window with his notebook, because he had worked out that the sun would rise at a certain point on the horizon at a certain minute and he wanted to see if he was right. Wren stood in the middle of the floor with her back to the racks of the hundred lamps and Cinder pressed against her shins, and the lamp in her hands.

Nobody talked much.

The night went on and on. The fires outside burned down to red. Somebody's baby woke and cried and was hushed. A long way off on the Burned Country an ash-crow called once, harsh, and stopped.

And then, a little before dawn, the air went still.

Wren noticed it first because the forge-fire's smoke — the Remnant had lit a small fire in the nearest forge, for the old people — stopped leaning out of the door and went straight up, like a string. Then the rustle of the grass outside stopped. Then the little sounds — the shifting, the coughs, the creak of somebody's knees. It was as if the whole Hill, and everyone on it, had drawn in one long breath at the same moment, and was holding it.

Cinder stood up.

Every hair on her ash-grey back stood up with her, and the spark at the end of her tail, which had been a quiet gold glow all night, went suddenly bright as a struck match. She did not growl. She was not afraid. She stood with her nose pointing out of the open doorway, towards the south and the Burned Country, and her whole small body trembling, the way a dog trembles when it hears its master's step a long way off on the road.

And then the air, which had been as cold as every dawn in Gloamhaven had been for three hundred years, began — very slowly, the way a stone in the sun begins — to get warm.

## The Gift

It came up out of the Burned Country like a breath.

That is the only way I know to tell it, and it is the way all of them told it afterwards, every one, in every town and farm in Gloamhaven, for as long as they lived. It was a wind; but it was not like any wind they had felt before. There was no cloud in it. There was no storm, and no dust, and no cold. It came across the miles of soft grey ash from the south, and you could see it coming, because where it passed, the ash lifted a little and lay down again, like the fur of an animal when a hand is run along its back. It came up the slopes of the Hill and through the grass, and the grass bowed to it. It came through the camp, and every tent-flap lifted, and every banked fire on the Hill woke up and roared.

And it was warm.

It smelt of bread. It smelt of summer: of hay, and hot stone, and apples, and the inside of a bakery in the early morning, and something else underneath that Wren had no name for and knew all the same, the way you know your own front door. It was the smell of the lamp. It had always been the smell of the lamp. She had just never smelt so much of it all at once.

It came in at the workshop door.

It went through the long low room the way a breath goes into a body: all at once, and everywhere. The tools on the walls rang faintly on their pegs, a little silvery chiming, like a shelf of glasses when a cart goes by. Mira's hair blew back off her face. The Chronicle's pages lifted on her knees and riffled over, all of them, from front to back, and lay still. Tobin's notebook flew out of his hand and up into the rafters, and he did not even look round for it.

And the forges lit.

Not the little one the Remnant had made up for the old people. All of them. Every cold forge down the whole length of the workshop, that had not held a fire for three hundred years — each one went up at once with a sound like a great soft clap of hands, and filled with fire: gold and red and white, roaring, so that the whole long room was suddenly full of light and heat and the shapes of people's faces leaping out of the dark.

And then the lamps.

Wren was standing with her back to the racks. She did not see the first one. She felt it: a warmth on the back of her neck, quite small and sudden, like somebody breathing. She turned round.

On the top shelf, at the very end, in one of the hundred unfinished brass lamps that had no chimney and no wick, a flame was burning.

It was small, and gold, and perfectly steady, and it was standing on nothing at all — just on the empty brass mouth of the lamp, where the wick ought to be, as if the lamp had been waiting all this time for exactly this, and had only needed to be asked.

Then the one beside it.

Then the one below.

Then they were all going — faster than you could count them, faster than Tobin could have counted them, one after another and then all at once, along the shelves and up and down the racks, a hundred little gold flames standing up out of a hundred empty brass lamps with a sound like a hundred small breaths let out together. *Ah.* And the light of them, added to the light of the forges, went over the whole workshop until there was not one shadow left in it anywhere.

Wren stood in front of the racks with her own lamp in her hands, and her mouth open.

---

That was not the end of it.

I wish I could tell you exactly what happened next, but I was not there, and the people who were never could quite agree. Mira said it was like a hand. Tobin said it was like a drop of water — like the first drop of rain on the top of your head, when you look up to see if it's raining. Sister Hale said it was like being kissed goodnight by her mother, and then she would not say anything else, because she always cried.

But they all agreed about what they saw.

Above the head of every person in the workshop — and outside it, too, on the grass and at the windows and the doors, every one of the hundreds of the Remnant on the top of the Hill, from the oldest old man to the smallest sleeping baby — there was, for a moment, a flame.

It was a very small flame. It was no bigger than the flame of a candle, and it was gold, like the lamp's, and it did not burn anybody's hair. It rested on the very top of each person's head, lightly, the way a hand rests on a child's head when you are blessing it, or measuring how much it has grown. It stayed there for about as long as it takes to say *good morning*.

And then it sank in.

Wren felt it. She had been looking at Mira — at the little gold flame on top of Mira's beaded braids, and Mira's face turned up towards it in astonishment — when she felt the warmth come down on her own head as well, and stop there, and wait.

She had not expected it. That was the odd thing. She had the lamp. She had always had the lamp. She had thought, if she thought anything at all, that whatever the gift was, it would be for the others, and she would stand by and watch.

But it came to her as well, exactly as it came to everybody else, no more and no less. And it was like — she tried, afterwards, to tell Tobin what it was like, and could only say — it was like somebody saying your name in a crowd. When you didn't know anybody knew you were there.

It sank in. It went down through her like warm water poured into a cold cup. It went into her chest, and her arms, and her hands round the lamp; and her hands, which had been cold every morning of her life, were warm.

---

Cinder was the first to make a sound.

She threw back her head and *yelped*, a high glad fox-yelp that went up into the rafters, and then she ran — round and round the workshop, between people's legs, under the bench, over the bench, round the forges, with her spark streaming out behind her like the tail of a comet — and did not stop until she had been round three times and knocked over Sister Hale's bucket.

Then Sister Hale began to sing.

She did not stand up. She could not, without her crutch, and her crutch had gone under the bench with the peas. She simply sat on the floor where the bucket had tipped her and lifted up her old cracked strong voice and sang, the way she had sung in barns and cellars and ditches for seventy years with the Guild's men going by on the road. But it was not quiet this time. It was the loudest thing Wren had ever heard. It was the Lamplighters' Psalm, the oldest verse, the one Ash had taught them on the Greywaste:

*Though I walk through the gloaming, I will fear no dark, for the Flame is with me…*

And everybody sang it.

The ones who knew the words sang the words, and the ones who didn't sang the tune, and the ones who didn't know the tune sang anyway. Wren sang. She had never sung in front of anybody in her life except Nettle, and she sang now as loud as she could, with her face wet. Tobin sang from the top of his ladder, horribly out of tune, with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead. Mira sang and clicked at the same time, which should not have been possible. And outside on the grass the hundreds of the Remnant took it up, until it was not a song any more but a sound, a great warm roaring sound like the forges, going out over the top of Cinder Hill and down its slopes and away across the Burned Country in the first grey light.

I am told — and I believe it, because I have heard it from more than one person who was there — that down at the foot of the Hill, where the new people were still arriving, there was a party of Delvers who had come up out of the Deeps that week, who had never in their lives heard anyone sing in the common tongue; and a Highland drover who spoke nothing else but his own hill-speech; and three fisherwomen from the coast below Vaelport, who said afterwards that they had only come because their neighbour had. And every one of them, standing on the ash in the dawn and looking up at the singing, heard it as if it were being sung to them by their own mothers, in their own mothers' way, the way it was sung when they were small. The Delvers wept. The drover took off his hat. The fisherwomen held each other's hands and climbed the Hill as fast as they could go.

Nobody ever explained that. Nobody tried. It was simply the way it was.

---

When the song was done, there was a silence; and in the silence, Wren heard a laugh.

It was Tobin. He had come down off his ladder at last and was standing in front of the racks with the light of the hundred lamps on his face, and he was laughing — not the polite nervous laugh he had used in the Warden's Hall when he did not know what else to do, but a real one, helpless, the kind he had not laughed since before the Glowstone City.

"What?" said Wren. "*What?*"

"It's not arithmetic," said Tobin.

He turned round to her. His spectacles were crooked and his face was shining.

"It's *not arithmetic*, Wren. I've been doing it wrong. All this time. I kept thinking — one flame, a thousand lamps, there ought to be nothing left. Divide one by a thousand. But you don't *divide* a fire." He flung his arm out at the racks, and nearly hit Mira. "You can't. It isn't a pie. It isn't a — a pound of butter, you don't cut it up into pieces until there's only a scrape left. It's *fire*. You don't divide a fire. You *spread* it. And the more you spread it, the more of it there *is*." He took his spectacles off and put them on again. "He said it was nearly ripe. The question. He said it was nearly ripe and he was *right*, it's — I've been carrying it about for a year and it's just — *gone*. I don't need it any more. I *know*."

"Well, I could have told you that," said Sister Hale, from the floor. "I've been saying it for seventy years."

"No, you haven't," said Tobin.

"Well, I *meant* it," said Sister Hale, and held up her good hand for somebody to pull her up.

---

It was a Remnant boy called Dick Crane who found out the rest.

He was about nine, a thin freckled tinker's boy, one of the family with the cart; and he had been sitting on the floor near the door all through the singing, with a stub of tallow candle in his fist that his mother had given him to hold in case the forges went out. When the singing stopped, he looked down at it.

Then — nobody ever knew why; I don't think he knew himself — he cupped his other hand round the top of it, the way you shelter a match from the wind. And when he took his hand away, the candle was alight.

He had no flint. He had no tinder. He had not been near the forges or the lamps. He had only put his warm hand round it.

He stared at it. Everybody near him stared at it. The little flame stood up on the end of the tallow stub, gold, steady, leaning ever so slightly to the east.

"Ma," said Dick Crane, in a very small voice. "*Ma.*"

And then everybody was trying it.

It was chaos, for a while. I will not pretend it was not. There were people holding their hands round candles and lamp-wicks and splinters of kindling and, in one case, a turnip. There were people laughing and people crying and one old man who sat down in the middle of the floor with a lit wick in his hand and simply would not get up. The retired Guild clerk lit a scrap of oily rag and then apologised to it. The tinkers' grandmother lit the end of her pipe, which she had not been able to light for a week, and said that if this was the end of the world it was very convenient. Three small girls lit the hem of Sister Hale's shawl by accident, and had to put it out, which they found they could also do, quite easily, by simply wishing it wouldn't.

But it was true, all of it. Every one of them could do it. Every one the little flame had rested on, every man and woman and child on Cinder Hill — they had only to hold their hands round a wick and wish it lit, and give it, and it lit. Not with oil-fire, or glowstone-light, or the grey half-light of the Guild's lamps. With the Flame. The true one. And when Wren, hardly breathing, held out her own lamp to the little candle in Dick Crane's fist — the lamp the Lampwright had carried down the Mountain — the two flames leaned towards each other, and touched, and were the same.

In the end Sister Hale had to find her crutch under the bench, and climb up onto the anvil with three people holding her, and bang the crutch on the anvil until it rang like a bell.

"*Ducks!*" she shouted. "*Ducks!* Before somebody burns the roof off!"

The noise died down, a bit.

"Now listen," said Sister Hale. She was still crying; she had not stopped since the wind came in. But her voice was as strong as ever. "You've had it given you. All of you. The gift. Him that waited seventy years and him that came up the Hill yesterday, all the same. I don't know what's going to happen now. I've never known less in my life. But I know this." She pointed with her crutch at the racks of lamps, burning. "There's a hundred of those, and a whole country out there sitting in the grey. And we've been told what to do. So we'd better have our breakfast, and then we'd better do it."

---

They had their breakfast. It was the best breakfast anybody on Cinder Hill had ever eaten, though it was only porridge and peas, because everybody's faces had come back.

That was the other thing that people noticed, as the light grew. The colour had come up in their faces. Not just the pinkness that comes from singing in a warm room, or from crying, but colour — real colour, as if it had been in them all along under the grey, like a fire under ash, and somebody had blown on it. The tinkers' faces were brown and their hair was a red that nobody had seen in Gloamhaven for three hundred years. Sister Hale's cheeks were like two apples. Mira's beads, which she had always been told were "colours" and had never quite believed were different from each other, were blue and yellow and green and a pink so bright that she sat and stared at them through the whole of her porridge and had to be reminded to eat.

And Wren's ribbon, the one from her mother, on her left wrist, which had been grey and then red and then grey again and then red, was red now all the way to the knot. And it stayed red, even when she put the lamp away inside her coat, and walked out of the circle of its light into the grey morning.

She stood on the brow of the Hill and looked at it for a long time.

---

Later — much later, when the sun was up as far as the pale sun of Gloamhaven ever went up, and the Remnant had gone down to the camp to argue about what to do next — Wren sat alone in the workshop doorway with the lamp on her knees.

It burned there, small and gold, exactly as it always had.

Behind her the hundred lamps burned on their racks, each one just like it. Down the Hill, from the camp, she could see little flames going about, carried in people's hands: lighting cooking-fires, lighting pipes, lighting each other. A child ran past with a candle and stopped to light another child's, and they both ran on. It was the most wonderful thing she had ever seen, and she had seen the Ring of Emberwick go gold all at once in the dark.

And she did not know what she felt.

She was glad. She was so glad she could hardly breathe. It was what she had wanted since the morning in Soot Lane when she had gone door to door: for it not to stop with her, for it to go on, and on. *What is given, grows.* Here it was, growing. All of it at once.

But there was something else under the gladness, small and sore, like a splinter under a nail; and she did not want to look at it.

"Then what am I *for*?" she said to the lamp.

She said it very quietly. She did not mean anyone to hear.

The lamp did not answer. It never had, except once, in a garret, a very long time ago. It only burned, gold and steady, leaning a little to the east.

Cinder came out of the workshop behind her, rather slowly, as if she were full of porridge, which she was. She did not say anything either. But she sat down on the step beside Wren and leaned her whole small warm weight against Wren's leg, the way she used to lean on Ash's; and after a while Wren put her hand on the fox's head, and left it there.

## Every Ring and Past Them

They argued about it for three days.

I do not say this to be unkind to the Remnant. It is only that there were several hundred of them, and every one of them had been given the same gift in the same moment, and every one had a different idea about what to do with it first. The cobbler wanted to go straight to Emberwick and pull down Prowle's bricks with his own hands. The tinkers wanted to go everywhere at once, which is what tinkers always want. The retired Guild clerk wanted to make a list. Several of the old people wanted to stay on the Hill for ever, in the warm, by the forges, and simply sing; and it was hard to blame them.

In the end it was Sister Hale who settled it, as she settled most things: by banging her crutch on the anvil until everyone stopped talking, and then telling them what they had all known already.

"He said every Ring," she said. "And past them. So we'll go to every Ring. And past them."

"But which first?" said the cobbler.

"All of them first," said Sister Hale. "There's enough of us now, duck. That's the whole point."

So they went out in bands.

They went two and three together, never fewer — Sister Hale was firm about that, because of the grey captain on the roads — and each band took lamps from the racks. A hundred lamps among several hundred people does not sound like very many. But Tobin pointed out, rather pleased with himself, that it did not need to be: every one of them could light a wick with a warm hand now, and the lamps were only for carrying, for the long nights and the wind. "It's not the lamps that matter," he said. "It's the — the people. The lamps are just *containers*." And Sister Hale said that was the cleverest thing he'd said all week, and Tobin was so pleased he walked into a tent-pole.

The tinkers went west to Thornmere, where the marsh-towns were. Three fisherwomen and a cooper went south-west to Vaelport, with a letter from Mira for Pim and another for the Pellows — Mira had written them herself, very slowly, and Tobin had only had to correct four words. The Delvers who had come up for Midsummer went back north to the Black Stair, with lamps tied to their packs, to carry the Flame down into Deepholm, where it had not been since the Warm Man. A party of farmers went out across the Burned Country to the farms. The clerk went with them, to make a list.

Sister Hale stayed. Somebody had to, she said: to keep the Hill, and the forges, and the people who would go on coming; and anyway her arm was not mended yet, and she was not as young as she had been, and if she went off down the road with a crutch and a sling the Guild would think it was Midsummer every day.

"You'll be all right, ducks," she said to Wren and Tobin and Mira on the last morning. "You're the ones I'm sending furthest."

"Where?" said Tobin.

"Emberwick," said Sister Hale.

Wren went cold and hot all at once.

"Home," said Sister Hale. She looked at Wren with her one bright eye. "It's the hardest. You'll find that out. It's always hardest at home. There's people there who knew you when you were a sweep and a thief, and they'll want you to be a sweep and a thief still, because then they know where they are. And there's Prowle, and his bricks, and his fee. And there's the grey captain somewhere between here and there." She patted Wren's cheek. "But it was your Ring first, duck. You lit it. You go and light it again."

---

So they went north and west from Cinder Hill, down out of the Burned Country, with the forge-fires of the workshop behind them and the green fingers of grass running down the slope after them as far as the plain.

They were three, and a fox: Wren, with her lamp inside her coat and two of the hundred lamps strapped to her pack; Tobin, with Ash's pole and a bag that clinked, because he had taken as many tools from the workshop wall as Sister Hale would let him; Mira, with the Chronicle in its flour-sack bag across her back; and Cinder, who did not carry anything, and ran ahead, and ran back, and ran ahead again, as if the whole of Gloamhaven were a field she had been let out into after a long winter.

It was different, walking now.

Wren noticed it on the very first day, and kept noticing it, the way you keep putting your tongue into the gap where a tooth has come out. On every road she had ever walked in Gloamhaven, she had been the one with the light. On the Greywaste, in the Deeps, on the Black Stair, in Vaelport, on the long road east to the Hill — always, when the dark came down, there had been one small lamp, and it had been hers, and everybody had stood close round it, and she had felt the weight and the warmth of it against her ribs like a second heart.

Now, at the end of the first day, when the dark came down on the plain and they stopped to make camp in the lee of a broken wall, Tobin knelt and cupped his ink-stained hands round a little heap of thornwood and blew on it, and it caught. And Mira lit a candle-stub with her fingers to read by, and set it on a stone. And there was light all round, from three places; and Wren sat with her own lamp in her lap and did not need to take it out at all.

It was wonderful. It was what she had wanted.

She went to sleep that night with her back to the fire.

---

On the third day they came to the Scarecrow Farms.

Wren knew it before she saw it: the smell of sour earth, and a stillness in the air, and a certain flatness in the grey light, as if the very ground were tired. Then they came over a low rise and there were the fields, stretching away on either side of the road — small, stony, walled, with crops in them so stunted and sour that you could hardly tell barley from weed.

And in the fields, the scarecrows.

Tobin stopped. Mira stopped. They had not been this way before. They had heard Wren tell it, but it is one thing to hear about a thing and another to see it with your own eyes. There they were, in every field, near and far: grey people, standing among the grey crops. Men and women, and some not much more than children. They did not move. They did not look up. They stood in the middle of the fields in the grey afternoon with their arms at their sides and their faces turned towards nothing, the way they had stood since one evening — weeks ago, or months, or years — when they had been working in the fields as the dark came down, and the Hollows came over the hills, and they had simply stopped. And never come in.

"Oh," said Mira, very quietly.

Tobin did not say anything. He took off his spectacles and cleaned them on his shirt, though they did not need it, and put them back on.

"Come on," said Wren. "It's not far."

---

The Heskeths' farm was the last on the road before the coast hills: a low stone house with a turf roof, and a byre, and a yard full of mud, and a field behind it where — Wren remembered with a lurch — one grey man had stood among the turnips and turned his head to look at the house as she walked away.

She did not have to knock.

The door burst open before she was halfway across the yard, and something small and ginger came out of it like a cork out of a bottle, and hit Wren in the middle at full speed, so that she sat down in the mud.

"You came *back*," said Posy Hesketh, into Wren's stomach. "Mam said you wouldn't. Mam said you'd *forgot* us. I said you hadn't. I said—" She sat up on Wren's knees and glared at her. "You've been *ages*."

"I've been on a hill," said Wren, winded. "Shelling peas."

"For *ages*?"

"For ages."

Posy considered this, and decided to forgive it. She was not grey any more. She was pink and brown and grubby and alive from her muddy boots to her hair, which stood out round her head like a marigold that had been through a hedge backwards; and she was a great deal heavier than she had been when Wren had last seen her, lying in a box-bed with her eyes on the dead fire, not opening her mouth. Wren held on to her for a moment longer than she needed to.

Then Mag Hesketh was in the doorway, bony and frowning and wet-eyed, with flour to her elbows, saying "Posy, you'll have her in the midden, get *up*—" and then not saying anything, but only coming across the yard and pulling Wren out of the mud by both hands and holding on to them. And behind her came Will Hesketh, long and silent, with Hal and Bess peering round his legs, exactly as before.

"You've brought friends," said Mag at last, looking at Tobin and Mira and Cinder in the gateway. Her voice had gone sharp to cover the wobble, the way it did. "Well, you'll have to share a bowl. We've not got enough for five extra. Four. Is the fox eating?"

"The fox is always eating," said Tobin.

"Then five," said Mag Hesketh, and went in to make the soup thinner.

---

It was not thin soup, as it turned out.

That was the first surprise. When Wren had been here before, the Heskeths had had a pot of grey turnip-water and one crust, kept back for a worse day. Now, though the fields round the house were still sour and stunted, there was a little patch of garden by the door where things were *growing* — beans, and kale, and onions, green and vigorous, as green as the grass on Cinder Hill. And there was bread. Mag had baked.

"It started the day you left," said Mag, ladling. "Will lit the fire with what you'd left in the grate. And from then on — I don't know. The garden. Just by the house. Just where the fire's warm through the wall." She banged the ladle on the pot. "Not the fields. The fields are as they were." She looked out of the door, and her face changed. "And *they're* as they were."

Nobody needed to ask who she meant.

"Will goes out to them," said Mag. "In the evenings. He takes a lantern, though it does no good. He stands with them. His brother's out there. And the Coles from over the way, and their eldest." She put the ladle down. "Hal was nearly one. Did you know? Last back-end. Came in at dusk with his face gone grey and couldn't say his own name. It was only his da carrying him in by force that brought him round." She looked at Wren, hard. "That little light of yours. Can it do them? Or is it only for the young ones?"

Wren put down her spoon.

"It's not only mine," she said. "Not any more."

---

They went out to the fields as the dark was coming down.

All of them went: Wren and Tobin and Mira, and Cinder, and the Heskeths, even Posy, who was not supposed to and came anyway, holding on to the back of Wren's coat. They went out through the gate and along the stony headland to the first field, where the nearest scarecrow stood — a tall man, grey from his boots to his bare head, with his hands hanging at his sides and his face turned towards the coast hills.

"That's Tom," said Will Hesketh.

It was the first thing Wren had ever heard him say.

She had meant to do it herself. She had been meaning to all through supper — to walk out to the field and take out the lamp, as she had in the box-bed, as she had in Soot Lane, as she always had. She had her hand inside her coat already.

But Mira was there first.

Mira did not take out a lamp. She went up to the grey man in the field — not afraid at all; she had stood among grey people before, in the dark of Deepholm when the Hollows came, and she was not afraid of them now — and she took his hanging grey hand in both of hers, as if she were introducing herself. And she said, "Good evening, Tom. I'm Mira."

And she *gave* it.

Wren saw it go. She had never seen it from outside before; she had always been the one holding the lamp. It went out of Mira's hands into the grey man's hand, a warmth you could see, like heat shimmering over a road in summer, and a faint gold glow that ran up his wrist under the grey skin and into his arm and his chest and his neck — and his face.

Tom Hesketh blinked.

He looked down at his hand in Mira's hands. He looked at Mira. He looked across the field at his brother, and the house beyond, where a window was lit; and the grey went out of his face like frost off a window when the sun comes round, and his face was brown, and his hair was brown, and his eyes were full.

"Will?" he said. His voice was rusty. "Will. I've been — what time is it? I've — I'm late for my supper."

Will Hesketh did not say anything. He walked across the furrows and put his long arms round his brother and held on to him, in the middle of the sour field, in the dusk. And Wren saw his shoulders shake.

---

After that, it went fast.

Tobin did the Coles' eldest, in the next field: a girl of about fifteen who woke up talking about a cow she had been supposed to be milking. Mira did two more. Wren did one, an old woman by the far wall, with her own lamp, and the old woman's hand came alive and gripped hers so hard it hurt. And then Will Hesketh — who was going from one to the next behind them, looking at each grey face as if he knew it, which he did — stopped in front of a grey boy of about Hal's age at the end of the headland, and stood there, and looked at his own big work-worn hands.

"I don't know how," he said. It was the second thing Wren had heard him say. "I'm not — I wasn't on your hill."

"Hold out your hands," said Tobin.

Will held them out.

Wren took out the lamp. She had not meant to; it was simply what her hands did. She held it out, the way she had held it out to Posy, with nothing asked. And the little flame inside the cracked chimney leaned out towards Will Hesketh's hands — and then, quite gently, went into them.

Not out of the lamp. The lamp burned on in Wren's fingers, exactly the same. But there was a second flame now, small and gold, standing for a moment on the top of Will Hesketh's bare head like a candle on a cake. And then it sank in.

Will looked at his hands. They were glowing faintly, as if he were holding them up to a fire.

Then he turned round and put them on the grey boy's shoulders.

---

By full dark they had done all the Heskeths' fields, and the Coles', and the next farm's.

By midnight there were lights going on across the whole valley.

Wren stood on the stone wall at the top of the Heskeths' last field with Posy asleep on her back and watched them. Will Hesketh had gone to the Coles' with a lantern, and Tom with him. From the Coles' a boy had gone running to the next farm with a candle in his cupped hands. From the next farm, two more lights had set out, and they had split at the crossroads and gone two ways. And so it went on, out across the dark Scarecrow Farms, field by field and house by house — a lantern here, a candle there, a fire lit in a cold kitchen, a window glowing — little lights going away from her in every direction, further and further, until they were only gold pricks in the black, like stars that had come down to walk about on the ground.

None of them was her.

She had not lit them. She had lit one old woman and given one flame to Will. All the rest had been lit by people she had never met and would never meet, going on into the dark with the Flame in their hands, not needing her at all.

It was what she had wanted. *What is given, grows.* It was the most wonderful thing in the world.

Wren stood on the wall with her arms round Posy's legs and found that she was crying, and did not know which kind of crying it was.

"They're going a long way," said Mira, beside her, softly.

"I know."

"Are you all right?"

"No," said Wren. "Yes. I don't know." She wiped her face on her shoulder, because her hands were full of Posy. "I'm just tired."

Mira did not say anything. She was very good at not saying anything; it was one of the things Wren liked best about her. She only stood on the wall beside her, and they watched the lights together.

It was Cinder who saw the rider.

She was sitting on the wall at Wren's feet, and she stopped watching the lights, and turned her head, and stared up the road towards the north, the way they would go in the morning. A low growl came out of her, so quiet it was almost only a feeling.

Wren looked.

On the skyline, at the top of the long rise where the road went over towards Emberwick, there was a horseman. He was only a shape against the very faint grey of the northern sky — a tall man on a tall horse, in a long cloak, quite still. He was not coming any closer. He was not going away. He was simply there, at the top of the road, watching the little lights go out across the valley one after another; and even from so far off, even in the dark, Wren knew exactly who he was.

Then he turned his horse and was gone over the rise.

## The Grey Captain

They smelt the barn before they saw it.

It was the fifth morning out of the Scarecrow Farms, on a lonely stretch of road through low grey hills, and the smell came down the wind to them — old smoke, wet ash, the sour stink of burned thatch that has been rained on. Cinder smelt it first. She stopped in the middle of the road with one forepaw lifted and her spark gone small and tight, and would not go on until Wren picked her up.

The barn stood in a hollow below the road, or what was left of it did. The stone walls were still there, blackened to the top. The roof had fallen in. The great doors lay flat on the ground in front of it, burned through, with the iron hinges still on them, twisted like liquorice. Round about in the grey grass were scattered things: a shoe, a broken stool, a tin cup, a child's rag doll trodden into the mud.

And on the lintel over the empty doorway, carved small and deep into the stone, a little flame.

"A meeting-house," said Mira quietly. "Sister Hale said. They carve the flame over the door, so the ones who know, know."

Tobin went down into the hollow. Wren did not want to, but she went after him, with Cinder in her arms and her heart going hard. Inside the walls there was nothing but black timbers and ash. No people. That was something. But on the beaten-earth floor near the door, where the rain had not reached, there were the marks of many feet, and the long scuffed lines where people had been dragged; and outside, going away up the slope to the road, the deep hoof-prints of heavy horses, and between them the prints of people walking. Close together. In a line.

"Roped," said Tobin. His voice was very flat. "The drover said. He ropes them."

"Where to?" said Wren.

Tobin looked up the slope, at the road, and the way the hoof-prints turned on it. North.

"The same way we're going," he said.

---

They saw the ash-crows after that.

There had always been one or two. They had followed Wren across the Burned Country in the spring, grey birds with grey eyes that sat on dead trees and watched; Sister Hale said they were the Queen's spies, and went to her with everything they saw. But now there were more. There was one on every second fence-post. There were three on the signpost at the crossroads, and when Wren threw a stone at them they only shuffled along a little and looked at her. There was one that followed them for a whole afternoon, flapping along a field's length behind and settling, flapping and settling, until Cinder lost her temper and sneezed a shower of sparks at it from under a hedge, and it went off squawking with its tail-feathers smoking.

"She knows we're coming," said Wren.

"She knew we'd come," said Mira. "She's not stupid. Only wrong."

---

That evening they came to Stallbridge.

It was a Ring-town, the first they had come to on this road: a small, tight, grey town of perhaps two thousand people, packed inside a stone wall on a hill above a river, with a bridge, and a gate, and on the top of the wall a Ring of lamps — thirty-six of them, Tobin counted, though only about two thirds were lit, and those burned the tired yellow-grey of Guild oil running low.

The gate was shut.

That was unusual. In Gloamhaven a Ring-town's gate was shut at dark, when the Hollows might come; but it was not dark yet. And there were guards on the gate-tower, more than you would expect for a small town, and they had seen the three children coming up the road a long way off and were leaning over the parapet to look.

"What's your business?" called the gate-captain.

"We've brought light," said Wren. "For nothing. For anybody that wants it."

There was a silence.

Then a different voice spoke from the tower, a man's, older, and cold. "So it's true. You're the Cinder sect."

He came to the parapet. He was a thin man in a fine grey coat with a chain round his neck — not gold like Prowle's, but silver, smaller: the Warden of Stallbridge. He looked down at them over his spectacles, and Wren thought, quite suddenly, of Lord Edric Harrowgate looking down at her from the steps of the Warden's Hall, the day he had demanded the lamp.

"We have had a letter from the Steward of Emberwick," said the Warden of Stallbridge. "And from Captain Varrick, who rode through here two days ago. We know what you are. Witches. Thieves of Guild light. You light lamps with your bare hands, and draw the Hollows down on every town you enter. Captain Varrick says that wherever the Cinder sect goes, the Queen's anger follows." He straightened up. "I will not have it in Stallbridge. Our Ring is weak enough. Go away."

"Your Ring's *failing*," said Tobin. "Sir. Respectfully. A third of your lamps are out. I can see from here. Your oil must be watered — the colour's wrong. If you'd only let us—"

"*Go away*," said the Warden, and went down from the parapet.

The gate stayed shut.

---

They camped that night by the river, under the bridge, out of the wind.

Wren was furious. She lay on her back on the stones with her arms folded and glared at the underside of the bridge and said everything she thought about the Warden of Stallbridge, and the Steward of Emberwick, and Captain Varrick, and wardens in general, and people with chains round their necks, and people who would sooner sit in the grey than say thank you; and Tobin, who on another night would have pointed out that some of it was unfair, said nothing at all, because he agreed with most of it.

"He's frightened," said Mira, when Wren had run out.

"*Everybody's* frightened. That's no excuse."

"No," said Mira. "But it's a reason." She was sitting with her back to a pier of the bridge, with her eyes shut, listening. "Hush a moment."

Wren hushed.

"Somebody's coming," said Mira. "Down the bank. From the town. Three — no, four. Light feet." She opened her eyes. "Children."

They came down the river-bank in the dark, slipping and sliding and shushing each other: four of them, the oldest about Wren's age, the youngest perhaps six, all thin and all grey-faced in the gloom. The oldest, a scrawny boy with a squint, held a stub of rushlight in a jar that hardly lit his own fingers.

He stopped at the edge of the bridge-shadow and looked at them.

"Is it true?" he said. "What they said. You can light it with your hands?"

"How did you get out?" said Tobin. "The gate—"

"Over the wall," said the boy, as if it were obvious. "There's a place by the tannery. Everybody knows it. Only the grown-ups." He looked at Wren. "My nan's grey. Since the winter. Bel's ma." He jerked his chin at the smallest girl, who was holding on to his coat. "The Warden says it's only the cold. He says it'll pass. It doesn't pass." His voice cracked, and he scowled to cover it, and Wren knew that scowl, because she had worn it herself every day of her life in Soot Lane. "So is it true? Or are you just — liars?"

Wren got up.

She did not take out her lamp. She took one of the two lamps from the racks of Cinder Hill out of her pack — a new, bright, undented brass one — and walked across the stones to the boy, and held it out to him.

"Here," she said. "It's for you."

The boy looked at it. He did not take it.

"How much?"

It was so exactly what she would have said, a year ago, that Wren nearly laughed.

"Nothing," she said. "It's given."

"Everything costs."

"Not this." She pushed it into his hands. "Go on. Take it. It's yours now. Light your nan."

And as the boy's hands closed on it, wondering, the little gold flame stood up out of its empty brass mouth — and leaned towards him — and on the top of his tufty head, for the space of a breath, another little flame stood and sank in.

The boy's face went brown. His squint did not change, but his eyes, which had been grey, were green.

He stood with the lamp in his hands and stared at it, and then at Wren, and then — without a word — he turned and ran back up the river-bank in the dark, with the other three scrambling after him, and the light going bobbing away up the slope towards the wall.

---

Half an hour later there was a light on the wall of Stallbridge, by the tannery.

Then two.

Then, one by one, on the dark top of the wall where the Warden's Ring-lamps were sputtering out, small gold lights began going along it from the tannery end — not Ring-lamps; children, running along the wall-walk in the dark, with candles in their hands, stopping at each failing Ring-lamp to hold the candle up to its wick. And the Ring-lamps of Stallbridge, one after another, went from tired yellow-grey to gold.

The three of them stood on the bridge and watched it, and nobody said anything for a long time.

"They'll be in such trouble," said Tobin at last. He sounded delighted.

"Good," said Wren.

They did not go up. The gate stayed shut, and it was not their gate to open; and Mira said, quite firmly, that the children of Stallbridge could do the rest, and they would only be in the way. So in the morning, very early, before the Warden could send anybody down to the bridge, they went on north.

Wren looked back from the top of the hill. The Ring of Stallbridge was gold all the way round.

---

It was two days later that the grey captain nearly caught them.

They were on a stretch of road where it sank down between high banks — a holloway, Tobin called it, worn deep into the ground by hundreds of years of carts, with hedges along the top meeting overhead, so that it was like walking along the bottom of a long green-grey tunnel. It was late afternoon. They were tired, and Cinder was riding on Tobin's shoulders, and nobody was talking.

Mira stopped.

She did not say anything. She held up one hand, and tipped her head, and the other two stopped dead behind her, because they had both learned a long time ago that when Mira tipped her head like that you did not ask questions.

Then she said, very quietly, "Horses. Behind us. A lot of them. Heavy. Walking." A pause. "And feet. Between the horses. People walking. Slowly." Another pause. "And something that rattles. Like — chains, or—"

"Ropes," said Tobin. "Ropes don't rattle." But his face had gone white.

"Buckles," said Mira. "On ropes." She turned round. "They're coming round the bend. Now. *Now*, Wren—"

There was nowhere to go. The banks of the holloway were higher than Tobin's head, and thick with hedge on top, and the road ran straight ahead for a quarter of a mile with no turning. But at the foot of the left-hand bank, where the rain ran off, there was a ditch: deep, narrow, half full of dead leaves and old black water, overhung with brambles.

They went into it.

It was cold. It was wet. It smelt of rotting leaves and frog. Wren lay flat on her face in the black water with her arms over the lamp inside her coat, to keep the light from showing, and Cinder flat under her chin, and Tobin's boot in her ear, and Mira's elbow in her back; and pulled the brambles down over them, and lay still.

The sound came round the bend.

Horses first. Great heavy grey horses, walking slow, their hoofs striking the stones of the holloway one after another — *clop … clop … clop* — and on them, high up, the Hollow Knights. Wren could see them through the brambles, a little, from below: grey armour, grey cloaks, grey faces under their open helms, empty eyes that did not look right or left. They rode in pairs. There were perhaps twenty.

And between them, walking, the prisoners.

They were roped at the wrists, and roped to each other, in a long shuffling line. Men and women and some children. Remnant folk, by their clothes: farmers and townspeople, an old woman, a boy with a bandaged head. Their faces were not grey. That was the worst of it. They had not been greyed; they were themselves, and they were frightened, and they walked with their heads down, and some of them were crying very quietly as they walked, and none of them looked up.

One of the knights at the back had a sack slung across his saddle. It was a big sack, and it bulged, and as he went past the ditch Wren saw that the sacking glowed faintly gold here and there through the weave, like a lantern with a cloth over it. And she knew, the way you know a smell, what was in it.

Lamps. Lamps from the racks of Cinder Hill. Taken.

And in the middle of the column, on a tall black horse, rode the captain.

---

He came past the ditch so close that Wren could have put out her hand from under the brambles and touched his stirrup.

She had seen him many times. On the Greywaste, a grey rider far off on a rise. On the Black Stair, with the Knights behind him. In the Glass Palace, in shadow behind Oskar Venn's chair. On the lighthouse at Vaelport, laughing. On the black hill at dawn, with her own lamp in his hand, being kind to her, and then — at the very end — staring at a stranger's scarred hands with a face like a man who has been hit.

She had never seen him like this.

He was grey still — grey armour, grey cloak, grey eyes. But the smoothness had gone out of him. His face, which had always been calm and pale and polite, like a carving, was drawn and hollow, with dark marks under the eyes as if he had not slept for weeks; and his mouth was a hard line; and he sat his horse not easily, as he used to, but hunched forward, as if he were riding into a gale that nobody else could feel.

His right gauntlet was off. It hung from his saddle by its strap. And with his left thumb he was rubbing the palm of his right hand — rubbing and rubbing at the old burn scar there, hard, over and over, so hard that the skin round it was red and raw.

He did not know he was doing it. Wren could see that. He was looking straight ahead up the holloway with those grey exhausted eyes, and his thumb went on rubbing, and rubbing, as if he were trying to rub something out.

Then he was past.

The column went on up the holloway, *clop … clop … clop*, with the shuffle of the prisoners' feet and the creak and jingle of buckled rope; and the sound got smaller, and went round the next bend, and was gone.

---

They did not get out of the ditch for a long time.

When they did, they were black with mud from head to foot and shaking with cold, and Cinder was so offended that she would not look at anybody. Tobin sat on the bank and emptied the water out of his boots, one after the other, and did not say anything. Mira wrung out the Chronicle's flour-sack and checked the book inside it page by page, and it was dry, and she let out a long breath.

Wren stood in the middle of the holloway looking up the road where they had gone.

She was shaking too, but not from cold. She had her fists clenched so tight that her nails were cutting into her palms. She was thinking of the boy with the bandaged head. The old woman. The sack, glowing. The people who had been given the Flame on Cinder Hill, and gone out with it on the roads, two and three together, as Sister Hale had told them, and been taken.

"I hope it burns him," she said.

Her voice was low and shaking and not like her own.

"I hope the Flame burns him to *nothing*. I hope he picks up one of those lamps in that sack and it burns his hand right off. I hope—"

"Wren," said Mira.

"I *hope* it does."

Nobody said anything else. After a while they went on, because there was nothing else to do. They went on up the holloway the same way the captain had gone, slowly, keeping well back, with Mira listening at every bend.

But at the top of the road the holloway came out of the ground and the hedges fell away, and the column had turned off — east, along a drove-road over the hills, towards the high grey moors on the edge of the sky. And their own road went on north, straight down a long slope towards a wide grey plain.

And on the plain, far off, they could see Emberwick.

Wren had not seen it since the morning she walked out of the Grey Gate with Ash and Tobin and Cinder, more than a year ago, onto the Greywaste. She had forgotten how big it was. It lay across the plain in the gloaming like a great dark beast asleep: the wall, the chimneys, the lamp-towers, the canal-glint, the Hill with the Warden's Hall on top; and all the way round it, on the wall, the Ring.

It was gold.

All one hundred and forty-four lamps of it, from the Grey Gate round to the South Gate and back: gold, true gold, not one of them dim, burning steady round the whole city in the dusk like a crown — the crown she had given it, a year ago, from the top of the Beacon.

Wren stood at the top of the hill and looked at it, and for a moment she forgot the ditch and the column and her nails in her palms.

Then she looked inside the Ring.

The Hill, where the rich lived, was lit; she could see the windows of the big houses, and lamps along the streets, and the Beacon in its square in the middle, burning gold at the top of its tower. But below the Hill, inside the gold Ring, on the low side of the city by the canal and the tanneries, where Soot Lane was —

There was nothing.

The Low Quarter was dark.

## The Steward's Fee

They came in by the South Gate in the morning, in the crowd of carts going to market, with their hoods up.

Nobody stopped them. It was a busy gate, and the guards on it were bored and cold, and three muddy children and a grey dog — Cinder had rolled in the ditch again, on purpose, to hide her spark under the mud, and was very pleased with herself — were not the sort of thing anybody looked at twice. But just inside the gate, on the wall of the gate-house, there was a notice. It was printed in big black letters, and it had a seal at the bottom, and it had been put up so recently that the paste was still wet.

*BY ORDER OF THE LORD STEWARD OF EMBERWICK.*
*Be it known that the FLAME of the GREAT BEACON is the PROPERTY of the LAMP GUILD, being housed in a Guild Tower, and is held IN TRUST for the Public Good.*
*Any Person desiring a Light therefrom shall present himself at the STEWARD'S GATE in BEACON SQUARE between the hours of Nine and Four, and pay the STEWARD'S FEE of ONE SILVER PENNY the Light.*
*The kindling of Lamps, Candles, Rushes or Fires by any OTHER MEANS — namely, by the Hand — is THEFT from the GUILD, and the practice of the so-called CINDER SECT, and shall be punished.*
*A REWARD is offered.*
*Signed: C. PROWLE, Lord Steward.*

"*By the hand*," said Tobin, reading it twice. "He's made it against the law to light a candle with your *hand*."

"He can't make it against the law," said Wren. "It's not *his*."

"He's the Steward," said Mira. "He can make anything against the law. That's what stewards are for." She looked at the notice for a long moment, as if she were memorising it. "It's a very frightened notice. Isn't it? You can hear it. All those capitals."

---

Emberwick had changed.

It took Wren a while to see how. At first glance it was the same city she had grown up in: the same crooked streets and steep roofs, the same chimneys, the same smell of canal-water and coal-smoke and tannery, the same noise. Carts. Barrows. A man selling hot chestnuts. Two dogs fighting over a bone. A woman leaning out of an upstairs window to shout at somebody in the street.

But the people looked up.

That was what she noticed first. When she had lived here, nobody in Emberwick had looked up much; there was nothing up there but grey sky and the pale sun like a coin seen through dirty water. Now, as they walked through the streets, people kept glancing up — at the top of the wall, where the Ring-lamps burned gold even in the daytime; and then, quickly, down again, as if they were not supposed to.

And the colour.

Under the gold Ring, Emberwick had colour again. Not much, and not everywhere. But Wren saw a door that was green. She saw a woman's shawl that was a deep warm brown, and a child's cheeks that were pink, and a cart of apples — real red apples, small and wrinkled from the farms, but *red*. The Ring had been burning true for a year, and a year of true light, even at a distance, even only on the top of a wall, had soaked into the city the way rain soaks into a dry field.

On the Hill, especially. As they went up the main street towards Beacon Square, the houses got bigger and the colours got brighter, until the fronts of the great houses of the rich were cream and red and blue, and the window-boxes had flowers in them.

And then, at the top, Beacon Square.

---

The Great Beacon stood in the middle of the square as it always had: the tallest tower in Emberwick, three hundred steps of spiral stair up to the vast iron bowl at the top. And in the bowl, the Flame. Wren tipped her head back and looked up at it, and her throat went tight. She had carried it up those steps herself, in the dark, with the Queen's voice in her ears and a warm hand on her shoulder at the top. She had given it to the Beacon. And from the Beacon it had gone down the copper flame-pipes under the city to all one hundred and forty-four lamps of the Ring at once, and Emberwick had come alive.

It was still burning. Gold, enormous, steady, like a second sun in the top of the tower.

But the door at the foot of the tower — the door that led to the stair, which had always stood open, which anybody could go in at — was gone. In its place was a wall. New bricks, raw red, mortared tight, filling the doorway from the step to the top of the arch.

And in front of the wall, a gate.

It was an iron gate, painted Guild-gold, with spikes on the top and a little sentry-box beside it with a window, and inside the sentry-box a clerk in a Guild coat with a ledger and a cash-box. A brazier stood next to the gate, burning with a pale gold flame; the clerk lit tapers from it and handed them out through the bars to whoever paid. Above the gate, on a board, in gold letters: *THE STEWARD'S GATE. ONE SILVER PENNY THE LIGHT.*

There was a queue. It was not long. The people in it were well-dressed — servants from the big houses, mostly, with lanterns, sent to fetch the light for their masters' lamps. As Wren watched, a footman in a blue coat paid his penny, and the clerk wrote in the ledger, and lit a taper from the brazier, and passed it out through the bars; and the footman lit his lantern from it and walked away up the street, and the clerk blew the taper out.

"He's selling it," said Wren. "He's *selling* it."

"It doesn't burn him," said Tobin, frowning. "The clerk. Selling it ought to burn him. It burned the pawnbroker's fingers, in Crookback Market. You told me."

"It's not the lamp," said Mira. "It's the Beacon's fire. It was given to the city, and it's still in the city; he's only put a gate round it. You can't take it. So he's — kept people away from it." She was looking at the brazier with her head on one side. "That's not the Flame, though. In the brazier. Listen to it. It's thin. He's lighting it from the Beacon, I expect, and then feeding it with oil, so it lasts longer and costs less. It's half Guild." She shook her head. "It'll go out by tonight."

"So people pay a penny for a light that goes out," said Tobin.

"And come back tomorrow," said Mira, "and pay another."

Wren could not speak at all.

---

It was a quarter of an hour's walk from Beacon Square down to Soot Lane, and she did it in five minutes, and the other two had to run to keep up.

Down off the Hill. Through the streets where the houses got smaller and closer and leaned together. Over Tallow Bridge, where the pie-man still had his stall, and was still too dear. Into the Low Quarter. And the colour went out of things as they went down, the way the warmth goes out of a bath; until at the bottom, in the streets by the canal and the tanneries, the doors were grey and the shawls were grey and the faces were grey, and the windows, even at noon, were dark.

Not dark like Emberwick had been before. Wren knew the difference. Before, the whole city had been grey, rich and poor together, under a failing Ring; and the poor had had Guild oil, watered, at prices they could not pay, and had sat in the grey dusk and gone without. Now the Ring was gold. The light was *there*. You could see it from Soot Lane, on the top of the wall, burning. But the people in the houses could not afford the Steward's penny, and were not allowed to light their own; and so they sat in the grey, inside a ring of gold, and looked up at it.

It was worse. It was much worse.

And then she turned the corner into Soot Lane, and the lane was there: crooked, narrow, leaning, with its washing lines and its gutters and its cobbles and its smell; Mott's yard on the left, and the house where her garret had been, and the cold chimney-breast she had slept against; and at the end, on the right, the bakery.

The bakery's windows were lit.

Not much. Only a glow, low down, from the back of the shop. But it was gold.

---

Wren did not remember crossing the lane. She remembered the door, which was the same green it had been the morning she gave Mrs Fenn back her children's names; and the bell over it, which jangled; and the smell of bread, which hit her in the face like a warm wet cloth; and then something small and hard and furious hitting her round the waist, for the second time in a fortnight, so that she sat down on the bakery floor.

"*WREN!*"

It was Nettle.

She was bigger. She was much bigger — she was eight now, and had grown at least a hand's breadth, and her two front teeth had come in, large and square and slightly crooked, like a rabbit's. But the lisp had not gone. And she was crying and laughing and hitting Wren on the shoulder with one fist, all at once, saying "Wren, Wren, you went *away*, you went away for a *year*, you never *thaid*, you never even *thent a letter*—"

"I can't write," said Wren, into Nettle's hair.

"Tobin can write! You could've athked *Tobin*!"

"I did write," said Tobin, from the door. "Twice. I don't think they got here."

"Well, they *didn't*," said Nettle, and burst into fresh tears, and held on.

She did not let go for an hour. I am not making this up. Mrs Fenn came out from the back with flour to her elbows and five children behind her and said "Oh, lovey — oh, *lovey*—" and hugged Wren with Nettle still attached. Mrs Fenn's five children hugged Wren, and Tobin, and Mira, who they had never met and did not mind. Cinder was hugged, and objected, and was given a currant bun, and stopped objecting. And all through it Nettle clung on to Wren's waist like a limpet to a rock, and when Wren sat down she sat on Wren's knee, and when Wren stood up she stood on Wren's feet, and when Wren went to the privy in the yard Nettle waited outside the door, talking to her through it the whole time.

---

Word got round Soot Lane in about a quarter of an hour.

Gristle Mott came first.

Wren heard him before she saw him: the wheeze, and the heavy boots, and the door-bell nearly jangling off its spring. And then he was in the doorway, filling it — big and red-knuckled, but not red-faced any more; he had lost a great deal of weight and gained a great deal of colour, and he smelt, astonishingly, of soap. When he saw Wren he stopped. His great face crumpled up like a paper bag.

"*Ashby*," he said. "Oh, Ashby. Oh, lass."

Wren had once owed this man everything she would ever earn. He had kept her in a cold garret with no coal and docked her pay for every scraped knuckle and told her a hundred times a day that she was worth nothing, and in the end he had sold her to the guards for the reward. And then the lamp had touched him, on the morning after the Ring was lit, and he had sat down in the middle of his yard and wept and said he forgave her debt, and she had not known what to do with that then, and she did not know now.

"Hello, Mr Mott," she said.

"I've got a hat for you," said Mott. He held it out. It was a tall black hat, very old, with a dent in the crown and a feather in the band. "It was my da's. My best. I been keeping it. In case you come back."

"I don't want a hat, Mr Mott."

"I know," said Mott. "I know you don't. Take it anyway." He pushed it at her. "I got nothing else. I been trying to — I give the lads their pay now. All of it. Every week. And coal in the garrets. And I took the bars off the yard gate. But it's not—" His eyes filled up. "It's not enough, is it. It's never enough."

"It's not supposed to be *enough*," said Wren. "That's not how it—" She stopped. She did not know how to say it. She took the hat. "Thank you, Mr Mott."

Mott wept.

---

Snib came second, and nobody saw him come.

The first anyone knew of it was Tobin saying "*Ow*—" and clapping his hand to his face. His spectacles had gone. They had been on his nose one moment and then, between one blink and the next, there was nothing there but a small greasy smudge, and a high squeaky giggle from the top of Mrs Fenn's dresser.

*Shiny-shiny, mine-mine,*
*glass-eyes, two-eyes, nob-boy's, mine!*

There on the top of the dresser, among the jam-jars, sat a Gutterling: knee-high and black as soot, with enormous white eyes and long spidery fingers, and Tobin's spectacles perched on the end of his soot-black nose, much too big, with the cracked lens making one of his white eyes look twice the size of the other.

"Snib," said Wren.

The Gutterling's ears went flat. He had seen her. He gave a squeak of pure delight and came down the dresser like a spider down a wall and across the floor and up her leg and onto her shoulder, where he sat and peered into her face from so close that she could smell him (soot, and something like old pennies), and patted her cheek with his long cold fingers.

"Sweep-girl," he squeaked. "Sweep-girl's back! Bright-sweep. Lamp-sweep. Snib remembers! Snib helped. Over the roofs! For nothing!" He thought about it. "Well. For one button. And then for nothing. *Snib remembers.*"

"I know you do," said Wren. "Give Tobin his spectacles back."

Snib considered this. Then, with enormous dignity, he took the spectacles off his nose and held them out to Tobin at arm's length, and as Tobin reached for them, whisked them back and put them on his own head like a crown; and it took Mrs Fenn's eldest and two buns to get them off him.

---

Captain Bracken came at dusk, and he came alone.

He was in uniform. It was a new uniform — not the old grey-and-black of the Warden's guards, but Guild-gold, with a gold sash and gold buttons and a gold badge on the shoulder with a little flame and the word STEWARD under it. It did not suit him. He looked, Wren thought, like a tired old dog that somebody had dressed up for a fair.

He stood in the door of the bakery with his helmet under his arm and looked at them for a long time. He looked at Wren longest.

"I heard you were back," he said at last.

"Hello, Sergeant Bracken."

"Captain, now." He said it as if it tasted bad. "Captain of the Steward's Guard. Lord Prowle's very generous to them that do as they're told." He looked down at his gold sash and then away. "You shouldn't have come, lass. Not here. He's frightened of you, Prowle is. Frightened men do daft things. And he's got friends now he never had before. Grey ones." He turned his helmet round in his hands. "Captain Varrick sends him letters. I've seen 'em. Sealed in grey."

"Captain Varrick," said Tobin, "ropes people and takes them away."

"I know," said Bracken. "I know he does. I've seen that too." His face was very tired. "I'm only telling you. Go away, if you've any sense. Go tonight. I'll not have seen you."

"We've got no sense," said Wren. "We've come to light Soot Lane."

Bracken looked at her for another long moment.

"Aye," he said. "I thought you'd say that." He put his helmet on. "Then I've not seen you *yet*. But I'll have to, soon. He's got eyes all over this city. And when I've seen you, lass, I'll have to do something about it." He went out, and then put his head back round the door. "I'm sorry," he said, and was gone.

---

That night, in the room behind the bakery, Wren gave Nettle a lamp.

She had not planned to. It was late, and the little ones were asleep, and Mrs Fenn was at the oven doing tomorrow's dough by the light of a single candle-end that she had paid the Steward's penny for that morning, and which was guttering. Nettle was in her nightgown on the floor by the stove, refusing to go to bed in case Wren went away again in the night. And Wren took one of the bright new lamps from Cinder Hill out of her pack, because it was the only thing she had to give her, and held it out.

"Here," she said. "Nettle. It's yours."

Nettle looked at it. She was eight; she had seen a great deal. She had been greyed by a Hollow on the night the Ring failed and forgotten her own name, and been brought back by the lamp on the morning after, and she had watched Wren go along Soot Lane door to door with it in her hands. She did not ask how much.

She reached out and took it in both her small hands.

And the little gold flame stood up out of its empty brass mouth, and leaned towards her; and on the top of Nettle's untidy head, for the space of a breath, another stood, and sank in.

Nettle's face went very still. Then very pink. Then she looked down at her own hands round the lamp, which were glowing faintly, the way the Heskeths' had, the way Will's had in the field.

"Wren," she whispered. "Wren. It'th *in* me."

"I know."

"It'th *warm*."

"I know."

Nettle stood up. She walked across the kitchen with the lamp in one hand, very slowly and carefully, as if she were carrying a full bowl; and she stopped by Mrs Fenn's guttering candle-end, and cupped her other hand round it.

It went up gold.

Then Nettle lit the next candle, on the shelf. And the lamp in the shop window. And the three candle-stubs on the mantelpiece, and the rushlight by the door, and the little tin lantern on its hook, and — before anybody could stop her — she opened the iron door of Mrs Fenn's great bread-oven and put her small warm hand flat on the heap of cold coal inside.

The oven went up with a *whoomph* like a dragon sneezing.

Mrs Fenn screamed. The dough-trough shot back across the floor. Every one of Mrs Fenn's five children woke up at once and came running in their nightgowns. Cinder barked. Snib, who had been asleep in the flour-bin, shot out of it covered in flour, and was white for the first time in his life, and was so horrified that he ran up the chimney. And Nettle stood in front of the roaring oven with the lamp in her hand and her face lit gold, laughing, laughing, as Wren had never in her life heard her laugh.

It was a long time before anybody went to bed.

---

In the morning they started on Soot Lane.

They did it the way Wren had done it the first time, a year ago: door to door, from the top of the lane to the bottom. Knock. Wait. *It's given.* But it was not the same.

The first time, Wren had done every door herself. She had gone all down the lane with the one lamp in her hands, and given it at every door, and she had been so tired by the end of the morning that she could hardly stand. This time she did the first door — old Mrs Peck's, at the top, whose eyes were so bad she could hardly see the flame — and Mrs Peck's grandson, who was about ten, held his hands round the candle while she lit it; and then he went next door with it, and did *that* house; and the woman in that house went across the lane with a rushlight to her sister's.

Nettle did six doors before breakfast. Mrs Fenn's eldest did four. Mott did his whole yard, every garret, and wept at every one. By noon the Flame was going down Soot Lane ahead of them faster than they could walk, and when they got to the bottom the windows were lit behind them all the way back to the top, gold in the grey; and people were standing in their doorways looking at their own hands.

And Wren had lit one door.

She stood at the bottom of the lane and looked back up it, at all the lit windows going up the hill into the grey, and felt the same thing she had felt on the wall at the Heskeths' farm: so glad it hurt, and something else under it, small and sore, that she did not want to look at.

Then Tobin touched her arm.

"Wren," he said, quietly. "Look."

At the top of Soot Lane, where it met the street that went up to the Hill, a carriage had stopped.

It was a fine carriage, gilded, with gold wheels and the Guild's flame painted on the door and two grey horses in the shafts, and it was quite still. One of the windows had been let down. And through it, looking down the length of Soot Lane at the lit windows, one after another, all the way to the bottom — at the people in the doorways, and the children running with candles, and the three of them standing there at the end with a muddy fox — was a round, glossy, lavender-scented face, with a gold chain under it, and an expression on it that Wren had last seen a year ago in the light of the Great Beacon, on the night it was lit.

It was the face of a man who has just seen something that is worth more than all the money in the world, and knows that he will never be able to buy it.

## The Price of Fire

Guild Master Cuthbert Prowle — Lord Steward of Emberwick, Keeper of the Beacon, Protector of the Ring, and several other things that were painted on his carriage door in very small gold letters — came to the bakery in Soot Lane the next afternoon in person.

He did not bring the guard. That was the first surprise. He came with one footman, who held the carriage door, and a strongbox, which the footman carried; and he came in at the bakery door with the bell jangling over his head, and stood just inside it, on the mat, and smiled.

He had not changed. He was still round, and glossy, and pink, like a ham that has been varnished. He still smelt of lavender oil so strongly that Cinder sneezed. He still wore the great gold chain of the Lamp Guild round his neck, and now, under it, another chain, thinner, with a big seal hanging from it that said STEWARD. His coat was plum-coloured velvet. His rings were gold. His smile went from one fat pink cheek to the other and did not go anywhere near his eyes.

"Miss Ashby," he said. "Miss Ashby, Miss Ashby. What a *pleasure*."

Wren was sitting on the counter, eating a bun. She did not get down.

"Hello, Mr Prowle."

"*Lord Steward*, I think, my dear, these days. But never mind. Never mind. Among friends." He looked round the bakery — at the shelves of loaves, and the lit candles, and the bread-oven roaring gold, and Mrs Fenn, frozen by the oven with a tray in her hands, and her five children peering round her skirts, and Nettle, who was standing in front of all of them with her arms folded and her chin out, glaring at him. "What a charming little establishment. And so very — *bright*." His eyes went to the candles. Then to the oven. Then, for a moment, very sharp, to the lamp burning in the window. "Is there somewhere," he said, "we might talk? Business. Between people of the world."

"Here's fine," said Wren.

---

Tobin and Mira came in from the back, where they had been mending Mrs Fenn's dough-trough after the oven-door had blown it across the floor. Tobin had a hammer in his hand. He did not put it down.

Prowle's eyes went to him, and widened, and his smile got a little wider too, the way a cat's does.

"And young Master Harrowgate! How *very* unexpected. Your father's son, in Soot Lane, with a hammer." He shook his head sorrowfully. "Your father would be most distressed. If he were here. Which, sadly—" He made a small gesture with his pink hand, as if brushing away a crumb. "But we won't speak of sad things. Not today. Today I have come to speak of *opportunity*."

He snapped his fingers. The footman put the strongbox down on the bakery floor and opened it.

It was full of gold.

Not silver pennies. Gold crowns, heaped up, shining in the candle-light, more money than Wren had seen in her whole life put together; more than she had ever imagined there being, outside of stories. Mrs Fenn's eldest made a small sound. Snib, who had been asleep in the flour-bin again, stuck his head up out of it and stared, and his long fingers began very slowly to reach.

"Now, Miss Ashby," said Prowle comfortably. "You and I have not always seen eye to eye. That's all water under the bridge, I hope. Spilt oil. I've always been a man to let bygones be bygones." He beamed at her. "Because the truth is, my dear, I *admire* you. I do. Tremendously. You have a — knack. A gift. I have seen it with my own eyes, in this very lane. You and your little friends go from door to door, and the candles light, and the people *glow*, and there's no oil and no flint and no Guild involved anywhere, and it's — well, it's very remarkable. Very remarkable indeed. I have been in the light trade for forty years, Miss Ashby, man and boy, and I have never seen anything like it."

"It's not a knack," said Wren.

"Of course it is, my dear. Everything's a knack, if you know how. And the Guild," said Prowle, "has always paid for expertise." He nudged the strongbox with the toe of his shoe, so that the gold slithered and chimed. "Name your figure."

Nobody said anything.

"For the secret," said Prowle, a little more slowly, in case they had not understood. "The method. How it's done. You show me, and my clerks, and I'll see it's put to proper use. *Regulated*. Not handed out to any tinker on the road, where anybody might get hold of it, but kept safe, the way the Guild has always kept the light safe. Every Ring in Gloamhaven. Licensed. Fair prices." His voice had gone soft and warm, like butter. "Think of it, Miss Ashby. You'd never be poor again. Not one day. Not your little friend there, or the baker, or any of them. I'd make you Mistress of the Guild, if you liked. A gold chain of your own." He spread his hands. "Name your figure."

---

Wren started to laugh.

She did not mean to. It came up out of her the way it had come up out of her at the breakfast fire on Cinder Hill, when the toast was black: helpless, from somewhere under the ribs. She sat on the counter with a half-eaten bun in her hand and laughed and laughed, until her eyes ran and she had to hold on to the edge of the counter not to fall off.

Prowle's smile went stiff.

"I'm sorry," gasped Wren. "I'm sorry, Mr Prowle. It's just — you've come to *buy* it." She wiped her eyes on her sleeve. "You've brought a *box*. Of *gold*. To *Soot Lane*."

"I fail to see," said Prowle, "what is so very amusing."

"Everything costs," said Wren. "That's what I used to say. Every day. Ask anybody in this lane. *Everything costs. If it doesn't cost you, it'll cost you later.*" She slid down off the counter. "And that's what you think. Isn't it? That's what you've always thought. Same as me. That's why you put a gate round the Beacon. You think if a thing's worth anything, somebody's got to be paid for it." She looked at the box of gold. "But you can't buy this. Nobody can. Not with all the gold in the Guild. It's not that kind of thing."

"Everything," said Prowle, and his voice was not like butter any more, "is that kind of thing, Miss Ashby. In the end. Every single thing in this world has a price. You simply haven't been offered yours."

"You can have it," said Wren.

Prowle stopped.

"For nothing," said Wren. "Right now. If you want it. I'll give it you. Not the secret — there isn't a secret — *it*. The Flame. Hold out your hands. That's all you do. You hold out your hands and you let it be given you, and you don't pay, and you don't owe, and it's yours." She held out her own lamp, from inside her coat, burning small and gold. "That's the only way there is. That's the only way it *comes*."

For a moment — just a moment — something went across Prowle's round pink face that Wren had not expected to see there. It was very quick. It was gone almost before she saw it. But she thought, afterwards, that it might have been longing.

Then his face shut like a box.

"I don't take *charity*," said the Lord Steward of Emberwick.

---

It was then that he made his mistake.

He had been looking, all this time, past Wren at the shop window, where one of the bright new lamps from Cinder Hill stood on the sill with its little gold flame burning, among the loaves. He had been looking at it the way Snib looked at buttons. And now, quite suddenly, as if he could not help it, he stepped past Wren — quicker than you would have thought a man that size could move — and snatched it up off the sill.

"Then I shall take *this*," he said, "for study. In the Guild's laboratories. As a *sample*. And you may call it a fine, Miss Ashby, for lighting without a licence, and think yourself very lucky I don't—"

He stopped.

His face changed.

It went from pink to red, and from red to a sort of mottled purple, and his eyes bulged; and a sound came out of him, high and thin, like a kettle beginning to boil. He was holding the lamp in his fat ringed hand, and the brass of it was not hot — Wren had held it a hundred times; nobody else had ever been burned by it — but his fingers were jerking open of their own accord, as if they had closed on a coal.

"*Hot!*" squealed Prowle. "Hot, hot, *hot*—"

He let go of it.

It fell. It turned over once in the air, and landed on the bakery's flagstone floor — upright, on its base, with a small clean *clink* — and stood there. It had not fallen over. It had not gone out. Its little gold flame stood up straight and steady out of its empty brass mouth, leaning a little to the east, burning on the floor between Prowle's buckled shoes as calmly as if it had been set down there on purpose.

Prowle stood over it, clutching his hand to his chest and whimpering.

Mrs Fenn's youngest walked across the floor in her bare feet, and picked the lamp up off the flags with both hands, quite easily, and put it back on the windowsill.

It did not burn her at all.

---

"I *told* you," said Wren. She was not laughing any more. "It can't be bought. And it can't be sold. And it can't be took. I told you, Mr Prowle." She looked at his hand, which he was cradling against his plum velvet chest. There was no mark on it. There never was. "It burns you if you try."

Prowle looked at his hand. Then at the lamp on the sill. Then at Wren.

His face had gone white, now, under the pink. White and shiny, like a candle. And Wren saw that he was afraid — not of being burned; he was over that already — but of something much bigger. He was looking round the bakery at the lit candles, and the roaring oven, and Nettle's glowing hands, and the people of Soot Lane who had gathered outside the window and were pressing their faces against the glass to see, and he was afraid of all of it, the way some people are afraid of the sea.

Because it was the one thing in the world he could not put a price on, and he had spent his whole life putting prices on things, and he did not know what he was if he could not.

"Pick that up," he said to his footman, pointing at the strongbox. His voice was shaking. "Pick it *up*."

The footman picked it up. Snib, who had got halfway across the floor towards it on his hands and knees, sat back on his heels in disgust.

At the door, Prowle turned round. He had got his breath back, and a little of his colour, and he had found his voice again, though it was higher than it had been.

"You will regret this, Miss Ashby," he said. "I give you my word. As Steward. You will be very, very sorry indeed that you laughed at the Lamp Guild in a *bakery*." He drew himself up. "I offered you everything. Remember that. When they come. I offered you a *gold chain*."

"I don't want a gold chain," said Wren. "I want the Beacon open."

Prowle went out. The bell jangled. The carriage door slammed, and the wheels rattled away up Soot Lane, and the people at the window parted to let it through, and closed up behind it, and stood looking after it.

Then they looked at Wren.

And somebody at the back of the crowd — Wren never found out who — began, quite softly, to clap.

---

The decree went up at four o'clock.

Mrs Fenn's eldest saw it being pasted on the wall at the top of the lane and came running back with it word for word, because he had read it three times over the shoulder of the man pasting it. *Be it known, by order of the Lord Steward, that the so-called Cinder sect are declared ENEMIES OF THE RING and THIEVES OF THE GUILD. Any Person sheltering them, or receiving Light from their hands, shall be held guilty of the same. The Steward's Guard is ordered to take them up wherever found, and especially the Girl Ashby, the Boy Harrowgate, and the Delver Girl, and a GREY FOX.*

"A grey fox," said Tobin. "He's put Cinder in it."

Cinder, who had been washing her paws by the oven, stopped and looked up, and sneezed one small gold spark, very pointedly, at nobody.

"You'll have to go," said Mrs Fenn. She was white as her own flour. "Lovey. You'll have to. Tonight. If they find you here—" She looked at her five children, and at Nettle, and her face crumpled. "I'm not afraid for me. I'm not. But the little ones—"

"We'll go," said Mira at once. "Of course we will. Right now."

"Where to?" said Wren. "There's nowhere. Every door in the Low Quarter, he'll—"

"The roofs," said Snib.

They all turned and looked at him. He was sitting on the edge of the flour-bin with his long legs dangling, licking flour off his fingers one at a time.

"Roofs," he said again, in his high squeak. "Sweep-girl knows roofs. Snib knows roofs. Guards don't know roofs. *Nobody* knows roofs." He considered. "Except chimneys. And cats. And Snib."

---

They waited for dark. It was the longest afternoon of Wren's life, after Midsummer Eve.

The guard came before it.

They heard them first: boots on the cobbles at the top of Soot Lane, a lot of boots, marching in step, and a voice shouting orders; and then doors banging, one after another, coming down the lane. They were searching every house.

"*Now*," said Wren.

There was no time to argue about it. They went up through the bakery to the attic — Mrs Fenn's eldest held the ladder — and out of the attic window onto the roof, into the grey dusk: Snib first, then Wren with Cinder under one arm, then Mira. Tobin was last. He was halfway out of the window, with his long legs still inside, when the bakery door crashed open below and the guards came in shouting.

And one of them came straight up the stairs.

Wren saw it all from the ridge of the roof. She was lying flat along it with Cinder held tight, and she saw the guard's helmet come up through the attic hatch, and the guard see Tobin's legs going out of the window, and make a grab for them.

He got one boot.

Tobin kicked. The boot came off in the guard's hands, and Tobin shot out of the window onto the slates — and slipped. He was not a sweep. He had never been a sweep. He had climbed rooftops in Emberwick exactly once before, chasing Wren on the first night they met, and he had not been very good at it then. He went sliding down the wet slates on his back towards the gutter, scrabbling, and caught himself on the edge of it with his scarred hands, and hung there with his legs over a forty-foot drop.

Mira was already going back for him.

"*Mira, no*—"

"Go on," said Tobin.

He was not looking at Mira. He was looking up the roof, past her, at Wren on the ridge, and his face in the dusk was perfectly white and perfectly calm.

"Go *on*, Wren. They want you most. You've got the lamp. Go." He pushed his spectacles up his nose with one hand, and nearly fell, and grabbed the gutter again. "We'll be all right. I *know* this Hall. I grew up in it. Go!"

And then there were guards at the attic window, two of them, and one at the next window along, and a ladder going up against the wall below; and hands reaching for Mira, who had got to Tobin and was trying to pull him up; and Mira's voice, very clear and steady over the noise, saying "Wren, *do* as you're *told*, for once in your *life*—"

Snib had Wren by the sleeve. His long fingers were cold and very strong.

"Come, come," he squeaked. "Come *now*, sweep-girl, come, come—"

Wren went.

She did not remember deciding to. She remembered the ridge of the roof under her, and the next roof, and a gap between chimneys that she jumped without looking, with Cinder held against her chest; and Snib running ahead, black against the grey, along a gutter and up a drainpipe and over a parapet; and the shouting going small behind her. And she remembered, at the very top of the old brewery's roof, two streets away, where the chimneys stood up like a forest and you could see down over the whole of Soot Lane, stopping, and turning round, and looking back.

The guards were coming out of the bakery door with lanterns.

Between them, with their hands tied in front of them, walked Mira, very straight, with the Chronicle still in its flour-sack across her back — they had not thought to take it from her — and Tobin, in one boot, limping.

He looked up.

He could not have seen her, Wren was sure of it. Not in the dusk, not two streets away, lying flat between the chimneys with a fox. But he looked up anyway, straight at the brewery roof; and he nodded, once, the way Ash used to.

Then the guards marched them away up Soot Lane, towards the Hill.

## The Warden's Hall

Tobin Harrowgate had been taken up the Hill to the Warden's Hall a great many times in his life, but never before with his hands tied and only one boot.

It was a strange feeling. He noticed it, the way he noticed most things — with a part of his mind that stood a little to one side and took notes. The cobbles were cold under his stockinged foot. The guard on his left smelt of onions. The guard on his right was very young, younger than he had first thought, and kept glancing sideways at Mira as if he were not quite sure what she was. The street up the Hill was the same street Tobin had walked down a thousand times on his way to the Guild Hall with his father, or to the bookshop on Lantern Row, or to nowhere in particular, to get out of the house; and every house on it was lit now, in colours, cream and red and blue, and he had never seen it like this, and it was beautiful, and he was a prisoner in it.

*Make a note*, said the part of his mind that took notes. *It is possible to be frightened and to notice that something is beautiful at the same time.* Ash would have liked that one.

"Are you all right?" said Mira, beside him, very low.

"Yes," said Tobin. And then, because he had promised himself on Cinder Hill that he would stop saying things only because they were the right thing to say: "No. Not very. Are you?"

"No," said Mira. "But Wren got away."

"Yes," said Tobin, and found that this made him feel very much better. "She did, didn't she."

---

The Warden's Hall stood at the very top of the Hill, as it always had: grand and cold and grey, with its tall windows and its stone steps and its great front door with the brass knocker shaped like a hand. Tobin had been born in it. His mother had died in it. He had lived in it for thirteen years with his father, in a silence so thick you could have cut it into slices like cake, and he had left it, in the end, by the back gate, with a letter on his father's desk and a lump in his throat, to follow a sweep and an old man and a fox out of the Grey Gate.

It looked smaller than he remembered. Everything does, he supposed, when you have been to the bottom of the world and back. And the windows were dark.

The Lord Steward did not, it turned out, live in the Warden's Hall. He lived in the Guild Hall across the square, which was warmer, and gilded, and smelt of lavender. But he used the Warden's Hall for business. The great front door had a Guild-gold flame painted on it now, over the brass hand. The hall inside was dusty. The portraits of Tobin's grandfathers on the stairs had sheets over them.

They took him and Mira up the stairs and along the gallery and through a door that Tobin knew better than any door in the world.

His father's study.

---

It was just the same.

That was what undid him, a little — not the guards, or the ropes, or the Steward. It was the study. The long oak desk under the window, with the green leather top. The two tall chairs. The shelves of law-books his father had never read and the maps of the Ring-towns his father had read every day. The fire-irons. The rug, with the worn place in front of the hearth where his father used to stand, with his hands behind his back, looking into the fire. The smell of old paper and cold ash and, very faint, under everything, his father's shaving soap.

The fire was not lit. Nobody had lit it for a year. And on the green leather of the desk, in the grey light from the window, there was dust. Thick soft grey dust, undisturbed, except in one place, where somebody had put down a letter and picked it up again.

Tobin's letter. The one he had left there the morning he went.

It was gone. But the shape of it was still there in the dust, a clean pale oblong, like a window into the leather underneath.

Tobin stood and looked at it, and could not look at anything else.

"Sit *down*, my boy, sit down," said the Lord Steward, behind him, very pleasantly. "You're home."

---

Prowle had not sat in Edric Harrowgate's chair. Tobin noticed that as well. He had pulled one of the tall chairs round to the side of the desk and sat in that, with his plum velvet knees together and his ringed hands folded on his stomach and his gold chains glinting; and he had left the Warden's chair empty, behind the desk, with the dust on its seat.

He was frightened of it, Tobin thought. He was frightened of an empty chair.

*Make a note.*

"I'll be quite frank with you, Master Harrowgate," said Prowle. "I bear you no ill will. None whatsoever. You're a young man who has fallen in with bad company, that's all. It happens in the best families." He shook his head. "And yours was the best. One of the very oldest in Emberwick. Your grandfather was a great friend to the Guild. Your father too, in his way. He understood bargains." He smiled sadly. "What a pity you don't."

"I understand bargains," said Tobin.

It came out more steadily than he expected. Prowle's eyebrows went up.

"I understand them very well, actually," said Tobin. "I made one. In Vaelport. I traded something that wasn't mine for something I wanted more than anything. My father's freedom. And I was cheated, and I lost everything, and somebody died." He looked at Prowle. "So I understand bargains, sir. That's why I don't make them any more."

Prowle looked at him for a long moment with his little pale eyes.

"Where is the girl?" he said.

"I don't know."

"Where is the *lamp*?"

"With her."

"And how," said Prowle, leaning forward, his voice going soft, "does it *work*? You're a clever boy. Everybody always said so. Your tutors. Your father, even, when he forgot himself — clever with his hands, clever with machines. Tell me how it works, Master Harrowgate. You've seen it done a hundred times. You must have worked it out. The — the mechanism."

Tobin thought about it.

He thought about it quite seriously, because it was a real question, and he had always found it very hard not to answer a real question properly. He thought about the hundred lamps in the workshop, standing up gold one after another with no wicks. He thought about Dick Crane's candle-stub, and Will Hesketh's hands in the field, and Nettle lighting her oven.

"It isn't a mechanism," he said. "I thought it was, for a long time. I thought if I could only take it to pieces I'd understand it. But you can't. It's a gift. You can't take a gift to pieces. You can only — *have* it, or not." He paused. "You could have it, sir. Wren offered. She meant it."

Prowle's face went very still.

Then he sat back in his chair, and smoothed his velvet over his knees, and smiled his wide pink smile.

"Take them down," he said to the guards. "The strongroom. They can think it over in the dark."

---

They went out of the study, and along the gallery, and down the back stairs, which were steep and narrow and wound round and round; and at the bottom of them, in the corridor that led to the kitchens and the cellars, the guards stopped to light a lantern, and argue about whose turn it was to go down.

It was Captain Bracken who took the lantern in the end.

"I'll take 'em," he said. "Go and get your suppers."

The young guard looked doubtful. "Steward said—"

"Steward said take 'em down. I'm taking 'em down. Go on, lad. I'm not going to *lose* 'em. There's only one way out of that hole and it's past my table." Bracken jerked his head. "Go *on*."

The guards went.

Bracken stood for a moment in the corridor with the lantern in his hand, looking at the two of them: at Mira, very straight, with the flour-sack still on her back; at Tobin, in one boot. He looked older than he had in the bakery. He looked, Tobin thought, like a man who has been carrying something heavy up a hill for a long time and has just been told the hill goes on.

"This way," he said.

But he did not go down to the strongroom stair. He went along the corridor the other way, quickly, and up a short flight of steps that Tobin knew — they led to the servants' passage behind the great hall, and from there, by a back stair that nobody but the maids and Tobin had ever used, up to the attics.

"Captain," said Tobin, "this isn't—"

"Hush," said Bracken. "I know where it goes. I've had a year to look round this house, lad, with nobody in it. I've looked round it a good deal." He climbed. "I want to show you something. And I want to tell you something. And I can't do either down there, because the walls have ears on that stair, and they're Prowle's ears."

---

The attic was just as Tobin had left it, as well.

His workshop. His bench under the round window, with his tools, and the brass shavings in the cracks between the floorboards, and his notebooks in a heap, and a half-finished clockwork mouse with its back off, which he had meant to finish the night he left and had forgotten all about. His bed in the corner. The little hook by the bed where his mother's night-lamp used to hang. It was empty; the night-lamp had gone with him, and been lost somewhere on the Greywaste, and he had not missed it for a long time.

And in the middle of the floor, on its stand, the Orrery.

It was a brass model of Emberwick's wall, as wide as a cartwheel, with a hundred and forty-four tiny glass beads set round it, one for every Ring-lamp, and thin copper wires running in from every bead under a little brass city to meet at a single larger bead on a spire in the middle, for the Beacon. Tobin had built it when he was eleven. It had taken him a year. When the real Ring-lamps burned, the beads glowed; and when they failed, the beads went dark; and on the night he had first seen a moving point of light in the Low Quarter where no Ring-lamp was, he had followed it down the Hill and found Wren.

All one hundred and forty-four beads were glowing now. Gold. As they had been since the night the Ring was lit.

But that was not what made Tobin stop in the doorway.

Inside the wall, across the little brass city, where there were no beads at all — where Tobin had never wired anything, because there was nothing to wire, because there had never been any true light in the streets of Emberwick to show — there were lights.

Tiny ones. Specks. Pin-pricks of gold, glowing faintly in the brass of the model itself, as if the metal were remembering something. They were all down at one end, the low end, the end where the Low Quarter was. Soot Lane. Tallow Bridge. The tannery streets. The canal.

And as Tobin watched, another one came on.

And another, beside it.

And another, two streets away.

He knew exactly what they were. He knew it the way he had known, on Midsummer morning, that it wasn't arithmetic. Down there in the Low Quarter, in the dark, people were going from door to door, lighting their neighbours' candles with their hands. And the Orrery — which he had built to feel the true Flame through copper, and which had been sitting up here in the dark for a year, with the Flame running through its wires — had learned to feel it in the streets as well.

Tobin began to laugh.

He could not help it. He stood in the door of his old attic with his hands tied in front of him and one boot on and laughed, out loud, until the tears ran down his face behind his cracked spectacles, while the little lights went on coming, one by one by one, all over the low end of his model city like stars coming out.

Bracken stared at him.

"It's *them*," said Tobin, when he could speak. "It's Soot Lane. Look. Look, Mira, it's *going*. He can lock us up as much as he likes. It's going without us."

Mira came and stood beside him, and looked at the tiny lights, and her face lit up, and she did not say anything at all, because she did not need to.

---

"That's what I wanted to show you," said Bracken, after a while. "I come up here of a night, sometimes. Since the spring. I don't know why. It's restful." He looked at the Orrery. "It started three days ago. Down by the canal. One. Then two. I didn't know what it meant. I knew it was something." He set the lantern down on the bench. "And now I want to tell you the other thing."

He turned round and faced Tobin.

"Your father," he said.

Tobin's laughter stopped.

"Everybody thinks he ran," said Bracken. "Prowle says so. Says the Warden lost his nerve and fled the city in the night, and left it to the Guild to hold. That's how Prowle's Steward. That's the paper with the seal. *In the absence of the Warden.*" His jaw worked. "It's not true."

"I know," said Tobin. His voice had gone small. "I know he's a prisoner. I saw his ring. In Vaelport. His signet. And a letter, in his own hand, begging. From a cell." He swallowed. "Varrick had it."

"Aye. Varrick had it." Bracken looked at the floor. "I was on the gate the night he was taken. It wasn't long after you lot went out onto the Waste. The grey captain came back, at night, with his Knights. He didn't break in. He didn't have to. He came to the Warden's Hall and he was *let in*, by the front door, because your father had made a bargain with him once, and thought — I suppose he thought — he could make another." Bracken's voice was very low. "I don't know what passed between them in that study. I wasn't let in. But when the grey captain came out, he had your father with him. Not roped. Walking. Like a man walking to his own hanging. And he looked at me, lad, as he went past — your father. Straight at me, on the gate. And he didn't say anything. But I knew he wanted me to know he was going."

Tobin could not say anything at all.

"I followed them," said Bracken. "Next day. On my own. I'm not proud of what I didn't do that night, but I did that. I followed them east as far as the Highland road, and I asked at every inn and every farm along it, and I paid for what I was told. They took him over the moors. To the coast. To the black water." He looked up at Tobin. "There's a tower, lad, on an island in the Sunless Sea, where the sun never comes up. Folk on the Highland coast won't even say its name. They spit, and make a sign with their fingers, and call it the Queen's house. But I heard it once, from an old fisherman who'd had too much to drink." He paused. "Nightfall Spire."

Mira's hand found Tobin's arm, and held on.

"That's where he is," said Bracken. "If he's anywhere. Nightfall Spire. And that's where the grey captain takes the ones he ropes on the roads, I reckon, though I can't prove it. East, over the Highlands, to the sea." He picked up the lantern. "I wanted you to know. Before — well. Before. I've been wanting to tell somebody for a year, and there's been nobody to tell."

"Thank you," said Tobin. It came out as a whisper.

"Don't thank me, lad. I'm still taking you down."

---

He took them down.

Back along the servants' passage, and down the back stairs, and along the corridor past the kitchens, and through a low door at the end of it that Tobin had not been through since the night of the strongroom, a year ago; and down.

Sixty-one steps. Tobin counted them. He had counted them the last time too, in the dark, going down to fetch the lamp out of the strongroom for Wren with his hands shaking so hard he could barely hold the candle. They were narrow and steep and worn hollow in the middle, and the walls on either side were cold stone, wet to the touch; and the lantern's light went down them ahead of the three of them like water poured down a well.

At the bottom, a little round stone room, with a table and a stool and a hook on the wall for the lantern. And a door.

It was the same iron door. It had no window in it, and no grille, and a lock as big as a loaf. On the night Tobin had last stood in front of it, the dark behind it had seemed to him like the end of the world. He had been afraid of the dark every night of his life since he was five years old. He had slept with his mother's night-lamp lit beside his bed until he was thirteen and ashamed of it every single night. And the strongroom of the Warden's Hall, sixty-one steps down under the Hill, where no light had ever been and no light ever would be, had been the very worst place he could think of.

Bracken unlocked it, and swung it open.

Beyond it there was nothing. Not shadow. Nothing. A black so thick it looked like a wall.

"I'm sorry," said Bracken. He untied their hands. "I'm sorry, lad. I'll be at the table. All night. If you want anything."

"It's all right," said Tobin.

And he was surprised to find, as he said it, that it was.

He took Mira's hand, and they walked into the dark together; and Bracken swung the iron door shut behind them, and they heard the great lock turn, and the bolt go across, and then nothing at all.

## Sixty-One Steps

It was very dark in the strongroom.

I want to be quite clear about this, because it matters. It was not the kind of dark you get in a bedroom at night with the curtains drawn, where after a while your eyes get used to it and you begin to make out the shape of the wardrobe. It was not even the dark of the Burned Country at midnight, with no moon. It was the dark of a stone box sixty-one steps under a hill, with an iron door shut on it, where no light had ever been at all. You could hold your hand in front of your face and not know it was there. You could open your eyes or shut them and it made no difference.

Tobin stood in it, holding Mira's hand, and waited for his own hands to start shaking.

They did not shake.

He waited a little longer, to be sure. He had been afraid of the dark for eight years, and he did not trust it to have stopped. He thought about the night on Cinder Hill when he had woken up in the warm ash and lain looking up into the blackness and found that it was only the night. He had thought then that perhaps it was because of where he was: because the Lampwright was sitting by the fire a few feet away, feeding it a stick at a time. Perhaps away from the Hill it would come back.

It did not come back.

He found, standing in the strongroom of the Warden's Hall with an iron door shut behind him, that he could feel the Flame. Not see it. There was nothing to see. But it was there, the way it had been there since Midsummer morning: in his chest, and his arms, and his scarred palms, warm and steady, like a coal banked for the night. It did not light anything. It did not need to. It was simply there.

"Tobin," said Mira's voice, in the dark. "Are you all right?"

"Actually," said Tobin, "yes."

"Really?"

"Really." He thought about it. "It's very odd. I was more frightened in the bakery. With the guards. I was frightened on the roof. I'm not frightened at all now." He heard her shift. "Are you?"

Mira laughed.

It was a small, surprised laugh, very close to his ear. "Tobin, I lived in the dark for twelve years. I was *born* in it. This is the most comfortable I've been since Midsummer." She clicked her tongue, once, and listened. "It's a small room. Square. A bit taller than you. The floor's stone and there's something in the corner — a chest, I think. Wood. Iron bands." She clicked again. "Nothing else. It's quite a nice room really. It's very *quiet*."

They sat down with their backs against the wall.

---

For a while they talked.

They talked about Wren — where she would be, what she would do. They talked about Bracken, and what he had told them, and Nightfall Spire, and Tobin's father. That was hard. Tobin had not let himself think about his father very much since Cinder Hill. He had thought about Ash, and about being forgiven, and about the three questions by the breakfast fire; and about his father he had only thought, sometimes, *he's a prisoner*, and then made himself think about something else.

Now he thought about him properly.

He thought about his father walking out of the front door of the Warden's Hall beside the grey captain, not roped, like a man walking to his own hanging. And looking at Bracken on the gate. And not saying anything.

"He wanted somebody to know," said Mira.

"I know."

"That's something."

"It isn't much."

"No," said Mira. "But it's something. It's more than he did before." She was quiet for a moment. "He's alive, Tobin. Bracken thinks so. And we know where. A year ago you didn't know anything."

Tobin pulled his knees up and put his arms round them, in the dark.

"I sold Ash for a letter," he said. "Because I wanted him back. My father. That's what I did. I'd have sold anything. And then on the Hill, the Lampwright asked me if I loved him. Three times. And gave me my work back." He swallowed. "I keep thinking — what would I say to him? To my father. If I ever saw him again. What *could* you say? After everything."

"I don't know," said Mira. "But I expect you'll know when it's time. Like the question."

"Like the question," said Tobin. And he found, a little to his surprise, that this was a perfectly good answer as well.

---

They had been in the strongroom perhaps four hours — it was very hard to tell — when Tobin said, "Mira. Do you know the Psalm?"

"Which one?"

"The Lamplighters' one. The one Ash taught us on the Greywaste. And Sister Hale sang, on Midsummer morning."

"Of course I know it." She sounded almost offended. "I know it better than you. I've got a better memory. You only know three verses."

"I know four," said Tobin. "Actually. Five, if you count the one with the goat in it, which Sister Hale says isn't proper."

"Why?"

"I don't know," said Tobin. "I just want to sing it."

So they sang it.

They sang it in the dark, under the Warden's Hall, sixty-one steps down, with the iron door shut. Mira sang it well and Tobin sang it badly — he had always sung badly; his father had said so, once, at a Guild dinner, in front of everybody — and neither of them minded at all. They sang the first verse, the old one, *Though I walk through the gloaming, I will fear no dark*. They sang the one about the lamp in the window and the traveller on the road. They sang the one about the city with no Ring, where the walls are made of light. They sang the one with the goat in it, twice, because it made Mira laugh.

And the strongroom, which had been built to keep a thing in silence and dark for ever, turned out to have very good echoes.

The song went round and round the stone walls and came back to them, fuller and warmer, as if there were more than two people singing. It went up through the cracks in the stone, Mira said afterwards; she could hear it going. It went up through the floor of the Warden's Hall, and through the walls, and out. And somewhere up there, far above them in the dark night of Emberwick, very faint, very far away, so faint that at first Tobin thought it was only the echo coming back—

"*Listen*," said Mira.

They stopped singing.

It went on.

It was so faint it was hardly a sound at all. It was more like a feeling in the stone at their backs. But it was the same tune, and it was singing, and it was not them. It was a great many people, a long way off, singing the Lamplighters' Psalm together in the open air.

---

I can tell you what it was, though Tobin and Mira did not know until the morning.

It was Soot Lane.

When the guards had marched Tobin and Mira away up the Hill, Wren had stayed on the brewery roof for a long time, lying flat between the chimneys with Cinder, too angry and too frightened to move. Then Snib had pulled at her sleeve and taken her down, by ways only Gutterlings know, to Mrs Fenn's back yard. And there in the bakery, in the dark, with the shutters closed, the people of Soot Lane had come one by one, the way people come to a house where somebody has died — Mott, and old Mrs Peck and her grandson, and the woman from across the lane and her sister, and Nettle, who had not gone to bed and would not — until the room behind the shop was full.

And nobody knew what to do.

"We could storm the Hall," said Mott. "There's enough of us."

"There's *forty* of us," said Mrs Fenn. "There's a hundred guards. And they've got swords, and we've got bread."

"We could go to the Steward and beg."

"We could pay the fee. All of us. Every penny we've got."

"It's not about the fee," said Wren. She was sitting on the floor by the oven with Cinder in her lap, and her face was grey and set, and she had not spoken for an hour. "It's never been about the fee. He doesn't want pennies. He wants it to be *his*."

"Then what do we do?"

And it was Nettle — eight years old, in her nightgown, with her two new front teeth — who said, "We could thing."

Everybody looked at her.

"*Thing*," said Nettle crossly. "Like on the wall. Like the people who uthed to go up on the wall at night and thing. Wren'th mam's thong. *Morning comth.* Mithith Peck thaid. Mithith Peck thaid they uthed to thing on the wall, in the old dayth, and it kept the Hollowth off." She looked round at all of them. "Tho we could thing."

Old Mrs Peck, in the corner, with her bad eyes, had gone very still.

"It's true," she said. "There were singers. On the North Wall. When I was a girl. Lamplighter folk. They went up at the turning of the night and sang by the Ring-lamps, the old songs, to keep the lamps company." She stopped. "The Guild stopped it. After. And then the grey captain came one night, and—" She stopped again, and shook her head, and would not go on.

But Wren had heard enough.

---

So at midnight they went up the Hill to Beacon Square.

Forty of them, and then sixty, because people came out of their doors as they went past, and then more. They went with candles in their hands, lit by their own hands, gold in the dark, against the Steward's decree, every one of them guilty of the same. They went past the Guild Hall, with its gilded windows shut. And they stood in the square, under the tall dark tower of the Beacon with its bricked-up door and its Guild-gold gate and its little sentry-box, empty now, with the brazier gone out; and they looked up at the Flame burning in the iron bowl three hundred steps above them.

And they sang.

They did not know many songs. Wren knew the Psalm, and her mother's lullaby, or the scrap of it. Mrs Peck knew some of the old wall-songs, half remembered. Mott knew a song about a sailor and a mermaid that was not suitable. But they sang what they knew, all together, as loud as they could, in the middle of the night in the middle of the city, with their candles held up; and the guards came out of the Guild Hall and stood on the steps, and looked at them, and did not know what to do either. You cannot very well arrest a whole street for singing.

And down in the strongroom, sixty-one steps under the Warden's Hall across the square, two children heard them, and began to sing again.

---

It was about an hour before dawn when the lock turned.

Tobin was half asleep. He had sung until his voice went, and then lain down on the stone with his head on Mira's flour-sack, and the Chronicle inside it was a hard pillow but a comforting one. Mira was still sitting up, humming under her breath.

Then she stopped.

"Tobin," she said.

He heard it too. A small, heavy, oiled sound, like a great key turning in a great lock — slowly, all the way round. Then the bolt: a long iron scrape, going back.

Then nothing.

Nobody spoke on the other side of the door. Nobody moved. There was no lantern-light under it. They sat in the dark and held their breath and listened, and Mira, who could hear a sneeze at the far end of the Whispering Galleries, said afterwards that there had been nobody there. No breath. No heartbeat. No footstep on the stone. Only the lock, turning, as if a hand had turned it.

And a warmth.

It came in round the edges of the door — a faint, clean, steady warmth, like a loaf just out of the oven, like summer on a wall; and it smelt, very faintly, of bread.

The iron door swung open.

It swung open by itself, slowly and quite silently, on its great hinges, all the way back to the wall. Beyond it was the little round stone room at the bottom of the stair. The lantern was burning low on its hook. And at the table, on the stool, with his head on his folded arms and his helmet beside him, Captain Bracken was asleep.

Tobin and Mira got up and walked out.

They did not run. Tobin noticed that, afterwards, and wondered at it. Nobody had told them not to run. But it did not seem like the sort of moment you ran in. They walked out of the strongroom side by side, and stood in the little stone room, and looked at the open door behind them and the sleeping man in front of them, and the lantern, and the stair going up; and they did not say anything at all.

Then Bracken woke up.

---

He woke the way people do when they have fallen asleep on duty and know it: all at once, with a great jerk, knocking his helmet off the table with a clang. He was on his feet before he was awake. He saw the two of them, standing there. He saw the iron door behind them, standing open.

His face went the colour of cold porridge.

"*No*," he said. "No, no — *no*—"

He went past them to the door, and stared into the strongroom, and back at them; and then at the great key, which was hanging on its nail on the wall by the stair, exactly where he had put it. And then at his own hands, as if they might have done it without telling him.

"I locked it," he said. "I locked it. I *know* I—" He sat down on the stool, hard. "He'll hang me. Prowle. He'll hang me for this. He's been looking for a reason for a year." He put his face in his hands. "Oh, my *word*. Oh, my word."

Then, slowly, he looked up.

"Why are you still here?"

Tobin did not know what to say.

"The door was open," said Bracken. "I was *asleep*. You could've been up them steps and out the back gate and halfway to the Grey Gate by now. Why didn't you *go*?"

"I don't know," said Tobin honestly. "It didn't seem right. To leave you."

Bracken stared at him.

He was a big grizzled tired man, with a scar through one eyebrow and grey stubble on his chin and a gold Steward's sash that did not suit him; and he had arrested Wren once, a year ago, on Lord Edric's orders, and been gentle about it; and he had shaken her hand on the morning after the Ring was lit; and he had watched Lord Edric walk away into the night with the grey captain, and not stopped it, and been ashamed of that for a year. And now he sat on a stool in a stone room under a hill and looked at two children who had not run away when they could, and his face changed.

"What do I have to do?" he said.

He said it very quietly. It was not the voice of a captain of the guard. It was the voice of a man at the end of a very long road, asking the way.

"What do I *have to do*, lad? To — to have what you've got. Whatever it is. Whatever's in you, that you sing in the dark down there like it was a — a kitchen. And the door opens by itself. And you don't run." His voice cracked. "I've been on that gate thirty years. I've done what I was told every day of it. And I've never once been — *warm*." He held out his hands, helplessly, palm up, the way Will Hesketh had held his out in the field, though he had never seen Will Hesketh. "What do I have to do?"

Tobin had once, a long time ago, been the sort of boy who would have answered that question with a speech. He would have explained the lamp's law, and the hundred lamps, and the Midsummer gift, and arithmetic, and fire, and why it was not a mechanism; and it would have taken a quarter of an hour, and been very accurate, and Bracken would have understood about one word in ten.

He did not do that.

"Hold out your hands," said Tobin.

Bracken's hands were already out. Tobin put his own scarred palms against them.

And Mira, beside him, said very softly, "It's given."

---

Here is what Bracken did next, and I think it was the bravest thing in this whole part of the story.

He did not hide them. He did not sneak them out of the back gate, though he could have. He put his helmet on, and took off his gold Steward's sash and folded it up neatly and left it on the table by the open door; and he took the lantern off its hook — and then, with a sort of wondering face, put his hand round its guttering wick, and it went up gold — and he walked them up the sixty-one steps and along the corridor and up the back stairs and through the great dusty hall with the sheeted portraits, and he opened the front door of the Warden's Hall, with its painted Guild flame over the brass hand, wide.

And he walked them out of it, down the steps, into Beacon Square.

The sky was going grey. The square was full of people. Soot Lane was there still, all of it, hoarse from singing all night, with their candles burned down to stubs; and more besides, from other streets, who had heard the singing and come to see. The Steward's guards were on the Guild Hall steps. And at the front of the crowd, under the bricked-up tower of the Beacon, with Cinder on her shoulder and Snib on her head and Nettle hanging on to her coat, was Wren.

She saw them come down the steps of the Warden's Hall.

She saw Bracken, with his helmet on and his sash off and a gold flame burning in the lantern in his hand. She saw Mira, very straight, with the flour-sack on her back. She saw Tobin, in one boot.

She did not say anything. She dropped her candle-stub and ran.

---

There is not much more to tell of that night, and I have told the best of it.

But I will tell you about Snib, because he would never forgive me if I did not.

He had been on Wren's head all night, holding her lamp up for her when her arms got tired — she had let him, because he begged, and because she was too tired to argue. And somewhere in the singing, without anybody noticing, without Snib himself noticing, the little gold flame had gone from the lamp into his long soot-black fingers, and stood for a breath on the top of his small black head, and sunk in.

He did not find out until dawn, when he went to scratch his ear and his finger was warm.

He looked at it. He looked at Wren. He looked at his finger again. His white eyes went enormous. And then — before anybody could stop him — he was gone, off her head and up the side of the Guild Hall like a spider up a wall, and over its gilded roof, and away across the rooftops of the Hill; and wherever he went, every chimney-pot he touched went up gold.

The first Gutterling ever to carry the Flame. The other Gutterlings, it is said, were deeply offended, and did not speak to him for a month.

And when the sun came up — pale, as it always did; but a little warmer, I think, over Emberwick that morning — the people of Soot Lane went to the bricked-up door of the Beacon and began, with their bare hands, to take the bricks down.

Nobody stopped them. The guards on the Guild Hall steps put down their swords and came and helped. And when the doorway was clear, and the stair stood open again as it always had, somebody went to find the Lord Steward to tell him.

But the Lord Steward was not in the Guild Hall. His carriage was gone from the yard. And the guards on the South Gate said afterwards that he had driven out of it at full gallop an hour before dawn, alone, with his strongbox on the seat beside him, going east.

"East," said Bracken, standing in the square in the morning with his gold lantern. "The same road. Over the Highlands. Where the grey captain took your father, lad." He looked at Tobin. "He's gone to *her*."

Tobin looked east, past the Beacon, past the wall and the gold Ring, to where the grey morning was coming up over the far, faint line of the hills.

"Then we're going too," he said. "Over the Highlands. There's a town on the Highland road, the last one before the moors. My family came from there, a long time ago, before we were ever in Emberwick." He pushed his spectacles up his nose. "It's got our name. Harrowgate."

## The Road to Harrowgate

This chapter is not about Wren, or Tobin, or Mira. I am sorry. They will be back very soon. But there is a part of the story that happened to somebody else, a long way off, on a road over the moors, and I cannot tell you the rest without it.

It is about the grey captain.

---

Captain Varrick had not slept since Cinder Hill.

That is not quite true. He had slept, a little, in snatches: on his horse, sometimes, with his chin on his chest, or sitting up against a wall in a burned barn with his sword across his knees. A quarter of an hour. Half an hour. But whenever he slept, he dreamed; and whenever he dreamed, he saw the same thing. A man walking up a black hill at dawn, carrying nothing, with the colour coming back into the ash behind him where he walked. And the man's hands.

So he had stopped sleeping, as far as he could, and taken to riding instead.

He rode now at the head of his column on the old drove-road over the Harrowgate Highlands, in the grey middle of the morning, with the moors going away on every side of him under a low sky. Heather, grey as old wool. Stone walls running up and over the hills and down again. Sheep, grey, that lifted their heads and stared as the column went by and then put them down again. Far off, a grey tarn like a coin dropped in a field. The road was a pale stony track worn between banks of peat, and it went on ahead of him over the next rise and the next, towards the town of Harrowgate and the coast and the black water beyond it.

Behind him rode twenty of his Knights, in pairs, as always. Behind them walked the prisoners.

There were fourteen today. He did not look round at them. He did not need to. He could hear them: the shuffle of their feet on the stones, and the creak and jingle of the buckled rope that joined their wrists, and now and then, from somewhere in the middle of the line, a child crying, very quietly, and being hushed. Farmers. A cobbler and his wife. A boy with a bandaged head. An old woman. Remnant folk, every one, taken from a barn at Low Coombe four nights ago where somebody had carved a little flame over the door.

And behind them, at the end of the column, the Knight with the sack.

He could feel it from here. That was the worst of it. He could feel it on the back of his neck the whole way: the sack, slung across the last Knight's saddle, bulging with the lamps they had taken from the barns. There were nearly thirty in it now. Bright brass lamps, new, undented, with no wicks, burning — still burning, every one, through the sacking, a soft gold glow showing through the weave like a lantern with a cloth over it. They had been burning since the first barn. They would not go out. He had tried. He had thrown water on the sack. He had buried it overnight in a peat-bog. In the morning, when the Knight dug it up, it was warm.

And it was heavy. Much heavier than thirty small brass lamps had any right to be. The horse that carried it had gone lame twice.

He should leave it. He knew that. He should leave it in a ditch on the moor and ride on and never think of it again. But the Queen wanted them. *Bring me every one,* she had said, in the voice like warm milk going cold. *Every one they carry. I want to see them.* And so he carried them, east, towards the sea, with the warmth of them on the back of his neck like a hand.

He rubbed his palm.

He did not know he was doing it. He had done it for thirty years, and never once noticed.

---

He was thinking, as he rode, about tea.

He did not want to think about tea. He wanted to think about the road, and the weather, and the time it would take to reach Harrowgate, and the boats at Skerrow, and what he would say to the Queen. He had been very good, for thirty years, at thinking about the things he wanted to think about. It was one of the first things she had taught him.

But since Cinder Hill it had not worked. Things came into his head that he had not put there. And this morning, riding over the grey moor, it was tea.

Dreadful tea. Tea so stewed and black and bitter, made in a pot so old and so seldom washed, that it tasted like a boot that had been boiled in a puddle. He had said exactly that, once, out loud. Thirty years ago. In a crooked tower on a wall, with a cracked cup in his hands, to a tall old man who was not so old then.

*Hm*, the old man had said, and poured him another cup.

---

His name — but no. He did not think his name. He had not thought it for thirty years. It was not a thing he did.

He thought instead, because he could not stop it, about the wall.

The North Wall of Emberwick, at night, at the turning of the night, with the Ring-lamps burning — yellow, then, not gold; the Guild had already started watering the oil — and the wind coming off the Greywaste; and a boy of nineteen walking along the wall-walk behind a tall old man with a brass-hooked pole. Learning the lamps. Learning the wicks. Learning the old words. *Though I walk through the gloaming.* The old man had been not so old then. His beard had been grey, not white. But he had already been saying *Hm*.

He thought about the oath.

He thought about kneeling on the stone floor of the Leaning Tower, with his right hand held out flat over a little brass bowl in which a flame was burning — the oath-fire of the Lamplighters, the last little ember of it, kept alive in that tower for longer than anybody could remember, so old and so low that it was nearly out. And the old man's voice, quite quiet: *Will you give it freely? To anyone who asks? And never charge a penny, as long as you live?* And his own voice, very young, very sure: *I will.* And the old man taking his hand, and laying it palm down in the flame.

It had not hurt. That was the strange thing. It ought to have hurt. It had left a scar right across his palm that he had carried for thirty years. But at the time it had only been warm, like putting your hand on a cat.

*Then you're a Lamplighter, lad,* the old man had said. *Hm. Welcome.*

He rubbed his palm.

---

And then he thought, because he could not stop it, about the night it all went wrong.

He did not think about it as a story. It was not a story. It was just a handful of pictures, sharp as broken glass. The Guild Hall, gilded, full of lamplight and lavender. The Masters of the Guild in their gold chains, at their long table. The old man standing in front of them in his patched blue coat, with his pole, saying *No. I will not charge for it. Not a penny. It was given.* And the Guild Master — not Prowle; the one before him, a long thin man with a voice like a door creaking — smiling, and saying *Then you are no longer a member of this Guild.*

And nothing happened.

That was what he remembered most. That nothing happened. He had stood at the back of the Guild Hall, nineteen years old, with his oath-scar still pink on his palm, and waited for something to happen. For the lamps to go out. For the Flame to *do* something. For the Lampwright himself to come walking in out of the old stories and set the Guild Hall on fire. Anything. And nothing had. The old man had simply turned round, and walked out of the Guild Hall, and gone back to his crooked tower on the wall, and gone on lighting lamps that the Guild let go dim, one at a time, every night, for nothing, while the whole city laughed at him.

And the boy had stood in the street outside, in the cold, and thought: *it's useless. It's a candle against the sea. It does nothing. It will never do anything.* And he had wanted, more than he had ever wanted anything, a power that would *do* something.

And a voice had spoken to him in the dark. Very kindly. Like warm milk going cold.

*I can give you that,* it had said.

---

He had served her thirty years.

He had been good at it. He had been the best she had. He had learned the offer, which was her favourite weapon: you never had to fight people if you could only find the thing they wanted most and offer it to them on the wrong terms. He had offered Lord Edric Harrowgate a seat at a larger table. He had offered Oskar Venn a world without Guilds. He had offered a sweep-girl on a burning tower her life and a full belly, and a boy in a lighthouse his father, and he had taken a lamp from that boy's burned hands and laughed. He had stood in the Forge under Vaelport and watched the old man hold the door — his old master, white-bearded now, lame, leaning on two poles — and heard him say *lad*, and hesitated for one long heartbeat.

And then chosen.

And then the tunnel had fallen.

And he had told himself, all the way back across the coast hills, that it was finished. That the old man was dead, and the lamp would follow, and he would never have to hear that voice say *lad* again.

And then, on a black hill at dawn, a stranger had come walking up out of the Burned Country with the pale sun behind him; and the Queen had screamed; and the Hollow Knights had fallen back like shadows from a lamp; and he — Varrick — had stood his ground the longest of all, because he did not believe it, because it could not be. And he had looked at the stranger's hands.

They had scars on them. Old round deep scars, like burns.

And he had looked down at his own right palm. At his own scar. The oath-scar. The one the old man had given him in the little brass bowl.

It was the same shape.

---

He rubbed his palm. He rubbed it until it hurt.

"Faster," he said to the Knight beside him, though the Knight could not hear him and would not have cared; the Knights did not care about anything. He kicked his horse on. He drove the whole column on over the moor, harder, faster, so that the prisoners stumbled on the stones behind him and the child stopped crying because it had no breath left to cry with. He would go faster than the tea, and the wall, and the oath, and the hands. He would get to Harrowgate by nightfall and Skerrow by tomorrow and the black water by the day after, and he would give the Queen her lamps and her prisoners and then he would sleep, at last, in the grey, where nothing came into your head that you did not put there.

*It is easier in the grey*, said the Queen's voice in his head, softly. *Lie down. It is easier.*

*Lad*, said the old man.

He rode faster.

---

It was noon when it happened.

It was noon on the high moor, the highest part of the road, where it ran along a ridge between two long shallow valleys with tarns in them, and there was nothing in any direction but grey heather and grey sky. The pale sun was as high as it ever went, which was not high, a dull coin in the cloud. There was no wind.

And then there was light.

It did not come from the sun. He knew that at once. It did not come from anywhere. It was simply there, all round him, all at once, as if somebody had opened a door in the middle of the moor and the whole of the inside of the world had come pouring out. It was gold. It was white. It was so bright that the heather went to nothing and the road went to nothing and the sky went to nothing, and he could not see his horse's ears in front of him or his own hands on the reins.

And it was warm.

It was so warm. It was like being inside the Flame. It was like being inside the little brass bowl on the floor of the Leaning Tower, with his palm laid flat in it, thirty years ago; except that it was not his palm now. It was all of him.

His horse screamed and reared and he went off it backwards into the heather. He hit the ground hard, on his back, and lay there, and the light did not stop.

He heard his Knights' horses bolting. He heard the prisoners cry out — not in fear; in astonishment. He heard the sack at the back of the column burst, a ripping of sacking, and a sound like thirty small breaths let out at once. *Ah.*

And then he heard a voice.

He knew it. That was the dreadful thing; that was the thing he had been riding from since the spring. He had never heard it before in his life, and he knew it the way you know your own name. It was low, and warm, and a little rough, like a man who laughs a good deal. It was not angry. It was very close. It might have been kneeling beside him in the heather.

"Aren," it said. "Why are you hunting me?"

Nobody had called him that for thirty years.

He tried to speak. His mouth would not work. He lay on his back in the blazing light with his eyes wide open and seeing nothing, and his scarred right hand was burning — not hurting; *burning*, the way it had in the brass bowl, warm all the way through — and at last he got out two words, in a voice he did not recognise as his own. A boy's voice. Nineteen.

"Who are you?"

He knew. He knew perfectly well. But he had to ask.

"I'm the one you're hunting," said the voice. "Every lamp in that sack is mine. Every one of those people on your rope is mine. Every barn you burned had my flame over the door." A pause. Not angry. Almost — he could not believe it — almost gentle. "You were mine first, lad. Before any of them."

He lay in the heather and wept.

He had not wept for thirty years either. It came out of him the way water comes out of a cracked dam: all at once, everywhere, with nothing to stop it. He lay on his back on the moor and wept like a child, and the light went on and on, and the warmth went on, and the voice did not say anything for a long time, only waited, the way the old man used to wait on the wall when a wick would not catch.

"Get up," it said at last. Kindly. "Go into the town. You'll be told what to do."

---

He got up.

The light had gone. He knew it had gone, because the warmth had gone with it, except in his palm. He could hear the wind, now — a little moor wind, getting up, cold, smelling of peat. He could hear sheep somewhere. He could hear, a long way off, the Knights' horses still galloping.

But he could not see.

He stood on the road with his eyes open, and there was nothing. Not grey. Not dark. Nothing. As if the light had been so bright that it had burned the seeing right out of him, the way you look at the sun too long and see only a black hole where it was. He put his hands up to his face. He could feel his eyes, open. He could not see his hands.

He stood there for a long time, swaying, in the middle of the drove-road on the high moor, alone.

Then he heard feet.

They came towards him slowly over the stones. Several people. They stopped a little way off. Nobody said anything. He could hear them breathing.

"Who's there?" he said.

His voice was hoarse. It was not smooth any more. It was not oiled iron. It was only a man's voice.

Nobody answered for a long time. Then a woman spoke — a Highland voice, low and frightened and very careful, the way you speak near a dog that has bitten.

"It's us," she said. "From Low Coombe. From the barn." A pause. "The ropes burned through. All at once. In the light. They just — fell off." Another pause. "Your Knights have gone. Off over the moor, like smoke. There's nobody here but us."

He did not say anything. There was nothing to say.

"The lamps came out of the sack," said the woman. "They're lying in the heather. All of them. Burning." Her voice wobbled. "We picked them up. They're ours."

"Yes," he said. "They're yours."

Another long silence. He could hear them whispering. He could hear a child, the one who had been crying all morning, asking a question in a small voice; and being hushed. He waited for them to go. He did not blame them. He waited for a stone, or a blow, or simply the sound of their feet going away down the road towards Harrowgate, leaving him blind on the moor with the wind getting up; and he thought he would not mind very much. He thought it would be fair.

The feet came closer instead.

One pair. Slowly. They stopped right in front of him.

And a hand took his hand.

It was a woman's hand, small and hard and work-rough, with the marks of a loom on the fingers; and it was shaking. She had been roped to the others by the wrist for four days, behind his horse. He could feel the raw rope-burn on her wrist against his own. And she took his scarred right hand in hers, and held it, the way you hold the hand of somebody who cannot see where they are going.

"Come on, then," she said. Her voice was shaking too. "It's not far. It's — I'll take you. Mind the stones."

And so it was the people he had captured who led their captor into Harrowgate, by the hand, in the grey afternoon: fourteen Remnant folk from a burned barn at Low Coombe, with thirty lamps burning gold in their arms, walking slowly down the drove-road off the high moor, and in the middle of them a tall grey blind man in grey armour, who stumbled on the stones, and did not let go.

---

That night, in a dark room in a house at the end of a street in a town he could not see, he lay on a straw mattress with his eyes open, and did not sleep, and did not want to.

He thought about tea. He thought about the wall. He thought about a voice in the heather, not angry. About his palm, which was still warm.

And then — for the first time in thirty years, very quietly, in the dark, to nobody at all — he said his own name out loud.

"Aren."

## A Man Who Cannot See

They went out of Emberwick by the East Gate two days later, and it took three hours to do it, because everybody in Soot Lane wanted to say goodbye.

Mott gave Wren another hat. Mrs Fenn gave them so much bread that Tobin had to take out half his tools to make room for it, and then she gave them more. Old Mrs Peck gave Wren a kiss on the forehead and a name — "Ashby," she said, holding Wren's face in her two dry hands and peering at it with her bad eyes. "Ashby. I knew an Ashby once. On the wall." And then she would not say any more, and Wren did not know what she meant, and did not have time to ask.

Nettle did not give them anything. She stood at the East Gate with her arms folded and her face red and furious and told Wren that if she went away for a whole year again without sending a letter she would never speak to her as long as she lived; and then she burst into tears; and then she said she was not crying, it was the wind.

"I'll come back," said Wren. "I promise."

"You *alwayth* thay that."

"And I always do."

Nettle considered this, scowling, and found that it was true, and was even more furious.

Bracken walked with them as far as the first milestone. He was the keeper of the Beacon now. Nobody had made him it; there was nobody to make him anything, with the Steward fled. But on the first morning, when the bricks were down, he had simply gone and stood at the foot of the stair with his gold lantern, and anybody who wanted the Flame he lit for them, for nothing; and by the second morning everybody in Emberwick called him the Keeper, and he had not taken his helmet off since, because he said he did not trust himself not to cry if he did.

"I'll keep it," he said, at the milestone. "Till you come back. Till the Warden comes back." He looked at Tobin. "Bring him home, lad."

"I'll try," said Tobin.

"No," said Bracken. "Don't try. *Bring him.*" And then he took his helmet off after all, and did exactly what he had said he would.

---

The Highland road went east out of Emberwick across the plain, and then up.

It went up so gradually at first that you hardly noticed it: the fields got stonier, and the walls got lower and older, and the farms got further apart. Then you noticed it in your legs. By the end of the first day they were climbing in earnest, on a stony track between banks of peat, with the plain behind them going away grey and flat to the gold Ring of Emberwick on the horizon; and ahead of them the moors.

Wren had never seen moors. She had grown up in a city, and walked the Greywaste, and gone under the ground and along the coast and across the Burned Country; but she had never been anywhere so *high*. The moors went on and on in every direction, grey-brown and rolling, like a huge rumpled blanket that somebody had thrown over the world and left; and the sky came right down to meet them; and the wind never stopped. It was cold, and it smelt of peat-smoke and sheep and something clean and sharp that Wren could not name. Tobin said it was the smell of height. Mira said that was not a thing. Tobin said it was, actually, and they argued about it for four miles.

There were sheep everywhere. Cinder had never seen sheep either, and was beside herself. She stalked them through the heather with her belly to the ground and her spark tucked under her tail, for hours, getting closer and closer; and every time she got close enough to pounce, the sheep looked round at her with its long grey face and said *meh*, and Cinder was so insulted that she sat down and sneezed.

They came to Harrowgate at the end of the second day.

---

It stood on a hill above a river, where the river came down off the moor in a long white weir: a small grey stone town, very old, with steep roofs and narrow streets that went up the hill in steps, and smoke going up from every chimney, and a low wall all round it. On the wall were the Ring-lamps — twelve of them, Tobin counted, one at every corner and every gate. Four were out. The other eight burned the tired grey-yellow of Guild oil nearly gone.

And above the town, at the very top of the hill, there was a ruin.

It had been a house once — a big one, Wren thought, as big as any on the Hill in Emberwick. Now it was a shell: four tall walls and the stumps of chimneys and empty windows, with the grey sky showing through them, and ivy growing up the stones.

Tobin stopped in the road and looked at it for a long time.

"That's ours," he said at last. "I think. My great-grandfather's. My father talked about it once, when I was small. Harrowgate House. The family lived here, before—" He frowned. "I don't actually know before what. He never said. Just that we came from here. And then we went to Emberwick, and we were Wardens, and nobody ever came back."

"It's very *ruined*," said Mira.

"Yes," said Tobin. "It is, rather."

---

The Highlanders of Harrowgate knew who he was before he had been in the town ten minutes.

Wren never found out how. Perhaps it was his face; perhaps there was a Harrowgate look, long and fair and all elbows, that the old folk remembered. Perhaps it was simply that a Highland town is small, and news goes round it like a cold. But they had not got halfway up the main street, with its steps and its gutters and its washing, when a woman came out of a door and stared at Tobin, and put her hand to her mouth, and said "*Young Harrowgate*," in a voice of awe; and after that it was all up.

"It's young Harrowgate!"

"Come home!"

"Where's your horse, lad? Where's your men?"

"Is it true you're the Warden of all Emberwick now?"

"Is it true you fought a dragon?"

They came out of every door. They crowded the steps. They looked at Tobin with shining faces — at his torn coat and his ink-stained fingers and his cracked spectacles and his one boot (Mrs Fenn had found him another, but it did not match, and was a size too big) — and they clearly expected something tremendous. A speech. A sword. A troop of horse coming up the hill behind him.

Tobin went red to the ears.

"I'm — actually, I'm not the Warden," he said. "Of anything. My father's the Warden. Was. Is. I'm — I don't have any men. Or a horse. I've got a pole." He held it up. "It's not mine either, technically. And I've never fought a dragon. There aren't any. That I know of." He pushed his spectacles up his nose, and they fell down again. "I'm very sorry."

There was a disappointed silence.

"He's very thin," said somebody at the back.

"He takes after his great-granddad," said somebody else. "*He* was thin."

And then an old man pushed to the front of the crowd, leaning on a stick: tiny and bent and brown, with a white beard down to his belt. He looked up at Tobin for a long moment, out of a face like a walnut.

"Never mind the Warden," he said. "Never mind the dragon. There's worse news than that, young Harrowgate, and you'd best hear it before you hear anything else." He jerked his head down the hill. "The grey captain's here."

Wren's hand went inside her coat to the lamp.

"Here?" said Tobin. "In Harrowgate? Where? With his *Knights*—"

"No Knights," said the old man. "No horse. No sword. He came in four days ago off the high moor, led by the hand like a little child, with the Low Coombe folk that he'd took from their barn walking all round him with their lamps. Struck blind on the drove-road at noon, they say. Fell off his horse in a light like the sun come down. And his ropes burned off their wrists." He spat. "Now he's lying in Ewan Moffat's back room at the end of Weir Street with his eyes wide open, and he's not ate or drunk for four days, and he's not said one word to anybody. And nobody in this town knows what to do with him."

---

They did not go to Weir Street that night. Wren would not.

They went instead to the inn — the Black Bull, at the top of the main street, a low smoky room with a fire and a landlady who would not take their money — and they sat round the fire with the Highlanders who crowded in to look at them, and heard it all again, from the Low Coombe folk themselves.

They were frightened. That was the first thing Wren noticed. They had been given the Flame on Cinder Hill at Midsummer, some of them, or had it given them since; their faces were brown and their hands were warm. But they were frightened all the same, and kept looking at the door. They had been roped to that man's saddle for four days. They had watched him burn their barn. And now he was lying in a house at the end of Weir Street, and nobody knew whether he was going to get up.

"I took his hand," said a woman by the fire. She was a weaver, small and hard and quick, with rope-burns on her wrists still raw. Isla Moffat. "On the road. I don't know why I did it. I shouldn't have. Ewan says I shouldn't have. He says we should have left him on the moor." She looked into her cup. "But he was just standing there. In the road. With his eyes open. Like a — like a thing that's been hit. And I thought—" She stopped. "I don't know what I thought."

"He took the Remnant from three barns," said somebody else. "Three that I know of. There were children."

"I know there were."

"And now you've got him in your back room. Where your own children sleep."

"I *know*, Fergus." Isla Moffat put her cup down. "I know. I don't know what to do. Nobody does." She looked across the fire at Wren, and Tobin, and Mira. "Do you?"

Nobody answered.

Wren had not said a word all evening. She was sitting on the settle with Cinder on her knee, staring into the fire, with her jaw set so hard it ached. She was thinking about the holloway, and the ditch, and the line of roped people going past with their heads down; and the sack, glowing; and a grey gloved hand on a black hill, holding her own lamp; and the face of the man in the lighthouse, laughing, while Tobin's palms burned.

*I hope it burns him*, she had said, in the holloway. *I hope it burns him to nothing.*

It had not burned him. It had blinded him. That was not the same.

She did not know what she felt about that, and she did not want to find out.

---

Mira woke in the middle of the night.

They were sleeping in the inn's loft, on straw, under the roof: Wren curled up with Cinder, Tobin flat on his back with his spectacles folded on his chest. Mira woke all at once, the way you do when somebody says your name; and she lay in the dark and listened, and there was nothing. The wind on the roof. The river on the weir. A sheep, very far off, on the moor.

And then a voice.

It did not come from anywhere. It was not in the loft, or outside it. It was simply there, close, the way the warmth had been there in the strongroom when the lock turned; and it was low and warm and a little rough, like mist lying on a slow river.

Mira knew that voice. She had heard it once in a boat on the Gloam River, when she was blind, and a hand had touched her eyes. She had heard it on Cinder Hill, at dawn, saying *there's room by the fire*. She would have known it anywhere.

"Mira," it said. "Go to the house at the end of Weir Street. There's a man there who cannot see."

Mira lay very still.

Then she sat up in the straw, and said, quite clearly, out loud, to the dark:

"*No.*"

Nobody woke. Wren did not stir. Tobin snored, very slightly.

"No," said Mira again, more quietly. Her heart was going very hard. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, but — no. You know who he is. You know what he did. He took the Remnant out of their barns. He took *children*. He chased us all the way across the Greywaste and down the Black Stair, and he stood behind Oskar Venn in the Glass Palace and smiled, and he took the lamp from Tobin in the lighthouse and *laughed*. And on the Hill he took Wren, and gave her to the Queen, so she'd blow it out." Her voice shook. "He nearly killed her. He nearly killed *all* of us. You *know* that."

There was a silence.

Then the voice said, very gently — and Mira thought, though she could not be sure, that there was something in it like a smile:

"Go. I have work for him."

Mira sat in the straw in the dark for a long time.

Then she got up, very quietly, and put her boots on, and the Chronicle in its flour-sack on her back; and she climbed down the loft ladder without waking anyone, and let herself out of the door of the Black Bull into the cold, and went down through the sleeping town to the river.

---

The house at the end of Weir Street was the last house before the weir: low and long, with the sound of the water going over the stones just outside its back wall, so loud that the whole house hummed with it. There was a light in the front window. Isla Moffat opened the door before Mira could knock. She had not been asleep either.

She looked at Mira for a long moment, and did not ask what she wanted.

"Through there," she said. "The back. He's not moved."

The back room was small and very dark. There was no lamp in it; Isla Moffat had not been able to bring herself to leave one. There was a straw mattress against the wall, under the little window that looked out on the weir. And on the mattress, on his back, lay a tall man in grey.

He had taken his armour off, or somebody had; it was in a heap in the corner. He was in his shirt and his grey breeches. His boots were still on. His hands were folded on his chest, the right one under the left, and his eyes were open, staring up at the ceiling, at nothing.

Mira stood in the doorway and clicked.

She did it without thinking. She had not needed to for a while, in the light; but this room was dark, and she wanted to know it. The click went out and came back: small room, low ceiling, a stool by the mattress, the window, the heap of armour. The man. He was very long. He was lying very still. He had heard the click — she heard his breath change — but he did not move.

She went in and sat down on the stool beside him.

---

For a long time neither of them said anything.

Mira listened. She was good at listening. She could hear his heart — slow and heavy, like a man very tired. She could hear his breathing, which was not quite even. She could hear the water going over the weir, and Isla Moffat in the next room, not going to bed.

"Who's there?" he said at last.

His voice was not what she remembered. It was not smooth. It was not oiled iron. It was hoarse, and dry, from not drinking, and quite flat.

"Mira," said Mira. "The Delver girl. From the Deeps."

She heard him stop breathing for a moment.

"The one the Ferryman healed," he said.

"Yes."

"The one who was blind."

"Yes," said Mira. "For twelve years. Since I was born. And then I could see." She folded her hands in her lap. "So I know what it's like. That's why I'm here, I think. I was sent."

He did not ask who sent her.

---

I will not tell you everything Mira said to him in that dark room by the weir, because a great deal of it was hers, and not mine to tell. But I will tell you some.

She told him about being blind. Not sad things; the useful things. How the dark is not empty. How a room has a shape, if you listen; how a voice has a colour; how you can tell a friend's step from a stranger's in the next street. How at first it is like being shut in a box, and then it is not.

"You click," she said. "Like this." She did it. "With your tongue. Against the top of your mouth. And then you listen to what comes back."

"I don't—"

"Try."

He tried. It was a dreadful click. It was a sort of wet cluck.

"No," said Mira. "That's a hen. You sound like a hen. Again."

He did it again. It was slightly less like a hen.

"Better," said Mira. "Again."

And so it went on, for a while — a twelve-year-old Delver girl, sitting on a stool in the dark, teaching the Queen's captain to click his tongue like a blind child in the Deeps; and he did it, over and over, clumsily, and she told him when it was wrong, which was nearly always. And once — just once — Mira heard him make a small sound in his throat that might, in another man, on another night, have been the beginning of a laugh.

Then she stopped.

"Give me your hands," she said.

He did not move.

"Give me your hands," said Mira again. "I can't do it if you don't."

Slowly, as if they were very heavy, he unfolded his hands from his chest, and held them out in the dark, palm up.

Mira took them in hers. She found the scar on the right one with her thumb at once — she did not need to look; she could feel it, a long rough ridge across the palm, old and deep. It was warm. It was much warmer than the rest of his hand.

She thought about the barns. About the children. About Wren in the Queen's tent with the lamp at her lips. She thought about it all, quite clearly, and did not pretend not to.

And then she gave it anyway.

"It's given," said Mira.

---

She felt it go out of her hands into his. She felt it go up his arms. She heard his heart jump, and then slow, and then — very slowly — begin to beat differently, the way a fire burns differently when the wind changes.

And then she heard something she did not understand at first. A tiny sound. A dry sound, like ash, or like grey flakes of something falling and landing softly on cloth. She heard it fall on his shirt, and on the mattress. She thought, absurdly, of the grey crusts that come away from a healing burn.

And he gasped.

He sat up. He sat straight up on the mattress with his hands still in hers, and his breath went in, long and ragged, and stopped. And Mira knew — without looking, without clicking, without anything — that he was looking at her.

It was dark in the room. But it was not quite dark. There was a faint gold glow, coming from somewhere — from their hands, she realised, his and hers together, glowing in the dark between them like a coal; and in that glow he could see her. Her face. Her beaded braids. Her eyes, looking back at him.

Out of all the world, she was the first thing he saw.

"I can see you," he said.

His voice cracked in half.

And then the Queen's captain put his face down on their joined hands, and wept like a boy of nineteen.

---

Mira stayed with him until he stopped. It took a long time. She did not mind.

When at last he had stopped, and lain back on the mattress, and was looking — just looking, at the window, and the faint grey of the coming dawn over the weir, and his own hands, turning them over and over in the light from them — Mira stood up, and stretched, because she was very stiff from the stool, and turned round to go.

There was somebody in the doorway.

She had not heard them. That was how she knew who it was. There was only one person in all of Gloamhaven who had picked pockets in Crookback Market since she was six years old, and climbed chimneys in the dark, and could stand in a doorway so still and so silent that even a Delver could not hear her breathe.

Wren was standing in the door.

She had followed her. All the way down from the loft at the Black Bull, through the sleeping town, to Weir Street; and stood there in the dark, in the doorway, the whole time. And seen.

Mira looked at her face.

And for the first time since the day they had met in the dark of the Deeps, she did not know what Wren was going to do.

## His Name Was Aren

Wren did not do anything.

That was the worst of it, Mira said afterwards. If she had shouted, or thrown something, or flown at him with her fists the way she had once flown at a Gutterling who stole Nettle's bread, Mira would have known what to do. But she did not. She stood in the doorway of the little dark room by the weir and looked at the man on the mattress — at his grey shirt, and his long grey face, wet, and his eyes, which were not grey any more but a kind of light hazel-brown, looking back at her — and her own face did not move at all.

Then she turned round and walked away.

Mira heard her go down the passage, and out of the front door, and up Weir Street towards the Black Bull. She did not run. Her footsteps were quite steady and quite quiet, and they did not stop, and they did not look back.

"That was the sweep," said the man on the mattress.

"Yes," said Mira. "That was Wren."

He did not say anything else. He lay back on the straw and looked at the ceiling, and his hands, which had been turning over and over in the glow, went still.

---

He told them who he was in the morning.

Not all of them. Wren would not come. She had gone back to the loft at the Black Bull and lain down in the straw with her face to the wall and Cinder against her back, and when Tobin came up to tell her that the grey captain had asked to speak to them, she did not turn round. "Tell him I'm busy," she said, to the wall. And Tobin, who had once been the sort of boy who would have argued, looked at the back of her head for a long moment, and went down the ladder without a word.

So it was only Tobin and Mira who sat in the Moffats' kitchen in the grey morning, at the long scrubbed table, with Isla Moffat by the fire not looking at anybody and her husband Ewan standing in the doorway with his arms folded and a face like a shut door; and the man came out of the back room and sat down across the table from them, and put his hands flat on the wood, and told them.

He did not make it sound better than it was. Tobin noticed that. He did not make it sound worse, either, the way people sometimes do when they want you to tell them it is not as bad as all that. He simply told it, in a plain flat hoarse voice, looking at his hands, and stopping sometimes for so long that Tobin thought he had finished, and then going on.

He told them about the Leaning Tower. About being a boy of fifteen with no mother and no father, sleeping in doorways in Emberwick, and a tall old man with a grey beard and a pole finding him one winter night on the North Wall, half frozen, and taking him in. About the tea.

"Dreadful," said Tobin, before he could stop himself. "Sorry. It's just—"

"Yes," said the man. And for a moment something went across his face that might have been the ghost of a smile. "Like a boot boiled in a puddle. I told him so. The first night."

"So did Wren," said Mira.

The man looked down at his hands again.

He told them about learning the lamps. About the wall at the turning of the night. About the oath, and the little brass bowl, and the scar. He told them about the night in the Guild Hall when the old man was thrown out of the Guild and nothing happened; and about standing in the cold in the street outside, nineteen years old, thinking *it's useless*; and the voice in the dark.

"She gave me a new name," he said. "She said the old one was a Lamplighter's name, and I wasn't a Lamplighter any more. She called me Varrick. It means — I don't know what it means. Something in an old tongue. Something grey." He turned his scarred hand over on the table and looked at the palm. "I served her thirty years. I was good at it. I don't — I'm not going to tell you all of it. Not now. You know a great deal of it already. You were there for some." He looked up at Tobin. "The lighthouse."

"Yes," said Tobin. His voice was very steady, which surprised him. "I was there."

"I'm sorry," said the man.

He said it plainly, the way he had said everything else. He did not say *I'm sorry, but*. He did not explain. He only said it, and then went on looking at Tobin, and did not look away.

---

"What do we call you?" said Mira, when he had finished.

He was quiet for a long time.

"He called me by my name," he said at last. "On the road. In the light. I hadn't heard it for thirty years. I'd — I'd stopped thinking it. I'd stopped *having* it." He looked at the table. "It was the first thing he said."

"What was it?" said Mira, very gently.

"Aren," said the man.

The kitchen was quite silent. The weir went on roaring outside the back wall. Isla Moffat, by the fire, had put her hand over her mouth.

"*Aren*," she said. "Oh. Oh, my word. There was an Aren. On the wall at Emberwick. My mother's cousin was in the Remnant there, and she used to write. Old Ash's lad. The boy who went bad." She stared at him. "We thought you were dead. She wrote and said the Hollows had took you. Thirty years ago. They sang for you."

"They should have," said Aren. "In a way they were right."

---

The town of Harrowgate would not have him.

You cannot blame them. I would not have blamed them, and I do not think you should. By the middle of the morning the news had gone right round the town and up into the farms on the moor, the way news does in the Highlands, and they came down to Weir Street to see for themselves: farmers and weavers and shepherds and their wives, and the Low Coombe folk from the barn, with their rope-burns, and a good many children, who had been told to stay at home and had not.

They stood in the street outside the Moffats' house in a crowd, and they did not shout. That was the frightening part. They only stood there and looked at the door.

"Send him out," said somebody, at last.

"Send him out on the moor. Where he came from."

"He took my brother from the barn at Fell End. In the spring. We never saw him again."

"He burned the meeting-house at Stannerside."

"He's the Queen's man. He's been the Queen's man thirty years. You don't stop being the Queen's man because you fell off a horse."

"Blind or not, I'll not have him in this town with my children—"

Ewan Moffat stood in his own doorway with his arms folded and said nothing. He did not argue with them. He agreed with them. You could see it in his face. But it was his door, and his wife had brought the man to it, and he would not move out of it.

Inside, at the kitchen table, Aren sat with his hands flat on the wood and listened, and did not get up.

"They're right," he said quietly. "I'll go. It's only fair. Tell them—"

Something hit the window.

It was a stone. Not a big one. Some boy at the back of the crowd had thrown it, and it struck the little leaded pane over the kitchen sink and cracked it across, and fell back into the street. There was a sudden hush outside, the kind that comes after a thing has been done that cannot be undone. And then a murmur, getting louder.

And Tobin stood up.

---

He had not known he was going to. He told Mira so afterwards. He had been sitting at the table, listening to the crowd, and thinking — he did not know what he had been thinking. About the lighthouse. About Aren saying *I'm sorry*, plainly, and not looking away. About a breakfast fire on a black hill, and a voice asking him three times if he loved him. And then the stone hit the window, and his legs simply stood him up, the way Wren's feet had once run her down the slope of Cinder Hill before she had decided to go.

He went out of the kitchen, and down the passage, and Ewan Moffat looked round at him in astonishment and moved out of the doorway to let him by.

Tobin stood on the Moffats' step, in front of the crowd.

He was shaking. He could feel it in his knees. He was thirteen, and thin, and all elbows, in a torn coat and two odd boots, with his spectacles cracked across one lens and sliding down his nose, and Ash's pole in his hand because he had forgotten to put it down; and he was standing in front of half the town of Harrowgate, who had wanted a Warden's son with a sword and a troop of horse, and he could not think of a single word to say.

"Young Harrowgate," said somebody in the crowd, uncertainly.

"Please," said Tobin. "Please don't throw any more stones. Please." He swallowed. "I want to say something. It won't take long. I'm — I'm very bad at it."

They were quiet. He supposed it was the name.

"He's done terrible things," said Tobin. "The man in there. Aren. Varrick. He did them. I know he did — I know better than any of you. He took the lamp out of my hands in a lighthouse and laughed at me while my hands were burning, and he lied to me about my father, and an old man I loved died because of it." His voice wobbled, and he made it stop. "So I'm not saying he didn't. I'm not saying you should like him. I don't like him. Not yet."

He took a breath.

"But I did something terrible too," he said. "In the same place. I sold the old man. Old Ash. I didn't mean to — I mean, I *did* mean to, I just didn't think it would — I traded the lamp, that wasn't mine, to the Queen's captain, for a letter. To get my father back. And I said I didn't know Ash. Twice. To his face." His hands were shaking on the pole. "And I thought nobody would ever trust me again. And they shouldn't have. I wouldn't have.

"But somebody did," said Tobin. "On Cinder Hill. Somebody asked me if I loved him. And gave me my work back. And then Wren forgave me. And Mira. And I didn't deserve it. Not one bit of it. That's the point, I think. That's the whole *point*." He looked round at all their faces. "So I know what it's like to be the one nobody should trust. And somebody trusted me anyway. And I'm here. I'm standing here."

He stopped. He did not know how to finish. He had never been good at finishing.

"He was given it," he said at last. "Last night. Mira gave it him. The same as you were. The same as me. He can see." He pushed his spectacles up his nose. "I don't know what happens now. But I don't think we get to throw stones at somebody who's been given it. I don't think — I don't think that's how it works."

There was a long silence in Weir Street.

Then Mira came out and stood on the step beside him. She did not say anything. She only stood there, very straight, with the Chronicle on her back, and took his hand.

Nobody threw any more stones.

But nobody came forward, either. And after a while the crowd began, slowly, to break up and drift away up the street, in twos and threes, muttering, looking back over their shoulders at the Moffats' door. They had not been persuaded. They had only been stopped. Tobin could see that, and it made his stomach hurt.

And Wren had not come.

He had looked for her the whole time he was speaking, at the back of the crowd, on the steps, at the windows of the Black Bull at the top of the street. She was not there.

---

It was evening when Aren went up on the wall.

Nobody asked him to. He had sat all day at the Moffats' kitchen table, saying nothing, while Isla Moffat worked at her loom in the next room with a sound like a slow heartbeat and Ewan came and went and would not look at him. Tobin had stayed. Mira had stayed. And at dusk, when the dark began to come down over the moors, as it always did in Gloamhaven, quickly, like a lid, Aren got up and went to the window and looked up the hill at the town wall.

Four of the twelve Ring-lamps were out. Two more were guttering.

"Is there a lamp?" he said. "One of the ones from the sack. I'd like to borrow one, if I might. Only for an hour."

Mira gave him hers.

He went out of the house and up through the town in the dusk with it in his hands, and Tobin and Mira went after him, and so did Isla Moffat, without her shawl. People saw him going, and stopped what they were doing, and came out of their doors to watch. By the time he got to the wall there was a crowd behind him again; not shouting; only following, the way people follow a thing they cannot look away from.

He climbed the steps to the wall-walk. He went along it, with the lamp, to the nearest of the dead Ring-lamps, by the West Gate: an old iron lamp on a bracket on the parapet, its glass black with soot, its wick dry.

And there he stopped. For a long time.

Tobin, watching from the bottom of the steps, thought that he was not going to do it. That he could not. He stood in front of the dead Ring-lamp with the little gold lamp in his left hand, and his right hand hanging at his side, and he did not move; and Tobin thought that he was standing on another wall, thirty years ago, at the turning of the night, behind a tall old man with a pole, being shown how.

Then he set Mira's lamp down on the parapet.

He did not use it. He reached out with his bare right hand — the scarred one — and cupped it round the dry black wick of the Ring-lamp, the way Dick Crane had cupped his hand round a stub of tallow on Midsummer morning. And he held it there.

The wick caught.

It went up gold. Not the tired yellow-grey of Guild oil; the true gold, steady, warm, leaning a little to the east. The light of it went out over the wall-walk and down into the street, and the people in the street, looking up, saw his face lit gold; and they saw his hand.

His palm was glowing.

It was the scar. The old burn-scar right across his right palm, from the oath-flame in the little brass bowl, that had been grey and dead for thirty years — it was shining. Gold, through his skin, from the inside, like the wire in Tobin's Orrery when the Flame ran through it; like a line of fire drawn across his hand. And it did not stop when he took his hand away from the wick. It went on shining.

Somebody in the street below drew in their breath.

Aren looked down at his own hand. He stood on the wall of Harrowgate in the gloaming, with the Ring-lamp burning gold beside him, and turned his palm over and back, and looked at it as if he had never seen it before.

Then he went on along the wall to the next dead lamp, and lit that too.

He lit all six. Nobody helped him, and nobody stopped him. When he came down the steps again at the far end of the wall the whole Ring of Harrowgate was burning — six lamps gold and six still Guild-grey, half and half — and the crowd in the street parted to let him through, in silence, and did not close up behind him for a long time.

---

He came back to the Moffats' kitchen and sat down at the table.

Tobin sat down across from him. Mira went to put the kettle on, because it seemed the sort of moment for a kettle.

"Thank you," said Aren. "For this morning. For what you said. On the step." He was not looking at Tobin. He was looking at his palm, which was still glowing, faintly, on the scrubbed wood. "I heard all of it. You're very like your father in some ways, Harrowgate. And not at all in others."

"Thank you," said Tobin. "I think."

"It was meant as a compliment."

"Yes. I thought it probably was." Tobin hesitated. "Wren didn't come."

"No," said Aren. "I didn't think she would."

He closed his glowing hand slowly into a fist, and then opened it again.

"There's something I have to tell her," he said. "Miss Ashby. Something I've known for — a long time. I've been — I should have told her on the Hill. Or before. In the Deeps, even. I've known since the very first night, on the Greywaste, when I heard her name." He stopped. "And I don't know how. I don't know how to tell her."

Tobin looked at him.

"Tell her what?" he said.

But Aren only shook his head, and put his glowing hand over his eyes, and did not answer.

## What Aren Remembered

Wren came down from the loft on the third morning, because she was hungry, and because Cinder had stopped speaking to her.

That is not quite true. Cinder never spoke to anybody. But for two days, while Wren lay in the straw with her face to the wall and would not come down, Cinder had lain against her back and kept her warm; and on the third morning Cinder got up, and stretched, and looked at Wren for a long moment with her bright fox-eyes, and then went down the ladder by herself without looking back. And Wren lay there and found that she could not bear it.

So she got up and went down.

The others were in the inn kitchen, having breakfast: Tobin and Mira, and the landlady of the Black Bull, and a crowd of Highland children who had taken to following Tobin about because he could mend anything, and had mended a girl's clockwork bird and a boy's catapult and the landlady's spit in one afternoon. Nobody said anything when Wren came in. The landlady gave her porridge. Mira moved up on the bench to make room. Tobin looked at her once, quickly, and away, the way you look at a bruise.

Wren ate her porridge.

"He's asked for you," said Tobin, when she had finished. Very carefully, as if he were walking on ice. "Aren. He says there's something he has to tell you. He says it's — important. He's said so every day."

"I don't want to hear it."

"I know. But—"

"I don't *want* to, Tobin."

Tobin was quiet for a moment.

"He lit the Ring," he said. "Did you see? Two nights ago. Six of the lamps. With his hand. His scar shone." He pushed his spectacles up. "The whole town saw."

"I saw," said Wren.

She had. She had been at the window of the loft, at the top of the street, with her chin on the sill, when the dead lamp by the West Gate went up gold, and the tall man in grey standing over it looked down at his own palm. She had watched him light all six. She had not been able to stop watching.

And she had felt — she did not know what she had felt. It was like being very hungry and very sick at the same time.

"He's sitting by the weir," said Mira quietly. "He sits there in the mornings. On his own. Nobody goes near him." She looked at Wren. "You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to," said Wren. She got up. "That's why I'm going."

---

He was sitting on the stones at the edge of the weir, below the Moffats' house, with his long legs drawn up and his arms round them, watching the water.

It came down off the moor brown and fast and went over the lip of the weir in a long white curve, roaring, and broke at the bottom into foam; and the spray of it hung in the air over the pool below. Wren had never seen so much water moving so fast. In Emberwick, water was the Soot Canal, which did not move at all. He was watching it the way Mira watched colours: as if he had forgotten there was such a thing, and was learning it again.

He heard her coming, and stood up.

He was very tall. She had forgotten how tall. She had seen him so many times on horseback, or at the top of stairs, or with his Knights behind him, that she had forgotten that he was only a man standing on his own two feet, the same as anybody; and he was standing on the wet stones of the weir in his grey shirt, with no armour and no cloak and no sword, and his hands at his sides.

She stopped a little way off. Out of reach.

"Miss Ashby," he said.

"Tobin says you want to tell me something."

"Yes."

"Well, then." She folded her arms. "Tell it."

He looked at her for a moment, as if he had expected more time. Then he looked down at the water.

"May we sit?" he said. "It's — it isn't short."

Wren did not want to sit. But her legs were tired from two days in the straw, and he had already sat down again on his stone, slowly, like an old man. So after a moment she sat too, on another stone, further off, with her knees up and her arms round them, the same as him, and Cinder in the gap between them, washing her paws.

---

"Eight years ago," said Aren, "the Queen sent me to Emberwick."

He did not look at her. He looked at the water going over the weir. His voice was quite flat and quite low, and she had to lean a little to hear it over the roaring.

"There were singers on the North Wall. I don't know if you know. You were very small. There'd always been a few. Remnant folk, mostly from the Low Quarter, who still remembered the old songs. At the turning of the night — the darkest hour, after midnight — they'd go up onto the North Wall by the Ring-lamps and sing. Not loudly. Only the old wall-songs, the Lamplighters' songs, that the Lamplighters used to sing on their rounds before there was a Guild. To keep the lamps company, they said." He paused. "The Guild didn't like it. They thought it made people believe the old stories. But they didn't stop it. It was only a few poor folk, singing."

Wren had gone quite still.

*Mrs Peck*, she thought. *There were singers. On the North Wall. When I was a girl.*

"The Queen didn't like it either," said Aren. "More than the Guild. Much more. I didn't understand why, then. It was only singing. But she said the songs were keeping the Ring alight longer than the oil could — that every time they sang, the lamps burned a little brighter, and a little longer, and the Hollows couldn't come in. She said they had to stop. She sent me to stop them."

He rubbed his palm. Then he noticed he was doing it, and stopped, and put his hand flat on the stone.

"I went up on the wall one night with twelve Knights. At the turning of the night. There were eleven of them singing. By the lamp over the tanneries, on the North Wall." He closed his eyes. "We took them. All eleven. The Knights' touch greyed them — not all at once; enough that they couldn't fight, couldn't sing, couldn't remember why they'd want to. And we took them down off the wall, and out of the Grey Gate, and away east. To the Spire. I gave them to her."

Wren could not speak.

"Two of them," said Aren, "were called Ashby."

---

The weir roared.

Wren sat on her stone with her arms round her knees and did not move. She was not sure she could. She felt as if somebody had pulled a plug out of the bottom of her and everything inside was running out, cold, very fast.

"Tam Ashby," said Aren. "And Linnet. Tam was a lamp-mender. He had a little shop on Pike Street, by the canal, mending Guild lamps for the poor, cheap, and the Guild hated him for it. Big man. Freckles. Very gentle. Linnet — your mother — was the singer. She knew more of the old songs than anybody. She was the one the others followed up the steps." He stopped. "She was small. Black hair. A sharp chin." He looked at Wren, for the first time, and then away again quickly, as if it hurt. "You have her face, Miss Ashby. I knew it on the Greywaste. The first time I saw you close. I knew it before I heard your name."

Wren's hand had gone to her left wrist. To the ribbon.

"There was a song," said Aren, very quietly. "Your mother's. The one she always sang last. It was the oldest of the wall-songs. *Morning comes.*"

"*Morning comes, morning comes*," said Wren. It came out of her in a whisper. She had not known she was going to say it. "*And the dark shall not keep us.*"

"Yes."

"That's all I've got. That's all I've *ever* had. Just that bit." Her voice cracked. "I don't know the rest. I never knew where it came from. I thought she made it up. I thought—"

"No," said Aren. "It's old. It's very old. I learned it on the wall myself. When I was a boy." He swallowed. "But I've forgotten it. All of it. She took it from me. Except that one line." He looked at his hands. "Funny. The same line."

---

"That's not all," said Aren.

Wren did not say anything. She was holding onto her ribbon so hard her fingers hurt.

"Before we came up the steps," said Aren, "your mother heard us. I don't know how. Perhaps she heard the Knights' armour on the stair. She had you with her — you were four, I suppose, or near it. She'd brought you up on the wall, because there was nobody to leave you with, and you'd been asleep in her shawl by the lamp. And when she heard us coming, she took you down the steps on the inside of the wall, quick, to the bottom, where there was a coal-hole under the stair — a little door, for the lamp-oil and the coal, with a grating. And she put you in it." He stopped. "I saw her do it. From the top of the steps. In the lamplight. She knelt down at the coal-hole and said something to you, and shut the little door, and ran back up the steps to the others. To be taken with them. So we'd not look for anybody else."

The weir roared and roared.

"I came down the steps after," said Aren, "when the others had been taken. And I went past the coal-hole. And I heard you."

He was not looking at her at all now. He was looking at the white water going over the lip of the weir, and his face was wet, though it might have been the spray.

"You were humming," he said. "In the coal-hole. In the dark. Very quietly. That tune. *Morning comes.* She'd told you to, I think. To hum, and not come out till morning. And you did." He shut his eyes. "I stood on the stair and listened to you. A child, in the coal, humming. I knew exactly what it was. I knew whose child. I had twelve Knights at my back and the Queen's orders in my head, and she'd have wanted you — she'd have wanted you most of all, a singer's child, a child who already had the tune. She'd have been — pleased."

He stopped. For a long time. Wren thought he was not going to go on.

"I walked past," said Aren.

---

"I didn't give you up," he said. "That's the only thing. In thirty years, the only thing I didn't give her. I don't know why. I've thought about it every day since the Greywaste, and I don't know. I told myself it was because you were only a child and of no use. That isn't true. I walked past the coal-hole and up the street and out of the Grey Gate with my Knights and eleven grey singers, and I never told her. I never told anybody." He opened his eyes and looked at the water. "A neighbour found you in the morning, I suppose. I didn't know what happened to you. I never let myself find out. And then eight years later a sweep-girl with a lamp in her coat walked out of the Grey Gate onto the Greywaste with an old man and a boy and a fox; and I saw your face; and I heard Ash call you *Wren Ashby*. And I knew."

"You *knew*," said Wren.

Her voice did not sound like hers. It sounded like something very far away and very cold.

"Yes."

"All that time. The Greywaste. The Deeps. The Black Stair. Vaelport. The lighthouse. The Hill." Each word came out separately, like a stone dropped into water. "All that time you knew who I was. You knew who my mam and dad were. You knew where they *were*. And you chased me. And you tried to make me give it up. And you gave me to *her*, on the Hill, in the tent, so she'd make me blow it out. And she showed me them. In the mirror." Her voice did not change. "She showed me my mam and dad. Singing. And you *knew*."

"Yes," said Aren.

He did not say *but*. He did not say anything else at all.

---

"Where are they?" said Wren.

"In the Spire," said Aren. "Nightfall Spire. On an island in the Sunless Sea. She keeps the greyed there, the ones she takes — thousands of them, in great halls. They don't die. They only stand, and forget." He looked at her. "I believe they're there still. Tam and Linnet. I have no reason to think they're not. And Lord Edric — Harrowgate's father — I took him there myself, in the winter, and he was alive when I left him. She keeps him clear. She likes him to know where he is." He hesitated. "And the old king."

"What old king?"

"Ardric," said Aren. "The one who made the bargain. Three hundred years ago. He's in the top of the Spire. On a throne. He can't die. She keeps him." He shook his head. "I've seen him. I wish I hadn't."

Wren stood up.

She stood up off the stone at the edge of the weir, and the spray blew in her face, and she did not wipe it off.

She was thinking — she could not stop thinking — all of it at once, so fast that it all ran together. That her mother had a name. Linnet. That her father had a name, and freckles, and a shop on Pike Street that she must have walked past a hundred times. That they had been Lamplighters — real ones, singers on the wall — and she had never known; she had grown up in Mott's yard thinking she was nobody's, that she had been *lost*, the way you lose a button down a drain. And she had not been lost. She had been *hidden*. Her mother had put her in a coal-hole in the dark and told her to hum, and run back up the steps to be taken, so they would not look for her. And the song Wren had carried her whole life, the scrap of it, the one line, had not been a scrap at all. It had been a gift. Her mother had given it her in the dark, in a coal-hole, as the last thing she had to give.

And the man who had done it — who had taken them, and walked past, and known, all this time — was sitting on a stone in front of her in his shirt, with his face wet, asking for nothing.

"I'll never forgive you," said Wren.

She said it quite quietly. She did not shout. She did not need to.

"Not ever. Not as long as I live. I don't care if you're sorry. I don't care if he called you by your name on the road and your hand glows. I don't care if you light every lamp in Gloamhaven." Her voice was perfectly steady and perfectly cold, like a pane of ice. "You took them. And you knew. And I will *never* forgive you for it, and I'd sooner the lamp went out than give you one spark of it, ever again. Do you hear me?"

"Yes," said Aren. "I hear you."

He did not argue. He sat on his stone and looked up at her, and did not look away, and did not say anything else.

Wren turned round and walked away from him.

---

She did not go back up into the town.

She went along the river, the other way, upstream, past the weir and out of the end of Harrowgate where the last houses gave out, onto the open moor. She did not decide to. Her feet did it. She walked up the river-bank in the grey morning with the water roaring beside her and the wind in her face, and she did not stop, and she did not look back.

Cinder came after her.

Wren heard her, and did not turn round. "Go back," she said. "Go *back*, Cinder. I don't want you."

Cinder did not go back. She trotted along behind Wren in the heather, a little way off, not too close, with her spark tucked down small; and when Wren stopped, she stopped; and when Wren went on, she went on. And after a while Wren gave up telling her.

She walked all morning. The town went down behind her into its valley and was gone. The river got smaller and wilder, and turned into a stream, and the stream went up into the moor between peat-banks and boulders. There was nobody anywhere. Only heather, and grey sky, and sheep, and the wind, and a curlew somewhere crying with a long sad bubbling cry that went on and on and stopped.

She did not cry.

She did not feel like crying. She felt cold — not on the outside, where the wind was; on the inside, in her chest, where the lamp's warmth usually was. As if something had gone out.

---

It was afternoon, and the light was beginning to go, when she stopped at last.

She was on the top of a long bare ridge, with the moor falling away on both sides and nothing in any direction but grey. Her legs were shaking. She had not eaten since the porridge. She sat down on a rock with her back to the wind, and Cinder sat down at her feet, and looked up at her.

Wren took the lamp out of her coat.

She did not know why. She wanted it, she supposed. She wanted the warmth of it, after the cold; she wanted the little gold flame in its cracked glass chimney, the one that had been with her since Thornbury House, through the Soot Canal and the Deeps and the Forge and the Hill. She wanted it the way you want your own bed.

She took it out, and held it up in front of her face, in the grey dusk on the high moor.

It was a coal.

Not out. It had never been out, not since the Glowstone City. But it had gone down. It was a small dull red glow at the bottom of the chimney, like a coal under ash — the way it had been for days after Ash died, in Vaelport, on the long road east; the way it had been when Wren had put it away in a drawer in the Pellows' cottage and stopped looking at it. It gave out hardly any light at all. It did not reach her fingers. And when she held it close to her face, she could hardly feel it.

Wren looked at it for a long time.

She knew why. That was the worst of it. She knew exactly why. It was the first thing Ash had ever taught her about it, in the Leaning Tower, on the night of the dreadful tea. *Used as a weapon, it barely glows. Held out as a gift, it blazes.* And she had stood on the stones of the weir and said *I'd sooner it went out than give you one spark of it*; and she had meant every word; and the lamp had heard her.

"Rot," said Wren, to the lamp, very quietly. "*Rot* to you."

It did not answer. It never had, except once.

The dark came down over the high moor, the way it always did in Gloamhaven, quickly, like a lid; and Wren Ashby sat on a rock on the top of the world, with a fox at her feet and a coal in her hands, and did not know where to go.

## A Coal and a Grudge

It was the sheep that found her, in the end.

Wren had sat on her rock on the ridge until it was quite dark, and then a while longer, because she could not think of anywhere to go and did not want to go back. Then the wind got up and the cold came in under her coat, and she got up and walked, because walking was warmer than sitting; and in the dark, with the lamp a coal in her pocket and no light to see by, she lost the stream, and then the ridge, and then any idea of where she was at all.

She walked into the sheep.

There were a great many of them, lying down in the heather in a hollow out of the wind, and she fell over the first one and landed on the second, and they all got up at once with a noise like a hundred wet blankets being shaken, and said *meh* in every direction. Cinder was beside herself. Wren lay in the heather with a sheep standing on her foot and said every word she knew that Nettle was not allowed to say.

And somewhere close by, a door opened, and light came out of it, and a voice shouted — a very old, very loud voice, high and cracked, with a Highland burr in it so thick you could have stood a spoon up in it:

"*WHO'S AT MY YOWES?*"

---

The voice belonged to Granny Kettle.

That was what she told Wren her name was, anyway, at the top of her lungs, while she hauled Wren up out of the heather by the back of her coat with one bony hand, and held a horn lantern up to her face with the other, and peered at her. She was very small and very bent and very old — older than Sister Hale, Wren thought, older than anybody she had ever seen — and wrapped in so many shawls that she looked like a heap of washing with a face at the top. The face was brown and creased all over like a walnut, and in the middle of it were two very sharp black eyes.

"*A LASSIE!*" shouted Granny Kettle. "*A WEE LASSIE AT MY YOWES IN THE DARK! AND A DUG!*"

"She's a fox," said Wren.

"*WHAT?*"

"*A FOX!*" shouted Wren.

"*A FOX?*" Granny Kettle peered at Cinder, who looked back at her with great dignity. "*WELL, SHE'S NO' TO GO AT MY HENS!*" She let go of Wren's coat. "*YE'LL BE COLD. AND HUNGRY. YE LOOK IT. COME IN.*"

And she turned round and stumped off through the sheep, with the lantern swinging, towards a little low stone hut on the edge of a black tarn, with smoke coming out of a hole in its turf roof and the door standing open; and after a moment, because there was nothing else to do and because she *was* cold and hungry, Wren went after her.

---

It was a bothy: one room, with a fire on a flat stone in the middle of the floor and the smoke going up through the hole in the roof — some of it — and the rest of it hanging about under the rafters like a grey ceiling. There was a box-bed in the wall. There was a table and a stool and a great black iron pot. There were three hens on a beam, asleep. There was a ewe lying by the fire with two lambs against her, who looked up at Wren when she came in and did not get up.

"*SHE'S POORLY*," shouted Granny Kettle, pointing at the ewe. "*SO SHE'S IN.* *SIT.*"

Wren sat on the stool. Granny Kettle gave her a bowl of porridge out of the black pot, so thick and so hot that the spoon stood up in it, with salt on and no sugar, the way they eat it in the Highlands. Wren had never tasted anything so good.

She ate it all. She ate a second bowl. Cinder had a bowl on the floor, and ate it, and then lay down against the poorly ewe, who did not seem to mind.

Granny Kettle did not ask her anything. She sat on the edge of the box-bed with her knitting, which was enormous and grey and appeared to be either a blanket or a very long scarf, and knitted, and looked at Wren from time to time with her sharp black eyes; and when Wren tried to say thank you, she shouted "*WHAT?*" and Wren gave up.

She was deaf. She was very, very deaf. It was oddly restful.

---

Wren slept in the bothy that night, on a heap of sheepskins by the fire, with Cinder against her stomach and the poorly ewe against her back.

She did not dream. Or if she did, she did not remember. But she woke in the grey before dawn with the fire down to red, and the hens shifting on their beam, and Granny Kettle snoring in the box-bed like a saw going through a log; and she lay there with her eyes open and did not want to think, and thought anyway.

She thought about the coal-hole.

She did not remember it. She tried. She lay on the sheepskins and shut her eyes and tried as hard as she had ever tried anything to remember being four, and small, and in the dark, with coal-dust under her fingers and the tune in her mouth and her mother's voice going away up the steps. Nothing came. Only the tune. *Morning comes, morning comes.* She had always had the tune. She had never known where from.

She thought about her mother kneeling at the little door. Saying something. What had she said?

*Hum, and don't come out till morning.*

And she had hummed. And she had not come out. And somebody had walked past.

*I walked past*, he had said, on the stone by the weir, with his face wet.

Wren rolled over on the sheepskins and put her face in Cinder's fur.

---

In the morning she helped Granny Kettle with the sheep.

She did not mean to. But Granny Kettle shouted "*YE CAN CARRY THAT*" and handed her a bucket, and it seemed rude not to, and anyway it was something to do. So she carried the bucket, and then she held a lamb while Granny Kettle looked at its foot, and then she chased three yowes out of the tarn, where they had no business to be, and fell in, and came out black with peat to the waist. Granny Kettle laughed so hard she had to sit down on a rock.

It was a good morning. Wren had not expected that. The wind dropped, and the cloud went thin over the moor, so that the pale sun showed through like a coin in dirty water, and the black tarn was still as glass, with the sky in it. And she was too busy to think, mostly. Sheep are very good at stopping you thinking.

But at noon, when they sat down by the bothy door with bread and cheese, she took the lamp out of her pocket, because she could not help it, and looked at it.

It was still a coal.

A small dull red glow at the bottom of the chimney. It had not got any worse in the night. It had not got any better. It sat in her palm and hardly lit her fingers.

Granny Kettle leaned over and looked at it too.

"*THAT'S A POOR SORT OF A LAMP*," she shouted.

"It's not poor," said Wren. "It's — it's just low."

"*WHAT?*"

"*IT'S JUST LOW!*"

Granny Kettle sniffed. She peered at the lamp with her sharp black eyes, and then at Wren's face, for a long time.

"*I'LL TELL YE SOMETHING*," she shouted, "*MY OLD MAM TELT ME*. *YE CANNAE KEEP A COAL AND A GRUDGE IN THE SAME POCKET, HEN.*" She jabbed a bony finger at the lamp. "*ONE O' THEM GOES OOT.*"

Wren stared at her.

"What?"

"*A COAL*," shouted Granny Kettle, "*AND A GRUDGE.* *IN THE SAME POCKET.* *YE CANNAE.* *ONE GOES OOT.*" She nodded several times, very satisfied, and went back to her bread and cheese. "*MY MAM ALWAYS SAID IT. WHEN WE'D BEEN FECHTING. ME AND MY SISTERS.*" She chewed. "*SHE MEANT THE FIRE, MIND. KEEPING A COAL IN YER POCKET FOR THE MORNING. TAE START THE FIRE. YE'D NOT TO PUT ANYTHING ELSE IN WITH IT.*"

"She meant the fire," said Wren.

"*WHAT?*"

"*SHE MEANT THE FIRE.*"

"*AYE*," shouted Granny Kettle, and looked at Wren with her sharp black eyes, and went on chewing. "*THE FIRE.*"

---

Wren walked down to the edge of the black tarn after that, on her own, and sat on a rock.

She did not want to think about it. She thought about it anyway. That was the trouble with being given things; they would not stop being given, even when you did not want them.

*Ye cannae keep a coal and a grudge in the same pocket.*

She had a grudge. She knew that. She had it in her chest like a stone, cold and heavy and sharp-edged, the stone she had picked up on the weir when he said *Ashby*. She had been carrying it all yesterday, all across the moor, and it had got heavier with every step. And she did not want to put it down. That was the thing. She wanted to keep it. She wanted to hold onto it with both hands, the way she used to hold onto a stolen bun in Crookback Market with Mott's lads after her, because it was *hers*, and it was the only thing she had, and if she let go of it she would have nothing.

And she had a coal.

She took it out again and looked at it. Low and red and nearly nothing.

She thought — she could not help it — about the toast.

About the man on Cinder Hill, crouching by the fire, laughing at the black toast. About him holding the second piece out to her across the fire, golden. *How much?* she had said. *No charge. It's given.* And she had not deserved it. Not one bit. She had said unforgivable things to Tobin. She had let the lamp go down to a coal in the Glowstone City and put it in a drawer. She had held it to her lips in the Queen's tent and breathed in. She had not deserved the toast.

He had given it anyway.

She thought about Tobin. About what she had said to him on the docks at Vaelport, with Ash just dead: *You sold him. You sold Ash for a letter. I wish it had been you.* She had meant it, then, the way she meant it now. She had meant it with her whole cold heart. And on the Hill she had asked his forgiveness, and it had cost her everything — and he had given it. Just like that. He had wrapped his scarf round her neck again.

And she thought about Ash.

That was the one she did not want. She tried to push it away, and it would not go. She thought about the Forge, under Vaelport, in the white glare: Ash leaning on his two poles at the forge-door, holding it, with the dark coming up behind; and Varrick in front of him, with his Knights. And Ash had looked at him. At the Queen's captain, who had been his own lad, the boy he had found half frozen on the wall and taken in and given his tea and his tower and his oath and his Flame; the boy who had gone over to the Queen and served her thirty years and hunted them all across Gloamhaven and was standing there in the Forge with his sword out, about to bring the roof down.

And Ash had said *lad*.

He had said it with grief. Wren remembered that now, sitting on the rock by the tarn. She had not understood it at the time. She had been too frightened and too angry and too busy. But Ash had looked at Varrick in the Forge, and said *lad*, and his face had been full of grief, not hate. Not even anger. Only grief, the way your face is when you look at somebody you love who is lost.

He had known. Ash had known everything Varrick was, and everything he had done — more than Wren knew, much more; thirty years of it. And he had never stopped calling him lad. Not once. Not on the Greywaste, or the Black Stair, or in the Forge. Right to the end.

*He called me lad*, Aren had said. *Right to the end. Every time.*

Wren put her face down on her knees.

---

She went back that afternoon.

I want to tell you that she went because she had decided to forgive him; that she had sat on the rock by the black tarn and thought it all through and come to the end of it, and put down her grudge, and stood up feeling light and glad and warm. But that is not how it happened, and I promised myself when I began this story that I would only tell you what was true.

She went back because she could not bear the coal any more.

She did not *feel* like forgiving him. Not one bit. She felt exactly the same as she had on the weir. Cold. Sick. Furious. She went back down the moor in the afternoon with Cinder at her heels, the way she had come, following the stream down into the valley, and every step of the way she felt like turning round. She did not want to. She wanted to keep her stone. She went anyway.

Granny Kettle stood at the door of the bothy and watched her go.

"*YE'LL COME BACK?*" she shouted.

"*YES*," shouted Wren. "*I PROMISE.*"

"*WHAT?*"

"*I PROMISE!*"

"*AYE*," shouted Granny Kettle, and waved her knitting. "*WELL, MIND YE DO. THAT YOWE LIKES YE.*"

---

It was dusk when she came into Harrowgate.

They were all in the Moffats' weaving-room — the big room at the front of the house, with the two great looms in it and the bolts of grey cloth stacked against the walls and the sound of the weir coming through the floor. Tobin and Mira. Isla and Ewan Moffat and their children. A handful of the Low Coombe folk, with their lamps. The old man with the walnut face and the white beard, from the street. And Aren.

They had been sitting there, Wren found out afterwards, since she went. Not all the time. But every evening. Tobin had wanted to go after her onto the moor, and Mira had stopped him, and said she would come back, and Tobin had said how did she *know*, and Mira had said because it was Wren.

They all stood up when she came in.

Nobody said anything. Wren did not look at any of them. She did not look at Tobin's face, which had gone white and then red, or Mira's, which was quite still. She looked at the man by the window, in his grey shirt, who had stood up slowest of all, and was standing now with his back against the window-frame and his hands at his sides, looking at her.

She walked across the room to him.

She did not walk fast. She did not walk slowly. She walked the way you walk across a room when every single step is costing you something, and you have decided to pay it anyway. Her boots were wet and black with peat from the tarn. Her face was set. She stopped in front of him, close, closer than she had been on the weir; close enough to touch.

And she took the lamp out of her coat.

It was a coal. Everyone in the room could see it: a small dull red glow at the bottom of the chimney, giving out no light at all. Tobin made a small sound.

Wren held it out.

Not up, the way you hold up a lamp to see by. Not forward, the way you thrust a light at somebody to make them back away, the way she had thrust it at Hollows in the dark more times than she could count. Out. Flat on her two hands, held out to him at the height of his chest, the way you hold out a loaf of bread to somebody who is hungry. The way somebody had once held out a loaf of bread to her.

"It's given," said Wren.

Her voice came out rough and low, like a door that has been shut all winter.

"Don't make me say it twice."

---

He did not take it.

Not at first. He stood against the window and looked down at the lamp in her hands, and then at her face, and his own face did something Wren had never seen any face do before: it seemed to break, very quietly, all the way across, like ice on a pond when the spring comes. And he did not say anything. He could not.

Then he put out his scarred right hand, the one with the oath-scar across the palm, and laid it over the top of the lamp, very lightly, over Wren's two hands.

And the lamp blazed.

It went up out of the bottom of its chimney like a fire when you open the door of the stove — not red; gold, white-gold, roaring, so bright that everybody in the room put up their hands to their eyes, and the light went out through the window into Weir Street and up the walls and across the bolts of grey cloth stacked against them, which were not grey at all, it turned out, but blue, and green, and a deep dark heather-purple that nobody in Harrowgate had known they had been weaving. Cinder yelped. The children shrieked. Isla Moffat sat down on her loom-bench with both hands over her mouth.

And on Wren's left wrist her mother's ribbon went red, red, red, all the way to the knot.

Aren's palm was shining through his skin, gold, along the line of the old scar. He stood with his hand on the lamp and the light on his face and did not move.

"Thank you," he said at last. It was barely a whisper.

"Don't," said Wren. Her eyes were running and she did not care. "Don't say thank you. I didn't do it because I wanted to. I *didn't* want to. I still don't. I'm — I'm still *angry*. I'll be angry for ages, I expect. I'm not good at stopping." She sniffed hard. "But it's given. So that's that."

"Yes," said Aren. "That's that."

"And stop calling me *Miss Ashby*," said Wren. "I hate it. It's what *she* calls me. In the tent. It's—" She stopped. "My name's Wren."

"Wren," said Aren.

And it was the strangest thing: when he said it, it did not sound like the grey captain at all. It sounded like a man who had just been given something back that he had lost a very long time ago, and did not know yet what to do with it.

---

Nobody said anything for a long while after that. The lamp went on burning, gold, in Wren's hands, and she did not put it away.

Then Mira said, softly, "Wren."

"What?"

"Your pocket."

Wren looked down.

The singing-stone — the smooth grey stone from the Delvers, the one that *heard the cold*, that she had carried in her coat pocket since the Deeps — had begun to hum.

It was very faint at first. She could feel it more than hear it, a buzzing against her hip like a bee in a jar. Then it got louder. It got louder while they stood there listening, until it was a real hum, low and steady and growing, so that the bolts of cloth seemed to tremble with it; and it did not stop.

Tobin went to the window and looked out.

Then he went very still.

"Wren," he said. "Aren. Come and look."

They came and looked.

Beyond the town wall of Harrowgate, beyond the river and the weir and the fields, the moor went up into the dusk, the way it always did, grey on grey. But it was not still. Something was moving on it. Something was coming down off the high moor from the east, out of the dark, slowly, the way a tide comes in over sand. It was grey, and it was wide — wider than the town, wider than the valley — and it did not make any sound at all.

Hollows. Hundreds of them. Thousands, perhaps. And among them, darker, harder, in ranks, on horseback, the Hollow Knights.

And in the middle of them, lurching over the rough ground of the moor with its gilded wheels and its gilt flame on the door, and its two grey horses blowing, a carriage.

The wind came round, off the moor, through the open window. And on it, faint but quite unmistakable, under the cold and the peat-smoke and the smell of the Hollows, which is no smell at all, Wren smelt something she knew.

Lavender.

## The Highland Ring

The bells of Harrowgate began to ring a little after dark.

There were only two of them, in the squat stone tower of the old meeting-house at the top of the town, and they were cracked, and nobody had rung them for anything but a funeral in forty years. But somebody rang them now, as hard as they could, so that the whole town shook with the jangle; and doors banged open all up and down the steep streets, and people ran out into them with whatever they could carry — pitchforks, and lanterns, and babies — and stood looking up at the wall.

Wren was on the wall already. So were Tobin and Mira and Aren and Cinder, and half the Low Coombe folk with their thirty lamps, and the old man with the walnut face, who had climbed the steps faster than anybody and was standing at the parapet over the East Gate with his stick in both hands like a club.

They looked out over the valley.

The Hollows had come down off the moor in the last of the light. Now they filled the fields beyond the river from side to side: grey, faceless, like smoke poured into the shapes of people, drifting rather than walking, a great slow silent tide of them, so many that you could not see the grass between. They did not make a sound. They never did. But the cold came off them ahead of them, up the slope and over the wall, so that the breath of everybody on the wall-walk went up white, and the stones were rimed with frost in the time it takes to say so.

Behind them, in ranks, on their great grey horses, the Hollow Knights. Two hundred, Tobin thought. Perhaps more. Grey armour, grey faces, empty eyes. Without their captain they did not move at all; they only sat their horses in the dark, waiting.

And in the middle of the Knights, on a little rise by the river, with its lamps lit and its door open and a footman holding a white flag on a pole beside it, stood the carriage.

"He wants to *talk*," said Tobin, disbelieving. "He's brought a *flag*."

---

The Lord Steward of Emberwick did not get out of his carriage. He leaned out of it, instead, with a speaking-trumpet in one fat ringed hand, and the other clutching the door-frame, because the ground was rough and the carriage was tilting; and his voice came up the slope to the wall, high and squeaky and much too loud.

"*PEOPLE OF HARROWGATE!*"

Nobody answered.

"*I AM THE LORD STEWARD OF EMBERWICK, KEEPER OF THE BEACON, AND PROTECTOR OF THE RING!*" The trumpet squealed. "*I COME UNDER A FLAG OF TRUCE, ON BEHALF OF HER MAJESTY THE QUEEN OF—*" He seemed to lose track of which Queen, and of what. "*—ON BEHALF OF HER MAJESTY!*"

"He's frightened," said Mira softly. "Listen to him."

"*HER MAJESTY*," said Prowle, "*HAS NO QUARREL WITH THE PEOPLE OF HARROWGATE! NONE WHATSOEVER! SHE WISHES ONLY TO OFFER YOU — HER PROTECTION!*" He paused, as if waiting for applause. "*THE PROTECTION OF THE STEWARD! YOUR RING IS FAILING! WE CAN ALL SEE IT! HALF YOUR LAMPS ARE GREY! WITHOUT HER MAJESTY'S PROTECTION, WHAT IS OUT HERE WILL BE IN THERE BEFORE MORNING!*"

He waved the trumpet at the Hollows, and nearly fell out of the carriage.

"*BUT IT NEED NOT BE SO! HER MAJESTY ASKS ONLY TWO SMALL THINGS!*" The trumpet squealed again. "*HAND OVER THE DESERTER VARRICK, WHO HAS BETRAYED HER! AND THE LAMP! THE GIRL ASHBY'S LAMP! HAND THEM OVER, AND HER MAJESTY WILL WITHDRAW, AND HARROWGATE WILL BE SPARED, AND I SHALL SEE TO IT PERSONALLY THAT YOUR OIL IS DELIVERED AT A VERY REASONABLE—*"

"*Go home, Cuthbert!*" shouted the old man with the walnut face, and threw his stick.

It fell a long way short. But everybody on the wall cheered.

---

Prowle withdrew into his carriage. The door slammed. The footman with the white flag looked round, uncertainly, and then lowered it.

And the Hollows came.

They came up the slope from the river all at once, like a wave breaking — silent, and grey, and fast, much faster than Hollows had any right to be, as if something behind them was driving them on. And the cold came with them. It hit the wall of Harrowgate like the wind off a glacier, and Wren felt it go through her coat and her shirt and her skin as if they were not there, and right into her chest; and she gasped, and her hand went to the lamp.

On the parapet beside her, the nearest of the Guild-grey Ring-lamps — one of the six that Aren had not lit — went out.

Then the next one.

Then the one by the South Gate, a long way round, a little point of grey light winking out in the dark like an eye closing.

"Three," said Tobin. His voice was very tight. "Three gone. There's only nine. Six of them true, but — it's a *gap*, Wren, look, all along the south side, it's dark, they'll—"

The Hollows had seen it too. The grey tide turned along the slope, the way water turns towards a hole in a dam, and went for the dark side of the wall.

Everybody on the wall ran.

They ran along the wall-walk with their lamps, and their candles, and their bare warm hands, towards the dark stretch by the South Gate; Wren in front, with Cinder, and Aren behind her, and the Low Coombe folk and the old man and Tobin with Ash's pole; and they got there just as the first of the Hollows came up the outside of the wall like smoke going up a chimney, and over the top.

---

It was bad, for a while.

I will not pretend it was not. There were not enough of them. There were perhaps forty people on the wall of Harrowgate who had been given the Flame, and thirty lamps, and a fox; and there were Hollows in their thousands coming up the outside of the wall all along the south side, in the dark, where the dead Ring-lamps were. Wherever a lamp was held out, the Hollows burned and shrieked and fled — the only sound they ever make — and went back down the wall like smoke blown off a fire. But for every one that went down, two came up. And the cold was so bad that people's hands were going numb round their lamps, and their lamps were going low.

Wren held hers out until her arm shook. It burned gold and steady, as it had since the Moffats' weaving-room, and every Hollow it touched burned away to nothing. But it was only one lamp. It could only be in one place. And she could see, all along the wall in both directions, the little lights of the others getting smaller, and further apart, and the grey getting between them.

Beside her, a Low Coombe farmer went down on his knees, grey to the elbows, with his lamp still in his hand.

"No," said Wren. "*No*—"

She turned to him. And as she turned, the Hollow she had been holding back came over the parapet behind her, into the gap.

Cinder went for it. Cinder, who was smaller than a terrier and had never been afraid of anything; she went for it with her spark blazing like a torch, straight at its grey middle, and it shrieked and went back over the wall. But there was another behind it. And another.

"There's too many," said Tobin. He was white to the lips. He was standing with Ash's pole held across the gap in the parapet in both hands, like a bar across a door, and it was glowing faintly gold where his scarred palms gripped it, and the Hollows would not come past it; but he was shaking. "There's too many, Wren, there's just — there isn't *enough* of us—"

And then Mira said, "*Listen*."

---

Nobody could hear anything but the Hollows shrieking. Nobody but Mira.

She was standing on the parapet itself, on the very top of the wall, with no lamp in her hands at all — she had given hers to the farmer — holding on to the iron bracket of a dead Ring-lamp, and leaning out into the dark, towards the west. And her face had changed.

"*Listen*," said Mira again. "On the road. The Emberwick road. Over the hill." Her voice went up. "*Wheels*. And feet. Hundreds of feet. And — and *singing*, Wren. Somebody's *singing*—"

Then Wren heard it too.

It came over the brow of the hill to the west, down the drove-road from the moor: faint, at first, and then louder, a song coming nearer through the dark. It was a cracked, strong, old voice, singing as loud as it could, and a great many other voices singing with it, badly, and all out of time.

*Though I walk through the gloaming, I will fear no dark, for the Flame is with me…*

And over the brow of the hill came the lights.

There were hundreds of them. They came over the skyline in a long line, like a river of gold pouring over the edge of the moor and down the road towards Harrowgate. Lamps — the lamps from the racks of Cinder Hill, held up high on poles and in hands. Candles. Lanterns. Torches. Hands held up in the dark with nothing in them at all, glowing. Carts with lamps on their shafts. A mule with a lantern on each ear.

And at the front, on the first cart, standing up in it — standing up, with three people holding her steady and her crutch in her good hand, waving it over her head like a banner — was Sister Hale.

---

Wren did not find out until afterwards how they had come. She will not mind my telling you now.

When the Lord Steward had galloped out of the South Gate of Emberwick before dawn with his strongbox, going east, Bracken had stood in Beacon Square with his gold lantern and thought about it; and then he had sent a boy on a pony south across the plain to Cinder Hill as fast as the pony would go, with a message for Sister Hale. *The Steward's gone east after the children. The grey captain's gone east. Something's coming in the Highlands.*

But Sister Hale had already left the Hill. She had been on the road north for a week, with fifty of the Remnant and the last of the hundred lamps, because — she said — she'd had a feeling, and she had learned at her age not to argue with her feelings, because they were nearly always right and it saved time. The pony met her on the road. She read the message, and said "*Oh*, duck," and turned every cart east.

They had picked up the Heskeths at the Scarecrow Farms on the way, because Will Hesketh had heard they were going, and simply put down his spade and walked out of his field. And the children of Stallbridge, who had come over the wall by the tannery and followed the carts for two days and would not be sent home. And in Emberwick, Bracken had come with them — leaving the Beacon to Mott, who had wept and said he was not worthy and then gone and stood at the foot of the stair with his best hat on and lit the Flame for everybody who came. And half of Soot Lane. And Mrs Fenn's eldest. And Snib, riding on the mule's head.

And Nettle.

Nettle had been told, very firmly, by everybody, that she was not coming. She was far too small. It was far too far. It was far too dangerous.

She had stowed away in a flour-barrel on the back of the bakery cart, and not been discovered until the second night, when Mrs Fenn's eldest went to get the flour for the morning's damper and found a small floury girl inside, fast asleep, with a lit candle in each fist.

---

They came down the road to Harrowgate singing, and the Hollows heard them, and turned.

I do not know if Hollows can be afraid. I think, perhaps, they can. Because when that long river of gold came pouring down off the moor towards them, in the dark, singing, the grey tide at the foot of the wall wavered — and some of it drew back, and some of it broke away along the slope; and the Hollows that were on the top of the wall shrieked and went back down it like smoke out of an open window.

Sister Hale's carts came up the road to the West Gate. The gate opened. And they came in.

They came up onto the wall of Harrowgate by every stair, in their hundreds — Remnant and Highlanders, farmers and townsfolk, Soot Lane and Stallbridge and the Scarecrow Farms; Will Hesketh with his big warm hands; Bracken in his helmet; Mrs Fenn's eldest with a lantern; Snib, who went up the outside of the wall instead of the steps, and over the top, and along the parapet like a spider, lighting every dead Ring-lamp he passed with one long finger. And Nettle, who went straight to Wren, and stood on the wall-walk beside her, and held up two candles, one in each fist, and glared out at the Hollows as if they had stolen her bread.

They stood along the whole of the wall of Harrowgate, shoulder to shoulder, all the way round. North side and south side. East Gate and West. Hundreds of them. Every one with a flame in their hands.

And the Ring was them.

That was what Tobin saw, and said, afterwards, in a voice full of wonder. The twelve old iron lamps on their brackets were all lit now, every one, true gold, by Snib's finger. But they hardly mattered. Between them, all the way round the wall, without a gap anywhere, there were people; and every person was a light; and the lights stood so close together that their glow ran into each other and made one long unbroken line of gold all the way round the town in the dark — not a Ring of lamps on a wall, with gaps between, that the Guild had to feed with oil and that could fail. A Ring of people. A Ring that could not fail, because it was given.

"It's not lamps," said Tobin. "It never was. It's — it's *us*. The Ring is *us*."

---

Aren went out of the East Gate alone.

Nobody saw him go, at first. He had been beside Wren on the south wall all through the worst of it, with his bare glowing hand held out over the parapet; and the Hollows would not come near it. And then, when Sister Hale's carts came over the hill and the singing began, he had looked out across the river, at the ranks of the Hollow Knights sitting their horses in the dark without moving. And he had turned round without a word and gone down the steps.

Wren saw him a moment later, from the wall: a tall figure in a grey shirt, with no armour, no cloak and no sword, walking out of the East Gate and down the slope towards the river, alone, with nothing in his hands but the glow of his palm.

"*Aren*—"

He did not turn round.

He went down through the fields, where the Hollows had been, and they drew back from him, shrieking. He went across the ford below the weir, up to his knees in the brown rushing water. And he came up out of it on the far side, and walked up the little rise by the river, past the tilted carriage with its door shut tight, to the front rank of the Hollow Knights.

They did not move. They sat on their great grey horses, in their grey armour, with their grey faces and their empty eyes, and looked over his head at nothing.

Aren stopped in front of the first one.

"Hob Farrell," he said.

His voice carried. It was not loud. But the night had gone quiet, and the river was quiet, and on the wall of Harrowgate, every one of the hundreds of lights stood still, and listened.

"Hob Farrell," said Aren. "Of Kettlebridge, in the Highlands. A shepherd. You had a wife called Mary and two boys. I took you off your hill in the winter nineteen years ago, and gave you to her, and she made you a Knight." He held up his glowing hand. "I know you, Hob. I know every one of you. I've known you all your grey lives. I'm sorry."

The Knight did not move.

Aren reached up and laid his hand on the Knight's grey gauntlet, on the reins.

For a long moment nothing happened at all. Then the Knight's grey head turned — very slowly, the way the scarecrow's head had turned in the Heskeths' field, the way a sleeper turns towards a voice — and looked down at him.

Aren did not let go.

And the grey began to go out of Hob Farrell. It went slowly, from the hand on the reins, up the arm, the way the colour had gone back into Posy from the roots of her hair. The grey went out of the armour and it was only steel, old and dull and dented. The grey went out of the face under the helm, and it was a man's face — a frightened, bewildered, weather-brown face, with a grey beard, which was real grey, an old man's grey, and blue eyes, which were blinking. And Hob Farrell of Kettlebridge looked down from the great grey horse at the tall man holding his hand, and round at the dark field, and the river, and the Ring of gold on the hill above; and he began to cry like a child.

"Where am I?" he said. "Where's — *Mary*? Where's my *boys*?"

"I don't know," said Aren. "But we'll find them. Get down, Hob. It's all right. Get down."

---

He went down the whole front rank like that.

He knew every one. Wren heard him from the wall, and so did everybody, and she never forgot it. He went from horse to horse in the dark, along the line of the Hollow Knights, and stopped in front of each, and said their name. *Dougal Reid, of Skerrow. Thomas Ancrum, of Low Fell. Jamie Coyle. Ruth Haldane, of the Black Mill, whose daughter was called Ann.* Their names, and where they came from, and when he had taken them. Every one. And laid his hand on theirs.

Some did not turn. Some sat their horses and stared over his head and were grey still when he moved on; and those, after a while, wheeled their horses and rode away into the dark, and were gone. Aren did not stop them. He did not go after them. He only watched them go, with his face very tired, and then turned to the next.

But some did turn. More than half. And by the time he had gone along the whole of the front rank, there were people standing in the field below the walls of Harrowgate who had been Hollow Knights for twenty years, in old dented armour, holding each other's hands, crying, and staring up at the gold Ring on the hill as if it were a sunrise.

---

And then the Queen's voice came.

It came out of the dark beyond the river, and the Hollows, and the last of the Knights — out of the east, out of the night itself — very soft, very reasonable, like warm milk going cold. Everybody on the wall heard it. Wren felt it go through her like the cold had.

*Little sweep*, it said. *How tired you must be.*

The lights along the wall wavered.

*All of you. So tired. Standing out there in the cold, holding up your little lights. And for what? There will always be more of the dark than there are of you. You know that. You have always known it. You cannot hold a whole world with a handful of candles.* The voice was gentle, sorrowful, kind. *Put them down. Go to bed. It is easier in the grey.*

All along the wall of Harrowgate, the lights began, very slightly, to go down.

Wren felt her own arms dropping. She felt the cold coming in again, under her coat, and the tiredness — the voice was right; she *was* tired; she had been tired since Soot Lane, since the Scarecrow Farms, since Midsummer — and she thought, *it doesn't matter. There's hundreds of them now. They don't need me. They don't need the lamp. I'm only one*—

And then she knew.

She did not know how she knew. It came to her all at once, standing on the wall in the dark with the Queen's voice in her ears and Nettle's two small candles shaking beside her: the answer to the question she had asked on Midsummer morning, sitting on the workshop step. *Then what am I for?*

She was not the only light. She never would be again. That was the gift; that was the whole point; that was what it was *for*.

But she had the first one.

She had the lamp the Lampwright had carried down the Mountain. The one that had waited fifty years in a flue for a sweep. The one every other light in Gloamhaven had been lit from, one way or another — the Beacon, and the Ring, and the waystones, and Posy, and Will Hesketh in the field, and Nettle, and Bracken, and Snib, and the hundred lamps of Cinder Hill — every one of them, if you went back far enough, had come from *this*. It was not the only light. It was the one that lit the lighters.

And the lighters were tired.

Wren climbed up onto the parapet of the wall of Harrowgate, where everybody could see her, and held the lamp up over her head in both hands.

She did not hold it out at the Hollows. She did not hold it out at the dark. She held it up, high, for the others. For every light on the wall. The way you hold up a lamp in a window, at night, for somebody coming home.

And every flame on the wall of Harrowgate leaned towards it.

All of them. All the way round the Ring, every lamp and candle and lantern and warm bare hand — Tobin's pole, and Mira's fingers, and Sister Hale's crutch, and Nettle's two candles, and Bracken's lantern, and Snib's long finger, and Aren's glowing palm, down in the field — every single flame leaned towards the little battered brass lamp held up on the south wall by a sweep, the way flowers lean towards the sun. And they burned up. They burned up bright, brighter than they had all night, gold and white and roaring, all round the wall at once, so that the whole hill of Harrowgate was crowned with fire; and the light went out over the valley like the sun coming up.

The Queen's voice stopped.

The Hollows broke.

They did not drift away, this time. They fled — shrieking, all of them, the whole grey tide of them, in their thousands, down the slope and across the river and away up onto the moor into the dark, like smoke blown off a field by a great wind; and the last of the grey Knights went with them, galloping. And in the middle of it all, on the little rise by the river, the Lord Steward's carriage, with its two grey horses screaming and plunging in the shafts, went over sideways into the ditch with a tremendous crash of gilded wood.

The door burst open. Prowle came out of it on his hands and knees, in his plum velvet, with his wig over one eye; and he looked up at the hill of Harrowgate, crowned with fire; and he got up and ran. He ran off into the dark after the Hollows, east, towards the sea, as fast as his fat legs would carry him, with his coat-tails flying.

He left his gold chain behind him in the ditch. Snib found it in the morning, and wore it for a week.

---

Wren did not remember getting down off the parapet.

She remembered Nettle's arms round her legs, and Tobin hugging her so hard that his spectacles came off, and Mira laughing. She remembered Sister Hale's voice, somewhere along the wall, singing. She remembered Cinder on her shoulder, with her spark as bright as a coal in a forge.

And she remembered the dawn.

It came up over the moors to the east, slowly, grey as it always did, the pale cursed sun like a coin seen through dirty water. But the whole of the hill of Harrowgate was still burning gold, every light on the wall, and the true Flame from all of them went out over the valley in the grey morning; and the heather on the hills all round, which everybody in the Highlands had always known was grey, as grey as old wool, as grey as everything else—

Was purple.

It was purple from one side of the valley to the other. A deep, dark, glowing, astonishing purple, as far as you could see; a colour so rich that the Highlanders on the wall had no name for it at all, because nobody in the Harrowgate Highlands had seen it for three hundred years.

They stood on the wall of Harrowgate in the dawn, hundreds of them, with their lights in their hands, and looked at it, and wept.

## Toward the Sunless Sea

They called it *heather-colour*, in the end.

There was a great deal of argument about it first. The Highlanders of Harrowgate argued about it for three days, in the Black Bull, and on the steps, and over the walls of their fields — because a colour has to be called something, and they were Highlanders, and they took naming seriously. Some wanted to call it *purple*, which Mira said was its proper name, from the Chronicle. Some said that was a foreign word and they would not have it. The old man with the walnut face said it should be called *Harrowgate*, after the town; and Tobin went red, and said that was very kind, but it might be confusing. Granny Kettle, who walked down off the moor on the second day to see what all the noise had been, said it was *blaeberry* and anybody who said different was a fool. And the children of the town called it *the Queen-runs-away colour*, and would not be talked out of it.

So in the end it was *heather-colour*, and everybody was a little cross, and everybody was quite happy.

---

Those were good days, after the siege. I want to tell you about them, because there are not many like them in this story, and you should have them.

The Knights who had come back stayed in Harrowgate. There were a hundred and twelve of them. They took off their old dented armour and piled it up in the square by the meeting-house, and it made a heap as high as a cottage, and the children climbed on it. Then they had to be found clothes, and fed, and told what year it was, which for some of them was very hard. Some had been Knights for twenty years. Some had wives and children who had grown up and gone away, or grown old, or died. Some did not remember their own names, at first, until Aren told them.

He went among them all day, every day. He did not say much. He sat with them, and told them their names, and where they came from, and listened. And one by one, as the Flame took hold in them and they began to remember, they went off — east and north and south, on foot, in twos and threes, over the heather-coloured moors, going home. Hob Farrell went first, to Kettlebridge, to find Mary. He came back two days later, alone, with his face wet; and then the next day a woman came down the road from Kettlebridge after him, grey-haired and running, with two grown men behind her. And Hob Farrell stood in the square of Harrowgate and held on to her and would not let go.

The Low Coombe folk went home to build their barn again. Will Hesketh went with them, to help, because he said he had built a barn once and it was not as hard as people thought.

Isla Moffat wove a length of cloth in heather-colour, the first that had ever been woven in Harrowgate since the curse, and gave it to Wren. Wren did not know what to do with it, so she wrapped it round Cinder, and Cinder slept in it for a week.

And Sister Hale sat on a bench outside the Black Bull in the sun — pale, but a little warmer, I think — with her crutch across her knees and her bad arm, at last, out of its sling, and sang, and was brought cups of tea by people who had never met her and wanted to, and was perfectly, entirely happy.

---

On the third day Tobin went up the hill to the ruin.

He went alone. He did not tell anybody; he slipped away after breakfast, with Ash's pole, and climbed the steep path from the top of the town to the old Harrowgate House, and went in through the gap where the front door had been.

It was only walls. Four great grey walls with empty windows, and the stumps of chimneys, and ivy, and rooms with no floors and the grass growing in them, and the sky for a ceiling. Rooks had built in the top of one chimney. A rowan tree was growing out of what had been a fireplace. It was very quiet.

He walked through it slowly, from room to room, and tried to imagine it with floors and ceilings and fires, and people in it. His great-grandfather. His great-great-grandfather. Harrowgates. Thin, all elbows, ink-stained, probably. Over-explaining. He found that he could imagine it quite easily, and that it made him want to laugh.

And then, on his way out, he looked up.

Over the gap where the front door had been, set into the stone of the lintel, there was a niche. A little square hollow, cut into the wall, at about the height of a tall man's head. Tobin had walked straight under it coming in and not seen it. It was the right size for a lamp. It was exactly the shape of the empty niches in the old waystones on the Greywaste, that Ash had relit one by one; and of the niche in the sealed flue of Thornbury House, where Wren had found the lamp.

And above the niche, carved into the stone, there was a flame. A small one. Worn smooth by three hundred years of Highland rain, so that you could hardly see it.

And below the niche, in old square letters, half covered in ivy, there were words.

Tobin pulled the ivy away with his hands, and read them, and then sat down on the step of his great-grandfather's house and did not get up for a long time.

*HARROWGATE*, said the letters. *LAMPLIGHTERS. GIVEN FREELY.*

---

"They were Lamplighters," he said to Wren that evening.

They were sitting on the town wall, by the West Gate, where Aren had lit the first lamp, with their legs hanging over the edge and the heather-coloured moors going away in front of them in the dusk. Cinder was between them. Mira was further along, with the Chronicle open on her knees, reading to a crowd of Highland children who could not believe there was a whole book of stories and had come every night since the siege to hear them.

"My family," said Tobin. "The Harrowgates. Before Emberwick, before we were Wardens — we were Lamplighters. Here. It's carved over the door." He pushed his spectacles up. "And my father never told me. I don't know if he knew. I don't think he knew. I think his father didn't tell him, and his father didn't tell him, all the way back, because somewhere along the line somebody decided it was better to be a Warden than a Lamplighter, and stopped saying." He was quiet for a moment. "My mother would have known. I think. She loved the old songs. My father forbade them, after she died. I always thought it was because they made him sad." He looked down at his scarred palms. "Maybe it was because they made him remember."

"You'll be able to ask him," said Wren.

"Yes," said Tobin. "I will, won't I."

---

They had the council the next night, in the Moffats' weaving-room, with the looms pushed back against the wall: Sister Hale on the loom-bench, and Bracken on a stool, and the old man with the walnut face, and Isla and Ewan Moffat, and Tobin and Mira and Wren. And Aren.

He told them about Nightfall Spire.

He told it the way he told everything now: plainly, not making it sound better or worse than it was. An island in the Sunless Sea, four days east of the Highland coast, where the sun never rose — the only place in all Gloamhaven where it did not even come up pale. A city on the island, grey and empty and always night. And in the middle of the city, the Spire: a tower of black glass, going up and up into a dark that had no top. He had lived in it for thirty years. He knew its stairs and its galleries and its halls.

"She keeps them there," he said. "The ones she takes. Thousands. In great halls, one above the other, all the way up. The Hall of Sleepers, she calls it. They don't die. They stand, and they forget, and they wait. She likes to walk among them." He looked at his hands. "Lord Edric is in a cell near the top. Clear-headed. I took him there in the winter. She keeps him clear on purpose. She likes him to know where he is, and why." He did not look at Tobin. "And at the very top, the old king. Ardric. On his throne. With the crown."

"The crown with the sun in it," said Mira softly. "From the Chronicle."

"Yes."

"And it's still *there*? After three hundred years? The sun's fire? In a crown?"

"Yes," said Aren. "I've seen it. I wish I hadn't."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"She's afraid," said Aren. "That's the other thing. You need to know that. Since Midsummer — since the Hill, but since Midsummer most of all — she's afraid. She's calling everything home. Every Hollow in Gloamhaven, every Knight, every ash-crow. You saw them run, the other night. They didn't run back to the moor. They ran east. To the sea. To her." He looked up. "She's gathering everything she has into the Spire. And when she's afraid, she's worse. Not louder. Worse."

---

"Then we must wait," said Sister Hale.

Everybody looked at her.

"Wait, and pray, and gather," said Sister Hale. She was sitting very upright on the loom-bench, with her crutch across her knees and her one bright eye going from face to face round the room. "That's what I'd say, ducks, if anybody asked me, and nobody has. The Flame's going out all over the land — to the Rings, and past them. Every day there's more of us. Give it a year. Give it two. Let it grow. Let her sit in her Spire and be afraid. And then, when we're enough—"

"I've waited my whole life," said Wren.

She did not say it loudly. She was sitting on the floor by the loom with Cinder in her lap, and she had not said anything all evening. But everybody heard it.

"My whole life," said Wren. "I was four. I've waited eight years. I didn't know I was waiting — I didn't know there was anything to wait *for* — but I was. In Mott's yard. In the garret. Every night, with the grate cold. I was waiting." Her voice did not shake. "And now I know where they are. My mam and dad. And they've been standing in a hall in the dark for eight years, forgetting, and *waiting*, too. And I'm not going to make them wait another year. Not one more day. I'm not."

Sister Hale looked at her for a long time across the weaving-room.

She did not argue. That was the thing about Sister Hale; she knew when not to. She sat on the loom-bench with her crutch across her knees, and looked at Wren with her one bright eye and her one milky one, and her old brown face went soft, and sad, and full of something that might have been pride.

"Yes, duck," she said at last, very gently. "I think you have."

---

So it was decided.

There were boats, as it turned out. Ewan Moffat said so. Not in Harrowgate — Harrowgate was a hill-town, twenty miles from the sea — but down at the coast, below the moors, where the land fell away in cliffs to the shore. A village called Skerrow. Fisherfolk. They had boats, old ones, for the inshore water along the coast. They had never sailed east, out past the headland, into the black water. Nobody had, for three hundred years. Nobody even looked at it, if they could help it. They fished with their backs to it.

"But they've had the Flame," said Aren. "Some of them. Since the siege. A few of the Knights who came back were Skerrow men." He looked at Wren. "It's four days' sail. If the boat holds. If there's wind. I don't know if there's ever wind on the Sunless Sea. I never sailed it. She has her own ways across."

"Then we'll find out," said Wren.

"We'll need a skipper," said Tobin. "Somebody who knows boats. I can work out the course — the compass still points to the Mountain, and if I take the angle off that, and the coastline—" He stopped. "But I can't *sail*."

"Then we'll find one," said Wren. "Somebody daft enough."

"*Very* daft," said Mira.

"Well, there's always somebody," said Wren.

---

It was the last evening in Harrowgate.

Wren went up on the moor above the town at dusk, on her own, because she wanted to look at the heather one more time before they went. Cinder came with her. They sat on a rock on the hillside, looking west, where the pale sun was going down over the moors in a smear of grey and gold, and the heather was going from heather-colour to dark.

There was singing, a little way off.

It was Aren. He was sitting on a wall further down the hill, with Tobin beside him, and he was teaching Tobin the old wall-songs: the Lamplighters' songs, the ones that used to be sung on the walls at the turning of the night, before there was a Guild. He had forgotten most of them. The Queen had taken them. But since his palm had begun to shine, bits of them had been coming back to him — a line here, a tune there, the way the colour had come back to Posy's hair from the roots. And Tobin was writing them down in his notebook as fast as Aren remembered them, in his small neat ink-stained hand, and asking questions, and getting the tunes wrong.

Wren listened to them for a while.

Then, without meaning to, she began to hum.

It was her mother's tune. The only one she had. *Morning comes, morning comes, and the dark shall not keep us.* She hummed it quietly, under her breath, the way she always had — in the garret, in the Deeps, on the Black Stair, by the Gloam River, on the long road east to the Hill — the way she had hummed it once, Aren said, in a coal-hole in the dark, when she was four.

And she got to the end of the bit she knew, the way she always did. *And the dark shall not keep us.*

And she stopped.

But this is the thing I want you to notice, because Wren did not notice it herself, not then, not for a long time. She stopped where she always stopped. But it was not the same stopping.

All her life, when she got to that place in the song, she had stopped because it hurt. Because that was where her mother's voice went quiet, like a lamp going out, and there was nothing after it but the dark and the cold grate and the being nobody's. She had stopped there the way you stop at the edge of a cliff.

Tonight she stopped there because she did not know the rest *yet*.

That was all. She was not at the edge of a cliff. She was at the edge of a page, the way Mira was every night at the end of the Chronicle, turning the blank pages one by one and saying good evening to them. There was more. She did not know what it was. But it was there, waiting, the way the hundred lamps had waited on their shelves, the way the heather had waited under the grey; and she would find it.

She sat on the rock on the moor above Harrowgate with her arms round her knees and the fox against her side, and found that she was smiling.

---

Tobin came up the hill to her when it was nearly dark.

He sat down on the rock beside her, rather breathless, with his notebook stuffed in his pocket and Ash's pole across his knees. He did not say anything for a while. Then he took something out of his coat.

It was his mother's compass.

It was small and brass and battered, the size of a large watch, with a lid that clicked shut. Wren had stolen it out of his pocket on the first night they ever met, on a rooftop in Emberwick, and given it back. Ash had borrowed it, on their last night in Emberwick, and given it back the next morning "tuned", and said that a compass ought to know the way home. It had pointed, ever since, north-and-a-bit-east, to the Bright Mountain, a faint pale tooth on the edge of the world: across the Greywaste, and down in the Deeps, and in Vaelport, and on the long road to the Hill. It had swung round once, on the long road to the Hill, and pointed east to the place where the Lampwright was waiting. It had opened the iron door in his workshop. And then it had gone back to the Mountain, and stayed there.

Tobin clicked the lid open, and they looked at it together in the last of the light.

Inside the lid, in small engraved letters, his mother's words: *Home is where the light is.*

And the needle—

The needle was moving.

It swung away from the north as they watched, slowly, as if it were being turned by a patient hand. Past north-and-a-bit-east, where the Mountain was. Past east-north-east. On round. And it settled, trembling a little, and then quite still, pointing due east: over the heather-coloured moors, and the coast, and the cliffs of Skerrow, and out — out over the black water, towards the sea where the sun had not risen for three hundred years.

Tobin put his finger very lightly on the glass over the needle.

"Wren," he said. "Feel."

She put her finger beside his.

The glass was warm.

Not warm from his hand. Warm from inside, the way the lamp was warm; the way the ground was warm on Cinder Hill. As if the little brass needle, pointing out into the dark towards Nightfall Spire, knew exactly where it was going, and was glad.

They sat on the hill above Harrowgate in the dusk, a sweep and a Warden's son and a fox, with their fingers on the warm glass, and looked east into the dark together; and neither of them was afraid.

"Well," said Wren at last. "Come on, then."

And they got up, and went down the hill to the town, to pack.
