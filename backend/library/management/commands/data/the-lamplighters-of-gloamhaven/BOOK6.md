# MORNING COMES — Book 6 bible & beat sheet
Book 6 of *The Lamplighters of Gloamhaven*, the last. 15 chapters, ~46,000–50,000 words. Ages 9–12.

**Read `OUTLINE.md` first** (series canon), then `BOOK2.md`–`BOOK5.md`. Then read Book 5's last chapter (`the-midsummer-flame.md`, "## Toward the Sunless Sea") so you know exactly where everyone stands. Every seed planted in Books 1–5 is harvested here; the checklist is §8.

Theme (never quoted): Revelation 21–22 (no more night; every tear wiped away; the city needs no lamp, for its light is the one who was slain; "I make all things new"); Romans 8 (the whole creation waiting); 1 Corinthians 15 (death swallowed up); Isaiah 60 ("Arise, shine, for your light has come"); Luke 15 (the father running to the son — here turned round, the son to the father). Core truths: **the Lampwright comes back, and makes all things new**; **the dark shall not keep us** — what the Queen took is given back; **a grasped thing can only be let go of, never taken**; **we do not win the morning; we carry the light until it comes**; **home is where the light is**. The book of the ending: it must be the biggest and most joyful book of the six, and it must still be small and particular at the moments that matter — a sweep, a song, a boy and his father, an old king.

## 1. Where Book 5 left them
- Late summer, the Harrowgate Highlands, after the siege. The Flame rests on everyone who received it at Midsummer and on everyone they have given it to since: hundreds of Flame-bearers across Gloamhaven; Rings relit; farms, mines and boats with lamps in them.
- **Wren** (12, going on 13): the lamp — the Lampwright's own — is still hers, and she knows now what it is for: it is the first light, that lights the lighters. She forgave **Aren** (Ch 13). She knows her parents' names, **Tam and Linnet Ashby**, Lamplighters of the Remnant, taken greyed from Emberwick's North Wall eight years ago to Nightfall Spire. She knows only "Morning comes, morning comes … and the dark shall not keep us" — and now stops there because she doesn't know the rest *yet*.
- **Tobin** (13): Ash's pole; scarred palms; the compass, which in the last lines of Book 5 swung from the Bright Mountain to **due east, toward the Sunless Sea**, and was warm. He knows his father is in the Spire. He found the Harrowgates were Lamplighters once.
- **Mira**: sees and clicks; carries the Chronicle, blank pages at the back; brave and funny.
- **Aren** (once Varrick): Ash's apprentice restored; oath-scar shining gold; quiet, plain, steady; knows the Spire and its ways.
- **Cinder**, **Sister Hale** (crutch mended with brass), **Bracken** (keeper of Emberwick's Beacon), **Nettle**, **Snib**, **the Heskeths**, the Highland folk.
- The Queen has called every Hollow home to **Nightfall Spire**. **Prowle** fled to her. **Lord Edric** is her prisoner. **Ardric**, the king who bargained away the sun, is alive in her tower and cannot die.
- The sun is a little warmer. Not golden. Not yet.

## 2. New cast (FIXED names)
**Skipper Mairi Dunlop** — a Highland fisherwoman of the coast village of **Skerrow**, under the moors; broad, red-handed, weather-beaten, sixty; one of the first the Flame rested on in the Highlands; owns the oldest boat on the coast, the ***Brave Gannet***, and sails her into the black water because "somebody has to be daft first". Swears by her grandmother's boots. Laughs at the sea.
**Tam Ashby** and **Linnet Ashby** — Wren's parents (Book 5 §4). Found greyed in the Hall of Sleepers (Ch 4). **Linnet**: small, sharp-chinned, black-haired — Wren's face, older; a singer; when restored she is shaky and slow for days, then laughs like Wren. **Tam**: big, gentle, slow-spoken, lamp-mender's hands, freckles; he cries easily and is not ashamed of it. They remember Wren as four. She is twelve. Both of them have to learn that, gently, and so does she. (Do not over-sweeten: Wren is shy of them for a while, prickly, does not know what to call them; it comes right slowly, and over something small — Tam mending her boots, Linnet cutting her hair properly.)
**Lord Edric Harrowgate** — Tobin's father, in a cell high in the Spire. The Queen has NOT greyed him: she keeps him clear-headed so that he can despair. Thin, unshaven, silver gone white, still upright; he has had a year alone with what he did (sold the lamp, sold his son's letter, sold the city). He does not ask to be forgiven, because he thinks he cannot be. He is not a villain; he is a proud frightened man come to the end of himself. He calls his son "Tobin" for the first time in the series in Ch 5, and it undoes them both.
**Ardric** — the king of the old bargain (OUTLINE §2; Book 4's Chronicle). Three hundred years old; he has not died and cannot. Tiny, bent, white as paper, weightless, cold; his eyes are the only living things in his face. He sits on a throne at the top of Nightfall Spire wearing **the Crown**: a plain circle of gold in which the sun's own fire burns — the fire he bargained out of the sky. It gives no warmth to him, only a terrible light he cannot shut his eyes against, and it is what keeps him alive. He cannot take it off; his hands have clenched on it for three hundred years. The Queen keeps him as her trophy and her proof: *see what the sun is worth.* He is frightened of dying more than of anything, still. His voice is a whisper like pages turning. He has not been spoken to kindly for three hundred years. (Read Book 4's Chronicle chapter: "He did not want to die.")
**Morvaine, the Umbral Queen** — in person (Ch 6, 10, 12–13). As in Book 4: tall, silver-grey, lovely, soft-voiced, reasonable, "like warm milk going cold"; never lies when a half-truth will do. In this book, for the first time, she is **afraid**, and fear makes her worse: by Ch 12 the loveliness is wearing thin like frost on a window, and the dark shows through. Her end (Ch 13) is not a fight: when the true morning comes she simply cannot be anywhere the light is, and the light is everywhere. She is never seen to die; she is a shadow at noon — "and then there was no noon left for her to be a shadow in."
**Guild Master Prowle** — at the Queen's court, miserable, cold, gold chain gone, wishing he had never come. Comic and pitiable. In the Long Night (Ch 10–11) he is left behind on the black shore; Mairi Dunlop hauls him into the boat by his collar. In Ch 15 he is back in Emberwick handing out free oil, grumbling that it's very bad business. (Grace for the ridiculous: let it be funny and real.)
**Old Ash** — returns (Ch 13–15). See §5.
**The Sleepers** — the greyed of three hundred years in the Spire's great halls: thousands, standing, silent, forgetting. Never horrible; sad, still, like statues in snow.
**The Gathering** (Ch 11–13) — everyone: Sister Hale and the Remnant; the Delvers (**Speaker Gorrim**, **Tavi**, **Mother Hessa**); Vaelport folk (**Pim**, **the Pellows**); the Heskeths and Posy; Bracken; **Nettle** and **Mrs Fenn**; **Mott**; **Snib** and a whole sulky troop of Gutterlings who have decided they want the Flame too; the Highlanders and Granny Kettle. Each gets one line or one gesture, not more.

## 3. Places & things
- **Skerrow** (Ch 1): the fishing village under the moors; tarred huts; nets; a stone harbour; the black water beyond the headland, where no one has sailed for three hundred years.
- **The Sunless Sea** (Ch 1–2): black, flat, oily, no reflection — not of the lamp, not of a star; no stars at all overhead after the first night; silence (no gulls, no waves to speak of); a cold that comes up through the planks. Sea-Hollows rise in the water like smoke under glass. **The Drowned Fleet**: greyed ships drifting with greyed crews, three hundred years of them, going nowhere.
- **Nightfall Spire** (Ch 3–9): an island of black rock with a city on it where it is always night; and in the middle a tower of black glass going up and up into a dark with no top — the Spire. Its inside: cold endless stairs; galleries; **the Hall of Sleepers** (great halls tier on tier where the greyed stand); the cells; **the Queen's table** (a long hall laid with a feast that has no smell and no taste); and at the very top, **the Crown Room**, round, open to the black sky, where Ardric sits on his throne in the light of the Crown. Mira's clicks are the only way to walk the Spire in the dark: it is built, she says, "to make echoes lie".
- **The Crown**: see Ardric. It **cannot be taken**, any more than the lamp can (the same law, turned inside out: a grasped thing can only be let go). The Queen has never been able to take it from him; she needs him to keep it. If he lets it go, the fire will be free — and he will be an old man, at last, who can die. It must go back to where it came from: the **Bright Mountain**, where Ardric bargained it away. Only someone who wants nothing from it can carry it (it burns the grasping, freezes the frightened): Wren, who has learned to give, carries it in her two hands, inside her coat, beside the lamp; it is heavy as a stone and does not burn her. (Never explain why; let the reader see it.)
- **The Long Night** (Ch 10–12): when Ardric lets go of the Crown, the Queen in rage and fear pours out all her dark at once: the Spire falls into the sea, and a night comes down over the whole of Gloamhaven, so thick that the pale sun does not rise. It lasts three days. Every Ring-lamp fails; every glowstone in the land cracks; all over Gloamhaven, only the true Flame holds — in hundreds of hands, little lights in a vast dark, like Tobin's Orrery at night. (The image: from the sea, Tobin sees the coast of Gloamhaven pricked with tiny gold lights, and every light is a person.)
- **The Bright Mountain** (Ch 11–15): far north, beyond the Greywaste and the Deeps; the faint pale tooth on the horizon since Book 1. Snow, a long road up, old Lamplighter waystones all the way with empty niches (Wren's company lights them as they climb — the light goes up the mountain behind them in a line). At the top: a plain flat place of snow and rock, open to the sky, with a ring of old standing stones, and in the middle a fire burning on the bare rock — the **Flame** where it began, which has never gone out.
- **The lullaby, finished** (Ch 14). These words are FIXED — use exactly these, once, sung by Linnet and then everyone:

  *Morning comes, morning comes,*
  *and the dark shall not keep us;*
  *for the one who went under*
  *has woken, has woken,*
  *and down from the mountain*
  *he comes with the fire,*
  *and the grey shall go golden,*
  *and the cold shall go home.*
  *Hush now, my wren, my own, hush now —*
  *morning comes.*

  (Linnet made up "my wren" when Wren was born; the old wall-song has "my dear". Let Wren notice.)

## 4. The lamp in Book 6 (OUTLINE §3 rules hold — keep them exact)
- It is still Wren's, given; it still leans toward the Lampwright, and now toward the Mountain.
- In the Spire it must be hidden (the dark there drinks light, and the Queen would know); Wren keeps it inside her coat, and its warmth is how she knows she's still herself.
- Given to the Sleepers it restores them — first Wren's mother (Ch 4), then (Ch 8) whole halls at once, because everyone the Flame rests on lights the next.
- **Ch 7/9**: Wren does not use it to make Ardric let go. It cannot do that; no one can. She only sits by him and is kind, and lets it warm him, and tells him about the toast.
- **Ch 12**: the Queen's last offer is for the lamp *and* the Crown; Wren refuses.
- **Ch 13**: At the top of the Mountain, Wren gives the Crown to the Lampwright (not to the Flame — to him). He takes the sun's fire out of the circle of gold in his scarred hands, as if he were lifting a coal, and gives it back to the sky. Then he gives the empty gold circle back to Ardric. And the lamp: he does not take it. Wren holds it up at the moment the sun comes up golden, and for the first time it is not the brightest thing anywhere, and she is glad.

## 5. The Lampwright and Old Ash (handle with great restraint, as in Book 4 §5)
- The Lampwright is not glimpsed in Ch 1–12 at all, except: the warm wind once at sea (Ch 2), and Wren hearing the tune hummed under the Long Night (Ch 11), very far off. His absence is felt; they carry the light until he comes. This is the waiting the whole series has been about.
- **Ch 13**: at the top of the Bright Mountain, before dawn, at the end of the Long Night, he is there by the Flame — sitting on a stone, as at the breakfast fire, as if he has been waiting for them; and with him is **Old Ash**. He comes (that is the point: he *comes*), not with fireworks: he stands up, and the dawn comes up behind him, and the Queen's dark goes out of the world the way night goes out of a room when somebody opens the shutters.
- **Old Ash**: as he was, and more: very tall, but not stooped; beard white, no longer singed; standing on two good legs with no poles at all ("Hm. I don't seem to need them."). Still "Hm." Still "child", "lad". He has been waiting, as the Lampwright said. He does not explain where; nobody asks. The tea joke (Ch 14): Ash makes tea on the Mountain, and it is still dreadful, and the Lampwright drinks a whole cup and says it is very good, and Ash says "Hm" and looks pleased, and Wren laughs till she cries.
- Ash and Aren (Ch 13): Ash says "Lad." Aren kneels. Ash pulls him up by the scarred hand. No speeches.
- **Ardric** (Ch 13): he died on the way north, in the Long Night, a very old man in a boat, asleep, with Wren's hand in his, unafraid at last (Ch 11 — gently, briefly, the way Book 3 wrote Ash's death: clear that it happened, not lingered on). At morning on the Mountain, among those who come down the far slope with Ash, is a young man in a plain coat, with ordinary brown hair, who stops in front of Wren and bows to her as if she were a queen. She does not know him. Then she does. Nobody says his name. The Lampwright gives him back the empty gold circle; he hangs it on a peg — no: he gives it to the Flame-keepers as a lamp-ring (decide in the writing; keep it small).
- He never says "I am…". He never explains. He calls them by name.

## 6. Voices (Book 6)
- **Wren**: Soot Lane, quick and cheeky, and older; brave in a quiet way now; with her parents shy and prickly; with Ardric astonishingly gentle (she talks to him the way she talked to Nettle); the narrator's last word is hers.
- **Tobin**: precise, warm, funny; the engineer of the escape (Ch 8: he rigs the Spire's own lift-chains and counterweights); with his father, the boy who was cut off at supper in Book 1 at last gets to finish a sentence.
- **Mira**: eyes and ears; leads through the Spire; carries the Chronicle, and at the end (Ch 15) she writes the first new words on its blank pages.
- **Aren**: few words; walks into the Spire he served for thirty years; faces the Queen, who calls him Varrick, and says "No. Aren."
- **Sister Hale**: comes on the boat because she has "waited seventy years and isn't waiting any more, duck"; seasick; sings anyway.
- **Mairi Dunlop**: loud, salty, fearless, funny.
- **The Queen**: soft, kind, reasonable — and, at the end, frightened, and then nothing.
- **Ardric**: whisper; frightened; then, once, a child's question: "Would he have me? After what I did?"
- **The narrator**: warm, present; near the end allowed one or two longer direct passages, as at the close of a long tale told by the fire. The very last lines are the narrator's, gentle and open: the story goes on, but this is the end of the part that can be told.

## 7. Chapter beat sheet (~3,000–3,400 words each)

### 1. The Black Water
- Skerrow at dawn: the *Brave Gannet*, Mairi Dunlop, nets, gulls (the last gulls they'll hear). The company: Wren, Tobin, Mira, Cinder, Aren, Sister Hale (who insisted), Mairi and two of her grandsons. Farewells: Bracken, Nettle (who wants to come and is told no, and stows away in a lobster pot, and is found, and has to be put ashore — comedy and tears).
- Out past the headland into the black water: the reflection of the lamp vanishes from the sea; the gulls stop; the cold. Sister Hale seasick and singing. Aren at the bow, quiet. The compass warm, pointing east.
- End: first night; Tobin looks up and there are no stars.

### 2. The Drowned Fleet
- Days in the black. The singing-stone hums. Sea-Hollows rising like smoke under glass. The Drowned Fleet: greyed ships, greyed crews; they give the Flame to one ship's boy on a drifting hulk — and he wakes and weeps; they can't save all; they promise to come back.
- Storm of cold (no wind, a pressing dark); the lamp nearly smothered; a moment when the warm wind comes over the black sea from behind them, out of the west, smelling of bread and summer, and fills the sail (the only touch of the Lampwright in the first half).
- End: rocks ahead in the dark; the *Gannet* strikes; the island of Nightfall Spire.

### 3. Nightfall Spire
- Wrecked on the black shore. The city that is always night: empty grey streets, no fire anywhere. Aren leads: he knows the ways. Cinder silent. Mira clicks — "it's built to make echoes lie". Up into the Spire by a servants' stair. The cold.
- Prowle glimpsed at a window, wretched, in a grey blanket, eating something with no taste.
- End: the doors of the first Hall of Sleepers.

### 4. The Hall of Sleepers
- Thousands of greyed standing in the halls, tier on tier. Wren goes along the rows looking at faces. Despair: how can you find two people among thousands? Mira listens.
- Wren hums. Very softly, the tune. And somewhere in the hall a woman's grey lips move. Wren follows the sound: a small, sharp-chinned grey woman by a window; beside her a big grey man with freckles under the grey.
- Wren gives her the lamp — not lends: gives it into her hands. Colour; Linnet's eyes; "Wren?" — she remembers a four-year-old. Tam wakes too. Very quiet, very short, very big. Wren does not know what to call them. Her mother sings "Morning comes, morning comes—" and stops, as Wren always has, because she is still half grey and the rest is lost in her too.
- End: a knight's step; they must hide the restored among the Sleepers and go on.

### 5. Edric
- Tobin and Aren climb to the cells. Edric, clear-eyed, wrecked (§2). He won't come: "Leave me. I sold you." Tobin tells him about breakfast on the Hill — the toast, "Do you love me?", being given his work back. "He gave me my work back. I sold Ash for a letter, Father, and he gave me my work back." Edric weeps. Tobin gives him the Flame. "Tobin," says his father. (First time.) Tobin, at last, finishes a sentence to his father without being cut off.
- End: the Queen's voice in the corridor, kindly. They are all, every one of them, already found. She has known since the shore.

### 6. The Queen's Table
- The feast with no taste. The Queen, gracious, afraid underneath. She knows the Midsummer gift has gone everywhere; she knows she's losing. Her offer: she will open every hall and let every Sleeper go home, and leave the land for ever, if Wren puts out the lamp. One breath. She shows Wren her parents, safe, if she will. Wren hesitates (honestly).
- Aren: the Queen calls him Varrick; he says "No. Aren." She smiles: "Then you will not mind seeing what you served." She takes Wren up to the Crown Room to show her the king.
- End: Ardric on his throne in the light of the Crown.

### 7. The King Who Could Not Die
- Ardric (§2). The Queen tells his story herself, gently, as a cautionary tale: *see what the sun was worth.* She leaves Wren with him, sure that the sight will make her give up.
- Wren alone with the king. She is frightened of him and then not: he is just very old and very frightened, like old Granny Kettle's sheep in a storm. She talks to him like a sweep. She asks why he doesn't take it off. He can't; his hands won't open; and if he does, he will die. She tells him about Crookback Market, the loaf, "No charge. It's given", and the toast on the Hill. He has never heard of the Lampwright coming back. Mira's Chronicle: what the Lampwright carried. The king listens, three hundred years cold. "Would he have me? After what I did?" Wren: "He had Tobin. He had me. He had *Varrick*."
- End: the guards take her away; Ardric's eyes follow her.

### 8. What Tobin Built
- The escape. Tobin has spent a night in a cell studying the Spire's great lift — chains, counterweights, a shaft through the whole tower, built by hollowed men — and works out how to drop the counterweight and break every lock on its chain at once. Mira guides by echoes; Aren knows the guard-rounds; Sister Hale sings to cover the noise (loudly); Cinder bites a guard.
- The halls open. They give the Flame to the first Sleeper, who gives it to the next, and the next: colour runs through the Hall of Sleepers like a fire through dry grass, tier on tier, thousands waking, crying out names. And at the shore — lights on the black sea: the Highland boats, and Vaelport boats, and every fishing-boat the Flame-bearers could find, with lamps on every bow, come after them: "Daft first, then everyone," says Mairi.
- End: Wren, in the uproar, slips away and climbs back up to the Crown Room.

### 9. Ardric's Choice
- Wren and the king. She doesn't ask him for the Crown. She sits with him and holds the lamp near his hands, so they're warm for the first time in three hundred years.
- The Queen comes — and for the first time she is not lovely: frightened, and the frost is thin. She tells Ardric that if he lets go he dies, that he will go into the dark for ever, that the Lampwright will not want him. The old lie.
- Ardric, whispering, to Wren: "You hold it. I can't — you hold it." He opens his hands. It takes everything he has; the fingers have not moved in three hundred years. He gives the Crown to Wren — gives, not hands over. The Crown does not burn her. It is heavy as a stone, and the fire in it lies down, quiet, like a dog that has found its owner.
- Ardric, all at once, is only an old man, light as a dry leaf. He laughs — a tiny dry astonished laugh.
- The Queen screams (the second time she has ever raised her voice) and the dark comes out of her, all of it.
- End: the Spire cracking from top to bottom.

### 10. The Long Night
- Flight down the falling Spire, Ardric carried in Tam Ashby's arms like a child. The boats; the black sea; the Spire going down into the water behind them; Prowle hauled in by the collar (§2). The Queen's dark pouring out over the sea and the land: the Long Night (§3).
- The sun does not rise. Total dark, for the first time in the series truly total — except the lamps. From the sea Tobin sees the coast of Gloamhaven: tiny gold lights everywhere, in every village and farm and Ring-town, each one a person holding on. "It's the Orrery," he says. "It's the whole country. It's every one of them." The Ring-lamps are failing, every glowstone is cracking — but the true Flame does not go out.
- Mairi: "Where to?" Wren, with the Crown in her coat: "Home. The Crown's going home." The compass swings north — to the Mountain.
- End: Ardric, in the stern, asleep with his head on Linnet's shawl.

### 11. The Road North
- Landfall in the dark; the long road north on foot through the Long Night, across the Greywaste, toward the Mountain. They are not alone: along every road come lights, Flame-bearers walking north. The Delvers come up out of the Black Stair. They walk together.
- Ardric dies (§5): very gently, by a waystone in the Greywaste at the end of the first night's march, with Wren's hand in his; he is not afraid; his last words are a question, half asleep: "Will there be toast?" Wren: "Yes. He burns the first bit." Ardric smiles. Clear that it happened; not lingered on. They bury him under the waystone, and Wren lights its niche.
- Then: very far off, in the dark, from the direction of the Mountain, someone humming — her mother's tune, past the end. Only Wren hears.
- End: the foot of the Bright Mountain; the waystones going up into the dark.

### 12. The Last Offer
- The climb in the dark and snow; they light every waystone, and a line of gold goes up the mountain behind them, and behind that the whole Gathering climbing — Remnant, Delvers, Highlanders, Gutterlings — each one carrying a flame.
- Near the top, the Queen: alone, no knights, no court, the loveliness half gone. She has nothing left but the offer. To Wren: "Give it to me and I'll go away for ever. You'll never see me again. Isn't that what you want?" Then: the lamp and the Crown together — "and I'll give you the rest of the song." Wren: "I don't want it from you. I'll have it given." Then the dark comes at them for the last time: cold, black, pressing; Tobin holds the path with Ash's pole and does not shake; Mira leads by echo when no light reaches; Aren stands between Wren and the Queen with nothing in his hands; Cinder goes ahead, her spark a single point. Wren climbs the last stretch with the Crown and the lamp inside her coat and her eyes shut, humming.
- End: she comes up onto the flat top of the Mountain. There is a fire burning on the bare rock. Someone is sitting beside it.

### 13. Morning Comes
- The top of the Bright Mountain, the last hour of the Long Night (§3, §5). The Lampwright by the Flame. Old Ash beside him, standing on two good legs. Wren cannot speak. Ash: "Hm. You took your time, child." She runs.
- The Gathering coming up behind. Ash and Aren (§5). Ash and Tobin: Tobin gives him back his pole; Ash looks at it, and gives it back again: "Keep it, lad. I don't seem to need them."
- Wren gives the Crown to the Lampwright. He lifts the fire out of it (§4) and gives it back to the sky. And the sun comes up.
- The true morning: golden, for the first time in three hundred years. Colour pouring down the Mountain and over the whole land like water out of a broken dam. The Queen — a shadow at noon (§2). And then there was no noon left for her to be a shadow in.
- End: the young man in the plain coat who bows to Wren (§5).

### 14. The Colours
- The first morning. Everyone names colours — the Highlanders their heather, the Delvers everything, Mira "gold, real gold", Posy "the green one". The Lampwright makes breakfast for a whole mountainful of people from one basket; burns the first piece of toast.
- Ash's tea (§5).
- Linnet, restored fully now in the sun, sings the old song, and gets to the place where it always stopped, and goes on; and the Lampwright sings it with her; and then everyone. The finished words (§3), exactly. Wren hears "my wren" and understands what she has been carrying all her life: not a scrap of a song but a promise.
- Wren and her parents, properly, at last: something small — her father mends her boots; her mother cuts her hair properly with a knife "the way you always did, I expect"; Wren laughs.
- End: the hundred lamps, and Tobin's question one last time — he doesn't need to ask it.

### 15. Home Is Where the Light Is
- Goodbyes and homecomings, light and quick. Rings are no longer needed: the walls stand open. The Lampwright walks down the Mountain and through the towns, and this time he does not vanish; "he is there yet, I am told, and you will find him if you look." Tobin and Edric go home to Emberwick together; Edric, the Warden again, has the Warden's Hall doors taken off their hinges. Prowle handing out free oil (§2). Mira writes the first new words on the Chronicle's blank pages — what does she write? The reader sees only the first line: *"In the last days of the long grey, a sweep found a lamp in a chimney…"* — the opening of this whole series.
- Tobin opens his mother's compass. It doesn't point anywhere in particular now; it turns gently, as if every way were the right way. He reads the inscription: *Home is where the light is.* And understands.
- Wren on the roof of Thornbury House, where it began, sitting astride a chimney-pot in the golden noon, eating a crust, with the sun on her face — the mirror of Book 1's opening image — and below her in Soot Lane her mother is singing and her father is mending a lamp. Cinder beside her.
- Close: the narrator. Gentle, open, warm: the story goes on, and there's more in it than can be told in a book, but this is the end of the part I can tell you; and the last line is about morning.

## 9. Rules for this book
- All OUTLINE §1 voice rules hold. Never name God, Jesus, the Bible, church, heaven, sin or salvation; never explain the allegory; "the Maker" rarely and plainly.
- No Scripture quoted. Echoes in sense only (no tears, no night, all things new).
- The Lampwright: never "I am…"; no speeches; no smiting. He comes; the morning comes with him.
- Peril real but not gory. One death (Ardric, Ch 11), gentle, chosen, unafraid, and answered at morning (Ch 13). The Queen's end is not violent.
- Every main thread closes. Nothing new is opened except the narrator's last, open line.
