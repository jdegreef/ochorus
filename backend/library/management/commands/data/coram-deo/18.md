## Raising Children in the Faith

> "Keep these words that I am commanding you today close to your heart. Teach them carefully to your children, and speak of them as you sit in your house and as you go on your way, as you lie down and as you rise."
> — Deuteronomy 6:6–7 (OWB)

In many villages the old stories are told in the evening. The fire is lit, the meal is finished, and the children gather round a grandmother or an uncle. They hear where the family came from, who their ancestors were, and what it means to belong to this people. Nobody writes it down. It passes from mouth to ear, from one generation to the next.

In the cities of today that circle around the fire has often disappeared. Parents leave home before dawn and return after dark. Children spend hours with screens, with teachers, with friends, with whoever is minding them. And so a question presses on every Christian parent: who is telling our children the story of God?

In the last chapter we saw that marriage is God's first institution, a covenant lived before His face. Very often marriage bears fruit in children. This chapter is about the great privilege and responsibility of raising them in the faith. It is written for mothers and fathers, but also for grandparents, aunts, uncles, teachers, and every believer who loves a child. Because in God's family, as we shall see, the whole Church shares this work.

### Children: a gift and a heritage

The Bible's view of children is warm and clear. "Children are a gift handed down from the LORD; the fruit of the womb is a reward" (Psalm 127:3). The psalmist goes on: "Children born to the young are like arrows in a fighter's hand" (Psalm 127:4). An arrow is not made to stay in the quiver for ever. It is shaped, straightened and then sent out towards a target. Parents have the children God gives them for a season, to shape them and then to send them out into the world for His purposes.

This means children are not a burden to be endured or a possession to be used. They are not a retirement plan, a source of family pride, or a way to fulfil our own unfinished dreams. They belong first to God. We are stewards of them, entrusted with their care before His face.

Jesus Himself showed how God sees children. When parents brought little ones to Him and the disciples tried to send them away, He was angry. "Allow the children to come to Me. Do not try to stop them, because it is to people like them that the kingdom of God belongs" (Mark 10:14). Charles Spurgeon, the great London preacher, wrote a whole book about that scene. In *Come Ye Children* he gave this warning to the Church:

> "Anything we do to hinder a dear child from coming to Jesus greatly displeases our dear Lord."
> Charles Spurgeon, *Come Ye Children*

A word here to those who long for children and have none. Your worth before God does not depend on the size of your family. Some of the greatest servants of God had no children of their own. As we will see at the end of this chapter, there is a spiritual parenthood open to every believer.

### Parents: the first disciple-makers

Jesus told His Church to make disciples of all nations. For Christian parents, the first nation is the one at their own table.

Deuteronomy 6 is one of the most important passages in the Bible on raising children. Moses was preparing Israel to enter the promised land, and he gave them the great confession of faith: "Hear, Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Then came the great command: "You are to love the LORD your God with your whole heart, with your whole being, and with all your strength" (Deuteronomy 6:5).

Notice the order. Before Moses says a word about teaching children, he speaks to the parents. "Keep these words that I am commanding you today close to your heart" (Deuteronomy 6:6). We cannot pass on what we do not have. A father who never opens his Bible cannot expect his son to love it. A mother who never prays cannot expect her daughter to treasure prayer. The first step in raising children in the faith is for the parents themselves to live *coram Deo*, before the face of God. Children learn far more from what they see than from what they are told.

Only then does Moses say, "Teach them carefully to your children" (Deuteronomy 6:7). God gave this task to parents, not first to priests, schools or Sunday school teachers. Church and school can help, and we thank God for them. But they cannot replace a mother and father. A child spends perhaps one or two hours a week in church. The rest of the week is spent at home and in the world. Long before God spoke to Moses, He had said of Abraham, "I have singled him out so that he will charge his children and his family after him to walk in the way of the LORD" (Genesis 18:19). The home is God's first school.

### Words, example and daily life

How are parents to teach? Deuteronomy gives a picture of ordinary life: "as you sit in your house and as you go on your way, as you lie down and as you rise" (Deuteronomy 6:7). In other words, all the time. Faith is passed on in the kitchen, on the road to school, in the field, at bedtime and at breakfast.

This takes some of the pressure off. You do not need to be a skilled preacher to teach your children about God. You need to bring God into the ordinary moments. When you plant seeds together, talk about the God who makes them grow. When a neighbour is sick, pray for her together, and visit her together. When your child is afraid of the dark, teach him that God watches over him at night. When money is short, let your children see you trust God and give thanks when He provides. When you lose your temper, let them hear you apologise, and see you ask God for forgiveness.

Deuteronomy expects children to ask questions: "Some day your son will ask you, 'What do they mean, these decrees, statutes and rulings that the LORD our God has commanded you?'" (Deuteronomy 6:20). And the answer Moses gives is not a list of rules but a story: we were slaves in Egypt, and the LORD brought us out with a mighty hand. When our children ask why we go to church, why we pray, why we do not lie, the best answer begins with the story of what God has done, in the Bible and in our own lives.

Psalm 78 shows the chain of generations that God intends. "We will not conceal them from their children; we will pass on to those who come after us the deeds for which the LORD is praised, His strength, and the marvels He has worked" (Psalm 78:4). Why? "So that those born later, children not yet born, would know them, and would grow up and tell them to their own children, so that they would rest their hope on God" (Psalm 78:6–7). Each generation is a link in the chain. Our task is to make sure the chain does not break with us.

What about the famous promise in Proverbs? "Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not leave it" (Proverbs 22:6). This is a proverb, a wise saying about how life usually works, not a guarantee that every child of faithful parents will follow Christ. Children are real people who make their own choices. Some wander for years. If that is your story, do not despair and do not stop praying. The seeds you sowed are still in the ground, and God is still at work. Many prodigals have come home.

### Susanna Wesley: a school in the rectory

Few mothers in church history have had a greater influence than Susanna Wesley. She was born in London in 1669, the youngest of many children of a Puritan pastor. She married Samuel Wesley, a clergyman, and they moved to the poor country parish of Epworth in Lincolnshire. She gave birth to nineteen children, and only about half of them lived beyond infancy. The family was often in debt. Her husband was frequently away, and he was not always easy to live with. In 1709 the rectory burned down, and five-year-old John was rescued from an upstairs window moments before the roof fell in.

In the middle of all this, Susanna made her home a school. She taught her children herself for six hours a day. And she taught them to pray almost from the time they could talk. Years later she described her method in a letter to her son John:

> "Our children were taught as soon as they could speak the Lord's prayer, which they were made to say at rising and at bedtime constantly, to which, as they grew bigger, were added a short prayer for their parents, and some collects, a short catechism, and some portion of Scripture as their memories could bear."
> Susanna Wesley, quoted in Eliza Clarke, *Susanna Wesley*

"At rising and at bedtime": she was living out Deuteronomy 6:7. But the most striking part of her method was the personal time she gave each child. With so many children, it would have been easy to treat them as a crowd. Instead, during a winter when her husband was away in London, she resolved to do more for the souls around her. "I resolved to begin with my own children," she wrote, and she described what she did:

> "I take such a proportion of time as I can best spare every night to discourse with each child by itself"
> Susanna Wesley, quoted in Eliza Clarke, *Susanna Wesley*

Each evening of the week belonged to a different child. Thursday evening was for John. Those quiet talks shaped a boy who would one day ride across England preaching the gospel, while his brother Charles wrote thousands of hymns that are still sung around the world. The revival that followed reached far beyond Britain.

We should be honest about Susanna. She lived three hundred years ago, and some of her methods were very strict, even harsh, by the standards of Scripture's own warnings to parents, which we will look at next. She wrote of "conquering" a child's will. We need not copy everything she did. But we can learn from what mattered most: her own life of prayer, her steady teaching of Scripture, her consistency, and her determination to know each child's heart. You do not need nineteen children or six hours a day. You need a little time, given regularly, with each child, before the face of God.

### Discipline and instruction with love

Children need boundaries. Scripture is clear that loving parents correct their children, just as God corrects His. "For the LORD corrects the one He loves, as a father corrects the son who is his joy" (Proverbs 3:12). A parent who never says no is not being kind. He is leaving his child unprotected.

But the Bible also gives a strong warning to parents, and especially to fathers. "Fathers, do not drive your children to anger. Instead, bring them up with the Lord's training and correction" (Ephesians 6:4). And again: "Fathers, do not keep provoking your children, or they will lose heart" (Colossians 3:21). In Paul's day fathers had almost unlimited power over their children. Paul limits that power. A Christian father must not crush his children.

How do parents provoke children to anger? By correcting in a rage. By punishing harshly or unfairly. By mocking, shaming or comparing them with their brothers and sisters. By making promises and breaking them. By never listening. By demanding a holiness from them that the parents do not show themselves. A child treated this way may obey for a time, but inside he loses heart, and often loses interest in his parents' God as well.

So discipline must be given with love. The word "discipline" is close to the word "disciple". The goal is not punishment but training: to help a child grow in wisdom and self-control. Explain why a rule matters. Correct calmly, and never in anger. Keep your word. Praise what is good, often and specifically. And after any correction, hold your child close, so that he knows your love has not changed. "At the time it is given, no discipline seems a matter for joy... But afterward it pays back those who have been trained by it with a harvest of peace" (Hebrews 12:11).

Children too have their calling: "Children, obey your parents in the Lord, because this is right" (Ephesians 6:1). Parents can help their children obey by making obedience a joy and not a fear.

### Worship, prayer and Scripture in the home

One of the simplest ways to pass on the faith is to worship God together as a family. Joshua stood before the whole nation and declared, "My household and I will serve the LORD" (Joshua 24:15). That decision is made again in every home that gathers to pray.

Family worship does not need to be long or formal. Ten minutes after the evening meal is enough. Sing a song, perhaps one the children can choose. Read a short passage of Scripture, or a Bible story for little ones. Ask one question about it. Then pray, letting each person who wishes say a short prayer. With small children, keep it short and lively. With teenagers, let them lead sometimes, and welcome their hard questions. There will be evenings when the baby cries, the teenager sulks and nothing seems to happen. Keep going. The fruit of family worship is not seen in a single evening but over many years.

Hudson Taylor, who later took the gospel deep into inland China, grew up in such a home in Yorkshire. As a teenager he drifted from the faith of his parents. His mother and sister did not stop praying. One afternoon, while his mother was away from home, she felt a strong burden to pray for her son's conversion, and she prayed until she was sure God had heard her. At that very time, the boy picked up a gospel tract in his father's library and came to faith in Christ. He wrote in *A Retrospect*:

> "Thus while my dear mother was praising God on her knees in her chamber, I was praising Him in the old warehouse to which I had gone alone to read at my leisure this little book."
> Hudson Taylor, *A Retrospect*

Never underestimate the prayers of a parent. They may be the most important thing you do for your children.

### Lois, Eunice and the whole Church

When Paul wrote to his young friend Timothy, he remembered where Timothy's faith began: "I recall your sincere faith, which first lived in your grandmother Lois and in your mother Eunice, and which I am sure now lives in you" (2 Timothy 1:5). Timothy's father was a Greek and, it seems, not a believer (Acts 16:1). The faith came through a grandmother and a mother. Paul goes on: "from infancy you have known the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" (2 Timothy 3:15).

This is an encouragement to every mother raising children with an unbelieving husband, and to every grandmother who prays for her grandchildren. Your faithfulness is not wasted. A Lois and a Eunice prepared the way for one of the great leaders of the early Church.

Paul himself had no children, yet he called Timothy his son in the faith. He could write to a church, "For it was I who fathered you in Christ Jesus, through the gospel" (1 Corinthians 4:15). In God's family, everyone can be a spiritual parent or grandparent. Paul tells Titus to teach the older women so that "they can train the younger women to love their husbands and children" (Titus 2:4). The old psalmist prayed that God would not abandon him "until I have told the generation after me what Your arm has done" (Psalm 71:18).

So the whole Church helps raise the next generation. The Sunday school teacher who prepares carefully each week. The youth leader who answers messages late at night. The single brother who becomes an uncle to a fatherless boy. The widow who knows every child in the congregation by name and prays for them. Charles Spurgeon often spoke of how much he owed to his mother, who prayed with her children and pleaded with them to come to Christ. But he was also shaped by his grandfather, a pastor, in whose home he spent much of his early childhood. It truly takes a family, and in Christ that family is the Church.

Do you know the children in your church by name? Do you pray for them? Could you be a Lois or a Eunice to a child whose own home is not a place of faith? This is how the chain of Psalm 78 holds firm: not only through parents, but through a whole people who live before God's face and will not let the next generation forget Him.

In the next chapter we leave the home and step into the world of work, where most of us spend the greater part of our waking hours, and where we are just as surely before the face of God.

### Questions for reflection

- Read Deuteronomy 6:4–9. Why do you think Moses speaks to the parents' own hearts before he speaks about teaching children?
- What ordinary moments in your family's day could become opportunities to speak about God?
- Susanna Wesley set aside time for each child individually. What would that look like in your home or among the children you care for?
- In what ways can parents "drive their children to anger"? Which of these do you most need to guard against?
- Who were the Lois and Eunice of your own life, the people who first showed you faith in God?
- Which child, outside your own family, could you begin to pray for and encourage?

### A step for this week

Begin a simple family worship time on at least three evenings this week. Choose a fixed time, perhaps straight after the evening meal, and keep it to ten minutes: one song, one short Bible passage (a story from the Gospel of Mark works well), one question, and a time of prayer in which each person who wishes can pray. If you do not have children at home, choose one child or young person in your church or neighbourhood, pray for them by name every day this week, and find one way to encourage them, such as a kind word after church or a short message.
