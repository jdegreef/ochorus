## Holiness and Victory over Sin

> "Be holy yourselves in all your conduct, just as the One who called you is holy, because it is written, 'You are to be holy, because I am holy.'"
> — 1 Peter 1:15–16 (OWB)

In Chapter 12 we learned to trust God: to take Him at His word when circumstances say otherwise. Now faith meets one of its hardest tests. Can God really change us? Not just forgive us, but change us, so that the sins we confessed last year are not still ruling us this year?

Many Christians quietly lose hope here. They believe God has forgiven them, but they have made a private peace with a temper that still flares or a habit that still wins in the dark. Holiness, they think, is for monks and missionaries, not for a trader with a family to feed or a nurse working night shifts.

The Bible will not let us settle there. God's call is plain: "Be holy yourselves in all your conduct, just as the One who called you is holy" (1 Peter 1:15). And the God who calls us to holiness is the God who makes it possible. This chapter is about how He does it.

### God's call to be holy as He is holy

When Peter wrote those words, he was quoting the book of Leviticus. There God had told Israel, "You must be holy, because I am holy" (Leviticus 11:44). The word "holy" means set apart. A holy thing belongs to God and is kept for Him. The bowls in the tabernacle were holy, not because they were made of a special metal, but because they were set apart for God's use and for nothing else.

So holiness begins with belonging: it is first a matter of whose we are. Paul could write to the church in Corinth, a church full of problems, and still say, "You have been washed clean; you have been made holy; you have been put right with God, in the name of the Lord Jesus and through the Spirit of our God" (1 Corinthians 6:11).

But belonging must show itself in living. A bride who has made her vows belongs to her husband on the wedding day. Over the years that follow, her love, her choices and her habits will show that she belongs to him. In the same way, God says to His people: you are Mine, now live like it. Peter says "in all your conduct", which means in every part of life. Holiness is not only for Sunday. It reaches the market stall, the office computer, the conversation at the well and the thoughts we think lying awake at night.

This is where the theme of this whole book meets us again. To live coram Deo, before the face of God, is to live a holy life. We are not holy when people are watching and different when they are not. The writer of Hebrews puts it strongly: "Go after holiness, since apart from it no one will see the Lord" (Hebrews 12:14). The face we long to see is the face of the Holy One, and He is making us fit to see it.

### What Christ did, and what He is doing

To understand holiness, we must hold two great Bible words side by side: justification and sanctification. They sound like long words for theologians, but they are simple and they are precious.

Justification is what God did for us, once for all, when we trusted Christ. He declared us righteous. He took our sin and laid it on His Son, and He took the righteousness of His Son and counted it as ours (see Chapter 2). A new believer who trusted Christ this morning is as fully justified as an old saint who has walked with God for sixty years.

Sanctification is what God is doing in us, day by day, for the rest of our lives. It is the work of making us actually holy, of shaping our desires, habits and character to look like Jesus. It grows, sometimes quickly and sometimes slowly.

The English bishop J. C. Ryle (1816–1900) wrote a famous book called *Holiness*, which is in the Ochorus library. He was worried that Christians in his day were mixing up these two things, and he set out the difference with great clarity. Here is part of what he wrote:

> "Justification is the act of God about us, and is not easily discerned by others. Sanctification is the work of God within us, and cannot be hid in its outward manifestation from the eyes of men."
> J. C. Ryle, *Holiness*

Why does this matter? If we think our acceptance with God depends on how holy we have become, we will live in fear. But if we think that because we are justified, holiness does not matter, we will treat grace as permission to sin.

Ryle kept both truths together. Our standing with God rests entirely on Christ. And the same grace that forgives us also goes to work in us. As Paul wrote, "Keep working out your own salvation... For it is God who is at work in you, producing both the wanting and the doing, for His own good pleasure" (Philippians 2:12–13). We work, because God works in us. Ryle also gave a definition worth memorising:

> "Holiness is the habit of being of one mind with God, according as we find His mind described in Scripture."
> J. C. Ryle, *Holiness*

Notice the word "habit". Holiness is not a feeling we have at a conference. It is a settled way of agreeing with God, of loving what He loves and hating what He hates, built up over years.

### Dead to sin, alive to God

So how does God change us? Romans 6 is the great chapter on this question, and it begins with an objection. If grace is so generous, why not keep sinning and let grace keep covering it? Paul answers: "Certainly not! We are people who died to sin; how could we still be living in it?" (Romans 6:2).

Paul does not first tell us to try harder. He tells us something that has already happened. When we were joined to Christ by faith, we were joined to His death and His resurrection. "Our old self was nailed to the cross with Him, so that the body of sin would be stripped of its power and we would no longer serve sin as slaves" (Romans 6:6). The old "you", the person who belonged to sin and could do nothing else, died with Christ. A new "you" has been raised with Him.

Think of a man who was once a slave on a farm. A law is passed that sets him free. The old master still shouts at him across the fence, and the old fear still rises in his chest, but the master has no more right to command him. He must learn to live as the free man he now is.

That is what Paul means when he says, "Count yourselves as dead to sin, and as living for God in Christ Jesus" (Romans 6:11). The word "count" is a word for accounts and money. It means to reckon something as true because it is true. We do not make ourselves dead to sin by counting. We count because God says it is so. Then Paul draws the practical conclusion: "Hand yourselves over to God, as people who were dead and are now alive" (Romans 6:13). And he gives a promise: "Sin will not be lord over you, for you do not live under the law but under grace" (Romans 6:14).

Paul says the same thing in his own testimony: "I am no longer the one who lives; Christ lives in me. Whatever life I still have in the flesh is a life lived by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and handed Himself over for my sake" (Galatians 2:20). Victory over sin is not mainly a technique. It is a Person living in us.

### Watchman Nee: from struggling to seeing

Few people have helped believers understand Romans 6 as much as a young Chinese preacher named Watchman Nee.

Nee was born in 1903 and grew up in Fuzhou, on the south-east coast of China. In 1920, when he was seventeen, he heard the evangelist Dora Yu preach and gave his life to Christ. He threw himself into studying the Bible and preaching, helped greatly by an older English missionary, Margaret Barber, who lived simply and prayed much. Soon he was planting churches across China.

But Nee had a problem, and he was honest about it. He had been taught that the secret of victory was to "reckon" himself dead to sin, as Romans 6:11 says. So he tried. He tried for years. In *The Normal Christian Life*, a book drawn from talks he gave in the 1930s and now in the Ochorus library, he describes what happened:

> "For years after my conversion I had been taught to reckon. I reckoned from 1920 until 1927. The more I reckoned that I was dead to sin, the more alive I clearly was. I simply could not believe myself dead and I could not produce the death."
> Watchman Nee, *The Normal Christian Life*

Perhaps you know that feeling. Nee describes believers who "begin to reckon furiously: 'I am dead; I am dead; I am dead!' but in the very act of reckoning they lose their temper."

Nee became so troubled that he told the Lord he would stop preaching until he understood. For months he sought God, sometimes fasting. Then one morning, sitting at his desk upstairs, reading the Bible and praying, he cried out, "Lord, open my eyes!" He wrote:

> "And then in a flash I saw it. I saw my oneness with Christ. I saw that I was in Him, and that when He died I died."
> Watchman Nee, *The Normal Christian Life*

What Nee saw was that Romans 6:11 rests on Romans 6:6. Counting ourselves dead does not make it true. We count it because it is already true. "Knowing" comes before "reckoning". His death to sin was not something he had to produce by effort; it was a fact accomplished at the cross, to be received by faith. From that morning his whole Christian life changed. He did not become sinless, but he stopped fighting for a victory Christ had already won and began to live from it.

Nee's later life was marked by suffering. Arrested in 1952 under the Communist government, he spent his last twenty years in prison and died in 1972, still a prisoner.

### The soul under the Spirit's rule

Nee had a second insight that many find helpful. In Chapter 3 we met his teaching that a human being is made of spirit, soul and body: the spirit is where we know God, the soul is our mind, will and emotions, and the body is our physical life. As we said there, many theologians see the human person as two parts rather than three, and both views are held by faithful believers. This book offers Nee's picture as a helpful lens, not as a test of faith.

Through that lens, Nee taught that the problem in many Christians is not that the Spirit of God is absent. He lives in every believer. The problem is that the soul, our strong opinions, our stubborn will, our unruly feelings, stands in the way like a hard shell around a seed. In a later book, *The Release of the Spirit*, Nee wrote about the "breaking of the outer man": God, in His love, uses the circumstances of life to break the hard shell of our self-will, so that the life of the Spirit within can flow out to others.

Jesus used a picture from the farm: "If a grain of wheat does not fall into the ground and die, it stays just a single grain; but if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). A seed in a sack is safe and unchanged. Only when its hard outer case breaks open in the soil does life come out of it.

The Word of God plays a part in this too. "The word of God is alive and at work, and it has a keener edge than any two-edged sword. It reaches right in, cutting through soul and spirit" (Hebrews 4:12). When we read Scripture with open hearts, it shows us what comes from God and what comes from our own pride or fear. In *The Normal Christian Life* Nee tells of breaking a biscuit in half at a supper table and fitting the two halves back together. It looked whole, he said, but it was never quite the same again. In the same way, a believer whose self-will has been broken by God "will yield ever after to the slightest touch from God."

This is not a call to crush our personalities. God wants our mind, will and emotions yielded to Him, so that we think His thoughts, choose His ways and feel what He feels. Chapter 14 will look more closely at how God heals and renews the soul.

### Practical victory

Grace does not make us passive. The same Paul who said "count yourselves dead" also said, "If by the Spirit you keep putting the body's practices to death, you will live" (Romans 8:13). The Puritan pastor John Owen (1616–1683) wrote a whole book on this verse, *The Mortification of Sin*, which is in the Ochorus library. "Mortification" is an old word that means putting to death. Owen did not soften the call:

> "Do you mortify; do you make it your daily work; be always at it whilst you live; cease not a day from this work; be killing sin or it will be killing you."
> John Owen, *The Mortification of Sin in Believers*

Sin never stays small. Either we deal with it, or it slowly deals with us. Here are four ways God has given us to fight.

**Confession.** "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and righteous: He will forgive our sins and cleanse us from every kind of wrongdoing" (1 John 1:9). To confess is to agree with God about what we have done, calling it by its true name. Not "I was a bit short with my wife", but "I was proud and cruel." Sin loves the dark. Confession drags it into the light, where it loses its power. "Whoever hides their sins will not succeed, but whoever admits them and turns away from them will receive mercy" (Proverbs 28:13).

**Repentance.** Confession names the sin; repentance turns from it. The Bible word means a change of mind that leads to a change of direction. If you have been cheating on your taxes, repentance means paying them. If you have been gossiping about a colleague, it means stopping, and perhaps apologising. Repentance is not a mood. It is a turn of the feet.

**Accountability.** God never meant us to fight alone. "Make it your practice to confess your sins to each other and to pray for each other, so that you may be healed" (James 5:16). One or two trusted friends who know your weak places, who will ask you hard questions and pray for you, are worth more than many good intentions. In the East African Revival, believers spoke of "walking in the light" with one another, drawing on 1 John 1:7. They found that sins brought into the open among loving brothers and sisters lost their grip.

**Walking in the Spirit.** "Walk by the Spirit, and you will never carry out what the flesh desires" (Galatians 5:16). Notice the order. Paul does not say, "Stop doing what the flesh desires, and then you will walk by the Spirit." He says walk by the Spirit, and the desires of the flesh will lose their power. This means staying close to God through the day: a quick prayer before a hard meeting, a verse remembered in temptation. This is what Chapter 8 called abiding. Victory comes less from fighting sin directly than from being filled with something better.

### God's training

Sometimes holiness comes through hard things. Hebrews 12 calls these hard things discipline, the training a loving father gives his children. "God does it for what truly benefits us, so that we may come to share in His holiness" (Hebrews 12:10). Then the writer is honest: "At the time it is given, no discipline seems a matter for joy; it seems a matter for sorrow. But afterward it pays back those who have been trained by it with a harvest of peace: righteousness" (Hebrews 12:11).

A farmer prunes his coffee bushes and they look bare for a season, but they will bear more fruit. When God allows a loss or a long wait, He may be cutting away something that would choke our growth (see Chapter 16).

The same chapter of Hebrews gives us the secret of running the race: "Let us strip off every burden, and the sin that wraps itself around us so readily... Let us keep our eyes on Jesus" (Hebrews 12:1–2). Holiness is not mainly staring at our sins. It is fixing our eyes on Jesus. We become like what we look at.

### Grace for when we fall

You will fall. Every believer does. John, who wrote so much about holiness, also wrote this: "I am writing this to you so that you will not sin. Yet if anyone does sin, we have an advocate in the Father's presence, Jesus Christ, who is righteous" (1 John 2:1).

When you fall, the enemy will whisper that God is tired of you. Do not believe him. You are not condemned. "Those who are in Christ Jesus now face no sentence of condemnation. For in Christ Jesus the law of the Spirit who gives life has freed you from the law of sin and death" (Romans 8:1–2). Your standing before God's face did not depend on your performance yesterday, and it does not depend on your failure today. It rests on Christ.

So get up quickly. Confess honestly. Make right what you can. Then go on, without wallowing. A child learning to walk falls many times a day, and a loving father simply picks the child up. Slowly, the falls become fewer.

Remember Nee's lesson. The battle is real, but the decisive victory is already won. You are fighting from victory, not for it. Paul says that God sent His own Son "as an offering for sin, and in the flesh He passed sentence on sin. He did this so that the law's righteous requirement would be fully met in us, who conduct our lives by the Spirit and not by the flesh" (Romans 8:3–4). What the law could not do, God has done.

To live coram Deo is to live as people already loved, already forgiven, already made holy in Christ, who are learning every day to become in practice what we already are in Him. In the next chapter we will see how this work reaches into the deepest places of the soul, including the wounds many of us carry from the past.

### Questions for reflection

- How would you explain the difference between justification and sanctification to a new believer? Why does it matter that we do not confuse them?
- Is there a sin you have quietly made peace with, believing it will never change? What would it look like to bring it into the light?
- Watchman Nee tried for seven years to "reckon" himself dead to sin before he saw that it was already true in Christ. How does knowing this change the way you fight temptation?
- Where might God be using hard circumstances to break your self-will, as Nee described? How are you responding?
- Do you have one or two people you can be fully honest with about your struggles? If not, who might you ask?
- When you fall, what is your first response: hiding, despair, or running back to God? What does 1 John 2:1 say to you?

### A step for this week

Choose one sin that keeps returning in your life, and fight it this week with the four weapons of this chapter. Each morning, read Romans 6:6–14 slowly and thank God that your old self was crucified with Christ. Each evening, confess honestly anything you have done, naming it plainly, and receive the promise of 1 John 1:9. Where you have wronged someone, put it right before the week ends. And before Sunday, tell one trusted, mature believer about this struggle and ask them to pray for you and to ask you about it again in a week's time.
