## Appendix A: Unity in Essentials: Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Beliefs

> "Spare no effort to keep the unity the Spirit gives, with peace as the bond that holds you together. There is one body and one Spirit, just as there is one hope that belongs to the call you received. There is one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, who is over all and through all and in all."
> — Ephesians 4:3-6 (OWB)

Imagine a large family gathered for a wedding in a village. Over the meal the brothers and sisters argue cheerfully about many things: which road is quickest, which football team is best. But no one argues about whose children they are. They share one father and one family name, and that settles who belongs at the table.

The Church is like that family. Christians agree on the great truths that make us one, and differ on many smaller matters. Trouble comes when we confuse the two: when we treat a small matter as if it decided who belongs to the family, or treat a great truth as if it were only a matter of taste.

This appendix is a guide to telling them apart. Its aim is that we hold firm on the essentials and extend grace on the rest. Paul gives the spirit of it in one line: "Welcome anyone whose faith is weak, but not in order to quarrel over their opinions" (Romans 14:1). Throughout this book, where faithful Christians disagree, we have set out the main views fairly and stood on the common ground, and where the book takes a view of its own, it has said so. This appendix gathers those questions in one place.

### Scripture, tradition and interpretation

Some teachings that all Christians hold are not stated word for word in the Bible but are drawn faithfully from it. The word Trinity, for example, never appears in Scripture. Yet the Bible teaches that there is one God (Deuteronomy 6:4), that the Father is God, that the Son is God (John 1:1), and that the Spirit is God. Jesus sends His disciples to baptize "into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19), one name, three persons. The word Trinity is simply the Church's way of summing up what the Bible says.

Other practices are church traditions that grew up over time: the shape of a service, the church calendar, the robes a minister wears. Some are wise and helpful; some are simply customs.

Both kinds should be weighed by Scripture. "All Scripture is breathed out by God and is useful for teaching, for rebuking, for correcting and for training in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16). The believers in Berea were praised because they "went through the Scriptures day after day to check whether what Paul said was true" (Acts 17:11). If even an apostle's teaching was tested by Scripture, so should ours be.

Interpretation matters too. The Bible's central message is plain enough for a child, but on some questions careful and godly readers have long disagreed. Augustine of North Africa, writing to help Christians read the Bible well, gave a wise guide for such times:

> "Whoever, then, thinks that he understands the Holy Scriptures, or any part of them, but puts such an interpretation upon them as does not tend to build up this twofold love of God and our neighbor, does not yet understand them as he ought."
> Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*

A reading that makes us proud and unloving has missed the point, however clever it is.

### Theological triage

In a busy hospital, when many patients arrive at once, a nurse does what is called triage: she sorts them by how serious their condition is. A man who has stopped breathing is seen first; a boy with a small cut can wait. Every patient matters, but not every injury is equally urgent.

Christians have borrowed this word. Theological triage means sorting beliefs by how central they are to the gospel. Some beliefs are life and death to the faith itself. Some are important but do not decide whether someone is a Christian. Some are matters of conscience where believers can freely differ.

A well-known motto puts it this way: "In essentials, unity; in non-essentials, liberty; in all things, charity." It is often said to be from Augustine, but it is usually traced to a seventeenth-century German Lutheran writer, Rupertus Meldenius, who wrote during a time of bitter quarrels among Protestants. Charity here means love: even where we must disagree, love governs how we do it.

### Primary issues: essential to the Christian faith

These are the truths that define the Christian faith. To deny them is to step outside historic Christianity. Jude urges us to "fight hard for the faith that was handed over to God's holy people once for all" (Jude 3).

- **The Trinity.** There is one God, eternally existing in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit (Chapters 1 to 3).
- **The full deity and full humanity of Jesus Christ, including His virgin birth.** Jesus is truly God and truly man. The angel told Mary, "The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will cover you with its shadow. So the child to be born will be holy, and will be called the Son of God" (Luke 1:35).
- **Salvation by grace through faith in Christ's death and resurrection.** We are not saved by our own goodness but by God's gift, received by faith. Paul handed it on as the first and chief thing: "that Christ died for our sins, in keeping with the Scriptures; that He was buried; that He has been raised on the third day, in keeping with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3-4).
- **The bodily resurrection and return of Christ.** Jesus rose bodily from the grave, and "He will come in the very way you saw Him leave for heaven" (Acts 1:11).
- **The authority and inspiration of Scripture.** The Bible is God's Word, breathed out by Him, and the final authority for what we believe and how we live.

Whoever confesses these truths from the heart is a brother or sister in Christ, whatever else divides us.

### Secondary issues: important, but not a reason to break fellowship

These matters are important. They often shape which church or denomination we join, because a congregation has to practise something. But they should not break our fellowship as fellow believers.

- **Baptism.** Some churches baptize the infants of believers; others baptize only those old enough to confess faith. Some immerse; others pour or sprinkle.
- **The Lord's Supper.** Christians differ on how Christ is present when we share the bread and the cup.
- **Church government.** Some churches are led by bishops (episcopal), some by elders in councils (presbyterian), and some by the whole congregation (congregational).
- **Spiritual gifts.** Christians differ on whether all the gifts continue today, and on baptism in the Spirit: whether it happens at conversion, as a later experience, or both. Chapter 3 sets out those views and lands on the common ground that every believer needs to be continually filled with the Spirit. The gifts are discussed further below.
- **Roles of men and women in church leadership.** This book's view, set out in Chapter 21, is that men and women are equal in worth and both made in God's image, with complementary roles in the church that mirror those of husbands and wives in the family, while honouring the vital ministry of women such as Phoebe and Priscilla. Egalitarian Christians read these passages differently, believing every office is open to both, and they too seek to obey Scripture.
- **Predestination and free will.** Christians differ on how God's sovereign choice and our real human choices fit together.

On each of these, godly believers have stood on both sides. You can hold your view with conviction and still welcome one who holds another.

### Tertiary issues: matters of conscience and preference

These are matters where we should freely differ and not let them trouble our fellowship at all.

- **The timing of the rapture and the details of the end times.** All Christians agree that Jesus will return. On the order of events, they differ widely.
- **Worship styles, music and liturgy.** Old hymns or new songs, written prayers or spontaneous ones.
- **Bible translations.** Many faithful translations exist, each with its strengths.
- **Days, foods and holidays.** Paul says plainly, "One person regards one day as more important than another, while someone else regards every day as the same. Each person must be fully convinced in their own mind" (Romans 14:5).
- **Many practical matters of Christian living,** from how we dress for church to how we school our children.

Romans 14 is Paul's long lesson in tertiary issues. His counsel is clear: "So let us stop judging one another" (Romans 14:13), and "So let us pursue whatever makes for peace and whatever builds one another up" (Romans 14:19).

### A brief map of church history

**The early Church and the creeds.** In the first centuries, Christians summed up the faith in short statements called creeds. The Apostles' Creed grew from the confessions made at baptism. The Nicene Creed, first agreed at Nicaea in 325 and expanded at Constantinople in 381, defended the full deity of Christ and of the Spirit. It is worth remembering that some of the greatest defenders of these truths were Africans: Athanasius of Alexandria in Egypt, and Augustine of Hippo in what is now Algeria. The creeds still state the primary beliefs that nearly all Christians share.

**The Great Schism of 1054.** The Greek-speaking East and Latin-speaking West slowly drifted apart, and in 1054 the break became formal, dividing the Eastern (Orthodox) churches from the Western (Catholic) Church.

**The Protestant Reformation.** In 1517 Martin Luther, a German monk and teacher, called the Church back to Scripture and to salvation by grace through faith. Many Protestant traditions followed, from Lutheran, Reformed and Anglican to Baptist, Methodist and Pentecostal.

**The global Church today.** In the last century the centre of the Christian world has moved south and east, and today the Church is growing fastest across Africa, Asia and Latin America.

### Is the Pope the leader of the Church?

The three main traditions answer this question differently. Here they are, stated fairly.

- **Roman Catholic.** The Pope, as Bishop of Rome, is the successor of the apostle Peter and the visible head of the Church on earth. Catholics draw on Jesus' words to Peter: "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build My church" (Matthew 16:18).
- **Eastern Orthodox.** The Bishop of Rome was historically honoured as "first among equals" among the great bishops, but not as having authority over the whole Church.
- **Protestant.** Christ alone is the head of the Church, and no single bishop holds authority over all. God "has given Him to the church to be head over all things" (Ephesians 1:22). "He is also the head of the body, the church" (Colossians 1:18).

This book's approach is to affirm Christ as the head of His Church, to explain the differences honestly, and to recognise as fellow believers people across all these traditions who confess the primary truths.

### Is Sunday the Christian Sabbath?

Christians hold several views here too.

- **Sunday as the Christian Sabbath.** The Sabbath command carries over to the Lord's Day, which should be kept holy as a day of worship and rest. This is the historic Reformed and Puritan view.
- **Sunday as the Lord's Day, distinct from the Sabbath.** The early Church gathered on the first day of the week to celebrate the resurrection: "On the first day of the week we met together to break bread" (Acts 20:7; see also 1 Corinthians 16:2 and Revelation 1:10). Christ Himself fulfils the Sabbath rest: "So a Sabbath rest is still waiting for God's people. For whoever has entered God's rest has come to rest from their own works too" (Hebrews 4:9-10).
- **Seventh-day Sabbath.** Saturday remains the Sabbath, as it was for Israel. Seventh-day Adventists and some others hold this view.
- **Freedom of conscience.** Paul writes, "So no one should sit in judgment on you over food and drink, or over keeping a festival, a new moon or the sabbath. These things are a shadow of what was coming; the substance is Christ" (Colossians 2:16-17; see also Romans 14:5-6).

This book's emphasis, set out in Chapter 11, is that Jesus is our Sabbath rest. He invites, "Come to Me, all of you who are worn out with work and weighed down with burdens, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). So every day becomes a day of resting in Him and relying on God for everything. At the same time, we honour the Lord's Day gathering and receive regular physical rest as God's good gift.

The common ground is wide: gathering on the Lord's Day, the gift of regular rest, and worship centred on the risen Christ.

### The gifts of the Spirit and tongues

Sincere, Bible-believing Christians hold a wide range of views here. This is a secondary issue that should not divide us.

- **Continuationist (Pentecostal and charismatic).** All the gifts, which the New Testament calls charismata, continue today. Many distinguish praying in tongues, a personal prayer language open to many believers, from the gift of tongues, a public message in the gathered church that needs interpretation. Some Pentecostals see speaking in tongues as the initial evidence of baptism in the Spirit.
- **Open but cautious.** The gifts may continue today, but they should be tested carefully and practised in order and love. Speaking in tongues is not required as evidence of the Spirit.
- **Cessationist.** The miraculous sign gifts, such as tongues, prophecy and healing, served to confirm the apostolic message and ceased with the apostles. God still heals and works in answer to prayer, but not through these gifts in the same way.

On tongues as a prayer language, Christians read 1 Corinthians 14 differently. Some see verses such as "Those who speak in a tongue build up themselves" (1 Corinthians 14:4) as describing private prayer in tongues; others read the chapter as referring only to the public gift. Chapter 22 teaches the distinction many continuationists make between praying in tongues and the public gift of tongues; this appendix sets that view beside the others so that readers can weigh them.

The common ground is rich. "Each person is given a way in which the Spirit is openly at work, and it is given for the good of all" (1 Corinthians 12:7). Love is greater than any gift: "I may be able to prophesy, and know every mystery and everything there is to know; I may have all the faith there is, enough to move mountains; but if I do not have love, I am nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:2). And "Everything should be done to build up the church" (1 Corinthians 14:26).

This book's posture is to teach clearly, to honour believers across these views, and to encourage every reader to seek the Spirit eagerly and walk in love.

### A heart right with God: John Wesley

One of the clearest guides to holding firm convictions with a generous heart is John Wesley, the eighteenth-century English preacher whose movement became the Methodist churches.

Wesley held his views strongly. His most painful disagreement was with his friend George Whitefield, the great open-air preacher. The two had been close since their Oxford days and both preached to vast crowds in the revival, but they disagreed sharply over predestination, and for a time the dispute divided their followers and strained their friendship.

Yet they refused to let it end their love for one another. When Whitefield died in America in 1770, he had asked that Wesley preach his funeral sermon in London, and Wesley did so, honouring his old friend's life and gospel.

Wesley set out the principle behind this in a sermon called *Catholic Spirit*. The word catholic here simply means universal: a love that reaches the whole family of God. Taking as his text Jehu's question to Jehonadab in 2 Kings 10:15, he asked whether believers who disagree on many matters can still be united in love:

> "Though we cannot think alike, may we not love alike? May we not be of one heart, though we are not of one opinion? Without all doubt, we may."
> John Wesley, *Catholic Spirit*

But Wesley was careful to say that this does not mean believing nothing in particular. A generous spirit is not a muddled mind. He wrote:

> "A man of a truly catholic spirit has not now his religion to seek. He is fixed as the sun in his judgement concerning the main branches of Christian doctrine."
> John Wesley, *Catholic Spirit*

That is the balance this appendix seeks: fixed on the essentials, generous on the rest.

Wesley was not alone. A century earlier, in the bitterly divided England of the civil wars, the Puritan pastor Richard Baxter of Kidderminster longed for Christians to unite around the central truths, and liked to call himself simply a mere Christian. Centuries later C. S. Lewis borrowed Baxter's phrase for his famous book *Mere Christianity*. Lewis pictured the Christian faith as a great hall with doors opening into several rooms. The hall is the common faith; the rooms are the different churches. He urged readers to find a room where they could be fed, and to be kind to those who chose other doors.

And in East Africa, the revival that began in the 1930s brought believers from different tribes, churches and backgrounds to walk in the light together at the foot of the cross. Festo Kivengere of Uganda, one of its best-loved preachers, carried that spirit across many nations: Christ at the centre, with repentance, forgiveness and fellowship flowing out from Him across every line that might divide.

### How to disagree well

Disagreement among Christians is not new: the first church council met because of "fierce dispute and argument" (Acts 15:2). What matters is how we disagree.

1. **Humility.** We all see in part. "For now we see by means of a mirror, as if in a riddle, but then we will see face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12). Hold your views, but remember you may be wrong on some.
2. **Listening.** Before you answer someone's view, make sure you can explain it in a way they would recognise.
3. **Charity.** Assume the best of your brother or sister. "Do not act out of rivalry or out of a craving for empty glory. Instead, be humble, and regard each other as standing above yourselves" (Philippians 2:3).
4. **Avoiding quarrels over words.** Paul warned Timothy "not to quarrel about words. It does no good and only ruins those who listen" (2 Timothy 2:14). "The Lord's servant must not be quarrelsome but kind to everyone, able to teach and patient with difficult people, gently correcting those who oppose him" (2 Timothy 2:24-25).
5. **Keeping the gospel at the centre.** The cross of Christ is what makes us one family. When we return to it, our smaller differences find their proper size.

Paul's plea to a divided church still stands: "agree with one another, all of you, and let there be no splits among you. Let what is torn be mended, so that you are joined together, one in mind and one in purpose" (1 Corinthians 1:10). And on the night before He died, Jesus prayed for us: "Father, I ask that all of them may be one. Just as You are in Me and I am in You, so may they too be in us, so that the world may come to believe that You sent Me" (John 17:21).

Our unity is a witness. When believers who differ on many things love one another before the face of God, the world sees something it cannot explain, and is pointed to the One who sent Jesus.

### Questions for reflection

- Before reading this appendix, which beliefs would you have placed among the essentials? Has your list changed?
- Why do you think Christians sometimes treat secondary or tertiary issues as if they were primary? What harm can this do?
- On which of the questions in this appendix do you hold a strong view? How can you hold it with conviction and still welcome those who disagree?
- Wesley asked, "May we not be of one heart, though we are not of one opinion?" Is there a fellow believer with whom you need to rebuild love despite a disagreement?
- Which of the five marks of disagreeing well do you find hardest, and why?
