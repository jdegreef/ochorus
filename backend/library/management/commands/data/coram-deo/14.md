## Spirit, Soul and Body: Healing and Growing the Soul

> "May the God of peace Himself make you holy in every part, and may your spirit, soul and body be kept whole and blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ."
> — 1 Thessalonians 5:23 (OWB)

God does not save only a part of us. He does not rescue our souls for heaven and leave the rest of us to fend for itself. Paul's prayer for the believers in Thessalonica reaches every corner of a human life: "May the God of peace Himself make you holy in every part, and may your spirit, soul and body be kept whole" (1 Thessalonians 5:23).

In Chapter 4 we met the idea that a human being can be described as spirit, soul and body. In Chapter 14 we saw how God makes us holy, breaking the hard shell of self-will so that His life can flow through us. This chapter brings those threads together. It asks a simple question: what is God doing with the whole of me, and when?

The answer has three tenses. God has made our spirit new. He is renewing our soul. And He will make our body new. Past, present and future. Seeing this pattern brings great peace, because it tells us where we are on the road, and it tells us that the God who started the journey will finish it.

### The whole person

Christians have described the human person in two main ways. Some, like Watchman Nee in *The Spiritual Man*, see three parts: the spirit, by which we know God; the soul, which is our mind, will and emotions; and the body, by which we live in the physical world. They point to verses like 1 Thessalonians 5:23 and Hebrews 4:12, which speak of soul and spirit side by side. Many other theologians see two parts: an inner self, which the Bible sometimes calls soul and sometimes spirit, and an outer, bodily self. They point out that the Bible often uses "soul" and "spirit" for the same inner life.

Both views are held by faithful, Bible-loving Christians, and this is not a matter on which believers should divide (see the Appendix). This book uses the three-part picture as a helpful lens, as it did in Chapter 4. But if you hold the two-part view, you can follow every step of this chapter. Simply read "spirit" as the deepest part of the inner self, where we are joined to God, and "soul" as the inner self as it thinks, chooses and feels.

What matters most is what both views agree on. God made us whole persons. Sin damaged every part of us. And God's salvation reaches every part of us too.

### Spirit made new: what God has done

Before we came to Christ, the Bible says we were dead toward God. We could be very alive in every other way, clever at school, successful in business, warm to our families, and still be dead to Him. "But God is rich in mercy. Because of the great love He had for us, even when we were dead in our trespasses, He made us alive together with Christ" (Ephesians 2:4–5).

This is the new birth that Jesus described to Nicodemus: "Whatever is born of the flesh is flesh, and whatever is born of the Spirit is spirit" (John 3:6). At conversion something happened deep inside us that we could not do for ourselves. The Holy Spirit gave us new life and came to live in us. God had promised it long before through Ezekiel: "I will give you a new heart, and I will place a new spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:26).

This is done. It is past tense. "If anyone is in Christ, that person is part of a new creation. The old things have gone, and look, new things have come into being" (2 Corinthians 5:17). On the day you trusted Christ, you were justified, declared righteous before God, and you were made alive to Him. "The Spirit Himself joins our own spirit in testifying that we are God's children" (Romans 8:16). Nothing that happens to you later can undo this.

### Soul being renewed: what God is doing

But if our spirit has been made new, why do we still struggle? Why does the believer still feel anxious, still lose his temper, still carry old fears? Because the renewing of the soul is a work in the present tense. It takes a lifetime.

Paul writes, "Do not let this present age press you into its mold, but be changed through and through as your mind is made new" (Romans 12:2). Notice the words "be changed" and "is made new". This is something happening to us now, step by step. Our minds learn to think God's thoughts. Our wills learn to choose God's ways. Our emotions learn to rest in God's love instead of being thrown about by every wind.

Think of a family that moves from a village into a new house in town. On the first day, the house is theirs. The keys are in their hand. But it takes months to feel at home: to learn which window lets in the morning light, to arrange the furniture, to stop reaching for a light switch where the old one used to be. In the same way, God has given us new life in our spirit, but our minds, wills and feelings still carry the habits of the old house. The Holy Spirit is patiently teaching us to live in the new one.

This is what the Bible calls sanctification, the work we looked at in Chapter 14. It is the slow, sure work of God in the present.

### Augustine: a restless heart and a wounded soul

No one in the history of the Church has written more honestly about the inner life than Augustine, who was born in 354 in Thagaste, a small town in North Africa, in what is now Algeria. His father was a pagan, his mother Monica a praying Christian. He was brilliant and ambitious. He studied in Carthage, became a teacher of public speaking, and later moved to Rome and then Milan, where he won a respected post.

Outwardly he was a success. Inwardly he was in pieces. In his forties he wrote *Confessions*, which is in the Ochorus library. It is a long prayer to God, telling the story of his life. Its first paragraph holds the words we met in Chapter 2: God made us for Himself, and our hearts are restless until they rest in Him. Augustine knew that restlessness from the inside.

Augustine did not only write about sin. He wrote about wounds. As a young man in Thagaste he lost a close friend to fever, and the grief overwhelmed him. Everything he looked at reminded him of his friend. He wrote, "I became a great riddle to myself, and I asked my soul, why she was so sad, and why she disquieted me sorely: but she knew not what to answer me." Many readers today know exactly that feeling: grief or pain so deep that we do not understand our own hearts.

For years Augustine tried to fill the emptiness with pleasure, success and clever ideas. Nothing healed it. He knew that the problem was not out there but within, and he could not fix it. Looking back, he wrote to God:

> "Thou wert with me, but I was not with Thee."
> Augustine, *Confessions*

In today's words: "You were with me, but I was not with You."

The turning point came in a garden in Milan in 386. Augustine was weeping, torn between the life he wanted to leave and the God he could not yet trust. He heard a child's voice from a nearby house chanting, "Take up and read." He picked up a book of Paul's letters and read words from Romans 13 telling him to put on the Lord Jesus Christ like clothing and stop making plans to satisfy the flesh (Romans 13:14). Light flooded his heart. He was baptised the following Easter.

That was the new birth, the spirit made alive. But the healing of his soul went on. Only months later his mother Monica died at Ostia, on the journey home to Africa. Augustine wrote that "my soul was wounded, and that life rent asunder as it were." He did not pretend he was not hurting. He brought his grief to God in prayer and in the company of his friends, who sang a psalm together in the house.

Augustine returned to North Africa, and in 395 or 396 he became bishop of Hippo, the city he served until his death in 430. His books shaped the faith of the whole Western Church. Yet his greatest gift to ordinary believers may be his honesty. He shows us that a person can be truly converted and still need healing, and that God is patient with both.

### Soul wounds and how God heals them

Many people come to Christ carrying deep hurts. A child who grew up with a father who drank and beat his mother. A woman abused by someone she trusted. A man who survived war or genocide and saw what no one should see. A young person crushed by years of being told they were worthless. Others carry wounds received inside the Church itself, from harsh words, betrayal or spiritual pressure.

These wounds are real. They shape how we see God, ourselves and others. A believer may know in her head that God is a loving Father, yet freeze inside every time she pictures Him, because the word "father" carries pain. Being born again does not erase memories in a moment. Sometimes God heals suddenly. More often He heals gently, layer by layer.

The good news is that healing the wounded is close to the heart of God. "He heals those whose hearts are crushed and bandages their injuries" (Psalm 147:3). When Jesus began His ministry, He read from Isaiah 61, which speaks of the Lord's servant sent "to bandage hearts that are broken, to announce freedom for captives... to console all who grieve" and to give "the oil of rejoicing in place of grief, a cloak of praise in place of a spirit burning low" (Isaiah 61:1–3). Charles Spurgeon, preaching on Psalm 147:3, marvelled that the God who names the stars also cares for broken hearts:

> "...the same mighty hand which rolls the stars along, puts liniments around the wounded heart."
> Charles Spurgeon, *Healing for the Wounded*

How does God heal the soul? The Bible and the experience of the Church point to several ways, which work together.

**His presence.** "He brings back my life and leads me in the tracks of what is right" (Psalm 23:3). Healing begins when we bring our pain into the light of God's face instead of hiding it. Many of the psalms are wounded people telling God exactly how they feel. We can do the same. Jesus invites us: "Come to Me, all of you who are worn out with work and weighed down with burdens, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

**His Word.** Lies often lodge in wounded hearts: "I am unlovable", "It was my fault", "God has abandoned me." Scripture replaces lies with truth. As we read, memorise and meditate on God's Word (see Chapter 6), our minds are made new.

**Forgiveness.** Some of the deepest wounds come from what others have done to us, and some of the strongest chains are bitterness and the longing for revenge. Forgiving does not mean saying the wrong did not matter, or trusting a dangerous person again, or that justice should not be done. It means handing the debt to God and refusing to let the wrongdoer go on ruling our hearts. "Forgive each other freely, just as God has forgiven you in Christ" (Ephesians 4:32). It is often a process, not a single moment.

The Ugandan bishop Festo Kivengere knew this from the inside. When Idi Amin's regime murdered Archbishop Janani Luwum in 1977, Kivengere himself had to flee the country. In exile he found bitterness rising in his own heart toward the man who had killed his friend and brought such suffering to his people. He came to see that he too needed the cross to heal him of hatred, and he wrote a short book with the startling title *I Love Idi Amin*. Forgiveness did not mean pretending evil was good. It meant refusing to let evil poison his soul.

**The community of believers.** God heals us in the company of His people. "Take up one another's heavy loads, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). A small group that listens without judging, a mature friend who prays with us, a church that welcomes the broken: these are God's hands.

**Pastoral care, counselling and medical care.** Some wounds go so deep that we need skilled help. A wise pastor, a qualified Christian counsellor, or a doctor may be part of God's answer to our prayers. Depression, trauma and anxiety can have physical as well as spiritual causes, and seeking treatment is not a lack of faith. Luke, who wrote a Gospel, was a physician. Prayer and good care are not rivals. They work alongside each other, and God can use both.

Jesus once asked a man who had been lying by a pool for thirty-eight years, "Do you want to be well?" (John 5:6). It is a real question. Healing can be frightening, because it asks us to face what we have avoided. But the One who asks is gentle, and He does not hurry us.

### Growing up in Christ

Healing is part of a larger journey: growing up. God does not want us to stay spiritual infants. Paul longs for the day when "we will be a mature adult, grown to the full measure of the stature that belongs to the fullness of Christ" so that "we will no longer be infants, thrown about by the waves" (Ephesians 4:13–14).

Maturity is not the same as age or knowledge. An old church elder can be spiritually childish, and a young believer can be wise. Maturity shows itself in steadiness, in love that does not depend on being loved back, in the ability to take correction, to forgive quickly and to trust God in the dark. It grows the way a tree grows: slowly, through seasons, with deep roots.

So be patient with yourself, and with others. You will not be finished in this life. But you are not stuck either. "The one who started a good work in you will keep working at it until it is finished on the day of Christ Jesus" (Philippians 1:6). God does not abandon half-built houses.

### Body made new: what God will do

Our bodies are also part of God's plan. Right now they are tired, ageing and often sick. Some of us live with pain every day. Every one of us will grow weaker with the years, unless Christ returns first. Paul does not deny this: "Our outer self may be decaying, but our inner self is being made new day after day" (2 Corinthians 4:16).

But decay is not the end of the story. On the day Christ returns, our bodies will be raised and transformed. "It is sown in decay; it is raised imperishable. In dishonor it is sown, in glory it is raised; in weakness it is sown, in power it is raised" (1 Corinthians 15:42–43). We are waiting for "a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who will change these bodies of our humble state and give them the same form as His glorious body" (Philippians 3:20–21).

This matters for how we live now. God does not despise the body. We care for our bodies as gifts on loan from Him. And when we suffer in the body, we can groan with hope, as Paul says we do, "while we long for our adoption as children, when our body will be redeemed" (Romans 8:23). Chapter 28 will look more fully at that great day.

### The pattern of salvation

Now we can see the whole pattern. Theologians use three words for it.

1. **Justification** (past): God declared us righteous in Christ, and our spirit was made alive to Him. We have been saved.
2. **Sanctification** (present): God is renewing our soul, making us holy and healing our wounds. We are being saved.
3. **Glorification** (future): God will raise our bodies and make us perfectly like Christ. We will be saved.

Paul joins these together in one sentence: "Those He called, He also declared righteous. And those He declared righteous, He also glorified" (Romans 8:30). Notice he speaks of glorification as if it has already happened, because in God's mind it is as sure as if it were done.

If you prefer the two-part view of the person, the pattern still holds. Our inner self was made alive at conversion, is being renewed day by day, and will be made perfect, and our body will be raised. Whichever way we describe the parts, the God who began the work will complete it.

To live coram Deo, before the face of God, is to live inside this pattern. We do not need to fear that God will give up on us halfway. We can bring our wounds to Him without shame, because He is already at work in us. And we can look at our ageing bodies without despair, because the same face we seek now will one day be seen with eyes made new.

The soul that is being healed is also a soul under attack. In the next chapter we turn to the unseen battle and to Christ's victory over every power that would keep us bound.

### Questions for reflection

- In your own words, what has God already done in you, what is He doing now, and what will He do in the future?
- Augustine wrote, "Thou wert with me, but I was not with Thee." Have there been seasons in your life when this was true of you?
- Are there wounds from your past that still shape how you see God, yourself or others? Which of the ways of healing in this chapter might God want to use?
- Is there someone you need to forgive? What would it mean to hand that debt to God, even if your feelings take time to follow?
- Have you ever felt that seeking counselling or medical help showed a lack of faith? How does this chapter answer that fear?
- How does the promise of a resurrection body change the way you think about sickness, ageing and death?

### A step for this week

Set aside one quiet hour this week with your Bible and a notebook. Read Psalm 147:1–6 and Isaiah 61:1–3 slowly. Then ask God to show you one hurt from the past that still affects you today, and write down honestly what happened and how it made you feel. Pray about it in your own words, and write one truth from Scripture beside it that answers the lie the wound has taught you. Before the week ends, share what you wrote with one trusted believer or your pastor and ask them to pray with you. If the hurt is very deep, ask them to help you find a qualified Christian counsellor.
