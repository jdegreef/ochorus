## Marriage: The First Divine Union

> "Then the LORD God said, 'It is not good for the man to be on his own. I will make him a helper who matches him.'"
> — Genesis 2:18 (OWB)

Think of a wedding day. In a village, the families have gathered since early morning. There is singing, there is food cooking over open fires, and the elders have spoken words of blessing. In a city, the hall is decorated, the cars are hooting outside, and a choir is warming up. A man and a woman stand side by side and make promises that will shape the rest of their lives.

So far in this book we have looked at who God is (Part One), how we walk with Him day by day (Part Two), and how He makes us like Christ, even through warfare and suffering (Part Three). Now, in Part Four, we turn to life as God's people. And we begin where the Bible begins, in the home.

Marriage is the place where living *coram Deo*, before the face of God, is tested most closely. In public we can wear a smile. At home, behind a closed door, the people who live with us see us as we really are. Yet even there, especially there, we are before the face of God. He was the first witness to the first marriage, and He is a witness to every marriage since. What we say to our husband or wife at the end of a hard day, how we spend money, how we speak about each other when the other is absent: all of it is lived in His sight. That is not a threat but an invitation.

### God's first institution

Before there was any government, before there was a temple, a priest or a church, there was a marriage. God made the man and the woman and joined them together in the garden. "So God created human beings in His image. In the image of God He created them; male and female He created them" (Genesis 1:27). Then He blessed them and gave them their first task together: "Be fruitful and multiply. Fill the earth and subdue it" (Genesis 1:28).

Genesis 2 tells the same story more closely. Everything God made so far was good, but now God says something is "not good": the man is alone. God brings the animals to Adam to be named, and Adam does his work well, but among all the creatures "no helper who matched him was found for the man" (Genesis 2:20). So God makes the woman and brings her to him, and Adam bursts into the first poem in the Bible: "At last, here is bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh!" (Genesis 2:23).

Notice the word "helper". In our languages a helper can sound like a junior servant. In the Old Testament the same Hebrew word is used of God Himself, who comes to the help of His people. So the word does not mean someone of lesser worth. It means someone who supplies what is missing. The woman matches the man. She is like him and yet different from him, and together they can do what neither could do alone.

Martin Luther, whose own marriage we will meet later in this chapter, saw that this order matters. Marriage is not a human invention that the Church later blessed. God gave it first. In *The Large Catechism*, explaining the commandment against adultery, he wrote:

> "Therefore He also wishes us to honor it, and to maintain and conduct it as a divine and blessed estate; because, in the first place, He has instituted it before all others"
> Martin Luther, *The Large Catechism*

Because marriage comes first, it is also the foundation of everything else. Families grow out of marriages. Villages and towns grow out of families. Nations are built from towns and villages. When marriages are strong, children grow up secure, the elderly are cared for, and communities hold together. Caring for your marriage is not a private hobby. It is one of the most important things you will ever do for your children, your church and your nation.

### Two become one: a covenant before God

The chapter that tells of the first marriage ends with a sentence that Jesus and Paul both quote: "This is why a man leaves his father and mother and clings to his wife, and they become one flesh" (Genesis 2:24).

There are three movements here. First, the man *leaves*. In many cultures a marriage joins two families, and that is a good thing. Parents and relatives are involved in the choice, gifts are exchanged, and the wider family promises to stand behind the couple. But Scripture says that something new begins on the wedding day. The husband and wife are no longer first of all a son and a daughter. They are a new household, and their first loyalty, after God, is now to each other.

Second, the man *clings*. The word speaks of holding fast, of glue that does not let go. Marriage is not a trial arrangement that lasts while the feelings are strong. It is a commitment to stay.

Third, the two *become one flesh*. This includes the physical union of husband and wife, which is God's good gift for marriage. But it is more than that. Two lives, two sets of plans, two incomes and two histories are woven into one. When Jesus was asked about divorce, He went straight back to Genesis: "Have you never read that the Creator, at the beginning, made them male and female?" (Matthew 19:4). Then He added His own word: "So they are not two any longer; they are one flesh. What God has yoked together, then, no human being may pull apart" (Matthew 19:6).

That is why the Bible calls marriage a covenant. A contract is an agreement between two parties, each protecting their own interests; if one side fails, the contract ends. A covenant is a promise made before God, binding yourself to another person for their good. The prophet Malachi rebuked the men of his day who were abandoning their wives, and he gave the reason it mattered so much: "the LORD was a witness between you and the wife you married in your youth. You have broken faith with her, though she is your partner and your wife by covenant" (Malachi 2:14).

The LORD was a witness. He was there when the vows were spoken, whether in a cathedral, a mud-brick church or a registry office. And He remains a witness every day afterwards. This is marriage *coram Deo*.

### The great mystery: a picture of Christ and His Church

Why did God design marriage this way? Paul gives the deepest answer in Ephesians 5. After quoting Genesis 2:24 again, he says something surprising: "There is a great mystery here, but I am speaking about Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32).

In the Bible a "mystery" is not a puzzle with no answer. It is something hidden for a long time and now made known. Paul is telling us that from the very beginning, marriage was meant to be a living picture of a greater love: the love between Christ and His people. The Old Testament prophets spoke of God as the husband of Israel. The New Testament calls the Church the bride of Christ, and the Bible ends with a wedding: "the Lamb's wedding has come, and His bride has made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7).

This means every Christian marriage is a kind of sermon. People around us who never open a Bible are watching how a believing husband treats his wife and how a believing wife treats her husband. When they see faithfulness, kindness and self-giving love, they are seeing a small picture of how Christ loves His Church. When they see bitterness and betrayal, the picture is spoiled. Your ordinary marriage, with its meals, quarrels and reconciliations, can point people to Jesus.

### Husbands and wives: distinct callings

Paul's teaching on marriage in Ephesians 5 begins with a verse for every believer: "And you will submit to one another in the reverent fear of Christ" (Ephesians 5:21). Christians are people who put others first. That sets the tone for what follows. Then Paul speaks to wives and to husbands in turn, and he gives each a different calling.

To wives he says: "Wives should submit to their own husbands, as to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:22). He sums it up at the end of the passage: "the wife must see to it that she respects her husband" (Ephesians 5:33). Peter says the same, adding that a wife's godly way of life can win an unbelieving husband "even without your saying a word" (1 Peter 3:1). A wife's calling is to respect and support her husband, to stand with him, to honour him in public and in private, and to help him lead the home in the ways of God. This is not the submission of an inferior to a superior. It is a free and willing choice made "as to the Lord", by a woman who is equally made in God's image.

To husbands Paul gives a calling that is, if anything, even heavier: "Husbands, love your wives the way Christ loved the church and handed Himself over for her" (Ephesians 5:25). How did Christ love the Church? He left heaven. He served. He washed feet. He went to the cross. Headship in the Bible is not a throne; it is a cross. A husband who is head of his home is the first to serve, the first to apologise, the first to pray, and the first to stand between his family and danger. He leads by laying down his life. Paul adds a plain warning: "Husbands, love your wives and never turn harsh or bitter against them" (Colossians 3:19). Peter tells husbands to live with their wives "with understanding", to give them honour, and to remember that she is one "who will inherit the grace of life together with you, so that nothing stands in the way of your prayers" (1 Peter 3:7). A man who mistreats his wife should not expect his prayers to be heard.

Christians read these passages in different ways. Many, often called complementarians, understand them as teaching distinct and lasting roles for husbands and wives, with the husband carrying a loving, servant leadership in the home. Others, often called egalitarians, read the passages in the light of their first-century setting and stress the mutual submission of verse 21, so that husband and wife share leadership equally. This book teaches the distinct callings as Paul and Peter give them, while recognising that faithful believers differ here, and that this is not a matter that should divide us as brothers and sisters (see Appendix A). On the central things, all agree: husband and wife are equal in worth before God, both are heirs of grace, both are called to put the other first, and harshness or control has no place in a Christian home.

One word must be said clearly. Nothing in Scripture asks a wife to accept violence or abuse. Headship is never permission to harm. If you are in danger in your home, go to trusted leaders in your church and, where needed, to the authorities. Protecting the weak is what Christ, the true Head, would do.

### Martin Luther and Katie: a marriage in the Reformation

In April 1523 a group of nuns escaped from a convent at Nimbschen in Germany. They had been reading the writings of Martin Luther and had become convinced that their vows were not what God required. With the help of a merchant who delivered goods to the convent, they were smuggled out in his wagon and brought to Wittenberg, where Luther lived.

Luther felt responsible for them and worked hard to find each woman a home or a husband. One of them, Katharina von Bora, was not easily placed. She was intelligent, strong-minded and of noble family. In the end, on 13 June 1525, the former monk married the former nun. He was forty-one; she was twenty-six. Luther said he married partly to please his father, partly to spite the Pope and the devil, and partly to show that he believed what he taught.

But it became one. The couple moved into the old monastery building in Wittenberg, which was soon full of children, relatives, students and guests. Katharina ran it all. She managed the farm, kept animals, brewed beer, tended a garden and fed the crowds who came to hear Luther talk at the table. He relied on her completely, and teased her by calling her *my lord Katie* because she ruled the household so well. They had six children. One daughter died as a baby, and their beloved Magdalena died at thirteen in her father's arms. They wept together, and they held on to God together.

Their marriage was not perfect. Luther was famously stubborn, and Katie was no quiet mouse. Luther himself admitted it, with a smile, in one of the sayings collected in *Table Talk*:

> "I, said Luther, must have patience with the Pope; I must have patience with heretics and seducers; I must have patience with the roaring courtiers; I must have patience with my servants: I must have patience with Kate my wife"
> Martin Luther, *Table Talk*

Yet the same man wrote in *The Large Catechism* that husband and wife "must by all means live together in love and harmony, that one may cherish the other from the heart and with entire fidelity." That is what he and Katie learned to do. Their home became a model for thousands of Protestant pastors' families. For centuries before, many had thought that the holiest life was one without marriage. The Luthers showed that a home, with its noise and work and laughter and grief, can be a holy place where God is served.

### Faithfulness, forgiveness and praying together

Three habits keep a marriage strong before the face of God.

The first is **faithfulness**. "All of you should treat marriage as precious and keep the marriage bed undefiled" (Hebrews 13:4). Unfaithfulness rarely begins with a single act. It begins with a glance, a secret message, a friendship that becomes too close, a habit of sharing your heart with someone who is not your spouse. Malachi's counsel is wise: "So guard yourselves in your spirit, and let no one be unfaithful to the wife of his youth" (Malachi 2:15). Guard your eyes, your phone and your time. Speak well of your husband or wife when they are not there.

The second is **forgiveness**. Two sinners cannot live under one roof for long without hurting each other. The question is not whether you will be hurt, but what you will do when it happens. Paul's command is for every home: "Be kind to one another and full of compassion. Forgive each other freely, just as God has forgiven you in Christ" (Ephesians 4:32). Forgiveness does not pretend that the wrong did not happen. It names it, and then it releases the debt, because God has released ours. Do not let the sun go down on your anger.

The third is **praying together**. Many couples have found that it is hard to stay angry with someone you have just knelt beside in prayer. The Preacher wrote, "a cord of three strands does not snap quickly" (Ecclesiastes 4:12), and Christians have long seen in that line a picture of a husband, a wife and God bound together. Praying together need not be long or eloquent. Five minutes at the end of the day, thanking God for one thing and bringing Him one concern, can change the climate of a home.

Jonathan Edwards, the eighteenth-century American pastor, and his wife Sarah were known for such a home. They married in 1727 and raised eleven children. When the evangelist George Whitefield stayed with them in 1740, he wrote in his journal how deeply their love for each other and for God had moved him, and it stirred him to pray about marriage himself. When Edwards lay dying in 1758, far from home, he sent a last message to Sarah, telling her that their uncommon union had been spiritual, and so would continue for ever. A marriage lived before the face of God does not end in dust.

### No one in God's family is single

Not every reader of this chapter is married. Some have never married. Some are widowed or divorced. Here is a truth for all of you: in God's family, no one is single. You may not have a spouse, but you belong to a family, the household of God. "You are citizens together with God's holy people, and you belong to God's household" (Ephesians 2:19).

The Bible does not treat singleness as a failure or a waiting room. Jesus Himself never married, and He was the most complete human being who ever lived. Paul was unmarried when he wrote most of his letters, and he could even say, "I would like everyone to be as I am. Yet God gives each person a gift of their own, to one this way and to another that" (1 Corinthians 7:7). Marriage is a gift; singleness is also a gift. Paul points out one of its blessings: "The unmarried man gives his care to the things of the Lord, how he may please Him" (1 Corinthians 7:32). He wants all believers "to stay close beside the Lord with nothing to draw you away" (1 Corinthians 7:35). A single believer can often give time, energy and attention to God's work in ways that a married person cannot.

Amy Carmichael, the Irish missionary who sailed to India in 1895, never married. In southern India she began rescuing children who were in danger, including girls who had been given to temples. Over the years hundreds of children came to live at Dohnavur, and they called her *Amma*, mother. She served there for over fifty years. She had no husband and no children of her own, and yet few women in history have had a larger family. In our own time, the English pastor and writer John Stott also remained unmarried, and gave his life to teaching the Bible and encouraging churches across Africa, Asia and Latin America.

God's promise is that He "gives the lonely a home to live in" (Psalm 68:6). Often He keeps that promise through His people. Jesus promised that those who leave home and family for His sake will receive "a hundred times as much now, in this present time: homes, brothers, sisters, mothers, children and land, together with persecutions" (Mark 10:30). Where do those homes and families come from? From the Church. That means the Church has work to do. Married believers should open their homes and tables to single brothers and sisters, include them in holidays and celebrations, and stop asking, "When will you marry?" as if their life has not yet begun. Single believers, in turn, can be aunts and uncles, mothers and fathers in the faith, to the children and young people around them.

Married or single, every believer is called to the deepest union of all, union with Christ. Earthly marriage is the picture; He is the reality.

In the next chapter we turn to the fruit that so often comes from marriage: children, and the great calling of passing on the faith to the next generation.

### Questions for reflection

- Why does it matter that God established marriage before government or the Church? How does that change the way you see marriage?
- What is the difference between a contract and a covenant? Where do you see a "contract" attitude creeping into marriages around you?
- Read Ephesians 5:21–33. If you are married, which part of your calling as a husband or wife do you find hardest? Why?
- Luther and Katie's marriage began without much romance but grew into deep love. What helps love grow over the years?
- Which of the three habits (faithfulness, forgiveness, praying together) most needs attention in your home, or in the homes you care about?
- How could your church become more of a family to those who are single, widowed or divorced?

### A step for this week

If you are married, set aside five minutes each night this week to pray with your husband or wife before you sleep. Each of you thank God for one thing about the other, and bring Him one concern you share. Keep it short and simple, and do it even on the nights you do not feel like it. If you are single, invite one married couple or family from your church to share a meal with you this week, or accept such an invitation, and pray for their marriage by name each day.
