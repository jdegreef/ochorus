## The Morning Watch

> "LORD, at daybreak You hear my voice. At daybreak I set everything out before You and keep watch."
> — Psalm 5:3 (OWB)

Long before the sun rises over the market town, the traders are awake. They load the vegetables, sweep the stalls and lay out the goods before the first customers arrive. The farmer is up too, walking to the field in the cool grey light, because the work done before the heat of the day sets the pattern for everything that follows. Ask either of them why they rise so early, and they will tell you: the first hours decide the day.

In Chapter 6 we learned to hear God in His Word. In Chapter 7 we learned to speak with Him in prayer. The morning watch brings these two together into a daily meeting with God, a set time at the start of each day when we come before His face, listen to Him, speak with Him, and give Him the day ahead.

The phrase "morning watch" has a long history. Watchmen on the city walls kept watch through the night and looked for the dawn. David wrote, "At daybreak I set everything out before You and keep watch" (Psalm 5:3). He laid his day before God like a trader laying out goods, and then looked up, expecting God to act.

### Jesus rose early

The best reason for a morning watch is that Jesus kept one. Mark describes a full and exhausting day in Capernaum. Jesus taught in the synagogue, cast out an evil spirit, healed Peter's mother-in-law, and then, as evening fell, the whole town crowded at the door with their sick, and He healed many. Then we read: "Long before daylight, He got up and went out. He went off to a lonely place and spent time there in prayer" (Mark 1:35).

Think of that. After the busiest day, when He might have slept late, Jesus rose before dawn to be alone with His Father. When the disciples found Him and said that everyone was looking for Him, He already knew what to do next. Time with the Father had set His direction.

This was not a single event but a pattern. Luke tells us that Jesus "kept slipping away to places in the wilderness to pray" (Luke 5:16). The prophet Isaiah, speaking of the Lord's Servant, had foretold the same thing: "Each morning He rouses me; He rouses my ear to listen as a learner listens" (Isaiah 50:4). The Son of God lived in perfect fellowship with the Father, and yet He still sought time alone with Him at the start of the day. If He needed it, how much more do we?

### Why the first hour matters

There is nothing magical about the morning. God hears prayer at noon and at midnight. Some believers, such as nurses on night shifts or parents of newborn babies, will need to find their quiet time at other hours, and God meets them there. Yet there are good reasons why so many of God's people across the centuries have given Him the first part of the day.

**The morning sets the day's direction.** Before the phone rings, before the news, before the demands of work and family, our minds are fresh. What fills them first tends to shape what follows. E. M. Bounds, the American Methodist preacher who wrote much about prayer, put it strongly in *Power Through Prayer*:

> "The men who have done the most for God in this world have been early on their knees. He who fritters away the early morning, its opportunity and freshness, in other pursuits than seeking God will make poor headway seeking him the rest of the day."
> E. M. Bounds, *Power Through Prayer*

**The morning is a first-fruits offering.** In the Old Testament, God's people gave Him the first of their harvest as a sign that all of it belonged to Him. Giving God the first moments of the day says the same thing about our time: this day is Yours.

**The morning is when mercy is new.** "It is by the LORD's loyal love that we have not been wiped out, for His mercies have not failed. They are new each morning; how great is Your faithfulness!" (Lamentations 3:22–23). Whatever failures yesterday held, the morning is a fresh start. In the wilderness the Israelites gathered manna "morning after morning" (Exodus 16:21); yesterday's manna would not feed them today. So too we need fresh grace for each new day.

**The morning is when we ask for guidance.** "Each morning let me hear of Your loyal love, since You are the one I rely on. Teach me which road to walk, for my whole being reaches up to You" (Psalm 143:8). It is wiser to ask for directions before the journey than after we are lost.

**The morning is a time for joy.** Moses prayed, "Fill us with Your loyal love at daybreak, and we will shout for joy and rejoice as long as we live" (Psalm 90:14). A heart filled with God's love in the morning carries that joy into the market, the office and the kitchen. Many of us wake already anxious, our minds racing through the problems of the day. The morning watch lets God's love speak first, before our fears do.

### Susanna Wesley: a prayer closet in a crowded house

If anyone had an excuse not to keep a quiet time with God, it was Susanna Wesley.

She was born in London in 1669, one of the youngest of a very large family; her father, Samuel Annesley, was a well-known Puritan minister. (The Puritans were English Protestants who wanted the church and the whole of life to be shaped by the Bible.) She married Samuel Wesley, a clergyman, and the couple spent most of their married life in the rectory at Epworth, a small town in Lincolnshire, England. Susanna gave birth to nineteen children. Many died as babies or in childhood, a grief that came again and again. Money was always short. Her husband was often away, sometimes in debt, and once even in prison for debt. In 1709 the rectory burned to the ground in the night. The family barely escaped, and young John, aged five, was rescued from an upstairs window moments before the roof fell in.

Through all this, Susanna ran the household and taught the children herself. She held a home school for six hours a day. She made time each week to speak alone with each child about their soul. She wrote out lessons and explanations of the faith for them. Two of those children, John and Charles Wesley, grew up to be leaders of the great eighteenth-century revival, John as a preacher and Charles as the writer of thousands of hymns.

Where did such a woman find time for God? She made it. She kept regular times of private devotion, morning and evening, and guarded them carefully. Family tradition tells a lovely story about how she did it in a crowded house with no room of her own. When she needed to be alone with God, she would sit down and pull her apron over her head. The children knew what it meant: Mother is praying, and must not be disturbed. Under that apron, in the middle of the noise, she had her inner room.

Her letters show how much she valued this habit. To her eldest son Samuel, away at school, she wrote:

> "Begin and end the day with Him who is the Alpha and Omega, and if you really experience what it is to love God, you will redeem all the time you can for His more immediate service."
> Susanna Wesley, quoted in Eliza Clarke, *Susanna Wesley*

In another letter to him she described the true Christian as one who, "in the morning or evening, or any other time when occasion is offered," will "be cheerfully disposed to retire from the world, that he may offer to his Creator his sacrifice of prayer and praise, and will account it his happiness, as well as his duty, so to do."

Notice the words "happiness, as well as his duty." Susanna was a disciplined woman, but her devotion was not grim. Time with God was her joy. And her example speaks to every busy mother, every worker with long hours, every student in a crowded room. You may not have a quiet house. You may not have a room of your own. But you can have an apron, a corner, a bench, a walk to work. Jesus said, "go into an inner room, shut the door, and pray to your Father who is there in secret" (Matthew 6:6). The inner room may be very small. What matters is that the door is shut to everything else for a while, and open to God.

### What a morning watch looks like

There is no single correct way to keep a morning watch. But most believers find that it includes five simple elements.

1. **Stillness.** Begin by being quiet before God. "Stop, and acknowledge that I am God" (Psalm 46:10). Take a few slow breaths. Let the noise in your mind settle. Remind yourself that you are before His face.
2. **Worship.** Turn your heart towards God with praise and thanks. A psalm, a hymn or a simple sentence of adoration will do.
3. **Scripture.** Read a passage of the Bible slowly, as we learned in Chapter 6. Listen for what God is saying.
4. **Prayer.** Let the Word turn into prayer, as George Müller found. Confess, give thanks, bring your needs, pray for others, and lay the coming day before God.
5. **Listening.** Before you rise, wait a little. Ask, "Lord, is there anything You want to say to me?" Write down anything that comes to mind, and test it as we learned in Chapter 7.

Some like to remember it as a conversation: God speaks in His Word, we answer in prayer, and we listen again.

### Worship: beginning the day with God

Worship deserves a special word. Many of us begin our prayer time with a list of requests, as though we had rushed into a king's court shouting our needs without bowing first. The psalmist shows a better way.

"Raise a glad shout to the LORD, all the earth! Serve the LORD and be glad doing it; come before Him with ringing cries of joy. Be sure of this: the LORD is God! He is the one who made us, and we belong to Him" (Psalm 100:1–3). "Come through His gates giving thanks, into His courtyards with praise" (Psalm 100:4). Thanksgiving is the gate; praise is the courtyard. We enter God's presence by remembering who He is and what He has done.

Morning worship has three movements: **adoration**, praising God for who He is; **thanksgiving**, thanking Him for what He has done, including the gift of a new day; and **surrender**, giving ourselves and the day back to Him. You might simply say, "Lord, this day is Yours. My time, my work, my words and my family are Yours. Use me as You wish."

That surrender is the bridge between private worship and the rest of life. Paul writes, "I appeal to you by the mercies of God: offer your bodies to God as a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to Him. That is your reasonable worship" (Romans 12:1). Worship does not end when we close our Bibles. It continues as we sing with God's people on the Lord's Day, and it continues in everyday obedience: in honest work, patient parenting, kind words and faithful service. The morning watch is where the living sacrifice is laid on the altar for the day.

### Practical help

**Time.** Choose a time you can keep. For most people this means getting up a little earlier, which usually means going to bed a little earlier. Start small. Fifteen minutes kept faithfully is better than an hour attempted once and then abandoned. As the habit grows, the time often grows with it.

**Place.** Find a place where you can be undisturbed: a chair in the corner, a bench outside, the kitchen before others wake. Some keep their Bible, notebook and pen there, ready each morning. Having a place helps the habit.

**A simple structure.** Use the five elements above, or something like this: two minutes of stillness, three minutes of worship, ten minutes of Scripture and prayer, and a moment of listening. Follow a reading plan so you do not waste time wondering what to read.

**When it feels dry.** Every believer has mornings when the Bible seems flat and prayer feels like talking to the ceiling. Do not be alarmed. Feelings come and go; God's presence does not. The psalmist knew this. "God, You are my God, and I search hard for You. My whole self thirsts for You; my body aches for You in a dry and worn-out land with no water" (Psalm 63:1). Dryness itself can become a prayer.

Hudson Taylor, the pioneer missionary to inland China, knew such mornings. He tells in *Separation and Service* of a night spent in a wicked town in the province of Cheh-kiang, where his heart was oppressed and could find no relief. He woke the next morning "much cast down, and feeling spiritually hungry and thirsty indeed." His regular reading that day was Numbers 7, a long chapter of repeated lists of offerings. He was tempted to skip it for something more comforting. But he decided to keep to his plan, praying God to bless him even through Numbers 7. "I fear there was not much faith in the prayer," he wrote, "but oh! how abundantly it was answered, and what a feast God gave me!" The chapter he had dreaded became one of the most precious in the Bible to him.

The lesson is simple. Keep coming. On dry mornings, read anyway, pray anyway, and leave the results to God. Faithfulness in the ordinary days prepares us for the feasts.

**With others.** The morning watch is personal, but it need not be lonely. Husbands and wives can share a few minutes of Scripture and prayer before the day begins. Parents can gather children for a short reading and prayer at breakfast, as Susanna Wesley had her children read a psalm and a chapter of the Bible each morning. Some friends agree to read the same passage each day and share by message what God showed them. Shared habits are easier to keep, and they teach the next generation that a day begins with God.

**When you miss a day.** You will. Illness, travel, a crying baby or a late night will interrupt the pattern. Do not give up, and do not punish yourself. Simply begin again the next morning. His mercies are new each morning, and that includes the morning after you missed.

### Grace, not legalism

Here we must be careful. The morning watch is a gift, not a law. It is an invitation from a Father who wants to meet His children, not a rule to earn His approval. God does not love you more on the days you keep it, or less on the days you miss it. In Christ you are already accepted.

Hudson Taylor urged believers to treasure this time. In *Union and Communion* he wrote:

> "There is no time so profitably spent as the early hour given to JESUS only. Do we give sufficient attention to this hour? If possible, it should be redeemed; nothing can make up for it."
> Hudson Taylor, *Union and Communion*

Notice his words "if possible." Taylor was not laying a burden on anyone. He was pointing to a treasure. When the morning watch becomes a box to tick, it loses its life. When it becomes a meeting with the One we love, it becomes the best part of the day.

Andrew Murray, the South African pastor, reminds us of its true purpose. In *The Inner Chamber* he warns against treating the quiet time as the goal in itself:

> "The morning watch must not be regarded as an end in itself. It is not sufficient that it gives us a blessed time for prayer and Bible study, and so brings us a certain measure of refreshment and help. It is to serve as a means to an end. And that end is to secure the presence of Christ for the whole day."
> Andrew Murray, *The Inner Chamber*

That is the heart of the matter. We do not meet God in the morning so that we can leave Him there and go about our business alone. We meet Him in the morning so that we can walk with Him all day. The morning watch is the door into a day lived coram Deo, before His face, from the first hour to the last.

How do we carry His presence from the quiet of the morning into the noise of the day? Jesus answers that question with a picture of a vine and its branches, and that is where Chapter 9 will take us.

### Questions for reflection

- What usually fills the first minutes of your day? How does that shape the rest of it?
- Jesus rose early to pray after His busiest day (Mark 1:35). What does that teach you about your own need for time with God?
- Susanna Wesley found her inner room under an apron. Where could your inner room be?
- Which of the five elements (stillness, worship, Scripture, prayer, listening) do you most need to add to your time with God?
- How do you respond to dry mornings? What can you learn from Hudson Taylor's experience with Numbers 7?
- Does the morning watch feel to you more like a gift or a rule? Why?

### A step for this week

Set your alarm fifteen minutes earlier for the next six days, and choose one place where you will meet God each morning. Each day, follow this simple pattern: one minute of stillness, praying "Lord, here I am before Your face"; a few minutes of worship, reading aloud Psalm 100 or another psalm of praise; a chapter of Scripture, read slowly; and prayer that grows out of it, ending with a sentence of surrender such as "This day is Yours." Write one line in a notebook each morning about what God showed you. If you miss a day, simply begin again the next.
