## Jesus Christ, Our Redeemer

> "For God's love for the world was such that He gave His one and only Son, so that everyone who believes in Him may not perish but have eternal life."
> — John 3:16 (OWB)

In Chapter 1 we saw that God is holy, almighty and good, and that He made us to know Him as our Father. But we also saw the problem. We are not holy. Like the first man and woman in the garden, we have hidden from God's face. How can people like us come near to a God like Him?

The answer is not a method or a set of rules. It is a person: Jesus Christ. In Him the face of God has come close to us, and through Him we are brought home. This chapter tells the story of how that happened, and what it means for you today.

### Made for the garden

The Bible's story begins in a garden. God placed the man and the woman in Eden, gave them good work to do, and walked with them. There was no shame, no fear and no distance. They lived openly before the face of God, and it was a joy, not a burden.

This is what we were made for. Not merely to exist, or to work, or to be religious, but to live in friendship with God. Every longing you have ever felt for a home where you are fully known and fully loved is an echo of Eden.

### The fall: hiding from His face

Then everything went wrong. The serpent tempted the woman to doubt God's word and God's goodness. She ate the fruit God had forbidden, gave some to her husband, and he ate too. Notice what happened next: "Then they heard the sound of the LORD God walking in the garden at the breezy time of the day, and the man and his wife hid from the LORD God among the trees of the garden" (Genesis 3:8).

The people who had walked with God now hid from Him. When God called, "Where are you?" (Genesis 3:9), Adam answered, "I was afraid because I was naked, so I hid" (Genesis 3:10). Fear, shame and blame entered the world. Adam blamed the woman. The woman blamed the serpent. Relationships with God, with one another and with the earth itself were broken.

This is what the Bible calls sin. Sin is not just breaking rules. It is turning away from God's face to go our own way. Isaiah puts it simply: "Every one of us has wandered off like sheep; we have each turned aside down our own path" (Isaiah 53:6). We see the results everywhere: in broken marriages, corrupt governments, tribal hatred, greed in the market, and the quiet selfishness of our own hearts. Worst of all, sin separates us from God, the source of life. Death came into the world with it (Romans 5:12).

### The promise of a Redeemer

Yet even in that dark moment, God made a promise. Speaking to the serpent, He said that a child of the woman would one day crush it: "He will strike your head, and you will strike his heel" (Genesis 3:15). Christians have long seen this as the first announcement of the gospel. One day a Redeemer would come from the human family, and in defeating the enemy He would Himself be wounded.

The rest of the Old Testament builds on that promise. God called Abraham and promised that through his family all nations would be blessed. He rescued Israel from slavery in Egypt through the blood of the Passover lamb. He promised David a son whose kingdom would never end. Isaiah spoke of a child called Immanuel, a name that means God with us (Isaiah 7:14). And Isaiah described a suffering Servant who would carry the sins of others: "it was for our rebellions that He was pierced through, for our guilt that He was crushed. On Him fell the discipline that won our peace, and His welts have brought us healing" (Isaiah 53:5).

For centuries God's people waited. Then, "when the time God had set was complete, God sent out His Son" (Galatians 4:4).

### The Word became flesh

John's Gospel opens with words that echo Genesis: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1). The Word is the eternal Son of God, the second person of the Trinity. He did not begin to exist in Bethlehem. He has always been with the Father, sharing His glory. Then John writes the most astonishing sentence ever written: "The Word became flesh and made His home among us" (John 1:14).

This is what Christians call the incarnation, which means "coming in the flesh". God the Son became a real human being. He was born as a baby, learned to walk and talk, worked with His hands as a builder, grew tired and hungry, wept at a friend's grave and felt the pain of nails.

Paul describes this in his letter to the Philippians. Christ, "being in the form of God, did not treat being equal with God as something to exploit for Himself. Rather, He emptied Himself, taking on the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:6-7). What did He empty Himself of? Not His deity. He never stopped being God. He laid aside the privileges and glory of heaven, and chose the life of a servant. He was fully God and fully man, at the same time, in one person.

Why does this matter? Because only one who is truly God can save us, and only one who is truly human can stand in our place. If Jesus were only a good man, He could not carry the sins of the world. If He were only God dressed up as a man, He could not die our death. He is both, and so He is the bridge between God and us.

### Born of a virgin, the sinless second Adam

The Bible tells us how the Son came into the world. The angel Gabriel was sent to a young woman in Nazareth named Mary. When she asked how she could have a child when she had never been with a man, the angel answered, "The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will cover you with its shadow. So the child to be born will be holy, and will be called the Son of God" (Luke 1:35). Matthew sees in this the fulfilment of Isaiah's promise: "See, the virgin will become pregnant and have a son, and people will call Him Immanuel" (Matthew 1:23).

Paul helps us see why this matters. He compares Adam and Christ. Adam was the head of the human race, and through him sin and death spread to all. Christ came as the head of a new humanity: "For the disobedience of one man made the many sinners, and in the same way the obedience of the one will make the many righteous" (Romans 5:19). Paul even calls Jesus "the last Adam" (1 Corinthians 15:45). Where the first Adam fell, the second Adam stood firm.

So Jesus was fully human, yet without sin. The letter to the Hebrews says He "has been put to the test in every way, as we are, but without sinning" (Hebrews 4:15). He knows what temptation feels like. He knows hunger, loneliness, betrayal and grief. But He never once turned from His Father's face.

Christians have explained the link between the virgin birth and Jesus' sinlessness in slightly different ways. Some point to the absence of a human father: Jesus did not come into the world simply as one more child in Adam's line. Others put the weight on the Holy Spirit's sanctifying work in His conception, the Spirit coming upon Mary so that the child would be holy. Both views agree on what matters most. Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of Mary, truly one of us and yet free from sin, so that He could be the Saviour we need.

### Athanasius: standing for the Son

In the fourth century, the Church faced a crisis over exactly this question: who is Jesus? A popular teacher in Alexandria, Egypt, named Arius taught that the Son of God was a created being, very great but not truly God. His ideas spread quickly. They were even said to have been put into simple songs that sailors, traders and dock workers sang around the Mediterranean.

Standing against him was a young man from the same city, Athanasius. Born around the end of the third century, he grew up in Alexandria, one of the great cities of Africa, and served as an assistant to its bishop, Alexander. In 325 he went with Alexander to the Council of Nicaea, where church leaders from across the empire confessed that the Son is truly God, of one being with the Father. A few years later Athanasius himself became bishop of Alexandria.

The struggle was far from over. Emperors changed their minds, and powerful men took Arius's side. Athanasius was driven from his city into exile five times. He spent years hiding, travelling and writing, sometimes sheltered by the desert monks of Egypt. People came to speak of "Athanasius against the world". He would not give way, because he knew that if Jesus is not truly God, then we are not truly saved.

Athanasius wrote a short book, *On the Incarnation*, that is still read today. In it he explained why the eternal Word took a human body. Humanity had turned from God and was sliding into ruin and death. The Word came, not because He had been far away, for He fills all creation, but to show His loving-kindness and to rescue us. He used a picture everyone in his day could understand:

> "And like as when a great king has entered into some large city and taken up his abode in one of the houses there, such city is at all events held worthy of high honour, nor does any enemy or bandit any longer descend upon it and subject it; but, on the contrary, it is thought entitled to all care, because of the king's having taken up his residence in a single house there: so, too, has it been with the Monarch of all."
> Athanasius, *On the Incarnation*

Imagine a poor neighbourhood where a king comes to live. The thieves stay away. The roads are repaired. Everyone in that place is honoured because the king lives among them. Athanasius said that is what happened when the Son of God took on our humanity. He came to live in our "city", and the enemy's hold over us was broken.

Athanasius died in 373, back in Alexandria. Within a few years, at the Council of Constantinople in 381, the Church reaffirmed the faith he had defended. When you recite the Nicene Creed, or simply confess that Jesus is Lord and God, you are standing where this African bishop stood.

### The cross: He took our place

Jesus lived a perfect life, taught with authority, healed the sick and welcomed sinners. Then He went willingly to the cross. Why?

The Bible's answer is that He died in our place. "Christ had never known sin, yet for our sake God made Him to be sin, so that, united with Him, we might become God's righteousness" (2 Corinthians 5:21). This is called substitution: Jesus stood where we should have stood and received what we deserved. It is also called atonement, which means making us "at one" with God again by dealing with our sin. "Christ died for our sins, in keeping with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3).

Remember from Chapter 1 that God is both just and loving. At the cross both are fully shown. God did not pretend that sin did not matter. The price was paid in full. But He paid it Himself, in the person of His Son. "But God proves how much He loves us: Christ died for us when we were still sinners" (Romans 5:8). Not when we were good. When we were still sinners.

In the eleventh century, Anselm, an Italian-born monk who became archbishop of Canterbury in England, wrote a book called *Cur Deus Homo*, which means "Why God Became Man". In it he and his student Boso talk through why only Jesus could save us. Humanity owed a debt to God that only a human being ought to pay, yet only God was able to pay it. Anselm concludes:

> "If it be necessary, therefore, as it appears, that the heavenly kingdom be made up of men, and this cannot be effected unless the aforesaid satisfaction be made, which none but God can make and none but man ought to make, it is necessary for the God-man to make it."
> Anselm of Canterbury, *Proslogium, Monologium and Cur Deus Homo*

Here again we see why the incarnation and the cross belong together. The Son became man so that He could die for us.

### Risen and ascended

The story does not end at the tomb. On the third day, God raised Jesus from the dead. "But as it is, Christ has been raised from the dead: He is the firstfruits of those who have died" (1 Corinthians 15:20). Firstfruits are the first part of the harvest, the promise that the rest is coming. Because Jesus rose, all who belong to Him will rise too.

The resurrection is God's public declaration that the price has been paid and that sin and death have been defeated. Forty days later Jesus ascended to heaven, where He reigns at the Father's right hand. God has "freely given Him the name that is above every name" (Philippians 2:9). One day every knee will bow to Him.

### How to receive Him

All this is wonderful news, but news must be received. Jesus began His preaching with these words: "Repent, and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15).

**Repentance** means turning around. It is more than feeling sorry. It means turning away from sin and from running our own lives, and turning towards God.

**Faith** means trusting Christ alone. Not Christ plus our good works, our church attendance or our family background. Faith is like stepping into a boat: you stop trusting your own swimming and put your whole weight on the boat. Paul writes, "If you declare with your lips that Jesus is Lord, and in your heart believe that God raised Him from the dead, then you will be saved" (Romans 10:9). And he makes clear that salvation is a gift: "For it is by this grace that you have been saved, through faith. None of this comes from you; it is God's gift" (Ephesians 2:8).

When we believe, God does three great things for us:

- **Justification.** God declares us righteous, not because we are good, but because Christ's righteousness is counted as ours.
- **Regeneration.** The Holy Spirit gives us new life. Jesus called this being "born from above" (John 3:7), and we will look at it more in Chapter 3.
- **Union with Christ.** We are joined to Jesus, so that what is His becomes ours.

Martin Luther, the German Reformer, rediscovered the joy of justification by faith after years of trying to earn God's favour as a monk. In his small book *Concerning Christian Liberty* he described union with Christ as a marriage, in which the bride and bridegroom share everything:

> "Christ is full of grace, life, and salvation; the soul is full of sin, death, and condemnation. Let faith step in, and then sin, death, and hell will belong to Christ, and grace, life, and salvation to the soul."
> Martin Luther, *First Principles of the Reformation*

If you have never received Christ, you can do so now. Tell God honestly that you have sinned and turned from His face. Thank Him that Jesus died for you and rose again. Ask Him to forgive you, and put your trust in Jesus as your Saviour and Lord. Then tell another Christian, and find a church where you can grow.

If you have trusted Christ, you can be sure you belong to Him. John wrote his letter "so that you may know that you have eternal life—you who believe in the name of the Son of God" (1 John 5:13). Your assurance rests not on your feelings, which change, but on God's promise, which does not.

### Adopted into God's family

Salvation is not only rescue from something. It is welcome into something. "But to all who received Him, who believed in His name, He gave the right to become children of God" (John 1:12). God does not merely forgive us and send us away. He brings us into His family.

In the Roman world, an adopted son received his new father's name and full rights as heir. Paul uses that picture: "So you are no longer a slave but a child, and if you are a child, then you are also an heir, made so by God" (Galatians 4:7). Many people carry wounds from their earthly fathers, from absence, harshness or neglect. In Christ, you have a Father who will never leave you.

This also changes who we belong to. "You were bought, and a price was paid for you. So bring glory to God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:20). Your body, time, money, gifts and relationships are no longer simply your own. You belong to the One who paid for you with His own blood. That is not a heavy chain. It is the deepest security a person can know.

Think about what this means for daily life coram Deo. You no longer stand before God's face as a criminal before a judge, or as a servant afraid of his master. You stand as a beloved child before a Father. You can wake each morning, go to work, sit with your family and face trouble knowing that you are accepted, forgiven and loved.

### Sent out as His family

Before Jesus returned to the Father, He gathered His followers and gave them a task: "Go, then, and make disciples of all the nations: baptize them into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teach them to keep everything I have commanded you. And remember this: I Myself am with you every day, until the close of the age" (Matthew 28:19-20).

The family of God is not a closed club. The redeemed are sent to bring others home. From a small group in Jerusalem, the good news has travelled to every continent, and today some of the fastest-growing churches in the world are in Africa, Asia and Latin America.

But Jesus also knew that His followers could not do this in their own strength. So He told them to wait for the gift His Father had promised. That gift is the Holy Spirit, and He is the subject of Chapter 3.

### Questions for reflection

- Adam and Eve hid from God after they sinned. In what ways do people, including you, still hide from God today?
- Why does it matter that Jesus is both fully God and fully man? What would we lose if He were only one or the other?
- Athanasius would not give way even when emperors and church leaders were against him. What truths about Jesus are under pressure where you live?
- Read 2 Corinthians 5:21 slowly. Put it into your own words, using "I" and "me".
- Have you repented and put your trust in Christ alone? If so, what gives you assurance?
- How might knowing that you are an adopted child of God change the way you think about yourself this week?

### A step for this week

Write out your own short testimony, no more than one page, in three parts: what your life was like before you knew Christ (or, if you grew up in the faith, when the gospel became real to you), how you came to trust Him, and what difference He makes now. Use simple words, as if you were telling a neighbour over a cup of tea. Then, before the week ends, read it aloud to one other Christian, and thank God together for bringing you out of hiding and into His family.
