## Finishing Well: Perseverance and Legacy

> "I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith. Now there is waiting for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will give me on that day."
> — 2 Timothy 4:7–8 (OWB)

Anyone who has watched a long-distance race knows that the start is easy to watch and the finish is hard to run. At the starting line everyone is fresh. The crowd cheers, the runners bounce on their toes and the first lap feels light. But somewhere in the middle of the race the legs grow heavy, the crowd thins and the finish line is still out of sight. That is where races are won and lost.

The Christian life is like that. Many begin with great joy. Fewer finish with the same fire. Some fall away openly. More simply drift: they do not deny Christ, but they slowly stop seeking His face. Prayer becomes a habit without warmth, Scripture a book on a shelf, church a duty.

Paul wrote the words above from a Roman prison, near the end of his life. He knew he was about to die, and he could look back without regret. He had not been perfect, but he had been faithful. He had fought, he had finished, he had kept the faith.

This chapter asks how we can do the same. How can we keep running when the legs are heavy, bear fruit when the body is weak, and pass the faith on to those who come after us? The answer is not a new technique. It is the same truth that runs through this whole book: we live before the face of God, and He is the one who holds us to the end.

### Running the race with perseverance

The letter to the Hebrews gives the clearest picture: "So then, since we too have so vast a cloud of witnesses all around us, let us strip off every burden, and the sin that wraps itself around us so readily, and let us run the race laid out ahead of us, and run it with endurance" (Hebrews 12:1).

Notice four things in this verse and the one that follows.

First, we are not alone. The "cloud of witnesses" are the men and women of faith in Hebrews 11, and all who have run before us. They are like a stadium full of former champions, not watching to judge us but showing by their lives that the race can be finished.

Second, we must travel light. A runner does not carry a sack of maize on his back. Some burdens are not sinful in themselves, but they slow us down: too many commitments, love of comfort, the endless pull of the phone. Other weights are plainly sin, the kind that "wraps itself around us so readily," like a long cloth catching around a runner's legs. Both must be stripped off.

Third, the race is "laid out ahead of us." We do not choose the course. God marks it out, with its hills and its straight roads, its seasons of plenty and of loss. Our task is to run the course He has set, not the one we wish for.

Fourth, and most important: "Let us keep our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer who leads the way in faith and brings it to perfection. For the sake of the joy that lay ahead of Him, He endured a cross and counted its shame as nothing" (Hebrews 12:2). Jesus is both the one who has run ahead of us and the one who will bring us home. When we look at our own strength, we will grow tired. When we look at Him, we find strength to continue: "Weigh up what He endured... Then you will not wear out and let your spirits give way" (Hebrews 12:3).

Paul knew this kind of running. To the elders of Ephesus he said: "All I want is to run my course to the end and carry out the ministry I received from the Lord Jesus: to bear full witness to the gospel of the grace of God" (Acts 20:24). And to the Philippians he wrote: "I put out of mind what is behind me and stretch out for what is in front of me, and I run straight for the goal to win the prize promised by God's upward call in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 3:13–14). Even near the end of his life, Paul did not think he had arrived. He kept pressing on.

### Polycarp: eighty-six years of faithfulness

Some time in the middle of the second century, in the city of Smyrna on the coast of what is now western Turkey, an old man was hunted by the authorities. His name was Polycarp, and he was the bishop of the church there. Irenaeus, who knew him as a young man, later wrote that Polycarp had been taught by apostles and had known John, the beloved disciple. He was a living link between the first generation of believers and the growing church of the second century. Smyrna is one of the seven churches addressed in Revelation, and the risen Christ had said to it: "Stay faithful even to death, and I will give you the crown of life" (Revelation 2:10).

The account of what happened next is one of the oldest Christian writings outside the New Testament, and it is in the Ochorus library as *The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp*. Friends persuaded Polycarp to leave the city, and he stayed at a country house nearby, where, the account says, he spent night and day praying for all people and for the churches throughout the world. But when the soldiers finally came to the house where he was staying, he refused to escape, saying, "The will of God be done." Then he did something remarkable. He ordered food and drink to be set before the men who had come to arrest him, and asked only for an hour to pray. The account says that "he stood and prayed, being full of the grace of God, so that he could not cease for two full hours, to the astonishment of them that heard him."

He was taken into the city and brought before the Roman governor in the crowded stadium. The governor tried to spare the old man. "Have respect to thy old age," he said. All Polycarp had to do was swear by the emperor's fortune and curse Christ, and he would be set free. Polycarp's answer has been remembered for nearly two thousand years:

> "Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?"
> *The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp*

The governor threatened wild beasts, then fire. Polycarp replied that the governor's fire "burneth for an hour, and after a little is extinguished," but that there is a judgment to come that matters far more. He was put to death in the stadium that day.

What strikes us most about Polycarp's last hours is not a sudden burst of courage. It is that his death matched his life. A man who had prayed for eighty-six years prayed for two hours before his arrest. A man who had served Christ for eighty-six years would not betray Him at the end. Faithfulness at the finish line was the fruit of faithfulness on every ordinary day before it. That is the secret of finishing well. We do not suddenly become faithful in the last lap. We become faithful in the thousand small choices of the middle of the race.

In his own letter to the church at Philippi, also in that library volume, Polycarp had urged believers: "Let us then continually persevere in our hope, and the earnest of our righteousness, which is Jesus Christ... Let us then be imitators of His patience." He lived what he wrote.

### Growing in faithfulness through every season

Life comes in seasons. There is the fresh zeal of new faith, the busy years of building a family or a career, the middle years when responsibilities pile high, and the later years when strength begins to fade. Each season has its own dangers.

The young believer may be tempted to think that early enthusiasm will carry him all the way. It will not. Feelings rise and fall. What carries us is the daily habit of meeting God, which Part Two of this book described: Scripture, prayer, the morning watch and abiding in Christ.

The person in the middle years faces a different danger: busyness and drift. Work, children, school fees, church responsibilities and aging parents crowd every hour. Many believers do not fall in a dramatic sin but in a slow cooling. They are too busy to pray and too tired to read. The cure is not to add more activity but to guard the centre. Five quiet minutes before the face of God each morning can keep a heart alive through years of pressure.

And what if you have already stumbled badly? Finishing well does not require a perfect record. Peter denied Jesus three times on the night of His arrest, yet the risen Lord met him by the lake, asked him three times, "Do you love Me?" and gave him work to do (John 21:15–19). Peter went on to feed Christ's sheep for the rest of his life. A race can be resumed. If you have fallen, do not lie on the track. Confess your sin, receive His forgiveness, get up and run again. God is far more interested in where you finish than in how many times you fell.

Success brings its own temptation. Some of the saddest falls in church history have come not in times of trouble but at the height of influence, when a leader stopped being accountable and began to believe his own reputation. Finishing well requires humility and honest friends who will ask hard questions.

Paul gives a word for every season: "So then, my dear brothers and sisters, be firm and unshakable, always doing more and more in the work of the Lord, since you know that in the Lord your hard work is never wasted" (1 Corinthians 15:58). Never wasted. Not the prayers that seemed unanswered, not the Sunday school class that seemed restless, not the years of caring for a sick relative. Nothing done for Christ is lost.

Some are called to be faithful under great pressure. Janani Luwum grew up in the Acholi region of northern Uganda, became a teacher, and came to living faith through the East African Revival. He rose to become Archbishop of the Church of Uganda in the dark years of Idi Amin's rule. When soldiers and agents of the government were killing ordinary Ugandans, Luwum and other bishops spoke out and protested to the president. In February 1977 he was arrested, and he was killed in custody. The government claimed he had died in a car accident, but few believed it. Luwum did not seek death, but he would not keep quiet to save his life. Like Polycarp, he finished faithful, and his witness has strengthened believers across East Africa ever since.

### Bearing fruit in old age, illness and decline

One of the most beautiful promises in the Psalms is for those whose strength is fading: "The righteous will thrive like the palm tree and rise tall as a cedar on Lebanon. Rooted in the LORD's house, they will bloom in the courtyards of our God. Even when they are old they will still bear fruit; they will stay rich with sap and green" (Psalm 92:12–14).

Many cultures honour the old, and many of our readers come from places where grandparents are respected. Yet even there, the elderly can feel useless when they can no longer farm, work or travel. Illness can bring the same feeling at any age. A person who once led a choir or ran a business may now spend most days in a chair.

But God's Word says the righteous "will still bear fruit." The fruit may look different. It may be prayer instead of preaching, wisdom instead of labour, patience instead of activity. An old woman who prays daily for each of her grandchildren by name is bearing fruit. A sick man who receives visitors with peace and speaks of Christ's faithfulness is bearing fruit. Paul knew that "Our outer self may be decaying, but our inner self is being made new day after day" (2 Corinthians 4:16).

God also gives a promise to carry us when we cannot carry ourselves: "Until you are old I am He, and until your hair turns gray I am the one who will carry you" (Isaiah 46:4). The same God who held us as newborn believers will hold us at the end. Perseverance is not only our holding on to Him. It is first His holding on to us.

### Leaving a spiritual legacy

Finishing well is not only about our own race. It is also about the runners who come after us. In a relay race, the most important moment is the passing of the baton. A team can run brilliantly and still lose if the baton is dropped.

Paul's final letter, written to his young friend Timothy, is full of this concern. "And what you heard from me before many witnesses, pass on to trustworthy people who will be able to teach others as well" (2 Timothy 2:2). Count the generations in that one verse: Paul, Timothy, trustworthy people, and others. Four links in a chain. That chain has continued for two thousand years, and it has reached you.

Polycarp himself shows how this works. He learned from apostles. He taught Irenaeus, who went on to serve the church in Gaul, in what is now France, and to write against the false teachings of his day. The faith passed from John to Polycarp to Irenaeus, and onwards to us. The psalmist prayed for the same thing: "Now that I am old and gray, God, do not abandon me, not until I have told the generation after me what Your arm has done" (Psalm 71:18).

John Bunyan, the tinker and preacher who spent twelve years in prison for his faith, painted this in the second part of *The Pilgrim's Progress*. When the old warrior Mr. Valiant-for-truth receives his summons to cross the river of death, he gathers his friends and says:

> "My sword I give to him that shall succeed me in my pilgrimage, and my courage and skill to him that can get it. My marks and scars I carry with me, to be a witness for me that I have fought His battles who will now be my rewarder."
> John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*

His sword and his skill he leaves behind for others. His scars he takes with him. That is a picture of a spiritual legacy.

What will you hand on? Most of us will not leave large sums of money or famous books. But every one of us can leave something more lasting: a faith that has been seen, tested and shared. Parents can pass on a daily habit of prayer and Scripture to their children (see Chapter 18). Older believers can take a younger believer under their wing, meet regularly, open the Bible together and pray. A farmer can teach a young neighbour both how to plant and how to trust God for the rain. A teacher can model honesty that students remember for life.

A legacy is built less by grand plans than by consistent presence. The young watch how we handle money, how we speak of our spouses, how we respond to disappointment, whether we still pray when life is hard. They learn more from a lifetime of quiet faithfulness than from any sermon.

### Looking ahead: the hope that keeps us running

Why do runners keep going when every muscle cries out to stop? Because they can see the finish line, or at least they know it is there.

Paul could face death calmly because he knew what lay beyond it: "Now there is waiting for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will give me on that day, and not only to me but also to all who have longed for His appearing" (2 Timothy 4:8). Notice the last words. The crown is not only for apostles. It is for all who long for Christ's appearing. If you love Him and long to see Him, that crown is waiting for you too.

This hope is not escapism. It is fuel. A runner who knows the prize is real will train harder, not less. A believer who knows that heaven is real will live more faithfully on earth. Polycarp could face the fire because he knew a greater judgment and a greater glory were coming. Luwum could face the soldiers because he knew whose face he would see next.

What exactly is that hope? What happens when we die, what will the judgment be like, and what will the new heavens and new earth hold? Those are the questions of Chapter 27, which explores the hope of glory fully. For now it is enough to know that the race has a finish line, and at the finish line is a Person. The One whose face we have sought every morning will be the One who welcomes us home.

### Questions for reflection

- Which season of life are you in now, and what particular danger to faithfulness does it bring?
- What "burdens" or entangling sins slow you down in your race, and which one could you strip off this month?
- Polycarp's faithful death grew out of eighty-six years of faithful days. What small daily habits are shaping how you will finish?
- If you are older or unwell, what fruit might God want you to bear in this season? If you are younger, which older believer could you learn from?
- Who has passed the faith on to you? Who are you passing it on to now?
- How does the hope of "the crown of righteousness" change the way you face hardship today?

### A step for this week

This week, choose one person younger in the faith than you, whether a child, a new believer, a student or a colleague, and arrange to meet with them. Open a passage such as Hebrews 12:1–3 or 2 Timothy 2:1–2 together, talk honestly about how you keep going in your walk with God, and pray for each other. If you are the younger believer, do the opposite: seek out an older Christian whose faithfulness you admire and ask them to tell you how they have kept running. Either way, before the week ends, write down the name of that person and commit to praying for them by name every day for the next month.
