## Stewardship: God's Managers of Time, Talents and Treasure

> "The earth is the LORD's, and all that fills it, the world and everyone who lives there."
> — Psalm 24:1 (OWB)

Imagine a man who manages a large tea estate in the hills. He drives the estate lorry, holds the keys to the store and hires the pickers. To the village below, he looks like a wealthy man. But he knows that the lorry, the land and the keys are not his. At the end of every month the owner's accountant sits down with him and goes through the books, line by line.

A good manager is not crushed by this. He is proud to be trusted, and he looks after the estate carefully precisely because it is not his. A bad manager forgets whose estate it is, sells a few sacks from the store, and tells himself that nobody will notice.

The Bible's word for the good manager is a steward: someone who looks after what belongs to another. Every one of us is a steward. Our money, our time, our abilities, our bodies, our families, our influence and even the gospel itself have been placed in our hands by God. We do not own them. We manage them, and we manage them before His face.

That is what it means to live coram Deo with our possessions. The question is not only, "What do I have?" but, "Whose is it, and what does He want done with it?"

### Everything belongs to God

The psalmist begins with a sweeping claim: "The earth is the LORD's, and all that fills it, the world and everyone who lives there" (Psalm 24:1). Not most of the earth, nor the parts we give to church. All of it.

God owns everything because He made everything. So every field, shop, bank account and pair of hands belongs to Him first. Gathering an offering for the temple, King David prayed, "Everything comes from You; what we have given You came from Your own hand" (1 Chronicles 29:14). Even a king's gift only gives God back what was already His.

This truth is both humbling and freeing. It removes our right to say, "This is mine, and I will do what I like with it." And it lifts a heavy weight: the owner is responsible for the estate; the manager is responsible only to be faithful. Stewardship does not remove the need for careful planning, but it changes the centre of our trust. The Owner does not abandon His own.

### Managers, not owners

This calling goes back to the very beginning. When God made the man and the woman, He gave them a task: "Be fruitful and multiply. Fill the earth and subdue it. Rule over the fish in the sea, the birds in the sky, and every living creature that moves along the ground" (Genesis 1:28). Then we read that "The LORD God took the man and settled him in the garden of Eden to work it and take care of it" (Genesis 2:15).

Those two verses belong together. Humanity was given authority, but on God's behalf. Adam was not the landlord of Eden. He was its gardener.

Sin turned this upside down. The fall was, among other things, a manager deciding he would rather be the owner. Much of the world's misery flows from that grab: land taken by force, workers cheated, rivers poisoned, families divided over inheritance.

In Christ, God restores us to our first calling. Paul describes Christian leaders in exactly these terms: "as assistants of Christ and as household managers put in charge of the mysteries of God" (1 Corinthians 4:1). Peter widens it to every believer: "Each of you has received a gift; use it to serve one another, as good managers of the richly varied grace of God" (1 Peter 4:10).

### What Jesus taught: faithful in little

In the parable of the talents, a man going on a journey "summoned his own servants and handed his property over to them" (Matthew 25:14). He gave five bags of silver to one, two to another and one to a third, "according to what each could manage" (Matthew 25:15). The first two servants went to work and doubled what they were given. The third dug a hole and buried his bag in the ground. When the master returned, he praised the first two with the same words: "Well done! You are a good and faithful servant. You proved faithful in small matters; I will put you in charge of great ones. Come in and share the joy of your master" (Matthew 25:21).

Notice three things. The servants received different amounts, and we are not judged by what someone else was given. The servant with two bags received exactly the same praise as the servant with five: faithfulness, not size, is what counts. And the servant who failed did not steal. He simply did nothing, out of fear. Jesus treats that burying as a serious failure.

Luke records a similar story, the parable of the minas, in which each servant receives the same amount and they produce different results. To the faithful one the king says, "Excellent, good servant!... You have been trustworthy in a very small matter, so you will have authority over ten cities" (Luke 19:17). A very small matter, and ten cities. The proportion is wildly generous. God takes our small faithfulness and answers it with a reward far beyond it.

In the parable of the shrewd manager (Luke 16:1–13), Jesus praises not a dishonest man's cheating but his foresight, and then draws the lesson: "Someone who is faithful in the smallest matter is faithful in large matters too, and someone who is dishonest in the smallest matter is dishonest in large matters too" (Luke 16:10). He ends with a warning that cuts through every excuse: "You cannot serve both God and wealth" (Luke 16:13).

Money is a small matter compared to eternal things, but it is a revealing one. A shopkeeper who gives short weight on a kilo of sugar would not be safer with a million shillings. Faithful in little, faithful in much.

### The day of accounting

Every one of these parables ends with the master's return and a reckoning. "A long time afterward the master of those servants returned and went over the accounts with them" (Matthew 25:19). Paul says: "So each of us will have to give an account of ourselves" (Romans 14:12). And he names the one thing that is required of a manager: "Now what is looked for in household managers is this: that each one proves trustworthy" (1 Corinthians 4:2).

For a believer, this accounting is not about whether we are saved. That was settled at the cross, as Chapter 28 explains. It is about how we used what was placed in our hands. It should not terrify a child of God, but it gives weight to ordinary choices: how you spend your evening, treat your workers or use your salary.

And there is a joy waiting in it. The highest hope of a manager is not a payment but a word from the owner's mouth: "Well done! You are a good and faithful servant." To hear those words from the One whose face we have sought all our lives will be worth every sacrifice. Living coram Deo means living now with that day in view.

### John Wesley: gain, save, give

Few people in Christian history took stewardship more seriously than John Wesley. Born in 1703 in the small town of Epworth in England, he became a scholar and fellow at Oxford University. In 1738, at a meeting in Aldersgate Street in London, he came to trust in Christ alone for salvation, and for the next half century he travelled Britain on horseback, preaching in fields, market squares and mining villages.

Wesley watched the poor working people who came to faith under his preaching stop drinking away their wages and work hard and honestly. Many became far better off. And he noticed a danger: their new prosperity was cooling their love for God.

So Wesley preached a famous sermon on the subject, now in the Ochorus library as *The Use of Money*. He boiled the teaching down to three rules, each of which he explained with care. First, "Gain all you can," by honest work and without harming your own body or soul or your neighbour's. Second, "Save all you can," by refusing to waste money on vanity, show and the desires of the flesh. Third, and this was the whole point of the first two, "give all you can." He explained why:

> "When the Possessor of heaven and earth brought you into being, and placed you in this world, he placed you here not as a proprietor, but a steward: As such he entrusted you, for a season, with goods of various kinds; but the sole property of these still rests in him, nor can be alienated from him."
> John Wesley, *The Use of Money*

Wesley practised what he preached. As a young man at Oxford he fixed the sum he needed to live on, and when his income rose he went on living on about the same amount and gave the rest away. Later in life his books sold widely and brought in large sums. He used the money for the poor, for schools, for medicine and for the spread of the gospel. When he died in 1791, at the age of eighty-seven, he left very little money behind. What he had managed, he had passed on.

Wesley even gave his hearers questions to ask before spending, among them: "Am I acting herein, not as a proprietor, but as a steward of my Lord's goods?" (from *The Use of Money*). That question is as useful in a market today as it was in eighteenth-century England.

### From the tithe to total surrender

How much should a Christian give? Here it helps to see how the Bible's teaching unfolds.

In the Old Testament, Israel was commanded to give a tenth. "All the tithe of the land belongs to the LORD, both grain from what is sown and fruit from the trees. It is holy to the LORD" (Leviticus 27:30). When the people neglected this, the prophet Malachi called them back: "Bring the full tithe into the storehouse, so that there will be food in My house. Put Me to the test in this, says the LORD of Armies, and see if I do not open the windows of heaven for you and pour out on you a blessing until you have more than enough" (Malachi 3:10). The book of Proverbs adds a call to give God the first share, not the leftovers: "Honor the LORD with what you own, with the first and best of everything your land produces" (Proverbs 3:9).

In the New Testament, the standard is not lowered but raised: all we are and all we have belongs to God. Paul urges us to "offer your bodies to God as a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to Him" (Romans 12:1). If our very bodies are offered to God, then a tenth is not the ceiling of our giving. Everything is His.

That is why the New Testament sets no fixed percentage. It points us to the cross. "After all, you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ: rich as He was, He became poor for your sake, so that His poverty might make you rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9). Christian giving is a response to that grace. And Paul is clear that it should be free and glad, not forced: "Each of you should give the amount you have settled on in your own heart, not with regret and not because you feel you must. God loves a giver who gives gladly" (2 Corinthians 9:7).

So what of the tithe? This book's view is this: tithing, and giving beyond it, are good and worthy practices. Many believers find that setting aside a tenth, from the first of their income, is a wise and joyful discipline, and many are led to give far more. But giving is guided by the Spirit and flows from grace and gratitude, not from rules or obligation. A tenth is a fine place to start, but it is neither a fee for God's favour nor a limit on love. Wesley himself put it strongly: give "not a tenth, not a third, not half, but all that is God's, be it more or less" (from *The Use of Money*), meaning that every coin is to be used for God, whether given away or spent wisely on our families and needs.

Two warnings follow. Beware of anyone who promises that if you give to them, God is bound to make you rich. Paul says the blessing of generous giving is that "you will have everything you need, with plenty to spare for every good work" (2 Corinthians 9:8): the plenty is for more good works, not more luxury. And beware of the opposite error: "Since there is no fixed rule, I will give whatever is left over." Freedom in grace should make us more generous than the law, not less.

### Generosity and contentment

Generosity and contentment are twins. You rarely find one without the other.

Paul wrote to Timothy: "Godliness is indeed great gain, when it comes with contentment. For we brought nothing into the world, and we can take nothing out of it. If we have food and clothing, we will be content with that" (1 Timothy 6:6–8). Then: "For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil. Some people, eager for money, have wandered from the faith and pierced themselves with many griefs" (1 Timothy 6:10). Money itself is not called evil; the love of it is, and a poor man can love it as fiercely as a rich one.

To those who do have more, Paul gives a different instruction. They are not to be proud, nor to put their hope in wealth, "but in God, who richly gives us everything to enjoy" (1 Timothy 6:17). Then: "Tell them to do good, to be rich in good deeds, to give generously and to share. In this way they will store up treasure for themselves as a firm foundation for the age to come, so that they may take hold of the life that is truly life" (1 Timothy 6:18–19).

Jesus said the same: "Do not pile up treasures for yourselves on earth, where moths and rust ruin them and thieves dig through walls and steal them. Instead, pile up treasures for yourselves in heaven" (Matthew 6:19–20). And He told us why it matters so much: "Your heart will always be where your treasure is" (Matthew 6:21). If you want your heart to move towards God, move your treasure towards His purposes.

Contentment is not laziness. It is the quiet confidence that God is enough. A mother in a rented room who shares her food with a hungry neighbour may be far richer in God's eyes than a businessman lying awake over his investments.

### Stewarding all of life

Money is only one of the things we hold in trust. Think of the whole estate God has placed in your care.

**Time.** Each of us is given a limited number of days. Moses prayed, "Teach us, then, to reckon our days rightly, so that our hearts may grow wise" (Psalm 90:12). Time wasted is part of our stewardship too. Chapter 8's morning watch is, among other things, a way of giving God the first of our time, as the tithe gave Him the first of the harvest.

**Talents.** Every gift, whether teaching, building, caring for the sick or fixing engines, is a bag of silver placed in our hands. Chapter 23 explores spiritual gifts more fully.

**Our bodies.** Paul writes, "your body is a temple where the Holy Spirit lives... You do not belong to yourselves. You were bought, and a price was paid for you. So bring glory to God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:19–20). Rest, food and sexual purity are matters of stewardship.

**Relationships and influence.** Our families, friendships and whatever influence we have are also entrusted to us. William Wilberforce, the English politician who spent decades fighting to end the British slave trade, saw his place in Parliament in exactly this light. In his book *A Practical View of Christianity*, now in the Ochorus library, he wrote that the Christian "is diligently to cultivate the talents with which God has entrusted him, and assiduously to employ them in doing justice and shewing mercy." The slave trade was abolished by Britain in 1807, and slavery itself in the British colonies just after his death in 1833.

**The gospel and creation.** We have been entrusted with the best news in the world, and keeping it to ourselves would be the worst kind of burying (see Chapter 25). And the command to care for the garden still stands: a farmer who protects his land from erosion for his grandchildren is a good steward.

### Work as worship

All of this brings us to our daily work. Chapter 20 showed that work is a calling from God. Stewardship adds that our work is also a form of worship. Paul tells even slaves, "Whatever you are doing, put your whole self into the work, as for the Lord and not for people, knowing that you will receive from the Lord the inheritance as your reward. It is the Lord Christ you serve" (Colossians 3:23–24).

The Dutch theologian Abraham Kuyper, who was a pastor, a newspaper editor, the founder of the Free University of Amsterdam and, for a time, prime minister of the Netherlands, is remembered for insisting that there is no corner of human life over which Christ does not claim lordship. Science, art, politics, business, education and farming all belong to Him. Kuyper's conviction is simply Psalm 24:1 applied to Monday morning.

So the nurse turning a patient at night, the trader keeping honest scales and the mother washing clothes by the river are doing holy work when they do it for Christ, not because it happens in a church, but because it is done before the face of the Owner, for His glory.

### Living as stewards before His face

A careless manager sweeps the yard only on the day the owner visits. A faithful one works the same every day, because he wants to please him. That is the steward's life coram Deo. God is not a distant owner who visits once a year. He is present in every transaction, every hour and every choice. And He is not a harsh master, as the fearful servant imagined. He is the Father who gave His own Son for us, who "richly gives us everything to enjoy" (1 Timothy 6:17), and who longs to say to each of His children, "Come in and share the joy of your master."

Faithful stewardship is not finished in a day or a year. It is the work of a whole lifetime, which leads us into Chapter 27 and the question of how we can run the race to the end and finish well.

### Questions for reflection

- If someone looked only at your bank statements and your diary for the last month, what would they conclude you treasure most?
- Which of your gifts, abilities or opportunities are you most tempted to "bury in the ground", and why?
- How do you respond to the teaching that the tithe is a good practice but not a fixed rule? Does it make you want to give more or less?
- Wesley asked, "Am I acting herein, not as a proprietor, but as a steward of my Lord's goods?" Where in your spending would that question make the biggest difference?
- What area of stewardship (time, body, relationships, influence, creation) have you thought about least, and what would faithfulness look like there?
- How does knowing that you will one day give an account to a loving Father change the way you see ordinary work?

### A step for this week

This week, sit down with a notebook and write two lists. In the first, list the main things God has placed in your care: your income, your time, your skills, your relationships, your influence and anything you own. In the second, write what you actually did with each of them over the past seven days. Then pray over both lists, thanking God as the Owner of everything. Choose one specific change: perhaps setting aside a first portion of your next income before anything else is spent, giving an hour of your time to someone in need, or using one skill you have neglected. Write it down, do it, and at the end of the week thank God for the joy of being His manager.
