## The Spiritual Disciplines

> "Train yourself in godliness. Physical training has some value, but godliness is valuable in every way, since it holds promise both for life now and for the life to come."
> — 1 Timothy 4:7–8 (OWB)

A young woman in Nairobi decides she will run the city marathon. On the first morning she runs as hard as she can, and after two kilometres she sits down by the road, chest burning, and decides she is simply not a runner. Her neighbour, who has run three marathons, tells her, "You cannot run forty-two kilometres by trying. You run it by training." So she starts again, slowly, three mornings a week. Month by month her body becomes able to do what it could not do before. On race day she is not trying harder. She is a different runner, made ready by many small, quiet mornings.

Paul uses exactly this picture. He tells Timothy to "train yourself in godliness" (1 Timothy 4:7). In the last chapter we saw that the Christian life is not first about effort but about abiding in Christ, the true Vine (see Chapter 9). Branches do not strain to produce grapes. Yet the same Jesus who told us to remain in Him (John 15:4) also went often to lonely places to pray, fasted in the wilderness, and kept the rhythms of worship. Abiding has a shape. The spiritual disciplines are that shape: practices that put us before the face of God again and again.

### Why disciplines matter: training, not trying

A spiritual discipline is a practice that we do, by God's grace, to place ourselves where He can change us. It does not earn His love; in Christ we already have that. A discipline is like a farmer ploughing his field. He cannot make a single seed grow; only God gives life, rain and sun. But the farmer who never ploughs or weeds will have a poor harvest. He works with God, not instead of God.

The American teacher Dallas Willard spent much of his life explaining this. Many Christians, he observed, try to become like Jesus in the moment of crisis: patient when the boss insults them, kind when the child is screaming. And they fail, because they are trying to do by sudden effort what can only be done by training. Jesus' public strength flowed from His hidden habits of solitude, prayer, fasting and Scripture. If we want to live as He lived, we must arrange our lives as He arranged His.

This is grace-filled effort. Paul writes as a runner:

> "Every competitor holds back from many things in training. They accept this to win a wreath that soon withers; we, to win one that never decays." (1 Corinthians 9:25)

Effort is not the enemy of grace; earning is. Peter says we must "put every effort into this: let your faith provide goodness, and goodness knowledge, knowledge self-control, self-control endurance, endurance godliness" (2 Peter 1:5–6). Grace is where we start and what carries us, but it does not make us passive.

Discipline is not always pleasant. "At the time it is given, no discipline seems a matter for joy; it seems a matter for sorrow. But afterward it pays back those who have been trained by it with a harvest of peace" (Hebrews 12:11). The first mornings of rising early may feel hard and dry. The harvest comes later.

The disciplines are often grouped in three families. The inward ones take place mainly in the heart: meditation, prayer, study and fasting. The outward ones shape how we live in the world: simplicity, solitude, silence and submission. The corporate ones we practise together: worship, confession, guidance and celebration. The Quaker writer Richard Foster made a grouping like this well known in *Celebration of Discipline* (1978), helping many Christians see these practices as the ordinary tools of God's people.

### The desert father: Antony of Egypt

To see a disciplined life, we travel to Egypt around the year 270. A young man named Antony, about eighteen or twenty, had lost his parents and been left land and the care of his younger sister. One day, walking to church, he was thinking about how the first Christians had sold their possessions for the needy. Athanasius, the great bishop of Alexandria who later wrote Antony's life, tells what happened next:

> "Pondering over these things he entered the church, and it happened the Gospel was being read, and he heard the Lord saying to the rich man, 'If thou wouldest be perfect, go and sell that thou hast and give to the poor; and come follow Me and thou shalt have treasure in heaven.'"
> Athanasius, *The Life of Antony*

Antony took the words as spoken to him. He gave his family's land to the villagers, sold the rest of his goods and gave the money to the poor, keeping a little for his sister. Soon after, hearing the words "be not anxious for the morrow," he gave away even that and placed his sister in the care of faithful Christian women.

What he did next is important. He did not rush off to be a hero. He went to learn from older believers living simple lives of prayer near the villages, watching each one carefully:

> "He observed the graciousness of one; the unceasing prayer of another; he took knowledge of another's freedom from anger and another's loving-kindness; he gave heed to one as he watched, to another as he studied; one he admired for his endurance, another for his fasting and sleeping on the ground."
> Athanasius, *The Life of Antony*

He was not idle. "He worked, however, with his hands," Athanasius writes, "and part he spent on bread and part he gave to the needy. And he was constant in prayer." Later Antony went further into the desert, first to the tombs and then to an empty fort near the Nile, where he lived alone for about twenty years in prayer, fasting and the Scriptures. When his friends finally broke down the door, they expected a man wasted or broken. Instead they found him healthy, calm and warm: "neither fat, like a man without exercise, nor lean from fasting and striving with the demons." His discipline had not destroyed him. It had made him whole.

The deserts of Egypt filled with men and women who wanted to learn the way of prayer, later known as the Desert Fathers and Mothers. Antony taught them that the battle for the heart is real, but God's weapons are simple. "A good life and faith in God is a great weapon," he told them, and he named the practices the enemy fears: "the fasting, the sleeplessness, the prayers, the meekness, the quietness, the contempt of money and vainglory, the humility, the love of the poor." Fasting and prayer stand side by side with humility and love of the poor. The disciplines were never meant to make anyone proud.

Antony died around the year 356, near the age of one hundred and five. About thirty years later in Milan, a restless young teacher from North Africa heard the story of Antony from a visitor. That teacher was Augustine (see Chapter 2), and the story shook him deeply and played its part in his conversion. One Egyptian's hidden, disciplined life helped to win one of the great teachers of the Church. Antony lived *coram Deo*, before the face of God, in the desert. God saw, and God used it.

You are probably not called to live in a desert. But Antony's story teaches us three things. Disciplines begin with hearing God's Word and obeying it. They are learned from others who are further along. And they are meant to make us more loving, not less.

### The inward disciplines: fasting

Meditation on Scripture and the study of the Bible were taught in Chapter 6, and prayer in Chapters 7 and 8. Here we look closely at the inward discipline that many Christians today have never been taught: fasting.

**What fasting is.** In the Bible, to fast means to go without food, for a time, for a spiritual purpose. It is not a diet, nor a hunger strike to force God's hand. It is turning from food, a good gift, to turn towards God with special focus.

**Fasting in the Bible.** Moses fasted on the mountain. David humbled himself with fasting when others were sick (Psalm 35:13). Ezra proclaimed a fast by the Ahava canal "so that we might humble ourselves before our God and ask Him for a safe journey" (Ezra 8:21). Jesus Himself "fasted forty days and forty nights" before His public ministry began (Matthew 4:2). The church at Antioch was worshipping and fasting when the Holy Spirit called Barnabas and Saul to their mission, and "after more fasting and prayer, they laid their hands on the two men and let them go" (Acts 13:3).

Most important of all, Jesus expected His followers to fast. He did not say *if* you fast, but *when*:

> "When you fast, do not put on a sad face as the hypocrites do. They make their faces look gloomy so that others will see they are fasting. Truly I tell you, they have been paid in full. But when you fast, put oil on your head and wash your face, so that no one but your Father, who is there in secret, will know that you are fasting." (Matthew 6:16–18)

When people asked why His disciples did not fast, He answered that the wedding guests do not mourn while the bridegroom is with them, but "a time will come when the bridegroom will be taken from them, and then they will fast" (Matthew 9:15). We live in that time now.

**Its purposes.** Scripture gives several reasons to fast: to humble ourselves before God, as Ezra did; to seek guidance, as the church at Antioch did; to express repentance; to strengthen prayer in times of great need; and to remind our bodies that we do not live by bread alone. Every pang of hunger becomes a bell calling us back to God.

John Wesley, who fasted regularly for most of his life, warned against making fasting everything or nothing:

> "It is not all, nor yet is it nothing. It is not the end, but it is a precious means thereto; a means which God himself has ordained, and in which therefore, when it is duly used, he will surely give us his blessing."
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

**Its motives.** Jesus' warning in Matthew 6 is about motive: the danger is fasting to be seen or to win a prize from God. Through Isaiah, God rebuked people who fasted while oppressing their workers. The fast He chooses is to "loose the bonds that wickedness has tied" and to "break your bread with someone who is hungry" (Isaiah 58:6–7). A fast that leaves us proud or harsh has missed the point. Give away the money you would have spent on food, and your hunger becomes someone else's meal.

**Its forms.** The Bible and the history of the Church show several kinds of fast:

- A normal fast: going without all food, but drinking water. Most fasts in the Bible were of this kind, often from morning until evening.
- A partial fast: going without certain foods, as Daniel and his friends ate only vegetables and water, or eating one simple meal in a day.
- A longer fast: several days, rarely and with care.
- A shared fast: a church or a family fasting together for a particular need, as the people did in the days of Ezra and Esther.

Many early Christians fasted on Wednesdays and Fridays until the afternoon. Wesley urged the same, telling those whose work or health would not allow it to fast "as often as thy strength will permit."

**Preparation.** Do not begin with a long fast. Begin by missing one meal and spending that time with God, then perhaps try a day from one evening meal to the next. Decide beforehand what you are fasting for, and write it down. Plan your time of prayer and Scripture. Tell only those who need to know, such as your husband or wife, or whoever cooks for the household. End the fast with something small and simple.

**Sensible health precautions.** God gave us our bodies, and fasting must never harm them. Drink plenty of water during any fast. Some people should not go without food at all, or only with a doctor's guidance: those with diabetes, those whose medicines must be taken with food, pregnant or nursing mothers, children, the frail elderly, and anyone who has struggled with an eating disorder. If you do heavy labour in the field or on a building site, a partial fast may be wiser. If you feel faint or very unwell, stop and eat. Others can fast from the phone, television, social media or a favourite food. The heart of fasting is the same: turning from something good to give more attention to God.

### The outward disciplines: simplicity, solitude, silence and submission

**Simplicity.** Simplicity is the freedom of a heart that seeks one thing: the Kingdom first (see Chapter 12). Simplicity is not poverty. A businesswoman in Lagos can live simply, and a poor farmer can be eaten up by envy. It means owning things without letting them own us: buying what we need rather than what will impress, giving away what we do not use, and refusing debt to keep up with the neighbours.

**Solitude.** Jesus loved the crowds, but He also left them. "Yet He kept slipping away to places in the wilderness to pray" (Luke 5:16). When His disciples came back tired from their mission, He said to them, "Come away on your own, to a place without people, and get a little rest" (Mark 6:31). Solitude is not loneliness; it is being alone with God. For a mother of five in a small house it may be a walk to the borehole, prayed through step by step. For an office worker it may be lunch alone in the park with a Bible.

**Silence.** Silence goes with solitude. Many of us cannot be quiet for five minutes without reaching for the phone. Yet God says, "Stop, and acknowledge that I am God" (Psalm 46:10). Silence means stopping our own talking, out loud and inside, so that we can listen. *The Imitation of Christ*, traditionally credited to Thomas à Kempis, a fifteenth-century monk in the Netherlands, has helped believers for over five hundred years. It says:

> "In silence and quiet the devout soul advances in virtue and learns the hidden truths of Scripture."
> Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*

Try it. Sit quietly for five minutes before you pray. At first your mind will run like a goat that has slipped its rope. Gently bring it back. In time the silence will become a place of meeting.

**Submission.** Submission is the discipline of laying down the need to have our own way. Paul writes, "And you will submit to one another in the reverent fear of Christ" (Ephesians 5:21). This does not mean putting up with abuse. It is freedom from the endless need to win every argument and to be first. Jesus, Lord of all, washed His disciples' feet. When we yield in small things, such as letting a colleague's plan go forward or listening before we answer, we learn His humility. Service is close kin to submission, and Chapter 24 will explore it fully.

### The corporate disciplines: worship, confession, guidance and celebration

Some disciplines we cannot practise alone. As Antony learned from many older believers, so we grow best among God's people.

**Worship.** Worship is the response of the whole person to the worth of God, and there is something special when His people gather. "Come, let us sing out with joy to the LORD and raise a glad shout to the Rock who saves us" (Psalm 95:1). The writer to the Hebrews urges us not to "walk away from our own gatherings, as some have made it their practice" (Hebrews 10:25). On the Sunday when we feel tired or discouraged, we go anyway, and often it is that very Sunday that God meets us.

**Confession.** James writes, "So make it your practice to confess your sins to each other and to pray for each other, so that you may be healed" (James 5:16). God forgives when we confess to Him, but there is special healing when we bring a hidden sin into the light before a trusted, mature believer, such as a wise pastor or an older friend. Sin loves the dark; confession opens the curtains. The East African Revival of the twentieth century was marked by this kind of honest walking in the light together.

**Guidance.** Sometimes God guides us through the counsel of the community. When the early Church faced a great question about Gentile (non-Jewish) believers, the leaders listened, prayed and searched the Scriptures together, and then wrote, "The Holy Spirit and we ourselves have decided" (Acts 15:28). Before a big decision, such as a marriage or a new business, invite two or three mature believers to pray with you and speak honestly.

**Celebration.** Joy is commanded: "Rejoice in the Lord always. I will say it again: rejoice!" (Philippians 4:4). When Ezra read the Law and the people wept, Nehemiah told them to go and eat, drink and share with those who had nothing, "for the joy of the LORD is your stronghold" (Nehemiah 8:10). Without celebration the disciplines turn grey. A church that fasts must also feast.

### Building a rhythm of life without legalism

Every good thing can be twisted. A discipline becomes legalism when we use it to measure ourselves against others, to feel proud, or to earn God's favour, or when we load it onto others as a burden God never gave.

The answer is not to throw the disciplines away, but to hold them as gifts. A young climbing bean needs a frame, or it will sprawl on the ground and rot. The frame does not give the plant life, but it gives life a shape in which to flourish. A rhythm of life is a trellis for the soul.

It will look different for a farmer and a nurse, a student and a grandmother, but it might include:

- Daily: a morning watch with Scripture and prayer (see Chapter 8), and moments of turning to God through the day.
- Weekly: gathering with the church for worship, a time of rest, and perhaps a regular fast from one meal.
- Monthly: a longer time of solitude, a half-day of prayer, or a fast day for a particular need.
- Yearly: a time to review your life before God with a trusted friend.

Begin small. As Thomas à Kempis put it, "Habit is overcome by habit." Choose one discipline you have neglected and practise it faithfully for a month before adding another.

When you fail, as you will, do not despair. Come back. Near the end of his life Antony urged the younger monks not to grow idle in their labours nor faint in their training. Grace meets us every morning. The disciplines are not a ladder to climb up to God. They are paths to the place where He has promised to meet us, before His face.

All of this belongs to a bigger calling. Jesus did not ask us to collect spiritual habits; He called us to follow Him. The disciplines are a disciple's daily training, and Chapter 11 asks what it means to be one.

### Questions for reflection

- What is the difference between "trying" and "training" in the Christian life? Where have you been trying hard and failing?
- What strikes you most about Antony's story: his obedience to the Word, his learning from others, or his health and warmth after years of discipline? Why?
- Have you ever fasted? Which form of fasting might be wise for you, given your health and work?
- Which outward discipline do you most need at this stage of life: simplicity, solitude, silence or submission?
- Who could you confess to, or ask for guidance from, among the believers around you?
- How can you keep celebration alive so that your disciplines remain a joy rather than a burden?

### A step for this week

Choose one day this week to fast from one meal, either breakfast or lunch, as your health allows. Write down beforehand one thing you are bringing to God. When the mealtime comes, spend that time reading Matthew 6:1–18 slowly and praying for the need you wrote down, and each time you feel hungry during the day, turn the hunger into a short prayer. Set aside the money you would have spent on that meal and give it to someone in need before the week is over.
