## Suffering, Trials and the Sovereignty of God

> "We know that, for people who love God, all things work together and lead to good. They are the ones called in keeping with His purpose."
> — Romans 8:28 (OWB)

Sooner or later, every believer meets suffering. A child falls sick and does not recover. A harvest fails. A job is lost, a marriage breaks, a body weakens, a church is closed by the authorities. Sometimes the pain comes suddenly, like a storm. Sometimes it settles in slowly and stays for years.

In Chapter 16 we saw that we have an enemy, and that Christ has defeated him. But victory over the enemy does not mean a life without pain. Some of the godliest men and women who ever lived suffered terribly. Jesus Himself, the Son of God, was "a man of pains, no stranger to sickness" (Isaiah 53:3). He told His disciples plainly, "In the world you have affliction. But have courage: I have overcome the world" (John 16:33).

This chapter does not offer easy answers. Suffering is a mystery, and anyone who claims to explain it completely has not understood it. But the Bible does give us light to walk by in the dark, and above all it gives us a God who walks with us. To live coram Deo, before the face of God, is to discover that His face does not turn away when the night comes.

### Why Christians suffer

The Bible gives several reasons why believers suffer. None of them explains every case, but together they help us.

**We live in a fallen world.** When sin entered the world, everything was damaged: our bodies, our relationships and the creation itself. "We know that all creation, to this very day, groans as one and suffers the pains of giving birth together" (Romans 8:22). Christians get malaria and cancer. Christian farmers face drought. Christian families are caught in war. Much suffering is not a punishment for any particular sin. It is simply part of living in a broken world that has not yet been made new.

**God uses trials to refine us.** James writes something that surprises us: "Whenever you find yourselves in trials of every sort, regard it as a reason for great joy, because you know that the testing your faith goes through produces endurance. Let endurance carry its work through to the end, so that you may be mature and whole, with nothing missing" (James 1:2–4). Gold is purified in fire. A goldsmith does not put gold in the furnace because he hates it, but because he values it. Paul says the same: "Trouble leads to endurance, endurance to proven character, and proven character to hope" (Romans 5:3–4).

**We share in the sufferings of Christ.** Some suffering comes precisely because we follow Jesus. Peter wrote to believers facing persecution: "Dear friends, the fire that is burning among you has come to test you; do not be taken aback by it, as though something alien to you were happening. No, the more you share in the sufferings of Christ, the more you should rejoice" (1 Peter 4:12–13). Paul longed "to know Him, to know the power of His resurrection and what it is to share in His sufferings" (Philippians 3:10). Millions of believers in our world today know this from experience.

### God's sovereignty and goodness in pain

Two truths hold the believer steady in suffering. God is sovereign: He rules over all things, and nothing happens outside His control. And God is good: He loves His children and intends their good. We need both. A God who was good but not in control would be kind but helpless. A God who was in control but not good would be frightening. The God of the Bible is both.

That is why Romans 8:28 is so precious: "We know that, for people who love God, all things work together and lead to good." Notice what this verse does not say. It does not say all things are good. Cancer is not good. Betrayal is not good. It says that God works in all things, even the evil ones, to bring about good for those who love Him. The "good" is defined in the next verse: being shaped into the likeness of His Son.

Joseph is the great example. His brothers sold him as a slave. He was falsely accused and thrown into prison. Years later, when he stood before those same brothers as the ruler of Egypt, he said: "Evil was what you planned against me; good was what God planned by it, so as to bring about what you see today: the lives of a great many people saved" (Genesis 50:20). The evil was real. So was God's purpose through it.

Sovereignty does not mean that God is the author of evil, or that He enjoys our pain. The brothers were guilty of what they did to Joseph; God did not make them sin. Nor does it mean we should accept every injustice without a word. Christians should fight disease, care for the poor and speak against cruelty. But it does mean that no suffering is meaningless, and that nothing has slipped out of God's hands. A mother in a refugee camp, a farmer watching his crops wither, a pastor in a prison cell: none of them is forgotten, and none of their pain is wasted.

Job lost his children, his wealth and his health in a single season. His first response is one of the most astonishing in Scripture: "He fell to the ground and worshiped. He said, 'I came naked from my mother's womb, and naked I will go back there. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away; may the name of the LORD be blessed'" (Job 1:20–21). Job grieved deeply; he tore his robe. But he grieved toward God, not away from Him.

The missionary Hudson Taylor, who knew great loss in China, put it this way in a meditation in the Ochorus library:

> "The believer does not need to wait until he sees the reason of GOD'S afflictive dealings with him ere he is satisfied; he knows that all things work together for good to them that love GOD."
> Hudson Taylor, *Blessed Adversity*

### Wang Mingdao: faithful, fallen and restored

Few lives show the reality of suffering, and the honesty of faith under it, more clearly than that of Wang Mingdao, one of the most respected Chinese pastors of the twentieth century.

Wang was born in Beijing in 1900, the year of the Boxer uprising, when many Christians in northern China were killed. He came to faith as a young man and gave himself to preaching the Bible. In the 1920s he began a church in Beijing that grew into the Christian Tabernacle, and for decades he also published a magazine of Bible teaching that was read across China. He was known for his plain, fearless preaching and for his insistence that the church must belong to Christ alone.

That conviction cost him dearly. During the Japanese occupation he refused to join a church union controlled by the occupiers. After 1949 the new government set up the Three-Self Patriotic Movement to bring the Protestant churches under state control. Wang refused to join it, believing that the church could not be placed under any authority but Christ's. In 1955 he and his wife were arrested.

Here his story becomes painfully honest. After months of pressure in prison, Wang signed a confession admitting to crimes he had not committed, and in 1956 he was released. But he found no peace. He was tormented by what he had done, and he compared himself to Peter, who had denied the Lord. He and his wife decided they could not live with the lie. They went to the authorities to take back his confession. In 1958 they were both arrested again, and Wang was sentenced to life in prison.

He spent more than twenty years in prison and labour camps. He was finally released in 1980, an old man, and spent his last years in Shanghai, where he died in 1991, still holding to the faith he had preached.

Wang Mingdao's story teaches us several things. Suffering for Christ is real, and it can last a very long time. Even strong believers can stumble under pressure. But failure is not the end. Like Peter, Wang was restored, and he went on to finish with faith. The God who rules over all things also rules over our weakest moments, and He works all things together for good, even through our falls.

### Honest questions

Sometimes our questions are not about why suffering exists, but about why God does not answer our prayers. We pray for healing, and it does not come. We pray for a son to return to the Lord, and he does not. We cry out for a sign, and heaven seems silent.

It is important to say clearly: God does heal, and we should pray for healing with faith. But God does not always heal in this life, and when He does not, it is not always because someone lacked faith. Paul prayed three times for his "thorn in the flesh" to be removed (2 Corinthians 12:7–8). The answer was no. Instead, the Lord said to him, "My grace is enough for you, for power comes to its fullness in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Paul went on: "That is why I am glad to have weaknesses, insults, calamities, persecutions and tight corners, for the sake of Christ. For it is when I am weak that I am strong" (2 Corinthians 12:10).

Joni Eareckson Tada knows this from the inside. In 1967, at the age of seventeen, she dived into shallow water in Chesapeake Bay in the United States, broke her neck, and was left paralysed from the shoulders down. In the months that followed she fell into deep despair and even wished she could die. She prayed earnestly to be healed, and she was not. Over time, through Scripture, friends and many honest struggles with God, she came to trust Him in her wheelchair. She learned to paint with a brush held in her teeth, wrote many books, and in 1979 founded a ministry, Joni and Friends, which has helped disabled people and their families around the world receive wheelchairs and hear the gospel. Later she also lived with chronic pain and cancer. Joni has often written that God may allow things He hates in order to accomplish things He loves.

Her story does not make suffering easy. But it shows that unanswered prayer is not the same as unloved prayer.

There are also seasons of doubt and of God's silence. Faithful believers have walked through them: times when prayer feels like speaking into an empty room, when the Bible seems to say nothing, and when the joy of earlier years feels like a memory. If that is where you are, you are not alone, and you are not lost. God's silence is not His absence.

What should you do in such a season? Keep doing the simple things. Keep reading Scripture, even a few verses. Keep gathering with God's people, even when you feel nothing. Tell a trusted friend or pastor honestly how you feel, instead of pretending. Be careful, too, of anyone who offers a simple formula: "If you just had more faith, you would be healed", or "You must have hidden sin." Job's friends said things like that, and at the end of the book God said they had not spoken rightly about Him. Some suffering has no explanation we can see in this life. We are asked to trust a Person, not to solve a puzzle.

### Lament: faithful prayer in pain

The Bible gives us a language for suffering that many Christians have forgotten. It is called lament. A lament is a prayer that pours out pain, confusion and even complaint to God, while still holding on to Him.

Around a third of the psalms are laments. Listen to David: "How long, LORD? Will You go on forgetting me for good? How long will You turn Your face away from me? How long must I keep turning plans over in my mind, with grief in my heart all day long?" (Psalm 13:1–2). Notice the theme of this book appearing even here. David feels that God's face is turned away, and he says so.

But lament does not end in despair. The same psalm ends: "But I trust in Your loyal love; my heart will be glad because You save. I will sing to the LORD, because He has been so generous to me" (Psalm 13:5). Lament moves from honest pain, through asking, to trust. Sometimes that journey takes a few verses. Sometimes it takes years.

On the cross Jesus Himself prayed a lament, the opening words of Psalm 22: "My God, My God, why have You abandoned Me?" (Psalm 22:1). If the Son of God could pray that, we may bring our hardest questions to the Father. Lament is not a lack of faith. It is faith that refuses to let go of God even when it does not understand Him.

Charles Spurgeon, who suffered from depression and painful illness for much of his life, preached a sermon called *Songs in the Night*, now in the Ochorus library. He wrote:

> "Any man can sing in the day. When the cup is full, man draws inspiration from it... It is easy enough for an Aeolian harp to whisper music when the winds blow—the difficulty is for music to come when no wind blows."
> Charles Spurgeon, *Songs in the Night*

God gives songs in the night. Often the first of those songs is a lament.

### Comfort received and comfort given

Suffering is never wasted in God's hands. One of its purposes is to make us comforters. "Blessed is the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the merciful Father and the God from whom all comfort comes. He comforts us in every hardship we face, so that we in turn can comfort people facing any hardship, passing on to them the same comfort God gives us" (2 Corinthians 1:3–4).

God's comfort is real and near. "The LORD stays close to those whose hearts are broken, and rescues those whose spirits are crushed" (Psalm 34:18). Often He comforts us through His people. Job's friends did best in the first week, when "they sat with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights. No one said a word to him, because they saw how great his suffering was" (Job 2:13). They did worst when they started to explain his suffering.

So when others suffer, be slow to explain and quick to be present. Bring food. Sit by the hospital bed. Pray with them, briefly. "Share the joy of those who are glad, and the tears of those who weep" (Romans 12:15). Jesus Himself, standing at the tomb of His friend Lazarus, knowing He was about to raise him, still wept (John 11:35).

Horatio Spafford was a lawyer in Chicago in the nineteenth century. He and his wife Anna had already lost a young son, and much of his property was destroyed in the great Chicago fire of 1871. In 1873 Anna and their four daughters sailed for Europe on the ship *Ville du Havre*. In the middle of the Atlantic it collided with another ship and sank within minutes. All four girls drowned. Anna was rescued, and when she reached land she sent her husband a telegram with two words: *Saved alone*. As Horatio sailed to join his grieving wife, he wrote the words of the hymn "It Is Well with My Soul". The Spaffords later moved to Jerusalem, where they and their friends spent years caring for the poor and the sick. Their comfort became comfort for many.

### The hope of glory

Finally, the Bible puts our suffering in the light of eternity. "I have weighed it up: what we suffer in this present time does not begin to balance the glory that is going to be revealed to us" (Romans 8:18). Paul was not a man who had suffered little. He had been beaten, stoned, shipwrecked and imprisoned. Yet he wrote: "Our troubles are light and last only a moment, and they are bringing about for us an eternal glory, weighty beyond all measure. All the while, we look not at what can be seen but at what cannot be seen" (2 Corinthians 4:17–18).

Imagine a set of old market scales. On one side put every sorrow you have ever known. On the other side put the glory God has prepared for His children. The scale does not just tip; it crashes down. One day "every tear He will wipe from their eyes. Death will be no more, and no more mourning, no crying, no pain" (Revelation 21:4). Chapter 28 will look at that hope in full.

Until then we live before His face, sometimes with joy, sometimes through tears, always held. He is the God who rules all things and loves us still. The next part of this book turns to how we live as God's people together, beginning with marriage in Chapter 18, where many of life's joys and sorrows are shared.

### Questions for reflection

- Which of the reasons for suffering in this chapter, a fallen world, refining, or sharing Christ's sufferings, helps you most in your own situation?
- Why do we need to believe both that God is sovereign and that God is good? What happens if we hold only one?
- How does Wang Mingdao's story of failure and restoration encourage you?
- Have you prayed for something, perhaps healing, that God has not granted? How do 2 Corinthians 12:9 and Joni Eareckson Tada's story speak to you?
- Have you ever prayed a lament? What might it look like to bring your hardest questions honestly to God?
- Who around you is suffering now, and how could you share God's comfort with them this week?

### A step for this week

Write your own lament this week, following the shape of Psalm 13. Take a page and write four parts: first, tell God honestly what hurts ("How long, Lord…?"); second, ask Him plainly for what you need; third, write down one thing you know to be true about His character, such as His loyal love; and fourth, end with a sentence of trust, even if your feelings have not yet caught up. Then think of one person you know who is suffering, and visit, call or write to them before the week ends, simply to be present and to pray with them.
