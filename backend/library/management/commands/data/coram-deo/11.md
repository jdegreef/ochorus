## Kingdom Life: The Sermon on the Mount

> "Seeing the crowds, Jesus went up onto the mountain. He sat down, and His disciples came to Him. And He began to teach them: 'Blessed are those who are poor in spirit: the kingdom of heaven is theirs.'"
> — Matthew 5:1–3 (OWB)

When a new government comes to power, it often publishes a manifesto: a statement of what it stands for and how the country will be run. In Matthew 5 to 7, Jesus gives the manifesto of the Kingdom of God. He sits on a hillside in Galilee, as teachers did in His day, with His disciples close around Him and the crowds listening behind. What He says turns the world's ideas of success, power and happiness upside down.

In the last chapter we saw that a disciple is an apprentice of Jesus (see Chapter 10). Here we sit at the Master's feet and listen. The Sermon on the Mount is not a list of rules for earning heaven. It is a portrait of the life Jesus is creating in His people, a life lived openly before the face of the Father "who sees what is hidden." Augustine of North Africa, early in his ministry, wrote a whole commentary on these chapters and called them a perfect pattern for the Christian life. John Wesley preached thirteen sermons on them. He insisted that the sermon must be taken whole, because its parts are:

> "all joined as the stones in an arch, of which you cannot take one away, without destroying the whole fabric."
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

So let us walk through it, stone by stone.

### The Beatitudes: the blessed life turned upside down

Jesus begins with blessing. Eight times He says "Blessed are..." The word means deeply happy, truly well off, approved by God. But listen to whom He calls blessed:

> "Blessed are those who are poor in spirit: the kingdom of heaven is theirs. Blessed are those who mourn: they will be comforted. Blessed are the gentle: they will inherit the earth. Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness: they will be satisfied." (Matthew 5:3–6)

The world says, "Blessed are the rich, the confident, the strong, the ones who get their own way." Jesus says the opposite. The *poor in spirit* are those who know they have nothing to offer God, and come to Him with empty hands. Wesley wrote that "the foundation of all is poverty of spirit." Those who *mourn* grieve over sin and over the brokenness of the world. The *gentle* are not weak; they are strong people who have placed their strength under God's control. Those who *hunger and thirst for righteousness* want God's way more than food.

Then come the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted (Matthew 5:7–10). "Blessed are the pure in heart: they will see God" (Matthew 5:8). Here is the promise at the centre of this book: the face of God, seen by those whose hearts He has made clean.

Notice that these are not eight different kinds of Christian. They are eight sides of one character, the character of Jesus Himself. He was poor in spirit, mourned over Jerusalem, was gentle and humble, hungered to do His Father's will, showed mercy, was pure, made peace and was persecuted. The Beatitudes describe what happens to anyone who walks closely with Him.

### Salt and light: our influence in the world

Next Jesus tells His small band of followers who they are:

> "You are the salt for the earth... You are the light of the world. A town built on a hilltop cannot be hidden." (Matthew 5:13–14)

In a world without fridges, salt kept meat and fish from going bad. It also gave flavour. Light shows the way and drives out darkness. Christians are meant to hold back the decay of society and to show the way to God. But salt does nothing in the shaker, and a lamp under a basket helps no one. Jesus says, "Your light must shine where people can see it, so that they will see your good deeds and give glory to your Father in heaven" (Matthew 5:16).

Wesley understood this well. Some Christians in his day thought the holiest life was one hidden away from the world. He disagreed:

> "Christianity is essentially a social religion; and that to turn it into a solitary religion, is indeed to destroy it."
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

Salt and light mean honesty in the market when others cheat, kindness on the bus when others push, integrity in the government office when others take bribes. The goal is not our glory but our Father's.

### Jesus fulfils the law by taking it to the heart

Some people thought Jesus had come to throw away the Old Testament law. He corrected them at once: "I have not come to do away with them but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17). He then gave six examples, each beginning, "You have heard... But I tell you." Two of them show the pattern clearly.

**Murder.** The sixth commandment says, "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13). Many people felt safe because they had never killed anyone. But Jesus said:

> "You have heard what our ancestors were told: 'Do not commit murder. Whoever commits murder must answer for it in court.' But I tell you that anyone who is angry with a brother or sister will have to answer for it in court." (Matthew 5:21–22)

Murder is not only the physical act. The anger that boils inside, and the contempt that calls another person "empty-head" or "fool," break the same commandment in the heart. Murder begins there. Jesus goes to the root.

**Adultery.** The seventh commandment says, "You shall not commit adultery" (Exodus 20:14). Again, many felt clean because they had never slept with another person's wife or husband. Jesus said:

> "You have heard the commandment, 'Do not commit adultery.' But I tell you, a man who looks at a woman with the aim of wanting her for himself has already committed adultery with her in his heart." (Matthew 5:27–28)

Adultery is not only the physical act. Lust in the heart, nursed and enjoyed, is adultery too. In a world where images are a click away on every phone, these words are as urgent as ever.

**The pattern.** The Old Covenant mostly addressed outward actions: do not kill, do not steal, do not commit adultery. A person could keep the letter of these laws while their heart was full of hatred and lust. Christ goes to the heart. And He does not leave us to change our hearts by our own effort, which we could never do. Through the prophet Jeremiah, God promised a new covenant: "I will set My instruction deep inside them and write it on their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33). Through Ezekiel He promised, "I will give you a new heart, and I will place a new spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:26). This is what the Holy Spirit does (see Chapter 3). He writes God's law within us, so that we begin to want what God wants. The Sermon on the Mount is not a heavier law laid on tired backs. It is a description of the new heart the Spirit is creating.

### Loving enemies: Martin Luther King Jr.

The last of Jesus' six examples is the hardest of all:

> "You have heard the saying, 'Love your neighbor and hate your enemy.' But I tell you: love your enemies, and pray for the people who persecute you, so that you will be children of your Father in heaven." (Matthew 5:43–45)

In the twentieth century, few people put these words to a harder public test than an American Baptist pastor named Martin Luther King Jr.

In 1954, at the age of twenty-five, King became pastor of the Dexter Avenue Baptist Church in Montgomery, Alabama. The southern United States was then ruled by segregation laws. Black citizens were forced to sit at the back of city buses and give up their seats to white passengers. In December 1955, a woman named Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat and was arrested. The Black community of Montgomery decided to stop riding the buses altogether, and they chose the young pastor King to lead them.

For more than a year, thousands of people walked to work, sometimes many miles a day, or shared cars. Their leaders were arrested and threatened. On the night of 30 January 1956, while King was speaking at a meeting, a bomb exploded on the porch of his home. His wife Coretta and their baby daughter were inside. By God's mercy, no one was hurt.

When King reached home, a large and angry crowd had gathered. Some carried guns and knives. The police were nervous, and violence could have broken out at any moment. King stood on the damaged porch and spoke to the people. He told them that he wanted them to go home and put away their weapons. Violence, he said, could not solve the problem. They must meet hatred with love. He reminded them of Jesus' command to love their enemies. The crowd slowly went home.

The boycott continued. In late 1956 the United States Supreme Court ruled that bus segregation was against the law, and the boycott ended in victory after more than a year. King went on to lead a wider movement for justice across the nation, always insisting that it must be nonviolent. His approach had many roots. He had studied the work of Mahatma Gandhi in India, whose own thinking had been shaped in part by the Sermon on the Mount and by the Russian writer Leo Tolstoy's reflections on it. But King himself pointed above all to Jesus. In a sermon later published as "Loving Your Enemies," he taught that love for enemies is not a weak sentiment but the only force that can turn an enemy into a friend. He was jailed many times. In 1968, at the age of thirty-nine, he was shot and killed in Memphis, Tennessee.

King's life shows that the Sermon on the Mount is not only for private devotion. It speaks to how we respond to injustice, how we treat those who wrong us, and how we work for a better society without becoming what we oppose. Paul puts it this way: "Do not let evil defeat you; defeat evil with good" (Romans 12:21). In many of our nations, believers face tribal hatred, political violence or religious persecution. Jesus' command remains the same: love, pray, do good, and leave judgment to God.

### Sabbath fulfilled in Christ

Before we go on to Matthew 6, we must consider an important question: how does Jesus fulfil the Sabbath? This is the book's main treatment of rest, and it matters for every worker, parent and leader.

The fourth commandment told Israel to rest on the seventh day, for "the LORD made the heavens and the earth, the sea and everything in them, in six days, and on the seventh day He rested" (Exodus 20:11). Jesus kept the Sabbath, but He also taught that "the Sabbath was made for people, not people for the Sabbath. So the Son of Man is Lord even over the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27–28).

Faithful Christians understand the Sabbath in different ways. Some hold that Sunday is the Christian Sabbath. Others see Sunday as the Lord's Day, distinct from the Sabbath. Others keep the seventh day, Saturday. Appendix A sets out these views fairly. Here is this book's view.

**This book's view:** Jesus is our Sabbath rest. He said, "Come to Me, all of you who are worn out with work and weighed down with burdens, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). The letter to the Hebrews explains that the rest God offered Israel pointed beyond itself:

> "So a Sabbath rest is still waiting for God's people. For whoever has entered God's rest has come to rest from their own works too, just as God did from His." (Hebrews 4:9–10)

We enter that rest by trusting Christ. We stop working to earn God's favour, because Jesus has finished the work. So every day becomes a day of resting in Him: relying on God for everything, abiding in Christ (see Chapter 8), and laying down the anxious striving that tries to carry the world on our own shoulders. Paul writes that the Sabbath was "a shadow of what was coming; the substance is Christ" (Colossians 2:17).

At the same time, regular physical rest remains God's good gift and a wise rhythm for workers and leaders alike. God made our bodies to need sleep and a regular day of rest. A farmer who never stops, a trader who opens her stall seven days a week, a pastor who never takes a day off, will sooner or later break down. Resting regularly is an act of trust. It says, "God, the world does not depend on me." Chapters 19 and 23 will return to this rhythm of work and rest. And whatever view we hold, we gladly gather with God's people on the Lord's Day to worship the risen Christ, and we give each other freedom: "Each person must be fully convinced in their own mind" (Romans 14:5).

### Secret devotion: before the Father who sees

In Matthew 6, Jesus turns to three practices of devotion: giving, prayer and fasting. He warns against doing them to impress people:

> "Take care not to put your righteousness on show for others to see. If you do, your Father in heaven has no reward for you." (Matthew 6:1)

When you give, He says, do it quietly. When you pray, go into an inner room and shut the door. When you fast, wash your face so no one can tell (see Chapter 9). Each time the promise is the same: "Your Father sees what is hidden, and He will repay you" (Matthew 6:6).

This is *coram Deo* in its purest form. The hypocrite lives before the faces of people, performing for their praise. The disciple lives before the face of the Father. What matters is not what others see, but what He sees. That frees us. We no longer need to look spiritual. We need only to be honest with God.

In the middle of this teaching, Jesus gives us the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13). It begins, not with our needs, but with our Father and His name, His Kingdom and His will. We have studied it in Chapter 6.

### Treasure in heaven and freedom from worry

Jesus then speaks about money and anxiety, two things that fill most people's minds most days.

> "Do not pile up treasures for yourselves on earth, where moths and rust ruin them and thieves dig through walls and steal them. Instead, pile up treasures for yourselves in heaven, where no moth or rust can ruin them and no thief can dig through and steal. Your heart will always be where your treasure is." (Matthew 6:19–21)

"You cannot serve both God and money," He says (Matthew 6:24). Money makes a good servant but a terrible master.

Then He speaks to the worried. Look at the birds, He says. They do not plant or harvest, "and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not worth far more than they are?" (Matthew 6:26). Worry cannot add a single hour to our lives. Instead:

> "Above all, seek the kingdom and His righteousness, and you will be given all these things as well. So do not be troubled about tomorrow; tomorrow can take care of its own worries." (Matthew 6:33–34)

Seeking the Kingdom first does not mean we stop working, planning or saving. It means God's rule comes first in our decisions, and we trust Him for the rest. The mother worried about school fees, the young man anxious about finding work, the farmer watching the sky for rain: each is invited to bring that worry to the Father and seek His Kingdom first. Chapter 12 will show how this kind of trust grows.

### Judging, asking and the narrow way

In Matthew 7, Jesus addresses how we treat others. "Stop passing judgment on others, so that you will not be judged" (Matthew 7:1). He pictures a man with a beam of wood in his own eye, trying to take a splinter out of his neighbour's. This does not mean we never discern right from wrong. It means we deal honestly with our own sin first, and we never look down on others.

Then He invites us to pray boldly: "Keep asking, and you will receive. Keep seeking, and you will find. Keep knocking, and the door will be opened for you" (Matthew 7:7). If earthly parents give good gifts to their children, how much more will our Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him.

He sums up the whole law in one sentence, often called the Golden Rule: "So in everything, treat others the way you would want them to treat you" (Matthew 7:12).

And He warns us that this way is not the crowd's way:

> "Go in through the narrow gate. The gate that leads to destruction is wide and the road to it is easy, and many go in that way. But the gate that leads to life is narrow and the road is hard, and only a few find it." (Matthew 7:13–14)

### Building on the rock: hearing and doing

Jesus ends with a story every builder understands. Two men build houses. One builds on rock; the other on sand. From the outside the houses may look the same. Then the storm comes.

> "So anyone who listens to what I have said here and acts on it will be like a sensible person who built a house on solid rock. The rain poured down, the floods rose, and the winds blew and beat against that house. But it did not fall, because its foundation was on rock." (Matthew 7:24–25)

The difference between the two builders is not that one heard Jesus' words and the other did not. Both heard. The difference is that one acted on them. It is possible to admire the Sermon on the Mount, preach it and even memorise it, and still build on sand. The storms of life, sickness, loss, temptation, persecution, will show which foundation we chose.

When Jesus finished, "the crowds were amazed at His teaching" (Matthew 7:28). The question for us is not whether we are amazed, but whether we will build.

This is a high calling, and no one keeps it by their own strength. It calls for faith: trust in the Father who feeds the birds, hears our prayers and sees in secret. That is where we turn next, in Chapter 12.

### Questions for reflection

- Which of the Beatitudes describes you most, and which least? What would it look like for that missing quality to grow in you?
- Where is God calling you to be salt and light: at home, at work, in your community?
- Jesus took the commands about murder and adultery to the heart. Where do anger, contempt or lust need His healing in your heart?
- What did Martin Luther King Jr.'s response to the bombing of his home teach you about loving enemies? Who is your "enemy" today?
- How can you rest in Christ every day, and how can you build a wise rhythm of physical rest into your week?
- In what area of life have you been hearing Jesus' words without acting on them?

### A step for this week

Read the whole Sermon on the Mount, Matthew 5 to 7, once this week in a single sitting; it takes about twenty minutes. As you read, ask the Holy Spirit to show you one teaching you are not yet putting into practice. Write it down, and choose one specific action to obey it before the week ends: forgiving someone, giving in secret, praying for an enemy, or laying down a worry before your Father. Then take one evening or half-day this week to rest from work completely, and use part of it to thank God that He holds your life.
