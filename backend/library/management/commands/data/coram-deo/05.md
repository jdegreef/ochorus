## The Word of God: Hearing Him Speak

> "All Scripture is breathed out by God and is useful for teaching, for rebuking, for correcting and for training in righteousness, so that the one who belongs to God may be fully capable, equipped for every good work."
> — 2 Timothy 3:16–17 (OWB)

Think of a young man who has gone to the city to find work, leaving behind the woman he hopes to marry. A letter arrives from her. He reads it on the bus, again at lunch, and once more before he sleeps. He notices every word. Why? Because he loves the one who wrote it, and through the letter he hears her voice.

In Chapter 5 we saw that love is the heart of the Christian life. Those who love God want to hear Him. Jesus said, "Anyone who loves Me will keep My word. My Father will love them, and We will come to them and make Our dwelling place with them" (John 14:23). Love listens. And the place where God has chosen to speak most clearly, most surely and most often is His written Word, the Bible.

To live coram Deo, before the face of God, is not to live before a silent face. The God whose face we seek is a God who speaks. This chapter is about learning to hear Him: reading the Bible, studying it, turning it over in our hearts, storing it in our memories and, above all, doing what it says.

### God speaks

The first thing the Bible tells us about God's activity is that He spoke. "God said, 'Let there be light'," and there was light. The God who made the world by His word has not fallen silent. He spoke through the prophets, and finally He spoke through His Son. And He made sure that what He said would be written down and kept for us.

Paul tells Timothy that "all Scripture is breathed out by God" (2 Timothy 3:16). The words did not simply come from good and wise people thinking about God. They came from God Himself, breathed out the way we breathe out words when we speak. Peter says the same: "no prophecy ever came because a human being willed it; rather, people spoke from God as the Holy Spirit bore them along" (2 Peter 1:21). The human writers were real people, with their own languages, styles and situations. Yet the Holy Spirit carried them along, the way the wind carries a sailing boat, so that what they wrote is truly God's Word.

This is why the Bible is our sure standard for faith and for life. Preachers, traditions, dreams and feelings must all be measured against it, as the believers in Berea knew. They "went through the Scriptures day after day to check whether what Paul said was true" (Acts 17:11). If even the apostle Paul's preaching was checked against the Bible, so must everything else be.

And this Word is not a dead letter. "For the word of God is alive and at work, and it has a keener edge than any two-edged sword. It reaches right in, cutting through soul and spirit, through joints and marrow, and it weighs the thoughts and purposes of the heart" (Hebrews 4:12). You may open it on an ordinary morning and find a familiar verse speaking straight to the worry you carried to bed. That is the living God speaking through His living Word.

### The whole story

Many Christians know parts of the Bible well but have never seen how it fits together. It is not a random collection of sayings. It is one great story, told across many centuries and many books, with one great Hero.

It begins with creation: a good world, with man and woman made in God's image to walk with Him. Then comes the fall: sin enters, the hiding begins, and death follows. The story narrows to Abraham's family, through whom God promises to bless all nations, and follows Israel through slavery and rescue, kings and prophets, exile and return. All the while the promise grows: a King is coming, a Servant who will suffer, a new covenant written on the heart.

Then the promise is kept. Jesus Christ is born, lives, dies for our sins and rises again. He pours out the Holy Spirit and sends His Church to every nation. And the story looks forward to its ending, which is really a new beginning: a new heaven and a new earth where God's people will see His face.

After His resurrection, Jesus walked with two discouraged disciples on the road to Emmaus. "Then, starting with Moses and going on through all the prophets, He explained to them what was written about Himself in every part of the Scriptures" (Luke 24:27). Later He told the apostles, "whatever the Law of Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms have written about Me must be fulfilled" (Luke 24:44). Jesus is the centre of the whole Bible. The Old Testament points forward to Him; the New Testament tells of Him and flows out from Him. When you read any part of Scripture, it is always worth asking, "How does this part of the story lead me to Christ?"

Jesus also warned the religious leaders of His day: "You pore over the Scriptures, since you think that eternal life is to be found in them; and they are the ones that testify about Me. But you do not want to come to Me so that you can have life" (John 5:39–40). It is possible to know the Bible well and miss the One it is about. We read the Bible not to collect facts but to meet a Person.

### Reading: the whole Bible, for relationship

The first step in hearing God is simply to read His Word, regularly and widely. A healthy body needs a balanced plate, not one favourite dish; a healthy Christian needs the whole counsel of God, not only favourite verses.

George Müller, the German-born pastor who cared for many thousands of orphans in Bristol, England, without ever asking anyone but God for money, was a man soaked in Scripture. He read the Bible through many times in his long life. In *Answers to Prayer* he gave this advice:

> "It is of immense importance for the understanding of the word of God, to read it in course, so that we may read every day a portion of the Old and a portion of the New Testament, going on where we previously left off."
> George Müller, *Answers to Prayer*

He went on to warn that "there are many believers, I fear, in our day, who have not read even once through the whole of the Scriptures; and yet in a few months, by reading only a few chapters every day they might accomplish it." That is still true. Reading three or four chapters a day takes about twenty minutes and brings you through the whole Bible in a year.

Here are some simple ways to begin:

- **A gospel first.** If you are new to the Bible, start with Mark or Luke, a chapter a day.
- **Old and New together.** Read a chapter or two from the Old Testament and one from the New each day, as Müller advised, keeping a bookmark in each.
- **A yearly plan.** Simple plans take you through the whole Bible in a year. If that feels too much, take two.
- **Listening.** If reading is hard for you, listen to the Bible read aloud. For much of history God's people have heard Scripture more than they have read it.

Above all, read for relationship, not just information. Before you begin, ask God to speak: "Take the veil from my eyes, so that I may see the wonders in Your instruction" (Psalm 119:18). As you read, notice what it shows you about God: His character, His promises, His ways. Read as the young man read his letter, listening for the voice of the One who wrote it.

### Pandita Ramabai: a Bible for her people

Ramabai was born in 1858 into a high-caste Brahmin family. Her father, a Sanskrit scholar, did something unusual for his time: he taught his wife and daughter the sacred Sanskrit texts. The family lived as wandering pilgrims, and when famine struck in the 1870s, Ramabai lost her father, her mother and her sister. She and her brother walked thousands of miles across India, reciting scripture in temples to earn a living. In Calcutta, scholars were so impressed by her learning that they gave her the titles "Pandita" and "Sarasvati," honours almost never given to a woman.

Yet her learning did not give her peace. She married, and within two years her husband died, leaving her a widow with a baby daughter, Manorama. She had seen how harshly widows were treated, and longed to help them. Through Christian friends she came into contact with the Bible, and in 1883, in England, she was baptised.

But Ramabai later wrote that for years she had found the Christian religion without truly finding Christ. It was through reading the Scriptures for herself that she came to see that she was a sinner in need of a Saviour, and that Jesus Christ had died for her. The Word that had been a subject of study became the voice of a Friend.

Back in India she opened homes for widows and abandoned girls, and later founded the Mukti Mission at Kedgaon, near Pune. *Mukti* means salvation or freedom. During terrible famines she took in hundreds of starving girls and women. They learned to read, to work, and to know the Lord. In 1905 a deep revival of prayer and repentance swept through Mukti, as girls and women gathered around the Word and cried out to God.

Then Ramabai set herself a task that would fill the last years of her life. She wanted the people of her own Marathi-speaking region, including the poor village women she loved, to have the whole Bible in language they could understand. So, already a scholar of Sanskrit and other languages, she learned Hebrew and Greek so that she could translate from the original texts. She finished the work shortly before she died in 1922, not long after the death of her beloved daughter.

Why would a woman who had lost so much give her last strength to translating a book? Because she had found in it the voice of the living God, and could not bear for her people to go without it. If she gave her final years so that others could read God's Word, how can we leave our own Bibles closed?

### Studying: seeing what is there

Reading is like walking through a field. Studying is like stopping to dig. Both are good. But some treasures lie below the surface.

Of the scribe Ezra we read, "Ezra had set his heart to study the law of the LORD, to practice it, and to teach its statutes and rulings in Israel" (Ezra 7:10). Notice the order: study, then practise, then teach. Ezra's study was not mere curiosity. It was the first step to obedience.

R. A. Torrey, the American Bible teacher and evangelist, wrote a great deal about how to study the Bible. In *How to Succeed in the Christian Life* he put it bluntly:

> "The one who neglects his Bible is bound to make a failure of the Christian life. The one who studies his Bible in the right spirit and by a true method is bound to make a success of the Christian life."
> R. A. Torrey, *How to Succeed in the Christian Life*

In the same chapter Torrey recalls a saying of his friend D. L. Moody: "In prayer we talk to God. In Bible study, God talks to us, and we had better let God do most of the talking."

A simple and time-tested method has three steps.

1. **Observation: what does it say?** Read the passage slowly, more than once. Who is speaking, and to whom? Which words are repeated? Many mistakes come from not seeing what is actually on the page.
2. **Interpretation: what does it mean?** Ask what the writer meant for his first readers. Pay attention to the context: the verses before and after, the book it belongs to, and where it sits in the whole story. Notice the kind of writing it is: a psalm is poetry, a proverb gives wise general patterns rather than promises for every case, a parable is a story with a point. Read each in its own way.
3. **Application: what does it mean for me?** Only after the first two steps do we ask how this truth should change what we believe, how we feel and what we do today.

One more key is to let Scripture interpret Scripture. God does not contradict Himself. When one verse is hard to understand, look at what the rest of the Bible says on the same subject. Clearer passages shed light on harder ones, and this guards us from building a teaching on one verse taken out of its place.

You do not need to be a scholar. A Bible, a notebook and an honest heart will take you a long way, and studying with others keeps us from strange ideas.

### Meditating: turning it over in the heart

Bible reading can be fast. Meditation is slow. Meditation in the Bible does not mean emptying the mind. It means filling it with God's Word and turning that Word over and over, the way a cow chews the cud, until its goodness is drawn out and becomes part of us.

God told Joshua, as he took up the leadership of Israel, "Keep this book of the law always on your lips, and reflect on it by day and by night, so that you take care to do all that is written in it. Then you will bring your ways to a good end, and then you will act wisely and succeed" (Joshua 1:8). The very first psalm paints the picture of a person whose delight is in God's instruction: "they recite it quietly to themselves day and night. That person is like a tree set out beside channels of water, which bears its fruit when its season comes, and whose leaves never dry up" (Psalm 1:2–3).

Anyone who has farmed in a dry land knows the difference between a tree beside a stream and a tree on the open hillside. In the dry season one withers; the other stays green. Meditation sends our roots down into the Word, so that when dry seasons come we still have water.

How do we meditate? Take a short passage, even one verse. Read it slowly several times, perhaps aloud, stressing a different word each time. Ask, "Lord, what are You saying to me here?" Then carry the verse with you through the day. The psalmist says, "How I love Your instruction! I turn it over in my mind all day long" (Psalm 119:97).

### Memorising: the Word hidden in the heart

"Deep in my heart I have kept Your word safe, so that I will not sin against You" (Psalm 119:11). When we memorise Scripture, we carry it with us into places where we cannot open a book: a hospital bed, a long journey, a sleepless night, a moment of sudden temptation.

Jesus showed us the power of this. After forty days of fasting in the wilderness, He was tempted by the devil (Matthew 4:1–11). Each time, He answered with Scripture He knew by heart, all of it from the book of Deuteronomy. When tempted to turn stones into bread, He answered, "No one lives on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God" (Matthew 4:4). When offered the kingdoms of the world in exchange for worship, He answered from Scripture again: "You must worship the Lord your God and serve no one but Him" (Matthew 4:10). The Son of God fought temptation with the sword of the Spirit. How much more do we need it?

Memorising is easier than many people think. Choose one verse a week, write it on a small card, and read it aloud several times a day. Review old verses as you add new ones. In a year you will carry more than fifty passages in your heart.

### Hearing and doing

All of this, reading, studying, meditating and memorising, has one aim. It is not that we become clever. It is that we become obedient, and through obedience, more like Christ.

James writes, "Be people who carry out the word, not just people who hear it. Otherwise you are fooling yourselves. Anyone who hears the word but does not carry it out is like a man studying his own face in a mirror. He looks himself over, goes away, and at once forgets what he looked like" (James 1:22–24). Imagine seeing a smear of dirt on your face in the mirror and walking out without washing it off. That is reading the Bible and changing nothing.

"But whoever looks closely into the perfect law of freedom and stays with it, not hearing and then forgetting but doing the work, will be blessed in what they do" (James 1:25). The blessing is not in the hearing alone. It is in the doing.

So when you finish reading, ask a simple question: "What is one thing God wants me to believe, or do, or stop doing, because of what I have read today?" Then do it. Perhaps it is to forgive someone, to speak more gently to your children, or to be honest in your business. Obedience is how the Word moves from the page into your life.

This is living coram Deo with an open Bible: not standing over Scripture as its judges, but sitting under it as listeners, ready to obey.

### From the Word to prayer

George Müller learned one more lesson from the Bible that changed his whole life with God, and it leads us straight into Chapter 7.

For years Müller had begun each day by trying to pray as soon as he was dressed, and often his mind wandered. Then, in 1841, he saw something new. He described it in his journal, later published as *The Life of Trust*:

> "I saw more clearly than ever that the first great and primary business to which I ought to attend every day was, to have my soul happy in the Lord."
> George Müller, *The Life of Trust*

So he changed the order. Instead of beginning with prayer, he began with the Bible, reading slowly and meditating on each verse, asking only for a few words of blessing first. And he discovered that, after a few minutes of meditation, his soul was "led to confession, or to thanksgiving, or to intercession, or to supplication; so that, though I did not, as it were, give myself to prayer, but to meditation, yet it turned almost immediately more or less into prayer."

That is the pattern of a true conversation. God speaks first; we answer. The Word stirs us, and our hearts respond in praise, confession and requests. Prayer that grows out of Scripture rests on what God has said.

The Bible is God's letter to His children. But He wants us to answer, and to talk with Him face to face. That is where we turn next.

### Questions for reflection

- How would you describe your relationship with the Bible right now: a duty, a struggle, a delight, or something else? Why?
- Jesus is the centre of the whole Bible (Luke 24:27). How might that change the way you read the Old Testament?
- What can you learn from Pandita Ramabai's love for God's Word and her work to bring it to her people?
- Which of the three steps of study (observation, interpretation, application) do you most often skip? What happens when you do?
- Think of a time when a verse you remembered helped you in a moment of temptation or fear. What happened?
- What is one thing God has said to you through His Word recently that you have not yet done?

### A step for this week

Choose one of the Gospels and read one chapter each day this week, using Müller's pattern. Before you read, ask God in a sentence to speak to you. Read slowly, stopping at any verse that catches your attention, and turn it over in your mind. Write down one sentence about what the chapter shows you about Jesus and one thing you will do because of it. Then turn what you have read into a short prayer. Choose one verse from the week, write it on a card, and carry it with you until you can say it by heart.
