## Abiding in the Vine

> "Remain in Me, and I will remain in you. A branch cannot bear fruit by itself; it must remain in the vine. In the same way, you cannot bear fruit unless you remain in Me. I am the vine; you are the branches. Whoever remains in Me, with Me remaining in them, is the one who bears much fruit, because you can do nothing apart from Me."
> — John 15:4–5 (OWB)

Cut a branch from a vine and lay it on the ground. For a little while it looks the same as the others: the same green leaves, the same shape. But it is already dying. It has no root of its own. By the end of the day its leaves droop; within a week it is dry. It did nothing wrong. It simply stopped being joined to the vine.

On the night before He died, Jesus took this ordinary picture from the vineyards of Israel and used it to describe the deepest secret of the Christian life. We are branches. He is the vine. Our life, our strength and our fruitfulness all depend on one thing: remaining joined to Him.

### The anchor of Part Two

John 15 gathers together everything we have learned so far in Part Two. In Chapter 4 we saw that love is the mark of a Christian; here Jesus says, "Remain in My love" (John 15:9). In Chapter 5 we learned to hear God in His Word; here Jesus speaks of "My words" remaining in us (John 15:7). In Chapter 6 we learned to pray; here Jesus promises, "ask for whatever you want, and it will be done for you" (John 15:7). In Chapter 7 we learned to meet God each morning; here we learn how to carry that meeting into every hour of the day.

The picture has three parts. "I am the true vine, and My Father is the vine grower" (John 15:1). Jesus is the vine, the source of life. We are the branches, joined to Him and carrying His life into the world. And the Father is the gardener, who tends the vine with skill and care. The whole Trinity is at work here: the Son gives life, the Father tends, and, as Jesus teaches in the chapters around this one, the Spirit lives within us to make Christ's presence real.

### What it means to remain

Older English Bibles use the word *abide*, and many Christians still speak of "abiding in Christ." The Open Word Bible uses the plainer word *remain*. Both mean the same thing: to stay, to dwell, to make your home. A branch does not visit the vine now and then. It stays joined, day and night, in every season.

Notice that Jesus does not say, "Become joined to Me," as if we had to work our way in. If you belong to Christ, you are already in Him. The Father has joined you to the vine by grace, through faith. Jesus says, "Remain." Stay where you have been placed. Keep drawing your life from Him.

Andrew Murray, the South African pastor whose books on the inner life have been loved for well over a century, wrote that many believers trust Christ for pardon yet have hardly realised the closeness He offers. In *Abide in Christ* he puts the promise simply: "only abiding in Jesus gives rest in Him."

How, then, do we remain? John 15:7 gives a beautiful answer: "If you remain in Me and My words remain in you, ask for whatever you want, and it will be done for you." Here the Word and prayer are joined together. His words remain in us when we read, meditate on and memorise Scripture, as we learned in Chapter 5. And when His words fill us, our prayers begin to match His will, so that we can ask with confidence, as we saw in Chapter 6.

Jesus adds a third thread: love and obedience. "You will remain in My love if you keep My commandments, just as I have kept the commandments My Father gave Me and remain in His love" (John 15:10). Remaining is not a feeling. It is a life of trusting, listening, praying and obeying. Elsewhere He said, "Anyone who loves Me will keep My word. My Father will love them, and We will come to them and make Our dwelling place with them" (John 14:23). When we remain in Him, God makes His home in us.

### Pruning: when the gardener cuts

"He takes away any branch in Me that bears no fruit, and He prunes clean every branch that does bear fruit, so that it will bear more fruit" (John 15:2). These are some of the most comforting and most uncomfortable words in the chapter.

Anyone who has grown grapes, coffee or tea knows that a plant left alone grows long, leafy shoots that use up its strength and bear little fruit. So the gardener takes his knife and cuts. To a watching child it looks cruel. Healthy green growth falls to the ground. But the gardener knows what he is doing. Next season there will be more fruit, not less.

Andrew Murray lived in Wellington in the Cape, surrounded by vineyards, and watched the vine growers at work. In *The True Vine* he noticed something surprising about pruning:

> "It is not the removal of weeds or thorns, or anything from without that may hinder the growth. No; it is the cutting off of the long shoots of the previous year, the removal of something that comes from within, that has been produced by the life of the vine itself."
> Andrew Murray, *The True Vine*

God does not only cut away obvious sin from our lives. Sometimes He cuts away good things: a plan, a position, a comfort, even a ministry that has become too much our own. He does it so that the life of Christ in us will go into fruit rather than into leaves.

How does the Father prune? Murray points out that Jesus' next words are, "Because of the word I have spoken to you, you are clean already" (John 15:3). The Word of God is the gardener's first knife. In Murray's own words, "it is the Word of God that is the knife." As we read Scripture, the Spirit shows us what needs to go, and we let it go.

But God also uses hardship. Illness, loss, failure, disappointment and opposition can all become His pruning knife when they drive us back to His Word and closer to Christ. "At the time it is given, no discipline seems a matter for joy; it seems a matter for sorrow. But afterward it pays back those who have been trained by it with a harvest of peace: righteousness" (Hebrews 12:11). If you are going through a season of cutting, take heart. The knife is in the hand of the Father who loves you, and He cuts only branches that bear fruit, so that they will bear more.

### Fruit that lasts

What is the fruit Jesus looks for? In John 15 He names at least three kinds.

**Love.** "My commandment is this: love one another in the same way that I have loved you" (John 15:12). The first fruit of abiding is love, the mark of a Christian we explored in Chapter 4. Paul lists love first among "the fruit the Spirit produces," followed by "joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control" (Galatians 5:22–23).

**Joy.** "I have said this to you so that My joy will be in you and your joy will be complete" (John 15:11). Not the passing happiness of good circumstances, but the deep gladness of Christ Himself, which remains even in sorrow.

**Answered prayer.** "I appointed you for this: to go and bear fruit, and for your fruit to remain, so that the Father may give you whatever you ask Him in My name" (John 15:16). Prayer that grows out of abiding is prayer that God delights to answer.

Notice, too, that the fruit is for others. A vine does not eat its own grapes. The fruit of our lives feeds our families, our churches, our neighbours and the world. And it brings glory to God: "This is how My Father is glorified: that you bear much fruit and so prove to be My disciples" (John 15:8).

How do we bear such fruit? Not by straining. A branch does not grit its teeth to make grapes. It simply stays joined to the vine, and the sap does the rest. Hannah Whitall Smith, the American writer whose book on trusting God has helped many anxious believers, described those who grow well in Christ this way:

> "Their Lord told them to abide in Him. He promised that if they did, they would bear much fruit. They focus only on abiding, which is their part. They leave the rest to their good Husbandman, who alone can manage it."
> Hannah Whitall Smith, *The Christian's Secret of a Happy Life*

"Apart from Me," Jesus said, "you can do nothing" (John 15:5). That is not a threat. It is a relief. The burden of fruitfulness is not on us. Our part is to remain.

### From servants to friends

Then Jesus says something astonishing. "I do not call you servants any longer, since a servant is not aware of what his master is doing. I have called you friends instead, because I have made known to you everything I heard from My Father" (John 15:15).

A servant does what he is told without knowing why. A friend is let into the master's heart. Jesus, the eternal Son of God, calls His disciples friends. He shares His purposes with them. He laid down His life for them: "No one has a love greater than this: that someone lays down their life for their friends" (John 15:13).

This changes how we live before God's face. We do not come to Him as nervous workers waiting for orders, but as friends who are glad to be with Him. Obedience remains: "You are My friends if you carry out what I command you" (John 15:14). But it is the obedience of friendship, freely given, not the obedience of fear. Abiding is friendship with Jesus, lived out all day long.

### Brother Lawrence: God among the pots and pans

How do we carry that friendship from the quiet of the morning watch into a busy day? Few people have answered that question more simply than a kitchen worker in seventeenth-century Paris.

He was born Nicolas Herman, in the region of Lorraine in eastern France, around 1614. As a young man he served as a soldier. At about eighteen something happened that changed his life. He later told a friend that one winter he saw a tree stripped of its leaves. He thought about how, in a little while, the leaves would return, and then the flowers and the fruit. In that bare tree he saw the power and care of God, and a love for God was kindled in him that never left.

After some years, including a time as a footman, he entered a community of Carmelite brothers in Paris as a lay brother, taking the name Lawrence. He was not a priest or a scholar. He described himself, with good humour, as "a great awkward fellow who broke everything." He was given work in the kitchen, a job he naturally disliked, and he served there for about fifteen years. Later he repaired sandals for the community. He died in 1691.

Yet people came from far away to speak with this humble cook. A church official named Joseph de Beaufort visited him, recorded their conversations, and after his death gathered these together with some of Lawrence's letters. The little book became known as *The Practice of the Presence of God*, and it has been read by Christians of many traditions ever since.

Lawrence's secret was simple. He resolved to live in a continual sense of God's presence, talking with Him throughout the day as with a friend. When he began a task, he asked God for grace to do it well. When he finished, he thanked Him. When he failed, he confessed it simply and returned to God without despair. When his mind wandered, he gently brought it back. He believed, as Beaufort recorded, "that our sanctification did not depend upon changing our works, but in doing that for God's sake, which we commonly do for our own."

Those who watched him in the busiest hours of the kitchen saw a calm, unhurried man. He told Beaufort:

> "The time of business does not with me differ from the time of prayer; and in the noise and clatter of my kitchen, while several persons are at the same time calling for different things, I possess God in as great tranquility as if I were upon my knees at the blessed sacrament."
> Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*

He did not find this easy at first. In one letter he admitted that he "found no small pain in this exercise," because his mind kept wandering. But he kept returning, again and again, and over the years it became the habit of his heart. He wrote to a friend in trouble that God "requires no great matters of us; a little remembrance of Him from time to time; a little adoration." And he added, "It is not necessary for being with God to be always at church: we may make an oratory of our heart wherein to retire from time to time to converse with Him in meekness, humility and love." An oratory is a small place of prayer. Lawrence carried his with him wherever he went.

### Practising His presence through the day

Brother Lawrence shows us what abiding looks like on an ordinary weekday. It is the morning watch stretched across the whole day. Paul gives the same vision: "In everything you say and everything you do, act in the name of the Lord Jesus, and through Him give thanks to God the Father" (Colossians 3:17). David wrote, "I have placed the LORD in front of me at all times; since He is there at my right hand, I will never stumble" (Psalm 16:8).

Here are some simple ways to begin.

- **Begin each task with God.** Before you start work, cook a meal, drive, study or open the shop, say a short sentence: "Lord, I do this with You and for You."
- **Use ordinary moments as reminders.** A ringing phone, a door, a call to prayer from a nearby mosque, a bus stop, washing your hands: let these become small signals to turn your heart to God for a moment.
- **Talk with Him as you go.** Thank Him for small mercies. Ask for help with small problems. Tell Him what you see. You do not need special words.
- **Return without shame.** You will forget Him for hours at a time, as everyone does at first. When you notice, do not scold yourself. Simply turn back, as Lawrence did, and say, "Here I am again, Lord."
- **Close the day with Him.** Before sleep, look back over the day with God. Thank Him for His presence, confess where you went your own way, and rest in His care.

The aim is not to think about God every second, which is impossible while we work. It is to live with a quiet awareness that He is near, as a child playing in the yard knows her mother is in the house.

### Trusting Him in trials, decisions and disappointments

Abiding is tested when life is hard. When a child is sick, when a business fails, when we must make a difficult decision, when a hope collapses, the temptation is to stop remaining and start striving: to worry, to plan in our own strength, to pull away from the vine.

Scripture calls us back. "Trust the LORD with your whole heart, and do not rely on your own insight. Keep Him in mind in everything you do, and He will clear a straight road ahead of you" (Proverbs 3:5–6). In decisions, abiding means bringing the choice to God in prayer and in His Word, seeking wise counsel, and then stepping forward in trust, believing that He will guide us. In trials, it means staying close to Him when we do not understand, remembering that the gardener's knife is in loving hands. In disappointments, it means pouring out our hearts honestly, as the psalmists did, and letting Him carry what we cannot.

"The mind that is held steady You guard in peace, unbroken peace, for it relies on You" (Isaiah 26:3). The branch does not have to understand the storm. It only has to hold on to the vine, or rather, be held by it.

This is the life of a disciple. "Anyone who claims to remain in Him ought to live in the same way that Jesus lived" (1 John 2:6). Jesus lived every moment in the presence of His Father. He invites us to do the same, through Him. In Chapter 9 we will look at the spiritual disciplines, the practices of grace that help us stay close to the vine through every season of life.

### Questions for reflection

- What would it look like for you to "remain" in Christ during an ordinary working day?
- John 15:7 joins the Word and prayer. How are these connected in your own life with God?
- Can you think of a season when God pruned something from your life? What fruit came from it, or might come from it?
- Which kind of fruit, love, joy or answered prayer, do you most long to see in your life?
- How does it change your relationship with Jesus to know that He calls you His friend?
- What strikes you most about Brother Lawrence? What is one ordinary task you could begin to do "with God"?

### A step for this week

Choose three ordinary moments that happen every day, for example starting work, sitting down to eat, and travelling home. Each time one of these moments comes this week, pause for a few seconds and speak to God in one simple sentence, such as "Lord, I am with You and You are with me," or "Thank You for being here." Before you sleep each night, look back and notice when you remembered Him and when you forgot, without blaming yourself. At the end of the week, read John 15:1–17 slowly and thank Jesus for calling you His friend.
