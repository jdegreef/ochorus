## Servant Ministry

> "The Son of Man Himself came, not so that others would serve Him, but to serve them and to give His life as the ransom price for many."
> — Mark 10:45 (OWB)

In many parts of the world, when an important visitor comes to a home, someone brings water to wash the guest's hands or feet. It is a kindness, but it is usually done by the youngest or the lowest in the household. No one expects the head of the family to kneel with the basin. And no one would ever expect the important visitor to do it.

Yet on the night before He died, the Son of God got up from the table, took a towel and a basin, and knelt before His friends. Everything we will say about ministry in this chapter flows from that scene.

In Chapter 21 we saw how Christ organises His Church, and in Chapter 22 how He gifts every believer. Now we ask: in what spirit should we use those offices and gifts? Jesus' answer is clear. We use them as servants.

### The basin and the towel

John tells the story with great care. He begins with what Jesus knew: "Jesus knew that the Father had put everything into His hands. He knew too that He had come out from God and was on His way back to God" (John 13:3). Then he tells what Jesus did: "He got up from the meal, put aside His outer garments, took a towel and tied it around His waist. Next He poured water into the basin and began washing the disciples' feet" (John 13:4–5).

Notice the order. Jesus did not wash feet in order to discover who He was. He washed feet because He already knew who He was. He was secure in the Father's love, sure of where He had come from and where He was going. Because He had nothing to prove, He was free to take the lowest place.

This is the secret of servant ministry. People who are insecure need titles, seats at the front and praise from others. People who know they are loved by the Father before His face can kneel with a basin without losing anything.

Then Jesus explained what He had done: "So if I, the Lord and the Teacher, have washed your feet, then you too have a duty to wash each other's feet. What I gave you was an example, so that you also would do as I have done for you" (John 13:14–15). And He added a promise: "Now you know these things; you are blessed if you act on them" (John 13:17). The blessing is not in knowing about service. It is in doing it.

### Leadership in the Kingdom

The disciples found this lesson very hard. Not long before, James and John had asked for the best seats in Jesus' coming Kingdom, and the other ten were angry with them, probably because they wanted those seats too. Jesus called them together and showed them two kinds of leadership.

The first is the world's kind: "You know that those who are thought of as rulers of the nations lord it over their people, and their important men use their power over them" (Mark 10:42). We have all seen this kind. A manager who shouts at workers. A politician who grows rich while the people grow poor. Sadly, even a church leader who expects to be served and is quick to remind others of his position.

The second is the Kingdom's kind: "That is not how it is among you. Instead, whoever among you wants to become great must be your servant, and whoever among you wants to be first must serve everyone as a slave" (Mark 10:43–44). And the reason is Jesus Himself: "The Son of Man Himself came, not so that others would serve Him, but to serve them" (Mark 10:45).

Kingdom leadership turns the pyramid upside down. The leader is not at the top with everyone serving him. He is at the bottom, holding others up. Paul says that Christ, though He was in the form of God, "emptied Himself, taking on the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:7), and that we should "think in the way that Christ Jesus also thought" (Philippians 2:5).

This does not mean leaders never lead. Jesus led His disciples firmly. He corrected them, taught them and sent them out. But He led for their good, not for His own comfort. A servant leader still makes decisions, still says hard things and still carries authority. The difference is in the heart: he uses his authority to lift others up, not to lift himself up.

Andrew Murray, the South African pastor, wrote a small book called *Humility* that has helped many leaders. He saw that our humility before God is tested in how we treat people:

> "Humility before God is nothing if not proved in humility before men."
> Andrew Murray, *Humility*

And he described what this looks like in daily life:

> "The spirit of Him who washed the disciples' feet makes it a joy to us to be indeed the least, to be servants of one another. The humble man feels no jealousy or envy. He can praise God when others are preferred and blessed before him."
> Andrew Murray, *Humility*

Can you praise God when someone else is chosen for the role you wanted? That is a good test of a servant's heart.

### Serving the poor, the widow, the orphan and the stranger

Servant ministry is not only for the church building. Throughout Scripture, God shows special care for four kinds of people who were often forgotten: the poor, the widow, the orphan and the stranger. God "sees that orphans and widows get justice, and He loves the outsider living among you and gives them food and clothing" (Deuteronomy 10:18).

Through Isaiah, God told His people that religious fasting meant little if they ignored the needy. The fast He wanted was this: "to break your bread with someone who is hungry, to take the wandering poor into your home, to clothe the naked person you meet, and never to turn your back on your own kin" (Isaiah 58:7). And He promised: "if you give of yourself to the hungry and meet the needs of those who suffer, then your light will dawn in the darkness" (Isaiah 58:10).

The prophet Micah summed it up: "And what does the LORD ask of you except to do justice, to love loyalty, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8). James echoes the same truth in the New Testament: "To God the Father, religion that is pure and untainted looks like this: going to the help of orphans and widows in their trouble, and keeping yourself unstained by the world" (James 1:27).

Then Jesus said something that should change how we see every needy person. In His picture of the final judgment, the King says, "For when I was hungry, you gave Me food; when I was thirsty, you gave Me a drink; when I was a stranger, you took Me into your home" (Matthew 25:35). The righteous are surprised, and the King explains: "each time you did it for one of these My brothers and sisters, even the least of them, you did it for Me" (Matthew 25:40).

This is coram Deo in its most practical form. When you carry a meal to a widow, you are not only before her face. You are before Christ's face. When you welcome a refugee family or sit with a prisoner, you are serving the King Himself.

Christians in Latin America such as René Padilla of Ecuador have called the Church to what they named integral mission: proclaiming Christ in word and serving the whole person in deed, never setting one against the other. Jesus did both. He preached the Kingdom and He fed the hungry. The Church at its best has always done the same, through hospitals, schools, orphanages and countless kitchens. In the nineteenth century, William and Catherine Booth (whom we met in Chapter 22) took the gospel into the poorest streets of East London. The Salvation Army they founded preached Christ to drunkards and thieves, and also opened shelters, served food and found work for the jobless. Today its officers serve in many nations, including across East Africa, often in places others avoid.

### Toyohiko Kagawa: a servant in the slums of Kobe

One of the most remarkable servants of the twentieth century came from Japan. Toyohiko Kagawa was born in Kobe in 1888. He lost both his parents when he was a small child and grew up a lonely boy in the home of his father's family. As a teenager he went to American missionaries to learn English, and there he met Jesus Christ. He became a Christian, and his family cut him off.

Kagawa studied for the ministry, but his health was poor and he became seriously ill with tuberculosis. He nearly died. When he recovered, he made a decision that shocked those who knew him. On Christmas Eve 1909, when he was twenty-one, he moved into Shinkawa, one of the worst slums in Kobe. He lived in a tiny room, only a few paces across, among thieves, beggars, gamblers, the sick and the dying.

He did not visit the poor from a distance. He lived among them. He shared his small room and his food with people who had nowhere else to go. He preached in the streets, cared for the sick and buried the dead. He caught trachoma, an eye disease that damaged his sight for the rest of his life. He married Haru, a young factory worker, and she joined him in the work.

Kagawa came to see that serving the poor meant more than giving bread. It also meant asking why so many were poor. He worked to help labourers organise for fair wages, started cooperatives so that poor families could buy and sell together, and was arrested more than once. In 1920 he published a novel drawn from his life in the slums, *Before the Dawn*, which sold widely across Japan, and he gave much of its income to his work among the poor. After the great earthquake that destroyed much of Tokyo in 1923, he led relief work among the homeless. Later he travelled the country preaching in what he called the Kingdom of God movement, calling his nation to Christ. He died in 1960.

Kagawa was not perfect, and like every servant of God he had his limits. But his life shows that the call of Mark 10:45 is not only for missionaries far away. It can be lived out by a sick young man in his own city, who decides that the face of Christ is to be found among the poorest of his neighbours.

### Ministry flowing from intimacy, not striving

There is a danger in all this. We can become so busy serving that we forget the One we serve. We can work for God while drifting far from God.

Martha welcomed Jesus into her home and worked hard to prepare a meal. Her sister Mary sat at Jesus' feet and listened. When Martha complained, Jesus answered gently: "Martha, Martha, you are fretting and in a stir about so many things. Yet there is need of few things, or only of one. Mary has picked the good share" (Luke 10:41–42). Jesus did not despise Martha's service. But He knew that service which does not flow from His presence soon becomes anxious, resentful and tired.

As we saw in Chapter 8, Jesus said, "Whoever remains in Me, with Me remaining in them, is the one who bears much fruit, because you can do nothing apart from Me" (John 15:5). Ministry is meant to be fruit, not factory work. Fruit grows from a branch that stays joined to the vine.

Andrew Murray put it plainly in his book *Working for God!*:

> "As waiting on God lies at the root of all true working for God, so working for God must be the fruit of all true waiting on Him."
> Andrew Murray, *Working for God!*

He also warned that the Church suffers "because so much working for God is done without waiting on God" (*Working for God!*).

Francis of Assisi understood this. Born in Italy around 1181 to a wealthy cloth merchant, he gave up his comfortable life to follow Christ in poverty. He served lepers, whom he had once found hard even to look at, and he later wrote that the Lord Himself led him among them. Francis and his brothers worked and begged among the poor, but they also withdrew often to lonely places to pray. His service was strong because his prayer was deep.

The same is true for us. The nurse who begins her shift with a few quiet minutes before God, the youth leader who prays over each name before the meeting, the farmer who gives thanks in the field before lending his ox to a neighbour: their service carries a fragrance that busy activity alone cannot produce. The first service we owe is to sit at Jesus' feet.

### Avoiding burnout: rest and healthy boundaries

Many faithful servants of God burn out. A pastor works seven days a week for years and then collapses. A mother cares for everyone but herself until she is empty. A church volunteer says yes to every request until she can no longer bear to enter the building.

Scripture takes this seriously. When Moses tried to judge every dispute among the people alone, his father-in-law Jethro said: "This way of doing things is not good. You are sure to wear out, both you and the people who are with you. The load is too heavy for you; you cannot carry it by yourself" (Exodus 18:17–18). So Moses shared the work with others, which is exactly the pattern of plural leadership we saw in Chapter 21.

When Elijah was exhausted and wanted to die, God did not scold him. He let him sleep, and then an angel touched him and said, "Get up and eat" (1 Kings 19:5). Sometimes the most spiritual thing a tired servant can do is rest and eat a good meal.

Jesus Himself cared for His tired disciples. When crowds pressed on them so that "they could not even find time for a meal", He said, "Come away on your own, to a place without people, and get a little rest" (Mark 6:31). Jesus regularly withdrew to pray, even when many still needed Him (Mark 1:35).

As Chapter 11 explained, Jesus is our Sabbath rest. He invites us: "Come to Me, all of you who are worn out with work and weighed down with burdens, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Every day we can serve from a place of resting in Him, relying on God rather than our own strength. Regular physical rest also remains God's good gift and a wise rhythm for servants and leaders alike.

Here are a few healthy boundaries for those who serve:

- **Remember you are not the Saviour.** Jesus is. You are His servant. The needs of the world are endless, but you are not called to meet them all.
- **Share the load.** Train others, delegate, and let the whole body serve, as Chapters 21 and 22 taught.
- **Guard your time with God.** Ministry that eats up your prayer life will soon have nothing left to give.
- **Guard your family.** Your spouse and children are your first ministry, not your last.
- **Take regular rest.** A day each week, and seasons of refreshment, are not laziness but obedience.

Paul encourages us: "We must not tire of doing good. When the right time comes we will bring in the harvest, as long as we do not slacken" (Galatians 6:9). The way not to grow weary over a lifetime is to keep returning to the One who gives strength.

### Serving before His face

Servant ministry is not about grand titles or famous projects. It is about a basin and a towel, and a heart that knows it is loved. It is the deacon fixing a leaking roof, the teenager reading to a blind neighbour, the mother of five who still finds room for one more child at her table, and the pastor who visits a dying man at midnight.

Paul adds: "So whenever we have the chance, we should do good to everyone, and above all to those who belong to the family of faith" (Galatians 6:10). Every day brings such chances. Most of them are small and easy to miss: a colleague who is struggling after a death in the family, a child at church who sits alone, a neighbour whose crops failed this season, a stranger at the bus stop who does not know the city. Servants learn to notice. They ask the Lord each morning, "Whom will You put in my path today?" and then they keep their eyes open. And every time we take one, we do it before the face of God, for Christ Himself.

This leads into Part Five and Chapter 24, where we see that the greatest service of all is to carry the good news of Jesus to those who have never heard.

### Questions for reflection

- Jesus washed feet because He knew who He was and where He was going (John 13:3). How does being secure in God's love free you to serve?
- Where have you seen the world's kind of leadership, and where have you seen Kingdom leadership? What made the difference?
- Who are the "least of these" in your neighbourhood or city? How might you serve Christ by serving them?
- What can you learn from Toyohiko Kagawa's decision to live among the people he served?
- Are you more like Martha or Mary at the moment? What would help your service flow from time with Jesus?
- Which of the healthy boundaries listed above do you most need to practise?

### A step for this week

Do one act of hidden service this week for someone who cannot repay you: a widow, an orphan, a stranger, a sick neighbour or a prisoner's family. Cook a meal, mend something, pay a bill, or simply sit and listen. Tell no one about it. Before you go, spend fifteen minutes alone with Jesus, reading John 13:1–17 slowly, and ask Him to give you His servant heart. Afterwards, thank Him that what you did for the least, you did for Him.
