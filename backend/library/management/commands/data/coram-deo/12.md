## Faith and Trusting God

> "And no one can please God without faith, since anyone who approaches God has to believe both that He exists and that He repays those who search for Him."
> — Hebrews 11:6 (OWB)

A tired woman walks into a village church and sits down on a wooden bench at the back. She does not first examine the bench, test its legs or ask who made it. She simply sits, and the bench holds her. She may know nothing about carpentry. Her faith is not in her knowledge, or in how strongly she feels about benches. It is in the bench. And because it holds, she can rest.

That simple picture tells us a great deal about faith. In the last chapter Jesus told His disciples not to worry, but to seek first the Kingdom of God and trust their Father for everything else (see Chapter 11). That is easy to say on a calm day. It is harder when the school fees are due, the harvest has failed, the diagnosis is frightening, or the job has gone. This chapter asks what faith really is, how it grows, and how we can trust God in the details of daily life. To live *coram Deo*, before the face of God, is to live by faith, because the God whose face we seek is unseen. "We make our way by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:7).

### What biblical faith is, and what it is not

The great chapter on faith in the Bible, Hebrews 11, begins with a definition:

> "Now faith is the assurance of what we hope for, the proof of realities we do not see." (Hebrews 11:1)

Faith is assurance. It is a settled confidence that God's promises are true, even when we cannot yet see them fulfilled. It treats unseen things as real, because God has spoken.

Then comes our opening verse. Without faith it is impossible to please God, because "anyone who approaches God has to believe both that He exists and that He repays those who search for Him" (Hebrews 11:6). Notice the two parts. Faith believes that God *is*: that He is real, present and exactly who He says He is. And faith believes that God *rewards* those who seek Him: that He is good, that He cares, and that coming to Him is never wasted.

It also helps to say what faith is not.

- **Faith is not a feeling.** Feelings rise and fall with our health, the weather and the news. Faith rests on God's Word, not on our mood. Some days we feel full of faith and are not; other days we feel weak and are trusting God more than we know.
- **Faith is not positive thinking.** Telling yourself that all will be well is not biblical faith. Faith is not confidence in our own minds; it is confidence in God.
- **Faith is not a force to make God do what we want.** Some teachers suggest that if we believe hard enough, God must give us wealth or health. But faith does not control God. It trusts Him, even when His answer is "no" or "wait."
- **Faith is not blind.** It does not ignore facts. It sees the facts clearly and then sees something bigger: the faithfulness of God.

The strength of faith lies not in how much faith we have, but in whom we trust. Jesus said that faith even "the size of a mustard seed" (Matthew 17:20) can move mountains. A small faith in a great God achieves more than great faith in a small god. When a desperate father cried to Jesus, "I believe! Help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24), Jesus did not send him away. He healed his son.

### Abraham, the father of faith

The Bible's great example of faith is Abraham. When God called him, he was living comfortably in Mesopotamia. God told him to leave his country and go to a land He would show him. "By faith Abraham obeyed God's call to leave for a land he would later be given as his own, and he set out without knowing where he was headed" (Hebrews 11:8).

Years passed, and Abraham and Sarah still had no child. One night God took him outside and told him to count the stars, if he could. God said, "Your descendants will be like that" (Genesis 15:5). And then comes one of the most important sentences in the whole Bible:

> "Abram put his trust in the LORD, and the LORD counted it to his credit as righteousness." (Genesis 15:6)

Abraham did not earn God's approval by his good works. He believed God's promise, and God counted him righteous. This is the gospel in seed form. Paul quotes this verse in Romans 4 to show that we too are made right with God through faith in Christ.

But Abraham's faith did not stop at that first moment. It was tested for years. He was nearly a hundred years old, and Sarah was long past the age of having children. Paul writes:

> "His faith did not weaken when he looked at his own body, as good as dead now that he was about a hundred years old, or at the deadness of Sarah's womb. He did not doubt God's promise or waver in unbelief. Instead he grew strong in faith and gave glory to God, fully convinced that God had the power to carry out what He had promised." (Romans 4:19–21)

Abraham did not pretend the facts were different. He looked straight at them. Then he looked at God, and God was bigger.

### Faith that grows through the Word and through testing

How does faith grow? The Bible gives two main answers.

**Through hearing God's Word.** "So faith comes from hearing the message, and the message is heard through the word about Christ" (Romans 10:17). We cannot trust a God we do not know, and we come to know Him through His Word. Every promise we read, every story of His faithfulness, every revelation of His character, feeds our faith. That is why the daily reading of Scripture (see Chapter 5) is not just a religious duty. It is how trust is built.

**Through testing.** James writes something surprising:

> "My brothers and sisters, whenever you find yourselves in trials of every sort, regard it as a reason for great joy, because you know that the testing your faith goes through produces endurance." (James 1:2–3)

A muscle grows by being stretched under weight. A young tree sends its roots deeper when the wind blows. In the same way, faith grows strong when it is tested. Peter says that faith "once tested and shown to be genuine" (1 Peter 1:7) is worth far more than gold.

George Müller, whose story we tell below, wrote from long experience:

> "Wherever God has given faith, it is given, among other reasons, for the very purpose of being tried."
> George Müller, *The Life of Trust*

He added that God tests us gently at first, little by little, as a wise teacher gives a pupil harder lessons only as they grow. If you are in a trial today, it does not mean God has forgotten you. It may mean He is strengthening you.

### The orphan father of Bristol: George Müller

George Müller was born in 1805 in Prussia, in what is now Germany. As a young man he was a liar and a thief. He stole money from his own father, ran up debts at hotels he could not pay, and was once put in prison. He was studying theology at the University of Halle, but only because a pastor's life seemed comfortable. Then, in 1825, a friend invited him to a small Bible study in a private home. He saw ordinary believers kneel and pray with sincerity, and something broke open in him. He gave his life to Christ.

Müller came to England as a young missionary and in 1832 settled in Bristol as a pastor. The city's streets were full of orphaned children, many hungry and uncared for. Müller longed to help them. But he had a deeper reason for starting an orphan house. Many Christians he knew believed in God in theory but did not trust Him in practice. He wanted to give them visible proof that God still answers prayer. He wrote:

> "Now, if I, a poor man, simply by prayer and faith, obtained, without asking any individual, the means for establishing and carrying on an orphan house, there would be something which, with the Lord's blessing, might be instrumental in strengthening the faith of the children of God."
> George Müller, *The Life of Trust*

In April 1836 he opened his first orphan house in a rented building in Wilberforce Street. He made a firm decision: he would never ask anyone for money, never go into debt, and never make the needs known except to God. He would simply pray.

There were many hard days. His journal records one of them, in November 1838:

> "Never were we so reduced in funds as to-day. There was not a single halfpenny in hand between the matrons of the three houses."
> George Müller, *Answers to Prayer*

That afternoon Müller prayed with his fellow workers and told them they must wait and see how the Lord would help. He felt cold and decided to walk home the long way. Twenty yards from his door, he met a believer who had twice called at his house while he was out. The man gave him money for the poor, and five pounds for the orphans. Had he come home half a minute later, Müller wrote, he would have missed him. "But the Lord knew our need, and therefore allowed me to meet him."

The work grew far beyond anything he could have planned. From 1849 he built large orphan houses on Ashley Down, on the edge of Bristol. In the end five great houses cared for more than two thousand children at a time. Over his lifetime, more than ten thousand orphans were cared for, fed, clothed and taught. Every penny came in answer to prayer. Near the end of his life, Müller could write:

> "Never since the Orphan work has been in existence have I asked one single human being for any help for this work; and yet, unasked for, simply in answer to prayer, from so many parts of the world, as has been stated, the donations have come in, and that very frequently at a time of the greatest need."
> George Müller, *Answers to Prayer*

Some people said Müller must have a special gift of faith that ordinary Christians do not have. He strongly denied it. The faith he exercised, he said, was "the self-same faith which is found in every believer," and it had grown little by little over many years. His secret was not a special gift. It was a daily habit of reading the Bible, believing its promises and bringing every need to God. He died in 1898, at the age of ninety-two. He had lived his whole life before the face of a God he could not see, and he found Him faithful every single day.

### Trusting God's promises when circumstances say otherwise

There will be times when everything you see seems to contradict what God has said. The promise says, "My God will fill every need you have" (Philippians 4:19), but the bank account is empty. The promise says He works all things together for good (Romans 8:28), but the doctor's report is bad.

The prophet Habakkuk faced such a time. He saw a terrible invasion coming on his people, and he could not understand why God would allow it. But at the end of his book he wrote one of the bravest songs of faith ever sung:

> "Though the fig tree does not flower and the vines bear no grapes, though the olive harvest fails and the fields yield nothing to eat, though the sheep vanish from the pen and the stalls hold no cattle, yet I will rejoice in the LORD; I will find joy in God, my Savior." (Habakkuk 3:17–18)

Any farmer understands what Habakkuk is describing: total failure of every crop and every animal. Yet he says, "yet I will rejoice in the LORD" (Habakkuk 3:18). His joy is not in the harvest. It is in God.

Faith in hard times does not deny the pain. It holds on to God's character. "God is not a human, who might lie, nor a mortal, who might change course" (Numbers 23:19). If He has promised, He will keep His word, in His way and in His time, which is always best. Sometimes the answer comes quickly, as it did for Müller on the cold walk home. Sometimes it comes after many years, as it did for Abraham. And sometimes, as Hebrews says of many faithful people, they "did not receive what was promised, but they saw it from far off and welcomed it" (Hebrews 11:13). Faith trusts God even with the promises that will only be fulfilled in eternity.

### Living by faith in finances, decisions and calling

Faith is not only for great crises. It is for ordinary days.

**In finances.** Müller's way of never asking for money was a special calling. Not everyone is called to run an orphanage without appeals or salary. But every believer is called to trust God with money. That means working honestly and diligently, giving generously, avoiding debts we cannot repay, and bringing our needs to God in prayer before we panic. Hudson Taylor, the pioneer missionary to China, learned this before he ever left England. As a young medical assistant, he decided not to remind his employer when his salary was due, but to ask God to remind him instead. He wrote that his aim was to learn:

> "to move man, through God, by prayer alone."
> Hudson Taylor, *A Retrospect*

**In decisions.** Proverbs gives a promise many believers hold close:

> "Trust the LORD with your whole heart, and do not rely on your own insight. Keep Him in mind in everything you do, and He will clear a straight road ahead of you." (Proverbs 3:5–6)

This does not mean we stop thinking. God gave us minds. It means we do not lean on our own understanding alone. We pray, seek His Word, ask counsel, and then step forward trusting Him to guide and correct us.

**In calling.** Sometimes faith means stepping into something God has called us to when the way ahead is not clear. Gladys Aylward was a parlourmaid in London, a servant who cleaned other people's homes. She believed God was calling her to China, but a missionary society turned her down because they thought she could not learn the language. She did not give up. She saved her small wages, and in 1932 she set off alone, by train across Europe and Russia, with almost nothing. In China she worked with an older missionary at an inn for mule drivers, telling Bible stories to the travellers in the evenings. She learned the local language well, became a citizen of China, and took in orphaned children. During the war with Japan, in 1940, she led about a hundred children on a long and dangerous journey over the mountains to safety. An ordinary woman with an ordinary education trusted an extraordinary God.

### Faith and works together

Some people worry that the Bible contradicts itself. Paul says we are saved by faith, not by works (Ephesians 2:8–9). James says, "It is the same with faith: if it has no works, it is dead in itself" (James 2:17). Which is true?

Both are. Paul is speaking about how we are made right with God: not by our works, but by trusting Christ. James is speaking about what true faith looks like: it always produces a changed life. Paul himself says straight after, "For we are God's own work. In Christ Jesus He created us for good works" (Ephesians 2:10). We are saved by faith alone, but the faith that saves is never alone.

Think again of Abraham. He believed God's promise, and that was counted as righteousness. But his faith also moved his feet. He left his home. Years later he obeyed God even when it seemed to cost him his son (Hebrews 11:17–19). His faith and his actions worked together.

Or think of Müller. He prayed and trusted God for every meal. But he also worked tirelessly, kept careful accounts, built houses, hired teachers and cared for each child. His faith was never lazy. Real faith works, and real works flow from faith. A tree is not made alive by its fruit, but a living tree will bear fruit.

### Living before the unseen face

Faith, in the end, is how we live before the face of God while we cannot yet see Him. Abraham "set out without knowing where he was headed" (Hebrews 11:8), but he knew who was leading him. Müller did not know where tomorrow's bread would come from, but he knew the Father who had promised daily bread. Gladys Aylward did not know the way over the mountains, but she knew the One who walked with her.

Faith says, "Lord, I cannot see You, but I know You are here. I cannot see the way, but I know You see it. I will trust You." And God, who rewards those who seek Him, is pleased.

Trusting God also leads us into holiness. When we believe that God is truly good and truly present, we want to please Him, and sin loses its attraction. That is where we turn next, in Chapter 13.

### Questions for reflection

- How would you explain biblical faith to a new believer? What is faith not?
- What part of Abraham's story encourages you most in your own waiting?
- What trial in your life right now might be God's way of strengthening your faith?
- What strikes you about George Müller's story? Where do you need to bring a practical need to God in prayer rather than worry?
- In which decision are you being tempted to lean on your own understanding alone?
- How do faith and works fit together in your own life? Is there an act of obedience your faith is calling for?

### A step for this week

Write down one practical need you are facing, such as money, work, health or a decision, and one promise of God from this chapter that speaks to it. Each morning this week, read that promise aloud and pray about the need specifically, telling no one else unless wisdom requires it. Then do the one practical thing that faith requires of you, whether a hard conversation, a job application or a budget. Keep a short record, as Müller did, of how God answers, and at the end of the week thank Him for what He has done and for what you are still trusting Him to do.
