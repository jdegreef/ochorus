## The Organisation of the Church: Ministries and Offices

> "It was He who gave some to be apostles, some prophets, some evangelists, and some shepherds and teachers. He gave them to equip God's holy people for the work of serving, so that the body of Christ would be built up."
> — Ephesians 4:11–12 (OWB)

Think of a house being built in a growing town. There are stones, timber, iron sheets and cement, and many willing hands. But if no one knows where the walls should stand, and no one checks that the foundation is level, the house will not survive the first rains. Willing hands are not enough. A building needs a plan, and people who serve the plan.

In Chapter 21 we saw that the Church is God's family, the body and bride of Christ. A body needs joints and ligaments as well as eyes and hands. So Christ, who loves His Church, has given it a shape. He gives some people to lead, teach and shepherd, and He gives every believer a part to play.

This chapter is about that shape: the ministries Christ gives, the offices of elder and deacon, how men and women serve together, and the truth that every Christian is a priest and a minister. This is not only church business for committee meetings. How a church is led shapes how its people grow. And all of it is done coram Deo, before the face of God, who sees the preacher in the pulpit and the deacon carrying chairs with the same eyes.

### Christ, the Head who gives leaders

Before we speak of any human leader, we begin with the true Head. God "has given Him to the church to be head over all things" (Ephesians 1:22). Christ "is also the head of the body, the church" (Colossians 1:18). No pastor, bishop or elder owns the Church. It belongs to Jesus, who won it "through His own blood" (Acts 20:28).

So leaders are not owners but stewards, under-shepherds serving the Chief Shepherd. And they are His gifts: "It was He who gave" (Ephesians 4:11). Why did He give them? "To equip God's holy people for the work of serving" (Ephesians 4:12). The leaders do not do all the work. They equip the saints, and the saints do the work. The goal is maturity, "until all of us arrive at the unity of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God" (Ephesians 4:13).

The measure of a good leader, then, is not how busy he is or how many come to hear him. It is whether the people around him are growing up into Christ. A coach is judged not by how well he plays, but by how well his team plays. Christ gives leaders so that His whole body "grows and builds itself up in love" (Ephesians 4:16).

### The equipping ministries of Ephesians 4

Paul names apostles, prophets, evangelists, and shepherds and teachers. Many readers take "shepherds and teachers" as one ministry, the pastor-teacher.

Faithful Christians differ on whether apostles and prophets continue today. Some believe these belonged to the founding of the Church, which is "built on the apostles and prophets as your foundation" (Ephesians 2:20), and a foundation is laid once. Others believe that, while the first apostles were unique, God still sends apostolic pioneers and still gives prophetic words. Many stand in between, preferring words such as "missionary" and "church planter".

Yet the common ground is wide. All agree that the Twelve and Paul were unique witnesses of the risen Christ, that no one today adds to Scripture, and that every word claiming to come from God must be tested by it. And all agree that the work these ministries describe is still needed.

**Apostles** are "sent ones". The first apostles laid the foundation of the Church with the teaching we now hold in the New Testament. In a wider sense, the church at Antioch sent out Barnabas and Saul after the Holy Spirit said, "Set Barnabas and Saul apart for Me, for the work I have called them to do" (Acts 13:2). Such people pioneer: they go where Christ is not known, plant churches and lay foundations.

**Prophets** hear and speak God's word to strengthen His people: "someone who prophesies speaks to people, and the words build them up, encourage them and comfort them" (1 Corinthians 14:3). This is not a new Bible, but a word in season, often bringing Scripture home to a particular people at a particular time. It must always be tested: "do not look down on prophecy. Test everything; keep hold of what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:20–21).

**Evangelists** proclaim the good news, like "Philip the evangelist" (Acts 21:8), who explained Isaiah to an Ethiopian official on a desert road. They also equip the whole Church to share the gospel. Paul told Timothy, a pastor, to "do the work of an evangelist" (2 Timothy 4:5). We return to this in Chapter 25.

**Pastor-teachers** shepherd, feed and protect the flock. God promised, "I will give you shepherds who are after My own heart, and they will shepherd you with knowledge and insight" (Jeremiah 3:15). A shepherd leads the sheep to good pasture, which for God's people is His Word, and guards them from wolves, which means guarding them from false teaching.

### The pastor's heart for soul care

One part of a pastor's work is easy to forget in a busy church: the care of souls. Through Ezekiel, God rebuked the leaders who fed themselves but not the flock: "You have not strengthened the weak or healed the sick or bandaged the injured. You have not brought back the strays or gone looking for the lost" (Ezekiel 34:4). Then He promised to do it Himself: "I will search for the lost and bring back the strays. I will bandage the injured and strengthen the sick" (Ezekiel 34:16).

That is the heart of the Good Shepherd, and it should be the heart of every pastor. In Chapter 15 we saw that many people come to Christ carrying deep wounds: rejection, grief, abuse, shame. Their spirit has been made new, but their soul still needs healing and growth. A central calling of pastors is to teach, help and heal believers, so that they grow to maturity in Christ. The Lord "heals those whose hearts are crushed and bandages their injuries" (Psalm 147:3), and He often does it through shepherds who sit with the wounded, listen, pray and open the Word. Wise pastors also know when to involve others, as Chapter 15 explained. But the sheep need to be known.

In the seventeenth century, Richard Baxter was pastor in the English town of Kidderminster. He decided that Sunday preaching was not enough. With his assistant he gave Monday and Tuesday each week to meeting families in their homes, about fifteen or sixteen households a week, to teach them the faith and speak with each person about their soul. Over the years the town was changed. In *The Reformed Pastor* he urged other ministers to do the same, but first to watch over their own hearts:

> "Take heed to yourselves, lest your example contradict your doctrine, and lest you lay such stumbling-blocks before the blind, as may be the occasion of their ruin; lest you unsay with your lives, what you say with your tongues."
> Richard Baxter, *The Reformed Pastor*

A pastor who helps heal others must himself live close to the Healer.

### Elders: shepherds who watch over souls

The New Testament gives the Church two lasting offices: elders and deacons. Elders are also called overseers, and the two words describe the same people (compare Titus 1:5 and 1:7). "Elder" speaks of maturity; "overseer" speaks of watching over the flock.

From the beginning, the apostles made sure each church had elders. Paul and Barnabas "appointed elders for the believers, and after praying and fasting, they placed them in the care of the Lord in whom they had believed" (Acts 14:23). Notice the plural: churches were led by a group of elders, not one person alone.

Elders shepherd, teach, guard sound doctrine and give spiritual oversight. Paul told the elders of Ephesus, "Be on your guard, for yourselves and for the whole flock" (Acts 20:28). Peter wrote: "Tend God's flock among you as shepherds do. Take care of it freely, the way God would have it, not because you are forced to; not to make money for yourselves, but gladly and readily. Do not rule harshly over the people given into your charge; set an example for the flock instead" (1 Peter 5:2–3). An elder "must hold firmly to the trustworthy message as it has been taught, so that he can encourage others with sound teaching and correct those who oppose it" (Titus 1:9).

Most striking is the list of qualifications in 1 Timothy 3 and Titus 1. Almost all of it is about character. An overseer must be "someone no one can fairly criticize: faithful to his one wife, clear-headed, self-controlled, respected, welcoming to guests, able to teach" (1 Timothy 3:2). He must not be a heavy drinker, violent, quarrelsome or a lover of money. He must manage his household well, must not be a recent convert, and must have a good name among outsiders. Only "able to teach" is a skill. The Church does not first ask, "Can he preach?" It asks, "Is he faithful at home? Is he honest in the market? Do his neighbours respect him?"

The flock, in turn, honours such leaders: "Follow your leaders and yield to them. They stay awake watching over your souls, as people who will have to answer for them" (Hebrews 13:17).

### Deacons: serving so the Word is not neglected

The second office grew out of a practical problem. In Jerusalem, the Greek-speaking widows "were being passed over when food was given out each day" (Acts 6:1). The apostles said, "It is not fitting for us to leave aside the word of God in order to serve at tables" (Acts 6:2). So the church chose seven men "of good reputation, full of the Spirit and of wisdom" (Acts 6:3), while the apostles would "hold fast to prayer and to the service of the word" (Acts 6:4). The result: "So God's word went on spreading" (Acts 6:7).

Deacons serve the practical needs of the body: caring for the poor, managing money and property, organising, visiting, mending. This is not lesser work. Among the first seven were Stephen, the first martyr, and Philip the evangelist. Paul's standard is high: "deacons must be worthy of respect, sincere in what they say, not heavy drinkers, and not greedy for money. They must hold on to the mystery of the faith with a clear conscience" (1 Timothy 3:8–9).

Churches arrange these offices differently: some have bishops, some are led by elders joined in a presbytery, some are governed by the congregation. The Appendix treats these as secondary matters, important but not reasons to break fellowship. What matters most is that every church has godly shepherds and godly servants, both under Christ.

### Men and women in the Church

Scripture begins with a clear truth: "So God created human beings in His image. In the image of God He created them; male and female He created them" (Genesis 1:27). Men and women are equal in worth and dignity. In Christ, "there is no male and female, for all of you are one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). Both receive the Spirit, both are heirs of grace, both are called to serve.

This book's view is that men and women, equal in worth, have complementary roles in the Church that mirror those of husbands and wives in the family, which we explored in Chapter 18. Paul writes that "Christ is the head of every man, man is the head of woman, and God is the head of Christ" (1 Corinthians 11:3). The Son is fully equal with the Father, yet He gladly honours the Father's headship, so headship and equality are not enemies. The passages on elders describe the overseer as a husband, and Paul gives instructions about teaching and authority in the gathered church in 1 Timothy 2:11–15. On this reading, the office of elder is entrusted to qualified men, while women serve in a wide range of vital ministries.

And that range is wide. Paul commends "our sister Phoebe, a deacon of the church in Cenchreae" (Romans 16:1), who was "a patron to many" (Romans 16:2). When the gifted preacher Apollos needed fuller teaching, "Priscilla and Aquila heard him there, and they took him in and taught him the way of God more precisely" (Acts 18:26). Paul greets Mary, "who has worked hard for you" (Romans 16:6). And it was women who stayed at the cross, and women who first found the tomb empty and carried the news of the resurrection to the apostles (Luke 24:1–10; John 20:17–18). Many churches in Africa, Asia and Latin America would hardly exist without praying mothers, Bible women and faithful sisters. A church that overlooks its women has not understood the New Testament.

We should also say honestly that many faithful Christians, called egalitarians, read these passages differently. They believe 1 Timothy 2 addressed a particular situation in Ephesus, and that all ministries and offices are open to both men and women. As the Appendix explains, this is a secondary issue. It may shape which church we join, but it should not break our fellowship as brothers and sisters in the same Lord.

### Character before gifting

A gifted leader with a crooked heart is one of the most dangerous people in any church. Gifts can draw crowds; only character carries weight over many years. Charles Spurgeon, who trained hundreds of young men at his Pastors' College in London, urged his students:

> "We must live as under the more immediate eye of God, and as in the blaze of the great all-revealing day. Holiness in a minister is at once his chief necessity and his goodliest ornament."
> Charles Spurgeon, *Lectures to My Students*

That is coram Deo in the life of a leader: in the study, at home, and when no one in the congregation is watching. Scripture adds three safeguards. Leadership is **servant leadership**, for "whoever among you wants to become great must be your servant" (Mark 10:43), as Chapter 24 explores. It is **plural**: a team of elders shares the load, combines gifts and guards against pride. And it is **accountable**: a leader who answers to no one is in great danger, and so is his flock.

### Bakht Singh: a church that belonged to its people

Bakht Singh was born in 1903 into a Sikh family in the Punjab, in a region now part of Pakistan. As a young man he went abroad to study engineering, first in England and then in Canada. He had no interest in Christianity. But in Canada, around 1929, he began reading the New Testament, and the Lord Jesus became real to him.

In 1933 he returned to India. His family found his new faith very hard to accept, and following Jesus cost him dearly. He gave himself to preaching, often in the open air, and many came to faith. But he soon saw a problem. New believers had nowhere to grow, and many churches of the time felt foreign to ordinary Indians, with leaders, money and ways of worship from overseas.

So Bakht Singh went back to the book of Acts. He became convinced that each local church should be led by its own elders, chosen from its own people, supported by its own members' giving and dependent on God rather than on foreign funds. In the 1940s an assembly began in Madras, now Chennai, and later the fellowship called Hebron in Hyderabad became a centre of the work. Prayer was at the heart of it. Believers gathered for long times of prayer and worship and studied the Bible with great seriousness. Each year thousands came to gatherings called Holy Convocations, living simply together for days of prayer, teaching and praise.

Over the following decades, hundreds of these assemblies sprang up across India and beyond. Bakht Singh trained local brothers to shepherd them, and he expected ordinary believers to witness, pray and serve. He died in 2000. His story shows this chapter's lessons at work: Christ, not a foreign board or a single strong man, is the Head; local elders can shepherd their own people; and when leaders equip believers rather than doing everything themselves, the Church grows.

### The priesthood of all believers

In the Old Testament, only priests from the tribe of Levi could offer sacrifices, and ordinary Israelites came to God through them. But when Jesus died, the curtain of the temple was torn, and the way into God's presence was opened to all who trust in Him.

Peter writes to ordinary believers, many of them poor: "you yourselves, as living stones, are being built up as a house for the Spirit, to serve as a holy priesthood and to offer, through Jesus Christ, spiritual sacrifices that God gladly accepts" (1 Peter 2:5). And again: "you are a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people God has claimed as His own" (1 Peter 2:9). Christ "made us a kingdom, priests in the service of His God and Father" (Revelation 1:6).

This means two wonderful things. First, every Christian has direct access to God through Christ. You may come before His face yourself, at any hour, in your own words. That is the heart of this whole book.

Second, every Christian is a minister. We often use "minister" for the pastor, but in Scripture every believer has a ministry. Leaders equip God's people "for the work of serving" (Ephesians 4:12). The pastor is not the only player while the congregation watches from the stands. The pastor is the coach; the whole church is the team.

A central part of this priesthood is prayer. Like the priests of old, who carried the names of the tribes before God, we bring people to God in intercession and bring God to people through our witness. "First of all, then, I urge that requests, prayers, intercessions and thanks be offered for everyone" (1 Timothy 2:1), because God "wants everyone to be saved and to come to know the truth" (1 Timothy 2:4). The grandmother who prays each morning for her neighbours by name is doing priestly work. So is the trader who tells a customer about Jesus. You need no title to serve as a priest. You need only to stand before God's face for others, and before others for God.

This leads into Chapter 23, where we ask what gifts the Spirit gives each believer for this ministry, and how we discover God's calling.

### Questions for reflection

- Ephesians 4:12 says leaders are given to equip God's people for the work of serving. Do you see yourself as someone being equipped, or as someone watching others do the work?
- Why do you think almost every qualification for elders and deacons is about character rather than skill?
- How have you seen a pastor or leader care for wounded souls? What made that care helpful?
- How can your church honour the ministry of women, as Paul honoured Phoebe, Priscilla and Mary?
- What does it mean to you that you have direct access to God through Christ, with no one standing in between?
- Who has God placed on your heart to carry to Him in prayer, as a priest carries others before God?

### A step for this week

Choose one leader in your church, a pastor, elder or deacon. Pray for them by name every day this week, using 1 Peter 5:2–3 as your guide, and send them a short message thanking them for one specific way they have served you. Then, as a priest yourself, write down three people outside the church whom you will carry before God each morning, and ask Him to open a door for you to serve one of them before the week ends.
