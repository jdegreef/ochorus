## Judgment, Paradise and the New Heavens and New Earth

> "See, God's dwelling is with human beings! He will make His home with them, and they will be His peoples; God Himself will be with them. Every tear He will wipe from their eyes. Death will be no more, and no more mourning, no crying, no pain."
> — Revelation 21:3–4 (OWB)

Every story needs an ending. A farmer plants with the harvest in mind; a builder lays a foundation with the finished house in mind. The end gives meaning to everything before it.

The same is true of the Christian life. This book began with the face of God: the man and the woman hiding from His face in the garden, Moses speaking with Him face to face, and the promise of Revelation that "they will look upon His face" (Revelation 22:4). We have spent twenty-six chapters learning to live before that face now, in prayer, work, family, suffering and mission. This chapter looks at where the story ends. What happens when we die? What will the return of Christ bring? What is the judgment, and what will the new creation be like?

These questions shape how we live today. Chapter 26 spoke of the hope that keeps us running. Here we look more closely at that hope, and at the sober truths that stand beside it.

### Death and paradise

Death is the one appointment none of us can cancel. Yet for the believer, death has lost its terror. One of the two criminals crucified beside Jesus turned to Him in his last hours and asked to be remembered. Jesus answered, "Truly I tell you, this very day you will be with Me in paradise" (Luke 23:43). Not after a long wait. Not after paying for his sins. This very day.

Paul agrees: "We have courage, then, and we would much rather move out of the body and make our home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8). Writing from prison, he could not decide whether he preferred to live or to die: "My desire is to leave this life and be with Christ, for that is far, far better" (Philippians 1:23).

So when a believer dies, the body is laid in the ground, but the person is at home with the Lord, conscious and at rest. This is far better than anything here. Yet it is not the final ending. The Bible's great hope is not only that our souls go to heaven but that our bodies will be raised and the whole creation renewed.

At a graveside we grieve, as Jesus Himself wept at the tomb of Lazarus, but not as those without hope. The believer we bury has simply gone ahead into the presence we have been seeking all our lives.

### The return of Christ and the resurrection of the dead

The Christian hope centres on a Person and an event: Jesus Christ will come again. When He ascended, the angels told the disciples, "This Jesus has been taken up from you into heaven, and He will come in the very way you saw Him leave for heaven" (Acts 1:11). Paul describes the moment: "The Lord Himself will come down from heaven at a shout of command, at an archangel's call and the sound of God's trumpet, and the dead who belong to Christ will rise first. Then those of us still living, who are left, will be swept up with them into the clouds to meet the Lord in the air. From then on we will be with the Lord always" (1 Thessalonians 4:16–17).

At His coming the dead will be raised. "For the trumpet will sound, and the dead will be raised, never again to decay, and we who are alive will be changed" (1 Corinthians 15:52). Our resurrection bodies will be real bodies, like the risen body of Jesus, but free from sickness, weakness and death.

Jesus taught that this resurrection includes everyone: "An hour is coming when everyone in the tombs will hear His voice and come out" (John 5:28–29).

Faithful Christians differ on many details of the end times, such as the order of events, the thousand years of Revelation 20 and the timing of the gathering of believers. This book treats these as matters on which we may freely differ (see Appendix A). But all the historic creeds confess together that Christ "will come again to judge the living and the dead." That is the common ground, and it is enough to live by.

### The final judgment

Scripture is plain that judgment is coming. "It is in store for human beings to die once and after that to be judged" (Hebrews 9:27). John saw the scene in a vision: "Next I saw a great white throne, and the one who sat on it; from His face earth and sky ran away, and no place could be found for them. I saw the dead, the great and the lowly, standing in front of the throne. Books were opened, and so was another book, the book of life" (Revelation 20:11–12).

Notice "the great and the lowly." Presidents and herdsmen will stand on the same level ground. No bribe will work there. For people who have suffered under corrupt officials, cruel employers or violent men, this is good news: every hidden injustice will be brought into the light.

Jesus pictured the same day as a shepherd separating sheep from goats: "The Son of Man will come in His glory, and all the angels with Him. Then He will take His seat on the throne of His glory. Every nation will be brought together before Him" (Matthew 25:31–32). Every nation. Every person. Every life laid open before the face of God.

This is the ultimate meaning of coram Deo. Today we may forget that we live before God's face; on that day no one will. The only question will be whether we meet that face as a judge to be feared or as a Father we have longed to see.

### No condemnation for those in Christ

Here is the heart of the gospel. If judgment were based only on our record, none of us could stand. But God has done something astonishing. At the cross, Jesus bore the wrath of God against our sin. "God put Him forward as the means of atonement, by His blood, to be received through faith" (Romans 3:25). Because of this, Paul says, "Since we have now been declared righteous by His blood, it is all the more certain that we will be saved through Him from God's wrath" (Romans 5:9).

So the believer can face the judgment without fear. "So then, those who are in Christ Jesus now face no sentence of condemnation" (Romans 8:1). Jesus Himself promised: "Truly, truly, I tell you, anyone who hears My word and believes the One who sent Me has eternal life. Such a person does not come under condemnation, but has already moved out of death into life" (John 5:24).

Notice the tense: "has already moved." The verdict for those in Christ has been spoken. The sentence we deserved has been carried by another. We are fully forgiven, and we will never face condemnation.

### Yet we will give an account

This does not mean that how we live makes no difference. Paul, who taught so clearly that we are saved by grace alone, also wrote: "For all of us must be laid open before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive what matches the things done in the body, whether good or worthless" (2 Corinthians 5:10). And again: "We will all stand before God's judgment seat... So each of us will have to give an account of ourselves" (Romans 14:10, 12).

Every believer will stand before Christ and be judged individually for how we lived and what we did with His great sacrifice. This judgment is not to decide our salvation. That was settled at the cross. It is for reward or loss.

Paul gives a vivid picture. Jesus Christ is the only foundation. On that foundation each of us builds with our lives, some with "gold, silver, costly stones," others with "wood, hay or straw." Then "the fire itself will prove what kind of work each one has done. If what someone has built on the foundation survives, that builder will receive a reward. If someone's work burns up, that person will suffer loss. The builder will still be saved, but only as one who comes out through the fire" (1 Corinthians 3:12–15).

Imagine two houses after a bush fire. One was built of stone and still stands; the other was grass and sticks, and only ashes remain. Both families escaped alive, but only one has something to show for years of labour. A believer can be saved and yet see much of a life's work burn away, because it was done for self or for show. This is why Chapter 25 spoke of faithful stewardship. What we do with our time, money and gifts matters to Christ.

### Rewards, crowns and "well done"

The New Testament is not shy about rewards. It speaks of "the crown of righteousness" for all who long for Christ's appearing (2 Timothy 4:8), "the crown of life that God has promised to those who love Him" (James 1:12), and "a crown of glory that will never wither" for faithful shepherds of God's flock (1 Peter 5:4).

Is it selfish to serve God for a reward? No. The rewards of Scripture are not wages that we earn. They are gifts of a generous Father, who delights to honour what His children did for love of Him. And notice what the elders in heaven do with their crowns: "They throw down their crowns in front of the throne" (Revelation 4:10). Our crowns will be things to give back to Him in worship.

The greatest reward of all will be a word. To the faithful servant, the master says, "Well done! You are a good and faithful servant... Come in and share the joy of your master" (Matthew 25:21). To hear those words from the lips of Jesus, to see His face and know that He is pleased, will be worth every hidden sacrifice and every unseen act of love.

### The reality of judgment for those who reject Christ

We must also speak, soberly and with tears, about those who reject Christ. Jesus Himself taught about it more than anyone. At the end of the sheep and goats He said, "These will depart to eternal punishment, and the righteous will go to eternal life" (Matthew 25:46). Paul writes that those who refuse the gospel "will pay the penalty of eternal destruction, cut off from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of His power" (2 Thessalonians 1:9).

The whole Bible is the story of God's face. To be cut off from that face for ever is the deepest loss a human being can know.

Christians have held differing views on the nature of hell. The view held by most Christians through history, including Augustine and Jonathan Edwards, is that the lost will experience conscious punishment that has no end. They point to Jesus' words setting "eternal punishment" beside "eternal life" in the same sentence. Other Bible-believing teachers, among them the respected English pastor John Stott, have suggested more tentatively that the lost, after a just judgment, will finally be destroyed and cease to exist. They point to words such as "destruction," to Jesus' warning about the One "who can destroy soul and body alike in hell" (Matthew 10:28), and to Revelation's "second death." Christians also differ on how far the Bible's pictures of fire and darkness are literal or are images of a reality worse than words.

Yet all these views agree on what matters most. Judgment is real. It is just. It is final. It means being shut out from the presence of God, the source of all life, love and joy. And God takes no pleasure in it: "As surely as I live, declares the Lord GOD, the death of the wicked gives Me no pleasure. I would rather that they turn from their ways and live" (Ezekiel 33:11). Peter tells us why the end has not yet come: "He does not want anyone to be destroyed, but wants all people to turn back in repentance" (2 Peter 3:9).

C. S. Lewis explored this in his short book *The Great Divorce*, a story he was careful to call a dream and not a map of the afterlife. In it, ghosts from a grey, dreary town take a bus ride to the edge of heaven. Heaven is so solid and real that the grass hurts their feet. Each is invited to stay, and most turn back, because they will not let go of their pride, their grievances or their self-pity. Lewis's point was that the lost are not people who longed for God and were refused. They are people who, in the end, would rather have themselves than Him.

This truth should never make us proud or cold. It should break our hearts and move our feet, and it is one of the deepest reasons for the urgency of the Great Commission (see Chapter 24). If judgment is real, then the gospel is the best news anyone can hear, and we must share it with love while there is time.

### The new heavens and new earth

The Bible does not end with souls floating in the clouds. It ends with a renewed creation. Long ago God promised through Isaiah, "Look, I am about to create new heavens and a new earth" (Isaiah 65:17). In that world, "They will build houses and settle in them, plant vineyards and enjoy their grapes" (Isaiah 65:21), and "None will hurt and none will destroy anywhere on My holy mountain" (Isaiah 65:25).

Paul says the whole creation is waiting for that day: "The created world cranes its neck in longing, waiting for God's children to be revealed... creation itself will also be released from its slavery to decay and will enter the freedom that comes with the glory of God's children" (Romans 8:19, 21). The droughts, floods, diseases and decay of this world are not the last word. Peter adds, "we are looking forward to what He promised: new heavens and a new earth, where righteousness is at home" (2 Peter 3:13).

And then John sees it: "Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth; the first heaven and the first earth had gone, and the sea was no more" (Revelation 21:1). God comes to live with His people. Every tear is wiped away. Death, mourning, crying and pain are gone. The One on the throne says, "See, I am making everything new!" (Revelation 21:5).

In the final chapter of the Bible we are back in a garden, now a garden city. A river of the water of life flows from the throne, and the tree of life grows on its banks, "and its leaves bring healing to the nations" (Revelation 22:2). Then comes the line this whole book has been moving towards: "His servants will offer Him worship; they will look upon His face, and His name will be written on their foreheads" (Revelation 22:3–4). The hiding that began in Eden is over for ever. We will live coram Deo, before the face of God, in perfect joy.

Augustine of Hippo, the great North African bishop, wrote *The City of God* after Rome was sacked in 410, when the world seemed to be falling apart. Its last pages look past every earthly city to the one that will never fall:

> "There we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise. This is what shall be in the end without end."
> Augustine, *The City of God*

### Jonathan Edwards: a world of love

Few people have thought more deeply about heaven than Jonathan Edwards. Born in 1703 in Connecticut, in the American colonies, he became pastor of the church in Northampton, Massachusetts, and was one of the leading preachers of the revival known as the Great Awakening. Many people know him only for one fierce sermon about judgment, *Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God*. But Edwards's deepest love was for the beauty of God, and the joy of knowing Him for ever.

His life was not easy. In 1750, after more than twenty years of ministry, his own congregation dismissed him after a painful dispute. He moved his large family to the frontier town of Stockbridge, serving as a missionary pastor to Native American families and writing some of his greatest books. In 1758 he was called to be president of the college at Princeton. Within weeks of arriving, he died after an inoculation against smallpox. He was fifty-four.

Edwards preached a series of sermons on 1 Corinthians 13, ending with one that described heaven as a world of love. On earth, he said in effect, love is mixed with selfishness and misunderstanding. In heaven, love flows perfectly from God to His people and between all His people, with nothing to spoil it, and it never ends.

In another sermon, *The Christian Pilgrim*, now in the Ochorus library, Edwards urged his hearers to see this life as a journey towards that world. He compared every earthly joy to the joy of God Himself:

> "Fathers and mothers, husbands, wives, or children, or the company of earthly friends, are but shadows; but the enjoyment of God is the substance. These are but scattered beams; but God is the sun. These are but streams; but God is the fountain. These are but drops; but God is the ocean."
> Jonathan Edwards, *The Christian Pilgrim*

Edwards loved his wife Sarah and his children deeply. But he knew that every good thing on earth is a small stream that flows from a great fountain. In the new creation we will have the fountain itself.

### Living now in the light of eternity

How then should we live? Peter answers directly: "Since everything is to be dissolved in this way, think what sort of people you ought to be! Live holy and godly lives, as you wait for the coming of the day of God" (2 Peter 3:11–12). John adds, "when He is revealed, we will be like Him, for we will see Him as He truly is. Everyone whose hope is set on Him in this way makes themselves pure, just as He is pure" (1 John 3:2–3).

The hope of eternity produces three things in us.

**Holiness.** If we will one day see God's face, we want to be ready, as a bride prepares for her wedding day.

**Hope.** The grief, injustice and pain of this world are real, but they are not final. A widow in a village, a refugee in a camp, a patient in a hospital ward can lift their heads. "Every tear He will wipe from their eyes" (Revelation 21:4).

**Faithful service.** Because judgment and reward are real, our daily work matters. Because hell is real, the gospel is urgent. Because the new creation is coming, nothing done for Christ is ever wasted.

The Bible's last prayer is "Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20), the cry of every heart that has learned to seek God's face. One day He will answer it, and we will see Him.

### Questions for reflection

- How does knowing that believers go at once to be with Christ change the way you think about your own death, or the death of someone you love?
- Why is the promise of "no sentence of condemnation" (Romans 8:1) such good news when we think about the final judgment?
- What in your life right now might be "wood, hay or straw," and what might be "gold, silver, costly stones"?
- How do you respond to the different Christian views on the nature of hell? What common ground do they share, and how should it affect the way you speak to others about Christ?
- What part of the picture of the new heavens and new earth gives you the most hope, and why?
- Edwards said earthly joys are streams and God is the fountain. Which "streams" are you most tempted to love more than the fountain?

### A step for this week

This week, set aside one evening to read Revelation 21:1–22:5 slowly, out loud if you can, alone or with your family. As you read, note every promise that touches a particular sorrow in your own life or in your community, and turn each one into a prayer of thanks. Then think of one person you know who does not yet know Christ. Pray for them by name each day this week, and look for one opportunity before the week ends to tell them, simply and kindly, why you have hope beyond death.
