## The Great Commission: Evangelism and Missions

> "Go, then, and make disciples of all the nations: baptize them into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teach them to keep everything I have commanded you. And remember this: I Myself am with you every day, until the close of the age."
> — Matthew 28:19–20 (OWB)

When a family receives wonderful news, such as the birth of a child, a healing, or a son's safe return from a long journey, they cannot keep it to themselves. They phone their relatives, they tell the neighbours, they stop friends in the market. Good news wants to travel.

The gospel is the best news the world has ever heard. In Chapter 24 we saw that the greatest service is to serve as Jesus served. Now, as we begin Part Five, we look at the greatest service of all: carrying the good news of Jesus to those who have never heard it, near and far.

### A command for every believer

Before Jesus returned to the Father, He gathered His disciples on a mountain in Galilee. He began with a claim: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been placed in My hands" (Matthew 28:18). Then He gave a command, which we call the Great Commission: "Go, then, and make disciples of all the nations" (Matthew 28:19). And He closed with a promise: "I Myself am with you every day, until the close of the age" (Matthew 28:20).

Some think this command was only for the apostles, or only for pastors and missionaries. But look at what it says. The disciples were to teach new believers "to keep everything I have commanded you" (Matthew 28:20). That includes this very command. Each new disciple is to make disciples, and so on, down every generation until the end of the age. The Great Commission has come all the way to you.

Charles Spurgeon, the London preacher, said this plainly in *The Soul-Winner*, speaking of the honour of leading people to Christ:

> "The honour does not belong to ministers only; they may take their full share of it, but it belongs to every one of you who have devoted yourselves to Christ."
> Charles Spurgeon, *The Soul-Winner*

And he added: "There is no man placed by God's providence where he cannot do some good" (*The Soul-Winner*). The office worker, the student, the grandmother and the motorbike taxi driver are all placed where they are by God, among people who need to hear.

### God's heart for all nations

Mission did not begin with the Great Commission. It runs through the whole Bible, because it flows from the heart of God.

When God called Abram, He promised: "I will bless you and make your name great, so that you will be a blessing ... and all the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (Genesis 12:2–3). From the start, God chose one family in order to bless every family. The psalmist sang, "Tell the nations of His glory, and all the peoples of the wonders He has done" (Psalm 96:3). Through Isaiah, God spoke of His Servant: "I will make you a light for the nations as well, so that you may be My salvation to the farthest edge of the earth" (Isaiah 49:6).

Even the reluctant prophet Jonah, who ran the other way rather than preach to Israel's enemies in Nineveh, learned that God's mercy reaches people we would rather leave out.

Then Jesus came as that light. After His resurrection He told His disciples, "when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, you will receive power, and you will bear witness to Me in Jerusalem, through all Judea and Samaria, and out to the farthest end of the earth" (Acts 1:8). The rest of Acts tells how the gospel crossed one border after another: from Jews to Samaritans, to an Ethiopian official, to a Roman soldier, to the cities of Asia and Europe.

And the story ends with a vision of the goal: "I saw a crowd so vast that no one could number it. They came from every nation and from all tribes and peoples and languages, and they stood before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). That crowd will include Maasai and Malay, Quechua and Korean, Yoruba and Ukrainian, all crying, "To our God, seated on the throne, and to the Lamb, salvation belongs!" (Revelation 7:10). Mission is simply joining God in filling that crowd. To live coram Deo is to share His heart for every face He has made.

### What the gospel is

To share the gospel, we must be clear about what it is. Many people have heard about church but have never heard the good news itself. Paul summed it up as "the first and chief thing": "that Christ died for our sins, in keeping with the Scriptures; that He was buried; that He has been raised on the third day, in keeping with the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).

Here is a simple way to explain it.

1. **God made us to know Him.** We were created to live before His face, in His love.
2. **We have turned away.** "Everyone has sinned, and all come short of God's glory" (Romans 3:23). Sin separates us from God and leads to death: "The wages sin pays are death" (Romans 6:23).
3. **God has come to rescue us.** Jesus, God's Son, lived the life we should have lived and died the death we deserved. He rose again, defeating sin and death. "God's free gift is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 6:23).
4. **We receive Him by faith.** "All who call on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Romans 10:13). We turn from sin, trust in Christ, and begin to follow Him.

Paul was proud of this message: "I am not ashamed of the gospel. It is God's own power at work to save everyone who believes" (Romans 1:16). The gospel is not good advice about how to improve ourselves. It is good news about what God has done for us in Christ.

### Sharing your own story of grace

You do not need to be a preacher to share the gospel. One of the most powerful tools you have is your own story of what God has done.

When Jesus set free a man tormented by demons, the man begged to travel with Him. But Jesus said, "Go home to your own people, and let them know all that the Lord has done for you, and the mercy He has shown you" (Mark 5:19). The Samaritan woman at the well had a poor reputation and no training. Yet "many of the Samaritans from that town believed in Him because of the woman's word" (John 4:39). She simply told them about the man who knew everything about her.

Your story need not be dramatic. Think about three parts: what your life was like before you knew Christ, how you came to trust Him, and what difference He has made since. Practise telling it in two or three minutes, in plain words, without church language. Then end by pointing to Jesus, not to yourself.

Peter tells us to be ready: "in your hearts set Christ apart as the holy Lord. Be ready at all times to make your defense to anyone who asks you for the reason behind the hope you hold" (1 Peter 3:15). Notice where it starts: in our hearts, before the face of Christ. Witness flows from worship.

### Evangelism empowered by the Spirit and backed by prayer

The disciples were not sent out the moment Jesus gave the command. He told them to wait for the Spirit's power (Acts 1:8). Evangelism is not a sales technique. It is the work of God, who opens blind eyes and gives new birth (see Chapter 4).

That is why prayer comes first. When the early church was threatened, they did not pray for safety. They prayed, "Let Your slaves speak Your word with complete boldness" (Acts 4:29). And "every one of them was filled with the Holy Spirit, and they went on proclaiming God's word fearlessly" (Acts 4:31). Paul, the greatest missionary of all, asked others to pray for him "that when I open my mouth I will be given the words to make known with boldness the mystery of the gospel" (Ephesians 6:19).

So pray for the people you hope will come to Christ. Pray by name, daily, for open hearts. Pray for open doors and the right words. Pray for workers to be sent to unreached peoples. Every great movement of the gospel in history has been carried on the shoulders of praying people.

### Love, courage and wisdom in conversations

Paul gives practical advice: "Live wisely with those outside the church, making the most of every opportunity. Let your conversation always be gracious and seasoned with salt, so that you know how to answer each person" (Colossians 4:5–6).

**Love** means we care for the person, not just their decision. Listen to their story. Ask questions. Respect their dignity. People can tell whether we see them as a project or as someone made in God's image.

**Courage** means we speak when it would be easier to stay silent. Fear of rejection keeps many believers quiet. But we are "ambassadors who speak for Christ, and it is God Himself who makes His appeal through us" (2 Corinthians 5:20). An ambassador does not speak in his own name, and he does not carry the result on his own shoulders.

**Wisdom** means we choose the right time and the right words. A grieving neighbour may need a meal and a prayer before a sermon. A curious colleague may welcome a Gospel of John and an invitation to read it together. "Seasoned with salt" means our words have flavour: they are honest, gracious and interesting, never harsh or dull.

### Alexander Mackay of Uganda: the engineer missionary

For many readers in East Africa, the story of the gospel in Uganda is part of their own family history. One of the first to bring it was a Scottish engineer named Alexander Mackay.

Mackay was born in 1849 in Rhynie, in the north-east of Scotland, the son of a minister. He trained as an engineer and worked for a time in Germany. In 1875 the explorer Henry Morton Stanley published a letter in a British newspaper saying that Mutesa, the Kabaka (king) of Buganda, would welcome Christian teachers. The Church Missionary Society answered the call, and Mackay offered himself.

The journey inland was terribly hard. Some of the first party died, and others turned back. Mackay reached Buganda in 1878. He did not only preach. He used his engineering skills, working with wood and iron, building and repairing, which drew the curiosity and respect of the people. He learned Luganda, translated parts of Scripture, including the Gospel of Matthew, and printed portions with his own hands so that people could read God's Word in their own language. Many young men at the royal court learned to read, and some came to faith in Christ.

Then came the cost. After Mutesa died in 1884, his son Mwanga became Kabaka and turned against the Christians. In 1885 Bishop James Hannington, travelling to Buganda, was killed on Mwanga's orders. In 1886 many young believers, both Anglican and Catholic, were put to death, many of them burned at Namugongo. They are remembered today as the Uganda Martyrs. Mackay stayed as long as he could, encouraging the believers and continuing his translation work, until he was forced to leave in 1887. He moved to Usambiro, at the southern end of Lake Victoria, and kept working on the Scriptures. He died there of fever in 1890, at the age of forty.

Mackay did not live to see the harvest. But the seed sown with his translations, his prayers and the blood of the martyrs grew. Within a generation the church in Uganda was spreading, carried largely by Ugandan evangelists, and later it became one of the centres of the East African Revival that touched many nations. Mackay's life reminds us that mission is costly, that it often means planting seeds others will harvest, and that faithful witness before God's face is never wasted.

### The history and cost of missions

Mackay was one among many. In the late eighteenth century William Carey, an English shoemaker, sailed to India and spent his life translating the Bible. In the nineteenth century Hudson Taylor founded the China Inland Mission and dressed as the Chinese did so that the gospel would not seem foreign. Mary Slessor, a Scottish mill girl, served for decades in Calabar, in what is now Nigeria.

And mission has never only flowed from the West. Samuel Ajayi Crowther was a Yoruba boy captured by slave raiders around 1821 and sold onto a slave ship. The ship was stopped by the British navy, and he was set free in Sierra Leone. There he came to Christ and was baptised. He became a teacher, then a minister, translated the Bible into Yoruba, and travelled up the Niger River on missionary expeditions. In 1864 he was consecrated as the first African bishop of the Anglican Church. His journal of one of those voyages records his simple prayer after preaching: "May the Spirit of God apply the word to each and all our hearts" (Samuel Ajayi Crowther, *Journal of an Expedition up the Niger*).

Many missionaries buried children, suffered disease and faced violence. Some, like the Uganda Martyrs, gave their lives. Their sacrifice should humble us, and it should also stir us. The task is not finished. Many peoples still have no church among them and no Bible in their own language.

### Going, sending, praying and giving

Not everyone is called to cross an ocean. But everyone has a part. Paul asks: "How, then, are they to call on Him if they have not believed in Him? And how are they to believe in Him if they have never heard Him? And how are they to hear without someone proclaiming? And how are people to proclaim it unless they are sent?" (Romans 10:14–15). Then he quotes Isaiah: "How lovely are the feet of those who bring the good news of good things!" (Romans 10:15).

There are four ways to take part:

- **Going.** Some are called to go: across the street, to another town, to another tribe or nation. Is God calling you?
- **Sending.** Churches send by choosing, training and laying hands on workers, as Antioch did (Acts 13:2–3, see Chapter 22).
- **Praying.** Every believer can pray for missionaries by name and for unreached peoples.
- **Giving.** John praised believers who supported travelling missionaries: "It is our duty, then, to take in and support such people, and so become fellow workers with the truth" (3 John 8). Your gift makes you a partner in their work.

### Partnership with the global Church

The centre of world Christianity has moved. A century ago, most Christians lived in Europe and North America. Today, most live in Africa, Asia and Latin America. Churches in Kenya, Nigeria, Korea, Brazil and India now send missionaries across the world. Latin American thinkers such as Samuel Escobar of Peru have described this as mission from everywhere to everywhere.

This calls for cross-cultural humility. Missionaries from any land can carry their own culture along with the gospel, and mistake one for the other. The gospel belongs to no single culture. It speaks every language and judges every culture, including our own. So we serve as partners, not masters. We listen before we speak, learn local languages, honour local leaders and remember that the Spirit was at work before we arrived.

For believers in East Africa this is a great opportunity. The churches that once received missionaries now send them, to neighbouring nations, to unreached peoples in the north and east, and to cities across the world. The circle of blessing promised to Abraham keeps widening.

### Before His face, for every face

The Great Commission ends with the words "I Myself am with you every day" (Matthew 28:20). We never witness alone. The One who sends us goes with us. That promise has carried believers into prisons and palaces, into refugee camps and university halls, into remote villages reached only on foot. It will carry you into your office, your classroom and your neighbour's home. You may feel weak, untrained or afraid. So did many of the witnesses in this book: Catherine Booth, as Chapter 23 told, was so timid that for years she dared not speak in public at all. What matters is not our strength but His presence. When we speak to a neighbour about Jesus, we do it before His face and in His company.

This leads into Chapter 26, where we consider how we manage the time, talents and treasure God has entrusted to us, including how we use them for His mission in the world.

### Questions for reflection

- Do you see the Great Commission as a command for you personally, or mainly for pastors and missionaries? Why?
- How would you explain the gospel in two minutes to a friend who has never heard it?
- What are the three parts of your own story of grace: before Christ, coming to Christ, and life since?
- What holds you back most from sharing your faith: lack of love, lack of courage or lack of wisdom?
- What does the story of Alexander Mackay and the Uganda Martyrs teach you about the cost and fruit of mission?
- In which of the four ways, going, sending, praying or giving, is God calling you to take part more fully?

### A step for this week

Write down the names of three people in your life who do not yet know Christ, and pray for each of them by name every day this week, asking God for open hearts and open doors. Then write out your own story of grace in three short parts and practise telling it aloud in under three minutes. Before the week ends, ask God for one natural opportunity to share it, or to invite one of the three to read a Gospel with you, and take it when it comes.
