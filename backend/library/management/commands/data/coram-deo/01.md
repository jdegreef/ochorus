## Almighty God, Our Father

> "The LORD is the God of all ages, the maker of the earth to its farthest edges. He does not tire or wear out, and His understanding is beyond all searching."
> — Isaiah 40:28 (OWB)

If we are going to live before the face of God, we need to know whose face it is. A child who is frightened of a strict father will hide from him. A child who knows her father is good will run to him. The same is true of us. How we picture God shapes how we pray, how we work, how we face trouble and how we treat other people.

A. W. Tozer, the American pastor, once wrote that what comes into our minds when we think about God is the most important thing about us. That is a bold claim, but it is true. If we think God is distant, we will stop praying. If we think He is harsh, we will serve Him out of fear. If we think He is small, we will trust Him only with small things. But if we come to know Him as He truly is, everything begins to change.

So this book begins where the Bible begins: with God Himself. In this chapter we will look at who God is, the names by which He makes Himself known, what He is like, and why He made us. In the next two chapters we will meet the Son and the Holy Spirit. Together these three chapters form the foundation for everything else, because all of the Christian life is a response to the God who has first shown us His face.

### The God who simply is

The Bible's first sentence does not argue for God. It simply announces Him: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1). Before there was a sun, a sea, a mountain or a single human being, God was already there. He did not begin. He has no birthday. Moses prayed, "from the endless past to the endless future You are God" (Psalm 90:2).

This is what Christians mean when they say that God is eternal and self-existent. Everything else in the universe depends on something outside itself. A tree needs soil, rain and sunlight. A child needs parents, food and care. Even the stars burn for a time and then fade. But God depends on nothing. Nobody made Him, nobody keeps Him alive, and nobody can add to Him or take from Him.

When God called Moses at the burning bush, Moses asked what name he should give to the people of Israel. God answered, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14). It is a mysterious name, but it tells us something wonderful. God does not say "I was" or "I will become". He simply is. He is always present, always Himself, always full of life.

Think of what this means for an ordinary day. The God who meets you in the morning is not tired from yesterday. Isaiah says He "does not tire or wear out" (Isaiah 40:28). Mothers grow weary. Farmers grow weary. Pastors grow weary. God does not. When you come to Him at the end of a long day in the market or the office, you are not troubling Him. He is not running low.

### The names that reveal Him

In the Bible, a name is more than a label. It tells you something about the person. God has given us several names for Himself, and each one opens a window onto His character.

**Elohim** is the name we meet in Genesis 1:1. It is the general Hebrew word for God, and it speaks of His strength and greatness as the Creator of all things. The God of the Bible is not one god among many local spirits. He is the maker of heaven and earth.

**Yahweh** is God's personal, covenant name, the name He gave to Moses at the bush. It is linked to the words "I AM". Many English Bibles, including the one quoted in this book, print it as "the LORD" in capital letters. When you see LORD written that way, remember that you are reading God's own personal name. He is not a nameless force. He is someone who can be known and called upon.

**El Shaddai** means God Almighty. When Abram was ninety-nine years old and still waiting for the son God had promised, God appeared to him and said, "I am God Almighty. Live your life before Me and be wholehearted" (Genesis 17:1). Notice those words: live your life before Me. Here, at the very start of God's story with His people, is the idea of this book. The Almighty God calls us to live in His presence, before His face.

**Abba** is an Aramaic word, the language Jesus spoke at home. It is the warm word a child uses for a father. Jesus used it when He prayed, and astonishingly He gives the same word to us. Paul writes, "You received the Spirit of adoption as children, and in the Spirit we call out, 'Abba, Father!'" (Romans 8:15). The eternal I AM, the Almighty, the Creator of the stars, invites us to call Him Father. That is why this chapter is called "Almighty God, Our Father". He is both. If we forget that He is Almighty, we will make Him small. If we forget that He is our Father, we will keep Him far away.

### What God is like

Theologians use a set of words to describe the attributes of God, the qualities that belong to Him by nature. Some of these words sound difficult, but each one carries comfort for daily life.

**Holiness.** God is holy. That means He is utterly pure and utterly different from us. In the year King Uzziah died, Isaiah saw a vision of God on His throne, and the angels around Him were crying, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of Armies! All that fills the earth is His glory" (Isaiah 6:3). Isaiah did not feel proud to be there. He cried, "Woe to me! I am finished" (Isaiah 6:5). The closer we come to God, the more we see our own sin. Yet that same chapter shows God cleansing Isaiah and sending him out. Holiness is not God's way of keeping us out. It is the fire that makes us clean.

**Sovereignty.** God rules over all things. Nothing happens outside His knowledge or beyond His control. Presidents, armies, droughts and diseases are not the final word. God is.

**Omnipotence.** This long word simply means that God is all-powerful. He can do everything that is consistent with His own good character. Nothing is too hard for Him.

**Omniscience.** God knows everything. David prayed, "LORD, You have looked deep into me, and You know me. Whether I sit down or get up, You know it; even from far away You grasp what I am thinking" (Psalm 139:1-2). That can be frightening if we are hiding something. But it is also deeply comforting. God knows the worry you have not told anyone. He knows the prayer you could not put into words. You never have to explain yourself to Him from the beginning.

**Omnipresence.** God is everywhere. David goes on, "Where could I go to get away from Your Spirit? Where could I run to escape Your presence?" (Psalm 139:7). Whether you are in a crowded city bus, a village hut, a hospital ward or a prison cell, God is there. This is the ground beneath the whole idea of coram Deo. We do not have to travel to find God's face. We are already before it.

**Immutability.** God does not change. "I am the LORD, and I do not change" (Malachi 3:6). People change their minds. Governments change their laws. Prices in the market change every week. But God's character and promises stay the same. The God who kept His word to Abraham will keep His word to you.

### What God's heart is like

Knowing God's power is not enough. We also need to know His heart. The devil believes that God is powerful, but he does not trust Him. What we need to see is that God's power is joined to perfect goodness.

When Moses asked to see God's glory, God passed in front of him and proclaimed His own name: "The LORD, the LORD, a God of tender compassion and grace, slow to grow angry, rich in faithful love and in truth" (Exodus 34:6). This is God's own description of Himself, and the rest of the Old Testament quotes it again and again. It tells us that He is:

- **Loving.** John puts it in four short words: "God is love" (1 John 4:8). Love is not just something God does. It is who He is.
- **Faithful.** He keeps His promises, even when we fail to keep ours.
- **Just.** The same passage says He "will never acquit the guilty" (Exodus 34:7). God takes wrong seriously. He is not indifferent to the widow cheated of her land or the child who is abused.
- **Merciful.** He does not give us what our sins deserve.
- **Good.** Everything He does is right and kind, even when we cannot yet see how.
- **Patient.** He is "slow to grow angry". Think of how long He has waited for you, and how often He has given you another chance.

Some people find it hard to put God's justice and His love together. But a judge who did not care about evil would not be loving. And a God who only punished, with no mercy, would leave us without hope. In the next chapter we will see how God's justice and His love meet perfectly at the cross of Jesus Christ.

In the fourteenth century, in the English city of Norwich, a woman we know as Julian lay so ill that she and those around her expected her to die. During that illness she received a series of visions of God's love, and after she recovered she spent many years living as an anchoress, a woman set apart for prayer in a small room attached to a church. She thought about those visions for the rest of her life. In one of them she saw something very small:

> "Also in this He shewed me a little thing, the quantity of an hazel-nut, in the palm of my hand; and it was as round as a ball. I looked thereupon with eye of my understanding, and thought: What may this be? And it was answered generally thus: it is all that is made."
> Julian of Norwich, *Revelations of Divine Love*

She wondered how something so small and fragile could last. The answer came: "It lasteth, and ever shall [last] for that God loveth it." Julian understood that the whole universe is held in being by the love of God. Years later, when she asked what the meaning of all her visions had been, she wrote that the answer was simple: "Love was His meaning."

### God the Creator

God's greatness and goodness are not hidden. He has written them across the sky. "The heavens tell over and over how glorious God is; the vault of the sky makes known what His hands have made" (Psalm 19:1). A herdsman watching the stars over the savannah, a fisherman on a quiet lake at dawn, a child staring at an ant carrying a crumb: all of them are looking at the handiwork of God.

Paul says that creation makes God known so clearly that no one can claim ignorance: "what cannot be seen of God, His everlasting power and divine nature, has been in plain view, understood from the things He made" (Romans 1:20). Every sunrise is a sermon.

But the high point of creation is not the stars or the mountains. It is us. "So God created human beings in His image. In the image of God He created them; male and female He created them" (Genesis 1:27). Of all the creatures God made, only human beings bear His image. That means we were made to reflect Him, to represent Him on earth, to rule the world with care on His behalf, and above all to know Him and be known by Him.

This has two great consequences. First, every human being has dignity. The street child, the elderly widow, the refugee, the person of another tribe or nation, the unborn child: each one bears God's image. We cannot despise any of them without despising the One whose image they carry. Second, we were made for relationship. A fish was made for water. A bird was made for the air. You were made for God. Nothing else will fully satisfy you, because nothing else is the thing you were made for.

### A restless heart finds its home

No one in the history of the Church has shown this more clearly than Augustine, the African bishop. He was born in AD 354 in Thagaste, a town in the Roman province of North Africa, in what is now Algeria. His mother, Monica, was a Christian who prayed for him constantly. His father was not a believer until near the end of his life.

Augustine was brilliant. He studied in Carthage, became a teacher of rhetoric, the art of public speaking, and rose to a respected post in the Italian city of Milan. But his heart was never at peace. He chased pleasure, lived with a woman he did not marry, followed one philosophy after another, and longed for fame. Each of these promised to satisfy him, and each one left him emptier than before. Later he looked back and wrote to God: "Thou wert with me, but I was not with Thee."

In the summer of 386, in a garden in Milan, the struggle came to a head. Augustine knew that the Christian faith was true, but he could not let go of his old life. He kept putting it off until tomorrow. Weeping under a fig tree, he heard what sounded like a child's voice from a nearby house, chanting, "Take up and read; Take up and read." He picked up the letters of Paul, opened them, and read the first words his eyes fell on, the call to put away drunkenness and immorality and to put on the Lord Jesus Christ (Romans 13:13-14). In that moment, he later said, the darkness of doubt fled away. The next Easter he was baptised in Milan. A few years later he returned to Africa, and in time he became bishop of Hippo, a port city on the coast of what is now Algeria, where he served for over thirty years until his death in 430.

Augustine wrote many books, but the best loved is his *Confessions*, a long prayer in which he tells God the story of his life. Near its very beginning he writes the words for which he is most remembered:

> "Thou awakest us to delight in Thy praise; for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee."
> Augustine, *Confessions*

In today's words: "You wake us up to delight in praising You, because You made us for Yourself, and our hearts are restless until they rest in You." That sentence has travelled through sixteen centuries and across every continent because it describes every human being. The businessman with a full bank account and an empty heart is restless. The young woman who has moved to the city in search of a better life and finds only loneliness is restless. The religious person who keeps every rule but has never met God is restless. We were made for God, and until we rest in Him we will keep searching.

Augustine also shows us why the knowledge of God matters so much. He did not simply learn facts about God. He came to know God Himself, and that changed everything: his desires, his friendships, his use of his gifts and his whole life's work. More than a thousand years later the Reformer John Calvin opened his great book, the *Institutes of the Christian Religion*, in the same spirit:

> "Our wisdom, in so far as it ought to be deemed true and solid Wisdom, consists almost entirely of two parts: the knowledge of God and of ourselves."
> John Calvin, *Institutes of the Christian Religion*

Calvin meant that the two belong together. When we see God as He is, we see ourselves truly: small, sinful, dependent, and yet loved. And when we honestly face our own need, we are driven back to God.

### Why it matters

Some people think that learning about God's attributes is only for scholars. But right knowledge of God shapes the whole of life. It shapes three things in particular.

**It shapes our worship.** We praise what we admire. When we see God's holiness, power, wisdom and love, praise rises naturally. A church that sings without thinking about who God is will soon grow cold. A church that knows God will worship Him with both joy and awe.

**It shapes our trust.** When the rains fail, when the business collapses, when the doctor brings bad news, what we believe about God is tested. If God is sovereign, good and unchanging, then we can trust Him even when we do not understand Him. A small God produces anxious people. A great and loving God produces people who can sleep in a storm.

**It shapes our obedience.** We obey people we respect and trust. If God is holy, we will take sin seriously. If God is our loving Father, we will obey Him not as slaves afraid of punishment but as children who want to please the One who loves them.

God Himself tells us where real pride and joy should be found. Not in wisdom, strength or wealth, but in this: "whoever boasts should boast of this: that they understand and know Me, that I am the LORD, who acts with loyal love, justice and righteousness on the earth" (Jeremiah 9:24). The greatest thing a person can say is not "I am rich" or "I am clever" but "I know God".

### Living before the face of the Father

All of this brings us back to the central idea of this book. To live coram Deo, before the face of God, is not to live under the eye of a stern inspector. It is to live in the presence of the Almighty God who is also our Father. He sees everything, so we do not need to pretend. He is everywhere, so we are never alone. He is all-powerful, so nothing is beyond His help. He is holy, so we can trust Him to make us clean. And He is love, so we can come to Him without fear.

Picture a small child playing in a field while her father works nearby. She runs, falls, gets up, picks flowers, and every few minutes she looks up to make sure he is still there. He always is. She plays with freedom because she knows his eye is on her. That is the life God offers us: work, rest, family, trouble and joy, all lived under the loving gaze of our Father.

But there is a problem. We have seen that God is holy, and we are not. Like Adam and Eve in the garden, we have hidden from His face. How can sinful people come near a holy God and call Him Father? That is the question of the next chapter, where we meet Jesus Christ, our Redeemer, who came to bring us home.

### Questions for reflection

- Before reading this chapter, what came into your mind when you thought about God? Has anything in that picture changed?
- Which of God's names (Elohim, Yahweh, El Shaddai, Abba) means the most to you right now, and why?
- Which attribute of God do you find most comforting? Which do you find most challenging?
- Read Psalm 139:1-12 slowly. How does it feel to be known so completely by God?
- Augustine said our hearts are restless until they rest in God. Where have you looked for rest apart from Him?
- How might knowing God more truly change the way you work, worry or treat other people this week?

### A step for this week

Each morning this week, before you check your phone or begin your work, read one part of Psalm 139 (verses 1-6 on the first day, 7-12 on the second, 13-18 on the third, and so on, beginning again when you reach the end). Choose one phrase that describes God and write it on a small piece of paper. Carry it with you through the day, in your pocket, on your desk or beside the cooking pot. Whenever you see it, pause for a few seconds and say to God, "You are here, and You are my Father." At the end of the week, notice how often you remembered that you were living before His face.
