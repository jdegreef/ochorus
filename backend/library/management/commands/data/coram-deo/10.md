## What It Means to Be a Disciple

> "Anyone who wants to come after Me must say no to themselves, pick up their cross every day, and keep following Me."
> — Luke 9:23 (OWB)

In many towns in Africa and Asia you can still see an apprentice at work. A boy of sixteen sits in a carpenter's shed beside an older man. He does not attend lectures on carpentry. He watches how the master holds the plane, how he chooses the wood, how he measures twice before he cuts once. He sweeps the floor, fetches the nails, makes mistakes and starts again. He eats with the master and hears how he talks to customers. Over months and years, the way the master works slowly becomes his own way. One day a customer walks in and cannot tell whose table is whose.

That is the picture behind the word *disciple*. In the New Testament a disciple is a learner, a pupil, an apprentice. The word appears more than two hundred and fifty times in the Gospels and Acts. The word *Christian* appears only three times in the whole New Testament, and it was first a nickname: "It was in Antioch that the disciples first came to be called Christians" (Acts 11:26). People were disciples first. The name came later.

In the last chapter we looked at the spiritual disciplines (see Chapter 9). The two words share a root, and they belong together. Disciplines are the training; a disciple is the person being trained. But the heart of discipleship is not a set of habits. It is a Person. To be a disciple is to live every day with Jesus, before His face, learning from Him how to live.

### The call to follow: more than belief

When Jesus called His first followers, He did not hand them a list of beliefs to sign. He walked along the shore of Galilee, found fishermen mending their nets, and said, "Come, follow Me... and I will make you fishers of people" (Matthew 4:19). They left their boats and went with Him.

Belief matters greatly. We are saved by grace through faith, and not by anything we do (see Chapter 2). But true faith is never merely agreeing with facts about Jesus. The demons believe that much. Saving faith is trust that attaches itself to Jesus and goes where He goes. It is like the difference between believing that a bus goes to Kampala and actually climbing aboard.

Jesus' invitation is warm and gentle:

> "Come to Me, all of you who are worn out with work and weighed down with burdens, and I will give you rest. Put My yoke on your necks and learn from Me, because I am gentle and My heart is humble." (Matthew 11:28–29)

A yoke joins two oxen so that they plough together. A young ox was often yoked beside an older, steady one, and it learned by walking in step. Jesus does not send us out to plough alone. He invites us under His yoke, to walk beside Him and learn His pace. Discipleship is a whole-life apprenticeship to the gentlest and wisest Master who ever lived.

So the question is not only, "Do you believe in Jesus?" It is also, "Are you following Him? Are you learning from Him? Is your life slowly becoming like His?"

### The cost of discipleship

Jesus never hid the cost. Large crowds were following Him on the road, and He turned and said something that must have shocked them:

> "No one can be My disciple who comes to Me without hating their father and mother, their wife and children, their brothers and sisters, and even their own life. Unless a person carries their own cross and walks behind Me, they cannot be My disciple." (Luke 14:26–27)

Jesus is not telling us to hate our families. He commands us to honour our parents and love our neighbours. He is speaking in the strong language of His culture, which compared loves by contrast. Next to our love for Him, every other love must look like hatred. He must come first, even before the people we love most and before our own life.

Then He told two short stories. A man who wants to build a tower first sits down and adds up the cost, or else he lays the foundation and cannot finish, and everyone laughs. A king going to war first sits down to see whether his ten thousand soldiers can face twenty thousand. Jesus concluded, "So it is with you: whoever does not let go of all that they have cannot be My disciple" (Luke 14:33).

Count the cost, Jesus says. Following Me will take everything.

In Luke 9 He names three parts of that cost. "Anyone who wants to come after Me must say no to themselves, pick up their cross every day, and keep following Me" (Luke 9:23).

- **Saying no to ourselves.** This is surrender. It does not mean hating ourselves or pretending we have no needs. It means taking ourselves off the throne. My plans, my comfort and my reputation are no longer in charge.
- **Picking up our cross every day.** In Jesus' day, a man carrying a cross was walking to his death. He was not coming back. To take up the cross is to accept that following Jesus may cost us our status, our money, our friends, and for some, our lives. Notice the words *every day*. The cross is not one great moment; it is a daily choice.
- **Keeping on following.** The cross is not the goal. Jesus is. We deny ourselves and take up the cross in order to walk behind Him.

And then the promise: "Anyone who sets out to save their own life will lose it. But anyone who loses their life because of Me will save it" (Luke 9:24). The way down is the way up. The life given away is the life truly found.

In the 1930s a young German pastor named Dietrich Bonhoeffer saw the churches of his country grow comfortable while a cruel government rose to power. Many Christians kept going to church and kept quiet. In his book, known in English as *The Cost of Discipleship* (1937), Bonhoeffer warned against what he called *cheap grace*: forgiveness preached without repentance, grace without the cross, Christianity without following Christ. Real grace, he said, is costly. It cost God the life of His Son, and it calls us to give our whole lives in return. It is grace because it gives us life; it is costly because it calls us to follow. Bonhoeffer trained young pastors in an underground seminary, joined the resistance to Hitler, and was arrested. He was executed in a concentration camp in April 1945, only weeks before the war in Europe ended. He did not simply write about the cost. He paid it.

### The sadhu who followed: Sundar Singh

Few lives show both the cost and the joy of following Jesus as clearly as that of Sundar Singh of India.

He was born in 1889 in the village of Rampur in the Punjab, in northern India, into a wealthy Sikh family. His mother was a deeply devout woman. She would rise before daylight to read the holy books of her religion, and she taught her son that he must seek God before he ate breakfast. Sundar grew up hungry for God. He read the Hindu and Sikh scriptures late into the night and questioned his teachers, but none of them could give him the peace his heart longed for.

When he was fourteen, his mother died, and then his older brother. His grief turned to bitterness. He went to a mission school, but he hated Christianity. One day, in front of his father and others, he tore up a copy of the Gospels and burned it.

For two days afterwards he was miserable. On the third night, he rose at three in the morning, bathed, and prayed that if there was a God at all, He would show Himself. If no answer came by dawn, he had decided, he would lie down on the railway line in front of the early train. He prayed and waited. Then, he later wrote, a light filled the room, and in it he saw not one of the gods he expected, but the living Christ, who said to him, "Why do you persecute Me? See, I have died on the Cross for you and for the whole world." Sundar fell on the ground before Him. In his own words:

> "My heart was filled with inexpressible joy and peace, and my whole life was entirely changed. Then the old Sundar Singh died and a new Sundar Singh, to serve the Living Christ, was born."
> Sadhu Sundar Singh, *With and Without Christ*

Now he learned the cost. His family begged, threatened and punished him. When he would not turn back, he was cast out. On his sixteenth birthday, in 1905, he was baptised in an Anglican church in Simla. Soon after, he put on the saffron robe of a sadhu, a wandering holy man, so that he could carry the message of Jesus in a form that Indian people would understand. He owned almost nothing and walked from village to village preaching Christ.

One evening he returned to his father's house. His father let him stay one night, but made him sit apart, as one who would pollute the family. He brought his son food, and gave him water by pouring it into his hands from a vessel held high above, as one would give water to an outcaste. Sundar wept. Yet he wrote that his heart was filled with peace, and he thanked his father both for his past love and even for this treatment, telling him that Christ had not forsaken him when he dishonoured Christ, and so he would not complain now. Then he slept under a tree in the fields.

On another night, after preaching until dark in a village near his old home, he lay hungry under a tree. He had eaten nothing all day. The tempter whispered that at home he had once had every comfort, and now he had nothing. Instead, joy rose in him so strongly that he began to sing, and he praised God until midnight. He wrote:

> "When I had ease and comfort at home, I knew nothing of this wonderful peace. But now when all is gone I have found in Christ this peace, which the world can neither give nor take away."
> Sadhu Sundar Singh, *With and Without Christ*

Sundar Singh travelled across India and into the high, cold mountains of Tibet, where he faced great hardship and danger for his faith. In the early 1920s he was invited to speak in Europe, America and Australia, and crowds came to hear the simple man in the saffron robe. He warned Western Christians that he had met many who were Christians in name but had never known the living Christ. In 1929 he set out once more for Tibet and was never seen again.

Many years after his conversion, he wrote, his long prayer was answered and his father also turned to the Lord. Looking back on all he had lost, Sundar said that Jesus' promise had proved true. Jesus said that anyone who has left home and family "because of Me and because of the gospel will receive a hundred times as much now, in this present time" (Mark 10:29–30), together with persecutions, and eternal life in the age to come. Sundar's comment was simple: "I have found not only a hundredfold, but a hundred times a hundredfold." He also wrote about the cross in a way that only someone who had carried it could:

> "Those who bear the cross know from experience that the cross bears them and takes them safely to their destiny."
> Sadhu Sundar Singh, *Reality and Religion*

Sundar Singh's calling was his own. Not every disciple wears a saffron robe or walks into Tibet. But every disciple counts the same cost and finds the same treasure.

### Learning from Jesus: His teaching, example and way of life

An apprentice learns in three ways: by listening to the master, by watching the master, and by sharing the master's life. So it is with us.

**His teaching.** "If you remain in My word, you really are My disciples. You will know the truth, and the truth will free you" (John 8:31–32). A disciple does not pick and choose among Jesus' words. We sit under them, read them, memorise them and obey them. The next chapter will open up the greatest single block of His teaching, the Sermon on the Mount, which ends with the call to hear His words and put them into practice (see Chapter 11).

**His example.** After washing His disciples' feet, Jesus said, "What I gave you was an example, so that you also would do as I have done for you" (John 13:15). We learn from how He treated people: the leper He touched, the woman at the well He spoke with, the children He welcomed, the enemies He forgave. In any situation we can ask, "If Jesus were living my life, in my house, at my job, how would He act here?"

**His way of life.** Jesus said, "Every disciple who is fully trained will become like the teacher" (Luke 6:40). The goal is not just to know what Jesus knew, but to become who He is: loving, truthful, humble, prayerful, brave. This is why the disciplines of Chapter 9 matter. Jesus slipped away to pray, fasted, knew the Scriptures deeply and kept the Sabbath rhythms. If we want His life, we take up His practices.

### Discipleship is lifelong

No one graduates from following Jesus. The new believer who came to Christ last month is a disciple. So is the grandmother who has walked with Him for sixty years. Both are still learning.

Paul, after decades of faithful service, wrote:

> "I do not mean that I already have all this, or that I have already been made perfect. But I keep pursuing it, trying to take hold of it, because Christ has taken hold of me." (Philippians 3:12)

This brings both comfort and challenge. The comfort is that you do not have to be mature to begin. Jesus called fishermen, tax collectors and zealots who argued about who was greatest. He was patient with them, and He will be patient with you. The challenge is that we must never stop growing. Some Christians stop learning after the first few years. They know the songs and the language, but their character has not changed in a long time. A disciple keeps asking, "Lord, what do You want to teach me now?"

Discipleship also looks different in each season of life. For a young believer it may mean learning to pray and read the Bible. For a young couple it may mean following Jesus in marriage and money. For a worker it may mean honesty when everyone else is cutting corners. For an older saint it may mean faithfulness through illness and the quiet ministry of prayer. Each season brings new lessons and new ways to take up the cross.

### Being discipled and discipling others

Jesus' last command to His followers was about this very thing:

> "Go, then, and make disciples of all the nations: baptize them into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and teach them to keep everything I have commanded you. And remember this: I Myself am with you every day, until the close of the age." (Matthew 28:19–20)

Notice that Jesus did not tell us to make converts or fill buildings. He told us to make disciples, and He described how: baptise them, and teach them to obey. Evangelism is the doorway (see Chapter 24), but discipleship is the house.

Paul shows the same pattern. He wrote to Timothy, "What you heard from me before many witnesses, pass on to trustworthy people who will be able to teach others as well" (2 Timothy 2:2). Count the generations in that one verse: Paul, Timothy, trustworthy people, and others. Faith is passed from life to life, like a torch passed from runner to runner.

This means two things for every believer.

**You need someone ahead of you.** Everyone needs an older brother or sister in Christ who can pray with them, answer questions, and speak the truth in love. If you have no such person, ask God for one, then look around your church for someone whose walk with God you respect, and ask if you can meet with them. In the eighteenth century, John Wesley organised his converts into small "classes" of about a dozen people who met every week under a leader. Each person was asked, in love, how their soul was doing. These simple meetings helped turn crowds of new believers into steady disciples.

**You need someone behind you.** You do not have to be a pastor or a Bible teacher to help someone else follow Jesus. A mother teaching her children to pray is making disciples. A believer in the office who invites a younger colleague to read John's Gospel over lunch is making disciples. A farmer who prays every week with the young man who works his land is making disciples. Paul said simply, "Take me as your model, just as I take Christ as mine" (1 Corinthians 11:1). Ask yourself: who could I help to take one step closer to Jesus?

Discipling others is not about having all the answers. It is about sharing your life. Jesus "appointed twelve to be with Him" (Mark 3:14) before He sent them to preach. Being with comes before being sent.

### Before His face, every day

A disciple lives *coram Deo*, before the face of God, not only in church but at the market, in the kitchen and in the fields. Sundar Singh's father turned his face away. But Sundar had seen another Face, glorious and loving, and that Face was enough. This is the secret of the cost of discipleship. We can let go of everything because we have found Someone better than everything.

Jesus once compared the Kingdom to a man who found treasure buried in a field. "Full of joy, he went and sold everything he owned and bought that field" (Matthew 13:44). The man was not sad to sell his goods. He was full of joy, because he knew what he was getting. That is discipleship: not a grim duty, but a joyful exchange.

In the next chapter we sit down at the Master's feet on the mountainside and listen to His teaching about life in the Kingdom of God (see Chapter 11). There we will see what kind of people His disciples are becoming.

### Questions for reflection

- How would you describe the difference between believing facts about Jesus and following Him as a disciple?
- Jesus told us to count the cost. What has following Him cost you so far? What might it cost you in the future?
- What part of Sundar Singh's story speaks to you most, and why?
- In which area of your life is Jesus calling you to say no to yourself and take up your cross today?
- Who has helped you to follow Jesus? Who is ahead of you now, and who is behind you?
- What is one way your character has changed since you began to follow Christ, and where do you most want to grow next?

### A step for this week

Think of one believer who is a little further along the road than you, and one who is a little behind you. This week, contact the first and ask whether you could meet, perhaps every two weeks, to pray together and talk about following Jesus. Then invite the second to read Luke 9:18–27 with you over a meal or a cup of tea, and talk together about what it means to take up the cross every day in your own homes, work and town.
