## Love: The Mark of a Christian

> "I am giving you a new commandment: love one another. In the same way that I have loved you, you too must love one another."
> — John 13:34 (OWB)

In the first three chapters we met the God whose face we seek: the Father who made us for Himself, the Son who came to bring us home, and the Holy Spirit who lives within us. Now we begin Part Two of this book, which asks how we walk with this God day by day. In the coming chapters we will look at Scripture, prayer, the morning watch, abiding in Christ and the spiritual disciplines.

But before any of those, we must speak about love. This order is deliberate. If we begin with practices, we may turn them into a new set of rules: read this much, pray this long, fast this often. Many sincere Christians have done exactly that, and they have grown tired, proud or discouraged. If we begin with love, everything else finds its proper place.

### Why love comes first

One day a teacher of the law asked Jesus which commandment was the greatest. It was a test, and the scholars of the day had long debated the answer. Jesus replied without hesitation: "'Love the Lord your God with your whole heart, with your whole soul, and with your whole mind.' This command is the great one, the first of all. A second command is of the same kind: 'Love your neighbor as you love yourself.' The whole of the law, and the prophets as well, hangs on these two commands" (Matthew 22:37-40).

Picture a coat hanging on a hook. Take away the hook and the coat falls to the floor. Jesus says that the whole of God's law hangs on love. Every command about worship, honesty, purity, generosity and justice is a way of loving God or loving people. Without love, obedience becomes a heap of rules lying on the ground.

The same is true of the habits we will explore in the next chapters. Reading the Bible, praying, rising early to meet God, fasting and serving are not ways of earning God's love. We already have it, as we saw in Chapter 2. They are ways of loving God back and of receiving more of His love. A husband who spends time with his wife is not paying for the right to be married. He is enjoying the marriage. So it is with us and God.

Paul makes the same point in his famous chapter on love. "I may be able to prophesy, and know every mystery and everything there is to know; I may have all the faith there is, enough to move mountains; but if I do not have love, I am nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:2). Great gifts, deep knowledge and impressive faith count for nothing without love. And when everything else passes away, love remains: "three things last: faith, hope and love. And of these three, the greatest is love" (1 Corinthians 13:13).

### God's love is the source of ours

Where does this love come from? Not from inside us. Our natural love is weak, selfish and easily worn out. We love those who love us, and we grow cold towards those who hurt us. The love God asks for must come from God Himself.

John writes, "This is where love is found: not in our having loved God, but in His loving us and sending His Son to be the atoning sacrifice for our sins" (1 John 4:10). And then, in one short sentence: "We love because He loved us first" (1 John 4:19).

Think of the moon. It has no light of its own. It shines only because it reflects the sun. When the earth comes between them, the moon goes dark. We are like that. We can only love as we stand in the light of God's love for us. When we drift away from Him, our love grows dim. When we return to Him, it shines again.

This is why living coram Deo, before the face of God, is the key to a life of love. The more we know His face turned towards us in kindness, the more His love flows through us. Paul says, "through the Spirit His love has flooded our hearts" (Romans 5:5). The Holy Spirit, whom we met in Chapter 3, is the One who pours God's love into us, and love is the first fruit He grows.

### Loving God with all we are

The greatest commandment goes back to the words every Jewish family recited daily: "Hear, Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one. You are to love the LORD your God with your whole heart, with your whole being, and with all your strength" (Deuteronomy 6:4-5).

Notice the word "whole". God does not ask for a portion of our love, like a tax collector taking his share. He asks for all of it: heart, soul, mind and strength. That means our feelings and desires, our thoughts and beliefs, our energy and abilities. It means loving God on Sunday morning and on Monday at work, in the field and in the kitchen, when we are happy and when we are grieving.

This does not mean we must stop loving our families, our friends or our homeland. It means God takes first place, and all our other loves are ordered under Him. When God is first, our other loves become healthier, not weaker. A man who loves God above all will love his wife better, not less.

In the twelfth century a French monk named Bernard, abbot of the monastery of Clairvaux, was asked a simple question by a church leader in Rome: why should we love God, and how much? He answered in a little book called *On Loving God*:

> "I should say that God Himself is the motive of our love to Him, and that the measure of due love is to be without measure."
> Bernard of Clairvaux, *On Loving God*

Bernard went on to describe how love grows, step by step. At first, he said, a person loves only himself. Then, discovering that he cannot manage without God's help, he begins to love God, but for his own sake. Over time, as he keeps coming to God in his need, he begins to taste God's goodness and love Him for God's own sake. At the highest step, which Bernard thought was rarely reached in this life, a person even loves himself only for God's sake. Whatever we think of his stages, Bernard's point is clear and freeing: you do not have to begin with perfect love. You can begin where you are, even with mixed motives, and God will grow your love as you keep coming to Him.

### Loving our neighbour, our family and the Church

Jesus joined the second command to the first: love your neighbour as yourself. John shows us why they cannot be separated: "If someone says, 'I love God,' but hates a brother or sister, that person is a liar. For anyone who does not love a brother or sister, whom they have seen, is not able to love God, whom they have not seen" (1 John 4:20).

Who is my neighbour? Jesus answered that question with the story of the good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37). Your neighbour is anyone God places in your path who needs your help, whatever their tribe, language, religion or social standing.

Love begins at home. It is often easier to be kind to strangers than to the people we live with. The husband who is patient with customers but harsh with his wife, the mother who serves the church but shouts at her children, the young person who is polite to friends but rude to parents: each needs to hear that love starts in the house. Paul's description of love is a good mirror for family life: "Love is patient; love is kind. Love does not envy, does not show off, is not puffed up. It does not behave shamefully, it does not look for its own advantage, it does not lose its temper, and it does not keep a tally of wrongs" (1 Corinthians 13:4-5).

Love must also mark the Church. On the night before He died, Jesus gave His disciples a new commandment, and then added: "If you have love for one another, everyone will know by this that you are My disciples" (John 13:35). Not our buildings, our programmes, our music or even our doctrine, important as these are. Our love for one another is the sign the world is meant to see. When churches split over pride, when believers of different tribes or classes will not eat together, when gossip runs through the congregation, the world sees something else.

Paul tells us how to dress for life together: "So put on, like clothing, a heart of compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness and patience" (Colossians 3:12). Then he adds, "Over all of these put on love, which binds everything together and makes it complete" (Colossians 3:14). Love is like the belt that holds all the other garments in place.

### Forgiveness and reconciliation

Nowhere is love tested more than when we have been hurt. Anyone who lives with other people will be wronged: by a spouse, a parent, a business partner, a church leader, a neighbour. What then?

The Bible's command is clear: "Be patient with one another's faults, and when someone has a grievance against another, forgive. The Lord has forgiven you freely; you must do the same" (Colossians 3:13). The reason we forgive is that we have been forgiven. We owed God a debt we could never pay, and He cancelled it at the cross. How can we refuse to cancel the far smaller debts others owe us?

It helps to be clear about what forgiveness is and is not. Forgiveness does not mean pretending the wrong did not happen, or that it did not matter. It does not mean that there are no consequences, or that we must immediately trust someone who has abused that trust. It means releasing the person from our desire for revenge and handing the matter to God, the just Judge. Reconciliation, the restoring of the relationship, is the goal wherever it is possible, but it needs both sides. Paul says, "If it can be done, and so far as it lies with you, keep peace with everyone" (Romans 12:18). We do our part, and we leave the rest to God.

### Corrie ten Boom: the hand she could not lift

Few people have shown the cost and power of forgiveness more clearly than Corrie ten Boom. She was born in the Netherlands in 1892 and grew up in the town of Haarlem, above her father's watch shop. The ten Booms were a warm, praying family who opened their home to anyone in need.

During the Second World War, when Germany occupied the Netherlands, the family hid Jewish people and members of the resistance in their house, including in a small secret room behind a false wall in Corrie's bedroom. In February 1944 they were betrayed and arrested. Corrie's elderly father died in prison within days. Corrie and her sister Betsie were eventually sent to Ravensbrück, a women's concentration camp in Germany. There, amid cold, hunger and cruelty, the sisters held secret Bible readings and prayed with other prisoners. Betsie grew weaker and died in the camp in December 1944. Shortly afterwards Corrie was released.

After the war Corrie travelled widely, telling people that God's love is deeper than the deepest pit of suffering, and that He forgives all who come to Him. In 1947, after speaking at a church in Munich in Germany, she was approached by a man she recognised. He had been one of the guards at Ravensbrück, where her sister had died. He did not remember her, but he told her that he had since become a Christian and knew God had forgiven him for the cruel things he had done. Now he wanted to hear forgiveness from one of the women who had suffered there. He held out his hand.

Corrie later wrote that she stood frozen. She had preached forgiveness to others, yet she could not lift her own hand. Memories of Betsie's suffering came flooding back. She knew that she must forgive, and she knew that she could not do it in her own strength. So she prayed silently, asking Jesus to help her. Woodenly, almost mechanically, she put out her hand and took his. As she did, she said, a warmth seemed to flow through her, and she found herself forgiving him from her heart. She later said that she had never known God's love so intensely as in that moment.

Corrie's story teaches us something vital. Forgiveness does not always begin with a feeling. Often it begins with an act of obedience, made in weakness, trusting God to supply the love we lack. When she could not love, God loved through her. "We love because He loved us first" (1 John 4:19).

### Loving our enemies

Jesus goes further still. Forgiving a friend who has hurt us is hard enough. But He says, "love your enemies, and pray for the people who persecute you, so that you will be children of your Father in heaven. He makes His sun rise on evil people and good people alike, and He sends rain on those who do right and those who do wrong" (Matthew 5:44-45).

This is the love that marks God's children. Anyone can love people who love them back. "If you love the people who love you, what reward is there in that?" (Matthew 5:46). But when we bless those who curse us, we look like our Father, who gives sun and rain even to those who reject Him. And we look like Jesus, who prayed for the soldiers nailing Him to the cross: "Father, forgive them. They do not know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34).

In Korea, a pastor named Son Yang-won lived out this command in a way that still moves people today. He served for years among people with leprosy in a colony in the south of the country, caring for those whom others shunned. Under Japanese colonial rule he was imprisoned for refusing to bow at Shinto shrines. Then, in 1948, during a violent uprising, his two eldest sons, both young Christians, were shot dead by a young man caught up in the rebellion. When the killer was captured and about to be executed, Pastor Son pleaded for his life. He then took the young man into his family as his own son, and led him towards Christ. Two years later, during the Korean War, Son Yang-won himself was killed by soldiers of the invading army. Many Korean Christians remember him as a man whose love for enemies came straight from the cross.

Most of us will never face such a test. But we all have people we find hard to love: a rival at work, a neighbour who wronged us, someone from a group that has hurt our people, a relative who betrayed us. Loving them does not mean approving of what they did. It means wanting God's good for them, praying for them, refusing revenge and doing them good when we can. This is only possible as we live close to the God who loved us when we were His enemies (Romans 5:8).

### From love to communion

There is one more thing to say about love. Love always wants to be near the one it loves. A young man in love does not need to be told to spend time with the woman he hopes to marry. He looks for every chance. A mother whose child has moved to a distant city waits eagerly for a phone call.

So it is with God. When we truly love Him, we want to hear Him and be with Him. Jesus promised this: "Whoever loves Me will be loved by My Father; I in turn will love them and show Myself to them" (John 14:21). And again: "Anyone who loves Me will keep My word. My Father will love them, and We will come to them and make Our dwelling place with them" (John 14:23).

Notice how love, obedience and communion are woven together. Those who love Jesus keep His word. Those who keep His word find the Father and the Son making their home with them. This is the life of coram Deo at its warmest: not a servant standing nervously before a master, but a friend sitting at a table with the One who loves him.

How does God speak to those who love Him? First and above all, through His Word, the Scriptures. That is where Chapter 5 begins. If love is the heart of our life with God, the Bible is the place where we most clearly hear the voice of the One we love.

### Questions for reflection

- Why do you think Jesus put love before every other command? How does that change the way you think about Bible reading, prayer and other spiritual habits?
- "We love because He loved us first" (1 John 4:19). When have you most clearly experienced God's love for you?
- Bernard of Clairvaux said that we can begin loving God even with mixed motives. Where are you on that journey?
- Read 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 and put your own name in place of the word "love". Which phrases are hardest to say honestly?
- Is there someone you need to forgive, or someone you need to ask for forgiveness? What would be the first step?
- Who is an "enemy" in your life, someone you find hard to love? How could you pray for them this week?

### A step for this week

Choose one person you find hard to love: a family member, a colleague, a neighbour or someone in your church. Each day this week, pray for that person by name, asking God to bless them in a specific way. Then, before the week ends, do one concrete act of kindness for them, such as a greeting, a small gift, a helping hand or a word of thanks. If there is a wrong between you that needs forgiving, ask God for the strength Corrie ten Boom asked for, and take the first step you can, even if your feelings have not yet caught up.
