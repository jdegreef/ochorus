## The Church: God's Family Together

> "They gave themselves fully to the apostles' teaching and to the shared life, to the breaking of bread and to the prayers."
> — Acts 2:42 (OWB)

On the day of Pentecost the Holy Spirit came, Peter preached, and about three thousand people were baptised. What happened next? They did not simply go home with a warm feeling. They became a community. Luke describes it in a few unforgettable sentences: they learned together, shared their lives and their possessions, ate together in their homes, prayed together and praised God together. "And each day the Lord brought into their company more people who were being saved" (Acts 2:47).

Two thousand years later, that same family meets under a mango tree in a village, in a rented school hall in a busy city, in a cathedral of ancient stone, in a house church whose members whisper their songs for fear of the police, and in a tin-roofed building where the drums can be heard three streets away. The Church is the most widespread family on earth.

So far in Part Four we have looked at marriage, children and work. Now we come to the family that holds all the others: the Church. Living *coram Deo* is never a private matter between me and God alone. We stand before His face together, as His people. In this chapter we will look at what the Church is, why we need one another, what the Church does when it gathers, how it holds together across its many differences, and how each of us can serve in it. The next chapter will look at how the Church is organised and led.

### The body and the bride of Christ

The New Testament uses many pictures for the Church. Two of the most important are the body and the bride.

First, the Church is the **body of Christ**. "You are Christ's body, and each of you individually is one of its parts" (1 Corinthians 12:27). Christ is the head: God "has given Him to the church to be head over all things. The church is His body" (Ephesians 1:22–23). A body is alive. It is not an organisation that people join like a club, but a living thing in which every part is connected to the head and to every other part. Through the Church, the risen Jesus still has hands to heal, feet to go, and a voice to speak in the world.

Second, the Church is the **bride of Christ**. We saw in Chapter 17 that marriage is a picture of "Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:32). Christ "loved the church and handed Himself over for her" (Ephesians 5:25). The Bible ends with the new Jerusalem "descending out of heaven from God, ready like a bride who has adorned herself for her husband" (Revelation 21:2). The Church is loved. Whatever her faults today, and they are many, she is the one Jesus died for, and He is making her beautiful.

Notice that in both pictures, the Church is people, not a building. We say "I am going to church", but in the Bible's language, you do not go to church: you *are* the Church. The building is only where the Church meets.

### Why we need each other: no solo Christians

Many people today say, "I love Jesus, but I do not need the Church." Some have been hurt by Christians. Some find church services dull. Some simply prefer to watch a sermon on their phone at home. These are real struggles, and churches often do need to repent of the hurt they have caused. But the Bible knows nothing of a Christian who belongs to Christ and not to His people.

Paul puts it this way: "So the eye cannot tell the hand, 'I can do without you,' and the head cannot tell the feet, 'I can do without you'" (1 Corinthians 12:21). A hand cut off from the body does not become free; it dies. In the same way, a believer cut off from other believers grows cold. A burning coal taken out of the fire soon turns grey. Put it back among the others, and it glows again.

So the letter to the Hebrews urges, "Let us also keep one another in mind, so as to rouse each other to love and good works. Let us not walk away from our own gatherings, as some have made it their practice, but keep urging one another on" (Hebrews 10:24–25). We need others to encourage us when we are weak, to correct us when we wander, to pray for us when we cannot pray, and to rejoice with us when God is good. And others need us. "If one part is in pain, all the parts share its pain; if one part is honored, all the parts share its joy" (1 Corinthians 12:26).

### Cyprian of Carthage: a bishop who held the family together

In the city of Carthage, on the coast of North Africa in what is now Tunisia, lived a wealthy and gifted teacher of public speaking named Cyprian. Around the year 246 he came to faith in Christ as an adult. He gave much of his wealth to the poor, gave himself to the study of Scripture, and within a few years the Christians of Carthage chose him as their bishop.

He led the church through some of its hardest years. In 250 the Roman emperor Decius ordered everyone in the empire to offer sacrifice to the gods. Many Christians were imprisoned or killed. Many others gave in to fear and sacrificed, or bought false certificates saying they had. Cyprian went into hiding and led his people through letters. When the persecution eased, the church faced a painful question: what should be done with those who had denied Christ and now wanted to return? Some said they should never be received back. Others said they should be welcomed at once, without any repentance. The church was in danger of breaking apart.

Cyprian called for real repentance and also a real welcome back, and he worked tirelessly to keep the church united. He wrote a short book, *On the Unity of the Church*, which has been read ever since. In it he used pictures from nature to show how one Church can be spread over many places:

> "As there are many rays of the sun, but one light; and many branches of a tree, but one strength based in its tenacious root; and since from one spring flow many streams, although the multiplicity seems diffused in the liberality of an overflowing abundance, yet the unity is still preserved in the source."
> Cyprian of Carthage, *The Treatises of Cyprian*

In the same book he wrote his most famous line: "He can no longer have God for his Father, who has not the Church for his mother." Christians of different traditions apply those words in different ways, and Cyprian himself was thinking of people who had broken away into rival groups. But the heart of what he said is true for all of us: God does not give us Himself as Father without also giving us His family.

A few years later a terrible plague swept through Carthage. People abandoned their sick relatives in the streets. Cyprian urged the Christians to care for the sick and bury the dead, not only their fellow believers but their pagan neighbours too. In his treatise *On the Mortality* (found in *The Treatises of Cyprian*) he said that the plague was testing everyone, to see "whether they who are in health tend the sick; whether relations affectionately love their kindred; whether masters pity their languishing servants". The Church was to be a family whose love spilled over its walls.

In 258, under the emperor Valerian, Cyprian was arrested and beheaded outside Carthage, with his people around him. Like Augustine after him, he is one of the great Christian leaders of Africa, and a reminder to African readers that the Church in their continent is not a recent arrival. It is as old as almost any church on earth.

### What the Church does together

Acts 2:42 gives four marks of the first church: "They gave themselves fully to the apostles' teaching and to the shared life, to the breaking of bread and to the prayers." These are still the heart of church life.

**Teaching.** The first Christians gave themselves to the apostles' teaching, which we now have in the New Testament. A healthy church is fed on the Word of God, explained clearly and applied to real life (see Chapter 5).

**The shared life.** The word often translated "fellowship" means sharing something in common. It is more than tea after the service. "Everyone who believed kept together and treated all they had as shared" (Acts 2:44). They sold land and goods to help anyone in need. Today that may mean bringing food when a family has a new baby, helping a brother find work, or paying a sister's hospital bill.

**The breaking of bread.** This includes ordinary meals in one another's homes, where "at home they broke bread, eating their food with joyful and simple hearts" (Acts 2:46), and it includes the Lord's Supper, which Jesus gave on the night He was betrayed. Jesus gave His Church two sacraments, outward signs of His grace: baptism and the Lord's Supper. In baptism we are marked as His, "buried with Him into death, in order that, as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might live a life that is new" (Romans 6:4). In the Supper we remember His death and are fed by Him: "For each time you eat this bread and drink the cup, you are announcing the Lord's death, until He comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26). Christians hold different convictions about who should be baptised and how, and about how Christ is present in the Supper. These are important matters, but they are secondary ones that should not break our fellowship as believers (see Appendix A). All agree that Christ commanded both, and that both point us to His death and resurrection.

**The prayers.** The first church prayed together, regularly and often. A praying church is a living church. Many of the great revivals in history began with a few believers who met to pray.

Out of these four flowed **worship**. They were "praising God and well regarded by all the people" (Acts 2:47). When the Church gathers, we come before the face of God together, to sing His praise, hear His Word and offer Him our lives.

### Unity in diversity across traditions and cultures

The Church is one, and yet it is wonderfully varied. It includes Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant believers; Anglicans and Baptists, Lutherans and Pentecostals, Methodists and Presbyterians and many independent churches. It includes every tribe and tongue. John saw the final picture: "a crowd so vast that no one could number it. They came from every nation and from all tribes and peoples and languages" (Revelation 7:9).

Paul tells us how to hold this family together: "Be humble and gentle in every way, and be patient, putting up with one another in love. Spare no effort to keep the unity the Spirit gives, with peace as the bond that holds you together" (Ephesians 4:2–3). Notice that we do not create this unity; the Spirit gives it. Our task is to keep it. And it rests on what we share: "There is one body and one Spirit... one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all" (Ephesians 4:4–6).

John Wesley, the eighteenth-century evangelist, had strong views and was not afraid to state them. Yet in his sermon "Catholic Spirit" (the word *catholic* here means universal, for the whole Church) he asked a question every Christian should hear:

> "Though we cannot think alike, may we not love alike? May we not be of one heart, though we are not of one opinion? Without all doubt, we may."
> John Wesley, *Sermons on Several Occasions*

Unity across cultures can be even harder than unity across traditions. In many places the deepest divisions in the Church are not about doctrine but about tribe, caste, class, race or language. Yet Peter writes to a mixed church of Jews and Gentiles: "At one time you were no people at all; now you are God's people" (1 Peter 2:10). In Christ we become a new people, "a royal priesthood, a holy nation" (1 Peter 2:9), with a new family identity that runs deeper than our earthly ones.

Bakht Singh of India showed what this can look like. Born into a Sikh family in the Punjab in 1903, he came to faith in Christ while studying in Canada, and returned to India in the 1930s as an evangelist. He planted congregations that were led by Indians and supported by Indian believers, simple in worship and devoted to prayer and the Word. At the large gatherings he led in Hyderabad, thousands of believers came together for days of worship and teaching, and people of different castes, languages and backgrounds sat together and ate together as one family. In a land where caste divided people at every meal, that shared table was itself a sermon.

The German pastor Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who led an underground seminary for the Confessing Church in the 1930s, wrote a small book called *Life Together* about the community he shared with his students. One of his warnings is worth remembering. Christian community, he said, is a gift God has already given us in Christ, not an ideal we must build. Those who fall in love with their dream of the perfect church end up despising the real one. The church you belong to will disappoint you. Love it anyway, because Christ does.

### Serving in the local church

The universal Church is made real in local churches, ordinary congregations with names, addresses and imperfect members. That is where we are called to belong and to serve.

"We have many parts in a single body, and the parts do not all have the same task. In the same way, many as we are, we are one body in Christ, and each of us is a part that belongs to all the others" (Romans 12:4–5). Every believer has a part to play. The church grows, Paul says, "as each part does its own work" (Ephesians 4:16). It is not meant to be a few leaders doing everything while the rest watch.

You do not need a title to serve. Arrive early and welcome newcomers at the door. Learn the names of the children. Visit the sick member nobody else has visited. Help clean the building, set out chairs, cook for a funeral, drive an elderly widow to the service. Join a small group where people know each other well enough to pray honestly. Give faithfully. Use the gifts God has given you, which we will explore in Chapter 22. And honour those who lead, while remembering that leaders exist to equip all of God's people for service, as we will see in Chapter 21.

When you serve in this way, you are not serving a human institution. You are serving Christ in His body, before His face. "So welcome one another, just as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God" (Romans 15:7).

### Questions for reflection

- Which picture of the Church means most to you: the body or the bride? Why?
- Have you ever been tempted to follow Christ without His Church? What drew you away, and what might draw you back?
- Cyprian worked to hold together a church divided by fear and failure. Where is your church in danger of division, and how could you help keep "the unity the Spirit gives"?
- Look at the four marks in Acts 2:42. Which is strongest in your church, and which is weakest?
- What divisions of tribe, class, language or background does your church need to overcome? What could a shared table look like for you?
- What is one way you could serve in your local church that you are not doing now?

### A step for this week

This week, do one thing to strengthen the family of your local church beyond attending the main service. Invite someone from church, preferably someone from a different background, age or tribe from yours, to share a meal in your home. Or visit or call a member who has been sick or absent. Before you go, pray Ephesians 4:2–3 for your church by name, asking God to help you keep the unity His Spirit gives.
