## Work and Workers: Serving as unto the Lord

> "Whatever you are doing, put your whole self into the work, as for the Lord and not for people, knowing that you will receive from the Lord the inheritance as your reward. It is the Lord Christ you serve as slaves."
> — Colossians 3:23–24 (OWB)

Before the sun rises, a woman in a market town is already setting out her tomatoes and onions in neat piles. A farmer is walking to his field with a hoe on his shoulder. A nurse is finishing a long night shift. A young man in a city office is opening his laptop, already tired at the thought of the week ahead. A mother is preparing breakfast for four children while planning how to stretch the money until the end of the month.

Most of us spend the greater part of our waking lives working, whether for wages, on our own land, in our own small business, or in the unpaid work of caring for a home and family. And many Christians quietly believe that this part of life does not really count with God. Sunday is for God; Monday is for survival.

This chapter says the opposite. Living *coram Deo*, before the face of God, does not stop at the church door or the front door of the home. The Lord who sees us at prayer sees us at the market stall, the office desk and the kitchen sink. In the last two chapters we looked at marriage and the raising of children. Now we step out into the world of work, where God is just as present and just as interested.

### Work was God's idea

Work is not a result of the fall. Before sin ever entered the world, "The LORD God took the man and settled him in the garden of Eden to work it and take care of it" (Genesis 2:15). Adam was a gardener before he was a sinner. God Himself is a worker: the Bible opens with six days of creating, and it describes God resting from "all the work" He had done. We are made in the image of a working God, and so we were made to work.

What the fall did was to make work painful. After Adam's sin God said, "Through painful work you will eat from it all the days of your life" (Genesis 3:17), and "You will eat bread by the sweat of your brow" (Genesis 3:19). That is why work is so often frustrating: weeds in the field, broken machines, unfair bosses, effort that comes to nothing. But the frustration is the curse; work itself remains a gift.

Jesus spent most of His earthly life not preaching but working with His hands. The people of Nazareth asked, "Is He not the carpenter, the son of Mary?" (Mark 6:3). For years the Son of God made doors, tables and ploughs, and every one of them was done to please His Father. Paul too, the greatest missionary of the early Church, earned his living by making tents (Acts 18:3). He told the believers in Thessalonica to "live calmly, look after your own affairs and earn your living with your hands" (1 Thessalonians 4:11).

Scripture honours good work. "Do you see someone who is skilled at their work? They will serve kings, not people of no account" (Proverbs 22:29). The carpenter who joins wood carefully, the teacher who prepares her lessons well, the driver who keeps his vehicle safe, the accountant who keeps honest books: all of them are doing something God values.

Work also gives us something to share. Paul told former thieves, "Instead they must work hard, doing something good with their own hands, so that they have something to share with anyone in need" (Ephesians 4:28). We work not only to feed ourselves but to bless others: to support our families, to help a neighbour in trouble, and to give to God's work. The money you earn honestly at the market or the office can become food for a widow, school fees for an orphan or support for a missionary. Seen this way, even the most ordinary job becomes a channel of God's care for the world (we return to this in Chapter 25).

Martin Luther saw this clearly in the sixteenth century. In his day many believed that monks and nuns lived a higher kind of life, while ordinary people with ordinary jobs served God less. Luther taught that every honest calling, or *vocation*, is a way of serving God and neighbour. The maid, the farmer and the shoemaker please God as much as any priest, when they do their work in faith. In *The Large Catechism* he imagined what would happen if a servant girl understood this:

> "If this truth, then, could be impressed upon the poor people, a servant-girl would leap and praise and thank God; and with her tidy work for which she receives support and wages she would acquire such a treasure as all that are esteemed the greatest saints have not obtained."
> Martin Luther, *The Large Catechism*

Your work, done for God, is not second-class service. It is part of your worship.

### Servants and masters: reading these passages honestly

The main New Testament passages on work are addressed to "slaves" and "masters" (Ephesians 6:5–9; Colossians 3:22–4:1). Many readers, especially in Africa and among people whose ancestors were enslaved, find these passages painful. We must read them honestly.

In the Roman world of the first century, slavery was everywhere. A large part of the population of many cities was enslaved. People became slaves through war, debt, kidnapping or being born to enslaved parents. Some were educated and held responsible posts in households; many others laboured in fields and mines under brutal conditions. All of them were legally owned by another person. The early churches were small, poor and often persecuted, and many of their members were slaves. The apostles did not call for an armed uprising, which would have been crushed. Instead they taught enslaved believers how to live faithfully where they were, and they taught masters a way of thinking that would, in time, undermine the whole system.

Look at what the New Testament actually says. In Christ, "there is no slave or free person" (Galatians 3:28); all are one family. Paul tells slaves who have the chance of freedom, "make the most of it" (1 Corinthians 7:21). He names "slave traders" among the lawbreakers whom God's law condemns (1 Timothy 1:10), echoing the Law of Moses, which said that whoever kidnaps and sells a person must die (Exodus 21:16). He tells masters that they and their slaves have the same Master in heaven, who "does not show favoritism" (Ephesians 6:9). And in his letter to Philemon, he sends the runaway slave Onesimus back to his master asking him to receive him "no longer as a slave but as more than a slave, a dear brother" (Philemon 1:16). Once a master sees his slave as a dear brother, slavery has lost its foundation.

So let it be said plainly: this book does not endorse slavery. The buying and selling of human beings, whether in the Roman Empire, in the Atlantic slave trade that carried millions of Africans across the ocean, or in the trafficking, forced labour and debt bondage that still exist today, is sin against God and against people made in His image. Those who once used these verses to defend slavery twisted them.

What, then, do these passages say to us? The principles God gave to slaves and masters in their hard situation apply with even greater force to employees and employers, who meet as free people. That is how most Christians have always applied them, and it is how we will apply them now.

### William Wilberforce: a statesman before the face of God

William Wilberforce was born in 1759 in Hull, in the north of England, to a wealthy merchant family. He was clever, charming and witty, and at twenty-one he was elected to Parliament. He loved society, cards and late nights. Then, in 1784 and 1785, during long journeys with a Christian friend, he read the New Testament and was converted. It changed the whole direction of his life.

His first thought was that he should leave politics and become a clergyman, because surely that was the way to serve God properly. He went to see John Newton, a pastor who had once been a slave-ship captain and had become a Christian and the writer of the hymn "Amazing Grace". Newton urged him to stay in Parliament. God, he believed, could use Wilberforce right where he was.

Wilberforce stayed. In 1787 he wrote in his diary that God had set before him two great tasks: ending the slave trade, and the reformation of the morals of his country. For twenty years he brought the matter before Parliament again and again. He was mocked, threatened and defeated many times. He worked alongside others, among them Olaudah Equiano, a man born in what is now Nigeria, kidnapped as a child and enslaved, who later bought his freedom, became a Christian, and wrote a book telling his story that moved thousands of readers. At last, in 1807, Parliament abolished the British slave trade. Wilberforce kept working for the freedom of all enslaved people in the British Empire, and the law ending slavery passed in 1833, just days before he died.

Wilberforce did not think his work in Parliament was less spiritual than preaching. In his book *A Practical View of Christianity* he noted that some early Christians had thought they should give up their ordinary affairs, and that the apostle corrected them:

> "But the Apostle most pointedly guarded them against so gross an error, and expressly and repeatedly enjoined them to perform the particular duties of their several stations with increased alacrity and fidelity, that they might thereby do credit to their Christian profession."
> William Wilberforce, *A Practical View of Christianity*

"Their several stations" means wherever God has placed each of us. Wilberforce's station was Parliament. Yours may be a farm, a classroom, a hospital, a shop or a home. The call is the same: serve there with energy and faithfulness, as unto the Lord. Elsewhere in the same book Wilberforce described the true Christian as one who desires to please God in all his thoughts, words and actions, and then added: "No calling is proscribed, no pursuit is forbidden, no science or art, no pleasure is disallowed, which is reconcilable with this principle."

### Employees: serving with integrity

Here is what Paul told slaves, and what he tells every worker today: "Do not work only while they are watching, to win their favor, but with an undivided heart, out of reverence for the Lord" (Colossians 3:22). That is a description of *coram Deo* at work. Most of us work differently when the boss is in the room. We look busy. We speak politely. When the boss leaves, we slow down, or spend the afternoon on our phones. But the Christian worker has another Master who never leaves the room. "Whatever you are doing, put your whole self into the work, as for the Lord and not for people" (Colossians 3:23).

What does this look like on Monday morning?

- **Honesty.** Paul tells workers not to steal from their employers, "but to show that they can be fully trusted" (Titus 2:10). Do not take what belongs to the company, whether money, supplies or time. Record your hours truthfully. Refuse bribes, even when "everyone does it".
- **Diligence.** Do your work well, even the parts no one will check. Arrive on time. Finish what you start.
- **A good attitude.** Do not join in the constant complaining about the boss. Speak respectfully, even when you disagree.
- **Trustworthiness.** When Daniel's enemies tried to find something to accuse him of in his government work, "they could find no grounds and no corruption, because he was trustworthy" (Daniel 6:4). Could that be said of you?

Not every job is pleasant. Brother Lawrence, the seventeenth-century French monk, was assigned to the kitchen of his monastery, and he did not like it. But he learned to do it all for God. In *The Practice of the Presence of God* we read that:

> "in his business in the kitchen (to which he had naturally a great aversion), having accustomed himself to do everything there for the love of God, and with prayer, upon all occasions, for His grace to do his work well, he had found everything easy, during fifteen years that he had been employed there."
> Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*

If you are in a job you did not choose and do not enjoy, you are not outside God's care. Ask Him for grace to do this day's work well, for His sake. And if you are without paid work, or your work is unpaid caring for children, the sick or the elderly, remember that God sees and values that labour too. "It is the Lord Christ you serve" (Colossians 3:24).

### Employers and leaders: a Master in heaven

Paul did not speak only to those at the bottom. "Masters, give your slaves what is right and fair, because you know that you too have a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1). In Ephesians he adds, "Stop threatening them" (Ephesians 6:9).

If you employ others, manage a team, run a farm with hired workers, or lead in any organisation, you also live before the face of God. You will give account to Him for how you treated the people under you. The Law of Moses said, "When you hire a laborer, the pay must not stay in your hands overnight until morning" (Leviticus 19:13). James warned rich landowners, "The pay you held back from the laborers who mowed your fields is crying out" (James 5:4). God hears the cry of the worker who is cheated.

So a Christian employer pays fair wages, and pays them on time. He keeps his workers safe. He does not shout, threaten or humiliate. He does not favour his own tribe or relatives in hiring and promotion. He keeps his promises. He treats the cleaner with the same respect as the manager, remembering that both are made in God's image, and that there is "no favoritism with the Lord" (Colossians 3:25).

### Witness in the workplace

The workplace is one of the main places where unbelievers meet Christians. Paul wanted believers to work quietly and well "so that outsiders will see you live rightly" (1 Thessalonians 4:12). Jesus said, "your light must shine where people can see it, so that they will see your good deeds and give glory to your Father in heaven" (Matthew 5:16).

Witness at work begins with the quality of your work and the quality of your character. A Christian who preaches to colleagues but arrives late and leaves early does more harm than good. A Christian who is honest, reliable, kind and calm under pressure earns the right to be heard. Then, when a colleague is sick, grieving or asking big questions, you will have opportunities to pray for them and to speak of Christ. Do not use your employer's time to preach, but do not hide your faith either. Be ready, at the right moment, to explain the hope you have.

### Rest alongside work

God designed us not only to work but also to rest. The fourth commandment gave Israel a rhythm: "You have six days to labor and do all your work, but the seventh day is a Sabbath for the LORD your God" (Exodus 20:9–10). Notice that the command protected servants, foreigners and even animals. Rest was a gift for the weakest, not only for the powerful.

In Chapter 11 we saw this book's view: Jesus Himself is our Sabbath rest. He calls, "Come to Me, all of you who are worn out with work and weighed down with burdens, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Every day, then, becomes a day of resting in Him, relying on God for everything rather than on our own striving. Christians hold different views about how the Sabbath command applies to Sunday or Saturday (see the Appendix), but all agree that regular physical rest remains God's good gift and a wise rhythm for workers and leaders alike.

Luther, explaining the commandment about the holy day in *The Large Catechism*, said that one of its purposes was simply the rest of tired workers: it was given "for the common people, man-servants and maid-servants, who have been attending to their work and trade the whole week, that for a day they may retire in order to rest and be refreshed." Many of us need to hear that. "It does you no good to get up early and stay up late, eating food earned by anxious work; He gives sleep to the one He loves" (Psalm 127:2). Working seven days a week is not a sign of great faith. It may be a sign that we trust our own effort more than God's provision. Rest is an act of trust.

In the next chapter we turn from the workplace to the place where God's people gather to rest, worship and grow together: the Church, God's family.

### Questions for reflection

- Do you tend to think of your daily work as "spiritual" or "unspiritual"? How does Genesis 2:15 change that?
- How would you explain to a friend why the New Testament passages on slaves and masters do not endorse slavery?
- Wilberforce thought of leaving politics to serve God, but was encouraged to stay. Where has God placed you, and how could you serve Him there?
- Colossians 3:22 speaks of working "not only while they are watching". In what areas of your work is this hardest for you?
- If you lead or employ others, which part of Colossians 4:1 or Leviticus 19:13 challenges you most?
- What does your weekly pattern of work and rest say about where your trust lies?

### A step for this week

Each working day this week, before you begin your work (whether at a job, on the farm, in a business or at home), pray this short prayer: "Lord Jesus, I do this work for You today. Help me to do it well and honestly, and to treat everyone I meet as You would." At the end of each day, take two minutes to look back: where did you work as for the Lord, and where did you work only for people? Thank God for the first, and ask His forgiveness and help for the second.
