We do not know exactly how it began. The arrest of Ignatius left no report, no transcript of a trial, no list of charges we can read today. What we know is the outcome, because he tells us himself: he had been condemned, and he was being taken to Rome to die. He was to be thrown to the wild beasts.

This happened in the reign of the emperor Trajan, who ruled from the end of the first century into the second. Tradition dates the death of Ignatius to around AD 108. It is worth being honest that the date is not certain — the letters are almost the only firsthand source we have, and only one of them carries so much as a day of the month, with no year attached. Some scholars have argued for a later date, even decades later, into the second century. The traditional reckoning of about AD 108, under Trajan, is the one this book will follow; but the reader should know the ground is softer than a single confident number would suggest.

What the letters are, and are not

Here we meet the strange fact that makes this book possible at all. Almost everything we know of Ignatius comes from Ignatius. The seven letters he wrote on his way to Rome are the great source for his life, his mind, and his death. Later writers add a little — Polycarp, who knew him; Irenaeus and Origen and Eusebius, who came after — but the living voice is his own, preserved in these few pages written under sentence of death.

Over the centuries other letters attached themselves to his name: longer, padded-out versions of the genuine ones, and later forgeries he never wrote. Careful study has sorted the true from the false, and modern scholars are agreed on the seven authentic letters that follow in this book. When Ignatius speaks in these pages, it is really him.

So we cannot narrate his arrest as a scene. We do not have the soldiers at the door, the words of the magistrate, the crowd in the square. There exists a later, dramatic account of his martyrdom, but its details are uncertain and much of it appears to have been added by later hands, and we will not dress it up as fact. What we can say is what he lets us see through his own writing — and that, as it turns out, is a great deal.

A sentence that meant Rome

Consider what his sentence actually required. To be thrown to the beasts was a public spectacle, and Rome was where the greatest spectacles were staged. A condemned provincial like Ignatius could be sent the long way to the capital to die before the crowds. That is why this is not a story of a man executed quietly in his own city. His punishment was a journey — hundreds of miles overland, under military guard, from Syria across Asia Minor and on toward Italy and the arena.

It is a cruel arrangement, and Ignatius knew it. Yet it is precisely this long road to death that gave the church its letters. Had he been killed at Antioch, we might have nothing from his hand at all. Instead he had weeks on the road, halting in city after city, meeting Christians who came out to see him, and in those pauses he wrote.

What is most striking is how he understood his sentence. He did not write as a man dragged unwilling to his end, protesting or despairing. He wrote as a man who had found in his condemnation a strange gift. To be carried to Rome to die was, to him, to be carried toward Christ. The chains did not feel to him like defeat. In the chapters ahead you will hear him say astonishing things about them — that he would not have his friends rob him of what lay at the end of the road.

That conviction is his own, formed in his own hour, and we should not flatten it into a rule for everyone. But it is the key to everything he wrote on the way. A man was being taken across the world to be eaten by beasts, and he sat down, again and again, to write letters not about his fear but about the unity of the church, the reality of Christ, and the hope set before him. The road to Rome is where we go next.
