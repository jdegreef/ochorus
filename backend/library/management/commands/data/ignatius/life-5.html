The road ended where he always knew it would. Ignatius reached Rome, and there he was put to death, thrown to the wild beasts under the emperor Trajan, around AD 108. That is the fact, plainly stated. The church remembered him ever after as a martyr of the apostolic age, one of the first great witnesses to seal his faith with his blood.

Later tradition placed his death in the amphitheatre of Rome, before the crowds who came to watch such spectacles. That setting may well be true, but it is worth saying honestly that the naming of the venue came later; the writers who mention it may have had little to go on beyond Ignatius's own words. What we can hold with confidence is the core: he died in Rome, given to the beasts, exactly as he had been sentenced and exactly as he had foreseen.

The wheat of God

He had foreseen it, and he had made his peace with it long before — not the grim peace of a man resigned, but something closer to eagerness. In the letter he sent ahead to the Christians at Rome, written while he was still far off on the road, he reached for an image that has never been forgotten. He thought of himself as grain going into the mill:

I am God's wheat, and I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts that I may be found pure bread [of Christ].

The teeth that would kill him were, to Ignatius, the millstones that would make him into something offered up and pure. He did not see the arena as the end of him but as the making of him. And so he could write, of the death rushing toward him, the quiet and staggering words:

Now am I beginning to be a disciple.

Not the end of discipleship, but its beginning — as though everything before had been only preparation for this. A lifetime as bishop of Antioch, and he called the hour of his death the first real step of following Christ.

Do not rescue me

There was one thing he feared, and it was not the beasts. He feared that the Christians of Rome, out of love, would use their influence to have his sentence lifted and rob him of the end he longed for. So the letter to the Romans is unlike all the others. It is not a warning against false teaching or a plea for unity. It is a plea for them to let him die. He begged them:

grant me nothing more than that I be poured out a libation to God, while there is still an altar ready.

He asked them to hold their peace, to pray not for his escape but for his strength, to let the altar stand while it still could. It is an extraordinary reversal — a condemned man pleading with his friends not to save his life. We should hear it as his own conviction, in his own extremity, and not as a rule for anyone else. But we cannot mistake how deeply he meant it. To Ignatius, to be delivered would have been the true loss.

Into the letters

And there the life story ends, as his own words end: on the road, still writing, still reaching toward the God he called himself the bearer of. We do not have the scene of his death, and we will not invent one. What we have is what he left — seven letters, written in haste and under guard, by a man who believed that dying for Christ was the beginning of living for him.

Those letters are what follow. You have now met the man: an early bishop of Antioch who called himself the God-bearer, condemned to the beasts, carried across the world to Rome, writing all the way. What he wrote is best heard now in his own voice. Turn the page, and let Ignatius speak for himself.
