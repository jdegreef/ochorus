# Tell Me the Story – Book 9: Songs and Wise Words

*A companion: the Psalms, Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes, for children aged 6 to 10*

Scripture quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible (BSB), public domain.

---

## Introduction: The Songs in the Middle of the Bible

> "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD!" — Psalm 150:6

If you open a Bible right in the middle, you will probably land in a book called Psalms. And next to it are books called Job, Proverbs and Ecclesiastes.

These books are different from the stories in the other books of Tell Me the Story. They aren't mostly stories. They are songs and poems and wise sayings.

The Psalms are the songbook of God's people. There are 150 of them. King David wrote many of them, and other people wrote others. Some are happy songs, full of praise. Some are sad songs, from people who were frightened or lonely or hurting. Jesus knew the Psalms and sang them. When He was dying on the cross, He prayed words from a psalm.

Proverbs is a book of wise sayings, many of them from King Solomon, the king who asked God for wisdom. They are short and easy to remember, and they teach us how to live well.

Job is the story of a man who lost almost everything, and what he learned about God. And Ecclesiastes is a book by a wise teacher who thought hard about what really matters in life.

In this book, each chapter takes one song or one wise saying. Sometimes the Bible tells us the story behind a psalm, and we'll tell it. Sometimes we'll imagine a child today, to help you see what a proverb means. When we imagine, we'll say so. Everything else comes straight from the Bible.

These songs and sayings are for you. You can sing them, pray them, and learn them by heart. They have helped God's people for three thousand years, and they can help you too.

*Dear God, thank you for the songs and wise words in the Bible. Please teach me to pray them, to sing them, and to live them. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What kinds of writing are in Psalms, Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes?
  A: Songs and poems, wise sayings, the story of Job, and a wise teacher's thoughts about what really matters.
- Q: Who wrote many of the Psalms, and who wrote many of the Proverbs?
  A: King David wrote many of the Psalms, and King Solomon wrote many of the Proverbs.
- Q: Some psalms are happy and some are sad. Why do you think God put sad songs in the Bible?
  A: Because God wants us to bring Him all our feelings, sad ones as well as happy ones. The sad psalms teach us how to talk to God when we're hurting.

## The Shepherd's Song

> "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want." — Psalm 23:1

Do you remember David, the shepherd boy who became king? Before he ever fought a giant, David spent his days and nights looking after his father's sheep on the hills around Bethlehem.

David knew all about being a shepherd. He led the sheep to find grass to eat and water to drink. He protected them from lions and bears. He used a long staff with a hook at the end to guide them and pull them out of trouble, and a club called a rod to fight off wild animals. At night he watched over them. He knew each one.

Years later, David wrote a song. And in his song, David wasn't the shepherd. David was the sheep. And God was his Shepherd.

This is David's song. It's called Psalm 23:

"The Lord is my shepherd. I shall not want.
He makes me lie down in green pastures.
He leads me beside quiet waters.
He restores my soul.
He guides me in the paths of righteousness, for the sake of His name.
Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death,
I will fear no evil, for You are with me.
Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.
You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies.
You anoint my head with oil. My cup overflows.
Surely goodness and mercy will follow me all the days of my life,
and I will dwell in the house of the Lord forever."

"I shall not want" means "I have everything I need." A sheep with a good shepherd doesn't need to worry about where its next meal is coming from. Its shepherd will take care of it.

The "valley of the shadow of death" is a dark, scary place. Sometimes life feels like that: frightening, or sad, or lonely. But David says, "I will fear no evil, for You are with me." Even in the darkest valley, the Shepherd is right there.

And at the end, David says that goodness and mercy will "follow" him. In the language David spoke, the word means "chase". God's goodness and mercy are chasing after us, every day of our lives!

This is one of the most loved songs in the whole world. People say it when they're happy and when they're sad, at weddings and at funerals, when they're scared and when they're grateful. Many people learn it by heart. You could too.

**Looking ahead to Jesus:** Jesus said, "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down His life for the sheep" (John 10:11). Jesus is the Shepherd David sang about. And He loves His sheep so much that He gave His life for them.

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 23; John 10:11–15

*Dear Lord, thank you that you are my Shepherd, and that I have everything I need. When I am in a dark valley, help me to remember that you are with me. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: In David's song, who is the shepherd and who is the sheep?
  A: God is the Shepherd, and David, and all of us who trust God, are the sheep.
- Q: What does David say God does for him, like a shepherd for a sheep?
  A: He gives him green pastures and quiet waters, restores his soul, guides him, stays with him in dark valleys, protects him, and fills his cup to overflowing.
- Q: Can you learn the first line of Psalm 23 by heart? When might it help you?
  A: "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want." It can help when you're worried, scared or sad, to remember that God takes care of you.

## You Made Me

> "I praise You, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made." — Psalm 139:14

Have you ever looked in a mirror and wondered why you look the way you do? Or why you like the things you like? Or wished you were different?

King David wrote a song about how well God knows us. It's Psalm 139.

"O Lord, You have searched me and known me," David sang. "You know when I sit down and when I get up. You understand my thoughts from far away. You know where I go and when I lie down. You know everything I do. Before I even say a word, You know what I'm going to say."

God knows everything about you. Not just your name, but every single thought, every worry, every secret, every dream.

That might sound scary. But David didn't find it scary. He found it wonderful.

"Where can I go from Your Spirit?" he sang. "Where can I run away from You? If I go up to the heavens, You are there. If I go down to the deepest place, You are there. If I fly away on the wings of the morning, and land on the far side of the sea, even there Your hand will guide me, and Your right hand will hold me tight."

You can never go anywhere that God isn't. You can never get lost from Him. Wherever you are, His hand is holding you.

Then David sang about how God made him.

"You made every part of me. You knit me together inside my mother. I praise You, because I am fearfully and wonderfully made! Your works are wonderful, and I know that very well. You saw me before I was born. Every day of my life was written in Your book before any of them had happened."

Imagine someone knitting a jumper, stitch by stitch, choosing every colour and every pattern with care. That's how David says God made you. Not by accident. Not carelessly. With care and love, every bit of you, inside and out.

So when you look in the mirror, or when someone says something unkind about you, remember Psalm 139. You are fearfully and wonderfully made. God made you on purpose, He knows you completely, and He loves you.

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 139:1–18

*Dear God, thank you that you know me completely and love me. Thank you that you made every part of me on purpose. I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does God know about us, according to Psalm 139?
  A: Everything: when we sit and stand, what we think, where we go, and what we're going to say before we say it.
- Q: How does David say God made him?
  A: God knit him together inside his mother, and made him fearfully and wonderfully, with every day of his life written in God's book.
- Q: Is there anything about yourself you sometimes wish were different? What does Psalm 139 say about you?
  A: Let your child share. Remind them that God made them on purpose, with care and love, and that they are "fearfully and wonderfully made."

## The Sky Is Singing

> "The heavens declare the glory of God; the skies proclaim the work of His hands." — Psalm 19:1

Have you ever lain on your back on a clear night and looked up at the stars? Or watched a sunrise turn the sky pink and gold? Or seen a sky full of storm clouds and lightning?

King David spent many nights outside, looking after sheep. He saw the stars every night. And he wrote songs about them.

"The heavens declare the glory of God," David sang in Psalm 19. "The skies show what His hands have made. Day after day they speak. Night after night they tell what they know. They don't use words. You can't hear their voice. And yet their message goes out to the whole earth."

The sky is like a giant painting that shows us how great and beautiful the Painter is. Without saying a single word, the sun and moon and stars tell everyone, everywhere: "Look how great God is!"

David wrote another song about the night sky. It's Psalm 8.

"O Lord, our Lord, how majestic is Your name in all the earth!" David sang. "When I look at Your heavens, the work of Your fingers, the moon and the stars that You put in their places, what is man that You think about him? What are human beings, that You care for them?"

Think about how many stars there are. More than anyone could ever count. And the universe is so huge that the light from some of them takes thousands of years to reach us.

The God who made all of that is so big. And we are so small. So why would He care about us?

But He does! David was amazed. The God who made the stars thinks about you. He cares about you. He made people to be the most special part of everything He made, even more special than the stars.

So next time you see a beautiful sky, you can say thank you to the God who painted it. And remember: the one who made all those stars knows your name.

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 19:1–6; Psalm 8

*O Lord, how majestic is your name in all the earth! Thank you for the sun and moon and stars. Thank you that you, who made them all, care about me. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does Psalm 19 say the sky is doing?
  A: Declaring the glory of God, showing everyone what His hands have made, without using any words.
- Q: What amazed David when he looked at the stars?
  A: That the God who made the huge sky and all the stars still thinks about people and cares for them.
- Q: What is the most beautiful sky you have ever seen? What does it tell you about God?
  A: Let your child describe it. It shows us that God is great, creative and loves beautiful things, and that He cares for us too.

## When I Am Afraid

> "When I am afraid, I put my trust in You." — Psalm 56:3

Do you remember when King Saul was jealous of David and tried to kill him? David had to run away and hide.

One day, the Bible tells us, David ran all the way to a city called Gath. It was a Philistine city, the home town of Goliath, the giant David had beaten! David was hoping nobody would recognise him there.

But they did. "Isn't this David?" the king's servants said. "Isn't this the one the women sang about, 'Saul has killed his thousands, and David his tens of thousands'?"

Now David was in terrible danger. He was surrounded by enemies. He was very much afraid.

So David pretended to be mad. He scribbled on the doors of the city gate, and let spit run down into his beard. The king of Gath thought he was out of his mind, and let him go. David escaped.

The heading of Psalm 56 tells us that David wrote it about that time, "when the Philistines seized him in Gath." And this is part of what he wrote:

"When I am afraid, I put my trust in You. In God, whose word I praise, in God I trust. I will not be afraid. What can man do to me?"

David was a brave man. He had fought a lion and a bear and a giant. But he still got frightened sometimes. And he didn't pretend he wasn't scared. He said, "When I am afraid..."

Not "if" I am afraid. "When." Everyone feels afraid sometimes, even brave people. Even grown-ups. Even kings.

But David knew what to do with his fear. He gave it to God. "When I am afraid, I will trust in You."

David also wrote that God keeps a record of all our tears and troubles (Psalm 56:8). God sees every time we're afraid. He cares about every tear.

The next time you feel afraid, at night in the dark, or before something hard, or when you're worried about someone you love, you can say David's words: "When I am afraid, I will trust in You." It's a short prayer. You can say it anywhere, any time.

**Read it in your Bible:** 1 Samuel 21:10–15; Psalm 56

*Dear God, when I am afraid, I will trust in you. Thank you that you see every one of my tears and care about every one of my worries. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: Why was David afraid in the city of Gath?
  A: Because he was in a Philistine city surrounded by enemies, and they recognised him as the man who had beaten Goliath.
- Q: What did David write in Psalm 56 about what to do when he was afraid?
  A: "When I am afraid, I put my trust in You."
- Q: What makes you feel afraid? Can you say David's prayer together?
  A: Let your child share, then say it together: "When I am afraid, I will trust in You." It's a good prayer to learn by heart.

## Be Still

> "Be still and know that I am God." — Psalm 46:10

Imagine the very worst storm you can think of. The wind is howling. The ground is shaking. Huge waves are crashing. Mountains are falling into the sea!

Psalm 46 imagines something like that. It was written by a group of singers called the Sons of Korah, who led worship in the temple.

"God is our refuge and strength," they sang, "a very present help in trouble. So we will not be afraid, even if the earth gives way, and the mountains fall into the middle of the sea, even if the waters roar and foam, and the mountains shake."

A refuge is a safe place to hide. Like a strong house in a storm, or a cave when it's raining, or a parent's arms when you've had a bad dream. God is our refuge. When everything around us is shaking, we can run to Him and be safe.

And God isn't far away when trouble comes. He is "a very present help". That means He is right there, right now, ready to help.

In the middle of all that noise and shaking, God speaks. And this is what He says:

"Be still, and know that I am God."

Be still. Stop rushing around. Stop panicking. Stop trying to fix everything yourself. Just be quiet for a moment, and remember who God is.

He is God. He is in charge. He is bigger than any storm, any trouble, anything that frightens you.

"The Lord of Hosts is with us," the song ends. "The God of Jacob is our fortress."

Sometimes the best thing we can do when we're worried is to stop, be quiet, and remember that God is God. You could try it now. Sit still. Close your eyes. Take a slow breath. And remember: God is here. God is in charge. God is your safe place.

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 46

*Dear God, thank you that you are my refuge and strength, and that you are always ready to help me. When I am worried, help me to be still, and to know that you are God. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What is a refuge?
  A: A safe place to hide, like a strong house in a storm. Psalm 46 says God is our refuge.
- Q: What does God say in the middle of all the trouble?
  A: "Be still, and know that I am God."
- Q: Can you try being still for a moment together? How does it feel to remember that God is in charge?
  A: Sit quietly together for half a minute, then share how it felt. Being still and remembering God is a kind of prayer we can do anywhere.

## Under His Wings

> "He will cover you with His feathers; under His wings you will find refuge." — Psalm 91:4

Have you ever seen a mother hen with her chicks? When there's danger, a hawk circling overhead, or a cold wind, or a storm, the mother hen calls her chicks. And they all come running and hide under her wings. She spreads her feathers over them, and they are warm and safe.

Psalm 91 uses that picture to describe God.

"Whoever lives in the shelter of the Most High will rest in the shadow of the Almighty," the psalm begins. "I will say to the Lord, 'You are my refuge and my fortress, my God, in whom I trust.'"

"He will cover you with His feathers," it says, "and under His wings you will find refuge. His faithfulness will be your shield."

What a beautiful picture! God is like a mother bird, spreading His wings over His children to keep them safe and warm.

Psalm 91 goes on to say that you don't need to be afraid of terrors in the night, or dangers in the day. "For He will command His angels to guard you in all your ways."

Do you remember Ruth, in the second book? Boaz said she had come to take refuge under the wings of the God of Israel. And Jesus Himself used the same picture. Looking over the city of Jerusalem, He said, "How often I have longed to gather your children together, as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings" (Luke 13:34).

Does this mean nothing bad will ever happen to people who love God? No. Jesus loved God perfectly, and He still suffered. Paul loved God, and he was beaten and shipwrecked. Psalm 91 doesn't promise that we'll never face trouble.

But it does promise that we never face trouble alone. Under God's wings, we belong to Him. And nothing can take us out from under His care, not even death.

At night, when you're in bed, you can imagine God's wings spread over you, like a mother bird over her chicks. You are safe with Him.

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 91:1–6, 9–16

*Dear God, thank you that you cover me with your feathers, and that under your wings I can find refuge. Please keep me safe tonight. You are my refuge and my fortress, my God, in whom I trust. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What picture does Psalm 91 use to describe how God protects us?
  A: A mother bird covering her chicks with her feathers and wings.
- Q: Does Psalm 91 mean nothing bad will ever happen to people who love God?
  A: No. Even Jesus and Paul suffered. But it promises that we are never alone, and that we always belong to God and are under His care.
- Q: When could you remember the picture of God's wings?
  A: At bedtime, when you're scared, or when something bad happens. You could picture God's wings over you as you pray.

## Make a Joyful Noise

> "Make a joyful noise to the LORD, all the earth." — Psalm 100:1

What's the loudest, happiest noise you can make? A shout? A cheer? A song at the top of your voice?

Psalm 100 is a song that tells everyone in the whole world to make a joyful noise to God. It's a short psalm, only five verses long. People in the temple sang it as they came in to worship God, and Christians have sung it for thousands of years.

"Make a joyful noise to the Lord, all the earth!" it says.

"Serve the Lord with gladness. Come into His presence with joyful songs."

Coming to God isn't meant to be boring or gloomy. It's meant to be glad and joyful!

"Know that the Lord is God. It is He who made us, and we are His. We are His people, and the sheep of His pasture."

Do you remember the shepherd's song? Here it is again. We belong to God, like sheep belong to their shepherd. He made us, and He looks after us.

"Enter His gates with thanksgiving, and His courts with praise. Give thanks to Him and bless His name."

Then comes the reason for all this joy:

"For the Lord is good. His loving devotion endures forever. His faithfulness continues to all generations."

God is good. His love never runs out. It goes on and on, for ever. And He is faithful, which means He keeps His promises, not just to us, but to our parents and grandparents and great-grandparents, and to our children and grandchildren too.

Psalm 103 is another happy song, by David. "Bless the Lord, O my soul," it begins, "and do not forget all His kind deeds." Then David makes a list of all the good things God does. "He forgives all your sins. He heals all your diseases." And he says something wonderful about forgiveness: "As far as the east is from the west, so far has He removed our sins from us."

How far is the east from the west? You can never reach the end of it! That's how far away God takes our sins when He forgives us.

So make a joyful noise! Sing, shout, clap, cheer. The Lord is good.

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 100; Psalm 103:1–13

*Dear God, I want to make a joyful noise to you! Thank you that you made me and I belong to you. Thank you that you are good, and your love lasts for ever. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does Psalm 100 tell everyone on earth to do?
  A: To make a joyful noise to the Lord, serve Him with gladness, and come to Him with joyful songs and thanksgiving.
- Q: Why does Psalm 100 say we should be so joyful?
  A: Because the Lord is good, His love lasts for ever, and He is faithful to every generation.
- Q: How far does Psalm 103 say God takes away our sins? What does that mean?
  A: As far as the east is from the west, which never ends. It means that when God forgives us, our sins are gone completely. You could sing a joyful song together to finish.

## Songs for Sad Days

> "Put your hope in God, for I will yet praise Him, my Savior and my God." — Psalm 42:11

Have you ever felt really sad, and you didn't even know exactly why? Or felt far away from God, as if He wasn't listening?

Psalm 42 was written by someone who felt like that.

"As a deer pants for streams of water," the psalm begins, "so my soul longs for You, O God."

Imagine a deer, hot and tired after running a long way. Its tongue is out. It's so thirsty, desperate for a drink. That's how the writer felt about God. He was longing for God, thirsty for Him. But God felt far away.

"My tears have been my food day and night," he wrote, "while people say to me all day long, 'Where is your God?'"

He remembered the happy days, when he used to go to the temple with crowds of people, singing and shouting for joy. But those days seemed far away now.

Then the writer did something interesting. He started talking to himself!

"Why are you so sad, O my soul? Why are you so upset inside me? Put your hope in God, for I will still praise Him, my Saviour and my God."

He didn't pretend everything was fine. He was honest about how sad he was. But then he reminded himself of the truth: God is still my Saviour. I will still praise Him again one day. Hope in God.

And he didn't say it just once. He said it three times in the song, as if he had to keep reminding himself.

Lots of psalms are like this. They are sad songs, honest songs, songs where people tell God exactly how they feel: "Why, God?" "How long, God?" "Where are You, God?" And then, most of the time, they turn back to trusting Him.

God put these sad songs in the Bible on purpose. He wants us to know that we can bring Him all our feelings, including the sad ones. We don't have to pretend to be happy when we talk to God. We can tell Him exactly how we feel.

And then, like the writer of Psalm 42, we can remind ourselves: "Put your hope in God."

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 42

*Dear God, thank you that I can tell you exactly how I feel, even when I'm sad. When I feel far away from you, help me to remember to put my hope in you. You are my Saviour and my God. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What did the writer of Psalm 42 compare himself to?
  A: A thirsty deer panting for water, because he longed for God so much.
- Q: What did he tell himself when he was sad?
  A: "Why are you so sad, O my soul? Put your hope in God, for I will still praise Him."
- Q: Why do you think God put sad songs in the Bible?
  A: To show us that we can bring Him all our feelings, sad ones too, and that it's all right to be honest with Him. Is there anything sad you'd like to tell God about now?

## Everything That Breathes

> "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD! Hallelujah!" — Psalm 150:6

The book of Psalms has 150 songs in it. Some are happy and some are sad. Some are quiet prayers and some are loud shouts. But the very last one, Psalm 150, is the loudest, happiest, noisiest song of all.

Every line of Psalm 150 says "Praise!"

"Hallelujah!" it begins. Hallelujah means "Praise the Lord!"

"Praise God in His sanctuary. Praise Him in His mighty heavens.
Praise Him for His mighty acts. Praise Him for His excellent greatness.
Praise Him with the sound of the trumpet.
Praise Him with the harp and lyre.
Praise Him with tambourine and dancing.
Praise Him with strings and flute.
Praise Him with clashing cymbals.
Praise Him with resounding cymbals."

Can you imagine it? Trumpets blasting, harps playing, tambourines shaking, people dancing, flutes whistling, and cymbals crashing! It's like a huge, joyful party, all for God.

And then the very last line of the whole book of Psalms:

"Let everything that has breath praise the Lord! Hallelujah!"

Everything that has breath. That means every person. Every child. Every grown-up. Every animal too. If you are breathing, you can praise God!

Take a deep breath now. Feel the air going in and out. That breath is a gift from God. And you can use it to praise Him.

It's interesting that the book of Psalms ends like this. Some of the psalms are full of tears and questions and trouble. But the book doesn't end with sadness. It ends with praise. That's what God's story is like too. It may have sad parts, but it ends with joy.

You don't need a trumpet or a harp to praise God. You can sing. You can shout. You can clap. You can dance. You can whisper "Thank You, God." As long as you have breath, you can praise the Lord.

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 150

*Hallelujah! I praise you, Lord, for your mighty acts and your great greatness. Thank you for every breath you give me. I want to use my breath to praise you! Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does "Hallelujah" mean?
  A: "Praise the Lord!"
- Q: What instruments does Psalm 150 mention?
  A: Trumpet, harp, lyre, tambourine, strings, flute and cymbals, and dancing too.
- Q: Psalm 150 says, "Let everything that has breath praise the Lord!" How could you praise God today?
  A: By singing, clapping, dancing, playing an instrument, or saying thank you to God. You could put on a favourite worship song and praise Him together.

## Two Paths

> "He is like a tree planted by streams of water, yielding its fruit in season, whose leaf does not wither." — Psalm 1:3

The very first psalm, Psalm 1, is like a doorway into the whole book of Psalms. And it's about two paths.

"Blessed is the one who does not walk in step with the wicked," it begins, "or stand in the way of sinners, or sit with people who make fun of God."

Notice how it goes: walking, then standing, then sitting. It's as if someone starts by walking along with people who do wrong, then stops to stand with them, then sits down and makes themselves at home. Bad choices can slowly pull us further and further in.

"But his delight is in the law of the Lord," the psalm goes on, "and he thinks about it day and night."

The happy, blessed person loves God's Word. They think about it all the time.

And then comes a beautiful picture.

"He is like a tree planted by streams of water, which gives its fruit at the right time, and its leaves never wither."

Imagine two trees. One is planted right next to a river. Its roots go down deep into the wet earth, and it always has water, even in the hottest summer. Its leaves stay green, and it grows big, juicy fruit.

The other tree is planted in dry, dusty ground, far from any water. When the hot summer comes, its leaves go brown and fall off. It never grows any fruit.

The person who loves God's Word is like the tree by the river. God's Word is like water that keeps them strong and growing, even when life is hard.

"Not so the wicked," the psalm says. "They are like chaff that the wind blows away." Chaff is the dry, light husk around grain. When farmers threshed their wheat, the wind blew the chaff away, and it was gone. It had no roots at all.

"For the Lord watches over the way of the righteous," the psalm ends, "but the way of the wicked will perish."

There are two paths, Psalm 1 says. One leads to life, and one leads to nothing. Proverbs says something similar: "The path of the righteous is like the first gleam of dawn, shining brighter and brighter until midday" (Proverbs 4:18).

Every day, we choose which path to walk on.

**Read it in your Bible:** Psalm 1; Proverbs 4:18

*Dear God, I want to be like a tree planted by streams of water. Help me to love your Word and think about it, so that I grow strong and give good fruit. Help me to walk on your path. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does Psalm 1 say the person who loves God's Word is like?
  A: A tree planted by streams of water, which gives fruit at the right time and whose leaves never wither.
- Q: Why does a tree by a river stay green, even in a hot summer?
  A: Because its roots reach down to the water. God's Word is like that water for us, keeping us strong.
- Q: How can you "plant yourself by the river" of God's Word?
  A: By reading the Bible, listening to Bible stories, learning verses by heart, and thinking about them during the day.

## Where Wisdom Starts

> "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and knowledge of the Holy One is understanding." — Proverbs 9:10

Do you remember King Solomon, who asked God for wisdom? God gave him so much wisdom that people came from all over the world to hear him. The Bible says Solomon spoke three thousand proverbs (1 Kings 4:32).

A proverb is a short, wise saying that's easy to remember. Many of Solomon's proverbs, and others too, are collected in the book of Proverbs.

The book of Proverbs begins by explaining what it's for. It's written to help people get wisdom, to understand what's right and fair, and to make good choices. And it says it's especially for young people.

Then it tells us where wisdom starts.

"The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge," it says. And later, "The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom."

What does "the fear of the Lord" mean? It doesn't mean being scared of God, the way you might be scared of a monster. It means having great respect and awe for God: knowing how great and holy He is, loving Him, and wanting to please Him.

It's a bit like this. Imagine you are standing at the edge of a huge waterfall, watching tons of water thunder down. You're not frightened in a bad way. But you're filled with awe. You know how powerful it is. And you'd never treat it carelessly.

That's the beginning of wisdom: knowing who God is, and taking Him seriously.

Lots of people are clever. They know lots of facts. But being clever isn't the same as being wise. Wisdom is knowing how to live well, how to make good choices, how to treat other people, how to live God's way. And it all starts with knowing God.

Proverbs talks about wisdom almost as if it's a person, calling out in the streets: "Come to me! Listen to me! I will make you wise!" Wisdom is better than silver or gold, Proverbs says, and more precious than jewels.

And the best news is that God gives wisdom to anyone who asks. "If any of you lacks wisdom, he should ask God, who gives generously to all" (James 1:5).

**Looking ahead to Jesus:** The New Testament says that Jesus "has become for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30). The wisdom Proverbs talks about is found most of all in Jesus. When we know Him, we have the wisest teacher of all.

**Read it in your Bible:** Proverbs 1:1–7; 9:10; 3:13–15

*Dear God, please give me wisdom. Help me to love you and respect you, and to make good choices. Thank you that Jesus is the wisdom of God. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does Proverbs say is the beginning of wisdom?
  A: The fear of the Lord, which means respecting and loving God and taking Him seriously.
- Q: What's the difference between being clever and being wise?
  A: Being clever is knowing lots of facts. Being wise is knowing how to live well and make good choices, God's way.
- Q: Is there a choice you need wisdom for at the moment? You could ask God for wisdom now.
  A: Talk about a decision your child is facing, big or small, and pray together for God's wisdom, as James 1:5 says we can.

## The Little Ant

> "Walk in the manner of the ant, O slacker; observe its ways and become wise." — Proverbs 6:6

Proverbs has a surprising teacher for us. A tiny, little ant!

"Go to the ant, you lazybones," says Proverbs. "Watch what it does, and become wise."

What can an ant teach us?

"It has no commander, no boss, no ruler telling it what to do," Proverbs says. "Yet it gathers its food in the summer, and stores up its food at harvest time."

Have you ever watched ants? They never seem to stop. They march along in long lines, carrying crumbs and leaves and seeds, often things much bigger than themselves. Nobody is standing over them telling them to work. They just get on with it. And because they work hard in the summer, they have food stored up for the winter.

Then Proverbs talks to the lazybones.

"How long will you lie there, you lazybones? When will you get up from your sleep? A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest, and poverty will come upon you like a robber."

Imagine a boy called Tom. His mum asks him to tidy his room. "In a minute," says Tom, and carries on playing. Then, "Just five more minutes." Then, "I'll do it after lunch." By the evening, his room is still a mess, and now he can't find his shoes for football practice in the morning!

A little bit of putting things off, and then a little bit more, adds up. That's what Proverbs means.

The ant doesn't need someone standing over it. It just does what needs doing, at the right time.

That doesn't mean we should never rest. God made rest too, and He gave us a whole day of rest each week. But it means that when there's work to be done, schoolwork or chores or helping at home, we can do it well, and not put it off.

Paul wrote, "Whatever you do, work at it with your whole being, as for the Lord and not for men" (Colossians 3:23). When you work hard, even at little jobs, you're doing it for God.

**Read it in your Bible:** Proverbs 6:6–11

*Dear God, thank you for the little ant. Please help me not to be lazy, but to work hard and do what needs doing, even when no one is telling me to. Help me to do everything for you. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does the ant do that makes it wise?
  A: It works hard gathering and storing food in summer, without anyone telling it to.
- Q: What happens to the "lazybones" who keeps putting things off?
  A: A little sleep and a little rest add up, and then poverty and trouble come on them suddenly, like a robber.
- Q: Is there a job you sometimes put off? How could you be more like the ant?
  A: Talk about a real chore or task. You could choose one thing to do straight away, without being asked, this week.

## A Gentle Answer

> "A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger." — Proverbs 15:1

Proverbs has a lot to say about words, the things we say to each other.

Here's one of the most famous proverbs about words: "A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger." Wrath means anger.

Imagine a girl called Maya. Her little brother comes stomping into her room. "You took my pens!" he shouts. "You always take my things!"

Maya has a choice.

She could shout back: "I did NOT! You're so annoying! Get out of my room!" And what do you think would happen next? He'd shout louder. She'd shout louder. Soon there would be a huge row, and somebody would end up in tears.

Or Maya could answer gently: "I didn't take them, but I'll help you look for them."

What do you think would happen then? Probably her brother would calm down. The anger would fizzle out.

That's what Proverbs means. A harsh word is like throwing petrol on a fire. It makes the anger blaze up even bigger. But a gentle answer is like throwing water on it. It helps put the fire out.

Proverbs has lots more to say about our words.

"Speaking rashly is like a piercing sword, but the tongue of the wise brings healing" (Proverbs 12:18). Hasty, unkind words can hurt people deeply, like a sword. But kind, wise words can help them feel better.

"Pleasant words are a honeycomb, sweet to the soul and healing to the bones" (Proverbs 16:24). Kind words are like honey: sweet and good for you.

"Anxiety weighs down the heart of a man, but a good word cheers it up" (Proverbs 12:25). When someone is worried, a kind word can lift them up.

Our words are powerful. They can hurt or heal, start fights or stop them, make people sad or cheer them up. Every day, we choose which kind of words to use.

It's not always easy to give a gentle answer, especially when someone is shouting at us. But we can ask God to help us.

**Read it in your Bible:** Proverbs 15:1; 12:18, 25; 16:24

*Dear God, please help me to use my words to help people, not hurt them. When someone is angry with me, help me to give a gentle answer. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does a gentle answer do, and what does a harsh word do?
  A: A gentle answer turns away anger. A harsh word stirs up more anger.
- Q: What does Proverbs say kind words are like?
  A: A honeycomb: sweet to the soul and healing to the bones.
- Q: Can you think of a time when someone's words hurt you, or helped you? How could you use kind words this week?
  A: Share stories together, then think of someone you could encourage with kind words, and how you could answer gently next time someone is cross.

## Trust with All Your Heart

> "Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding." — Proverbs 3:5

Have you ever been in a dark room and had to find your way out? Or walked through a forest without a path? It's easy to get lost, or to bump into things, when you can't see where you're going.

One of the most loved verses in all of Proverbs is about finding our way.

"Trust in the Lord with all your heart," it says, "and lean not on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge Him, and He will make your paths straight."

"Trust in the Lord with all your heart." Not just a little bit. Not just when it's easy. With all your heart.

"Lean not on your own understanding." To lean on something means to rest your whole weight on it. If you lean on a wobbly chair, you might fall. Our own understanding is a bit like that wobbly chair. We don't know everything. We can't see the future. We make mistakes. So we shouldn't put all our weight on what we think. We should lean on God, who knows everything and never makes mistakes.

"In all your ways acknowledge Him." That means remembering God in everything we do: at school, at home, when we play, when we choose our friends, when we make decisions. Asking Him for help. Doing things His way.

"And He will make your paths straight." When we trust God and follow Him, He will guide us. He'll show us the way to go, like a clear, straight path through the forest.

Imagine a boy called Sam who has to decide whether to join a new club at school. He's not sure if it's the right thing. He could just decide on his own. Or he could pray about it, ask his parents what they think, and think about what God would want. When Sam does that, he is "acknowledging God in all his ways". And God promises to guide him.

This doesn't mean everything will always be easy. But it means we never have to work out life on our own. God is with us, leading the way.

**Read it in your Bible:** Proverbs 3:1–12

*Dear God, I want to trust you with all my heart. Help me not to lean on my own understanding, but to remember you in everything I do. Please show me the way to go. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does it mean to "lean not on your own understanding"?
  A: Not to put all our trust in what we think, because we don't know everything, but to trust God, who does.
- Q: What does God promise to do when we trust Him and acknowledge Him in everything?
  A: He will make our paths straight, guiding us and showing us the way.
- Q: Can you learn Proverbs 3:5–6 by heart? When could it help you?
  A: Say it together a few times. It can help when you have a decision to make or don't know what to do.

## A Friend Who Sticks Close

> "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity." — Proverbs 17:17

Proverbs has some wise things to say about friends.

"A friend loves at all times," says Proverbs, "and a brother is born for times of trouble."

A real friend doesn't only love you when everything is fun. A real friend loves you "at all times": when you're happy and when you're sad, when you're winning and when you're losing, when you're popular and when you're not.

And "a brother is born for times of trouble." That means that true friends and family stick by you when things go wrong. That's what they're there for.

Do you remember David and Jonathan, in the third book? Jonathan was a true friend. He loved David even when it cost him a lot, even when his own father was trying to kill David. That's a friend who "loves at all times".

Here's another proverb about friends: "A man of many companions may come to ruin, but there is a friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24).

Some people try to have lots and lots of companions, but none of them are real friends who will stay by them. It's better to have a few true friends than lots of people who don't really care about you. And sometimes a really good friend can be even closer than a brother or sister.

Proverbs also tells us to choose our friends wisely. "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20). The friends we spend time with change us, for better or for worse.

Imagine a girl called Grace who has just started at a new school. Some children want to be friends with her, but they're unkind to other children. Another girl, Priya, is quieter, but she's kind to everyone. Proverbs would say: choose Priya! Good friends help us become better people.

But the best thing about this proverb is that it reminds us of the very best friend of all. Jesus called His followers His friends (John 15:15). He is the friend who loves at all times, who never leaves us, who sticks closer than a brother. He even laid down His life for His friends.

**Read it in your Bible:** Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 13:20

*Dear Jesus, thank you that you are my friend who loves at all times and sticks closer than a brother. Please help me to be a good friend, and to choose good friends. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does Proverbs say a real friend does?
  A: Loves at all times, and sticks by you in times of trouble.
- Q: Why does Proverbs say we should choose our friends wisely?
  A: Because the friends we spend time with change us. Walking with wise people makes us wise.
- Q: What makes someone a good friend? How can you be a good friend?
  A: Talk about qualities like kindness, loyalty, honesty and forgiveness. Choose one way to be a good friend to someone this week.

## Job Loses Everything

> "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the name of the LORD." — Job 1:21

Long ago, in a land called Uz, there lived a man called Job. He was a good man who loved God and stayed away from evil. Job had seven sons and three daughters, and thousands of sheep and camels and oxen and donkeys, and many servants. He was the greatest man in all the east.

The Bible tells us that one day Satan came before God. And God said to Satan, "Have you noticed My servant Job? There's no one else like him on earth. He is a good and honest man, who loves Me and stays away from evil."

"Of course Job loves You," said Satan. "You protect him, and You've given him everything. But take it all away, and he will curse You to Your face!"

"Very well," said God. "Everything he has is in your power. But you must not harm Job himself."

Then, all in one terrible day, Job lost almost everything. Messengers came running, one after another. Raiders had stolen his oxen and donkeys. Fire had burned up his sheep. Other raiders had taken his camels. And then the worst news of all: a great wind had knocked down the house where his children were having a meal together, and all of them had died.

Job was heartbroken. He tore his robe and shaved his head, which is what people did in those days when they were grieving. Then he fell down on the ground.

And he worshipped God.

"I came into the world with nothing," Job said, "and I'll leave it with nothing. The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away. Blessed be the name of the Lord."

In all this, Job didn't sin or blame God for doing wrong.

Then Job became ill too, covered in painful sores from head to toe. Three of his friends heard about all his troubles, and they came to comfort him. When they saw him, they hardly recognised him. They sat down on the ground with him for seven days and seven nights. Nobody said a word, because they could see how much he was suffering.

That was the kindest thing they did. Later, they started talking, and they got it all wrong. They kept telling Job that he must have done something terrible to deserve all this. But he hadn't.

Job didn't understand why all this was happening. He was honest with God about how much it hurt. He asked God, "Why?" again and again.

The book of Job teaches us that sometimes very bad things happen to good people, and it isn't because they did something wrong. We don't always know why. But we can still bring our questions to God.

**Read it in your Bible:** Job 1:1–22; 2:11–13

*Dear God, when sad things happen and I don't understand why, help me to keep trusting you, like Job. Help me to be a good friend to people who are sad, and to sit with them. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What did Job do after he lost almost everything?
  A: He grieved, but he also worshipped God, saying, "The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away. Blessed be the name of the Lord."
- Q: What did Job's friends do that was kind, and what did they get wrong?
  A: They sat with him in silence for seven days, which was kind. But later they wrongly told him his suffering must be a punishment for something he'd done.
- Q: Sometimes bad things happen and we don't know why. What can we do?
  A: We can be honest with God about how we feel, keep trusting Him, and be kind to people who are suffering, just sitting with them if we don't know what to say. (This is a sad story; take it gently and let your child ask questions.)

## God Speaks from the Storm

> "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" — Job 38:4

For a long, long time, Job sat in his sadness, with his sores and his questions. His three friends kept arguing with him. They said he must have done something terrible. Job said he hadn't. He wanted God to answer him. "Why, God? Why is this happening to me?"

At last, God answered Job. He spoke to him out of a great whirlwind, a mighty storm.

But God didn't explain why everything had happened. Instead, He asked Job some questions.

"Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" God said. "Tell Me, if you understand. Who decided how big the earth would be? Who stretched a measuring line across it? Where were you when the morning stars sang together, and all the angels shouted for joy?"

"Have you ever told the morning to come, or shown the sunrise where to start?

"Have you ever walked into the storehouses of the snow, or seen where I keep the hail?

"Can you send the lightning on its way? Does it come to you and say, 'Here we are'?

"Who gives the lion its food? Who feeds the baby ravens when they cry out to God? Do you know when the mountain goats give birth? Did you give the horse its strength? Is it by your wisdom that the hawk flies?"

God went on and on, describing the wonders of His world: the stars and the sea, the snow and the rain, the wild animals and the birds. Things Job could never understand or control.

And Job realised how great God is. "I know that You can do anything," Job said, "and nothing can stop Your plans. I talked about things I didn't understand, things too wonderful for me to know. I had heard about You before, but now I have seen You with my own eyes."

God never told Job why he had suffered. But Job didn't need to know any more. He had met God. He knew that God is so great, so wise, so powerful, that he could trust Him, even without understanding.

Then God told Job's friends they had been wrong about Job. And Job prayed for his friends. After that, God gave Job a new family, seven sons and three daughters, and twice as many animals as he had before. The Lord blessed the end of Job's life even more than the beginning.

The New Testament says, "You have heard of Job's perseverance and have seen the outcome from the Lord. The Lord is full of compassion and mercy" (James 5:11).

**Read it in your Bible:** Job 38:1–11; 42:1–17

*Dear God, you are so great and so wise. When I don't understand what's happening, help me to trust you anyway. Thank you that you are full of compassion and mercy. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: How did God speak to Job?
  A: Out of a great whirlwind, a mighty storm.
- Q: Did God tell Job why he had suffered? What did He do instead?
  A: No. Instead, God asked Job questions about the wonders of creation, showing him how great and wise God is.
- Q: Job said, "I had heard about You, but now I have seen You." What do you think he meant?
  A: That he used to just know about God, but now he really knew God for himself. Sometimes hard times help us to know God better.

## A Time for Everything

> "To everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven." — Ecclesiastes 3:1

Have you noticed how the year changes? In spring, flowers bloom and new leaves grow. In summer, it's warm and sunny. In autumn, the leaves turn red and gold and fall off the trees. In winter, it's cold, and sometimes it snows. And then spring comes round again.

Every season is different. And every season has its own time.

The book of Ecclesiastes was written by a wise teacher who thought very hard about life. In one famous part, he wrote a poem about how there's a right time for everything:

"There is a time for everything,
and a season for every activity under heaven:
a time to be born and a time to die,
a time to plant and a time to pick,
a time to cry and a time to laugh,
a time to be sad and a time to dance,
a time to hug and a time not to hug,
a time to look for something and a time to stop looking,
a time to keep and a time to throw away,
a time to tear and a time to mend,
a time to be quiet and a time to speak,
a time to love and a time to hate,
a time for war and a time for peace."

Life has different times. Happy times and sad times. Times to laugh and times to cry. Times to talk and times to listen. Times to start new things and times when things come to an end.

Sometimes we wish the happy times would last for ever, and the sad times would hurry up and finish. But the teacher says that God is in charge of all the seasons of our lives.

"He has made everything beautiful in its time," the teacher wrote.

Even when we're in a sad or difficult season, God is still working. He can make even the hard times beautiful in the end, in His own time. We may not understand it now. But we can trust Him.

And then the teacher said something very interesting. "He has also set eternity in the hearts of people." Deep inside, all of us long for something that lasts for ever. That's because God made us for Himself, to be with Him for ever.

**Read it in your Bible:** Ecclesiastes 3:1–14

*Dear God, thank you that you are in charge of every season of my life. When I'm in a sad time, help me to remember that you make everything beautiful in its time. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What does the teacher in Ecclesiastes say there's a time for?
  A: Everything: to be born and to die, to cry and to laugh, to be sad and to dance, to be quiet and to speak, and more.
- Q: What does "He has made everything beautiful in its time" mean?
  A: That God is working in every season of our lives, and in His own time He can bring good and beauty, even out of hard times.
- Q: What "season" are you in at the moment? A happy one, a sad one, a busy one?
  A: Let your child describe it, and thank God together that He is with them in every season.

## Remember Your Creator

> "Remember your Creator in the days of your youth." — Ecclesiastes 12:1

The wise teacher who wrote Ecclesiastes thought very hard about what really matters in life. He had tried all sorts of things to make himself happy. He built great houses and planted vineyards and gardens. He had lots of money, lots of servants, and lots of music and fun. He became very wise and very famous.

But in the end, he said, all of it was like trying to catch the wind. None of it could make him truly happy, and none of it lasted. People grow old, and their riches and their buildings and their fame all fade away.

So what does matter? At the very end of his book, the teacher had some advice. And it was especially for young people.

"Remember your Creator in the days of your youth," he wrote, "before the days of trouble come, and the years come when you will say, 'I find no pleasure in them.'"

The teacher was saying: don't wait until you're old to think about God. Remember Him now, while you are young! Get to know Him, love Him, and follow Him now, while you have your whole life ahead of you.

Why is that so important? Because the habits we form when we're young often last our whole lives. And because every day of our lives is a gift from God, our Creator, who made us and loves us. Why would we want to waste any of them?

Then, at the very end of the book, the teacher summed up everything he had learned:

"When all has been heard, the conclusion of the matter is this: Fear God and keep His commandments, because this is the whole duty of man."

After all his thinking, and all his searching, and all his trying, this was what the wise teacher decided really mattered most: love God, respect Him, and do what He says.

That's a good way to end this book of songs and wise words. And it's a good way to start every day.

You are young now. This is the best time to remember your Creator. To get to know Him. To sing His songs. To learn His wise words. And to follow Him, all the days of your life.

**Read it in your Bible:** Ecclesiastes 12:1, 13–14; 2:4–11

*Dear God, my Creator, I want to remember you while I'm young. Thank you that you made me and love me. Help me to love you, respect you, and follow you all the days of my life. Amen.*

### Talk about it together

- Q: What had the wise teacher tried, to make himself happy?
  A: Building houses, planting gardens, getting rich, having servants, music and fun, and becoming wise and famous. None of it truly satisfied him.
- Q: What did he tell young people to do?
  A: "Remember your Creator in the days of your youth" — to know and follow God now, while they're young.
- Q: What did the teacher decide matters most of all? How could you remember your Creator each day?
  A: To fear God and keep His commandments: to love, respect and obey Him. You could remember Him each morning with a prayer, a Bible verse, or a song.
