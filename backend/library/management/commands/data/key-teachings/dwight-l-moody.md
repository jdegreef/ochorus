  This is not a book by Dwight L. Moody. It is a companion to him — an independent work of exposition and appreciation, written by Ochorus, that gathers the heart of his teaching into seventeen short chapters for the ordinary reader, with a chapter on his life, a conclusion and a guide to his books.

  Moody preached one message in a thousand forms: God loves you, Christ has died for you, and the door is open now. From a shoe shop in Boston to the largest halls in Britain and America, he told plain people that they were lost, that they were loved, and that they need not wait another night to come home. This volume follows that message through its parts — the fall, the blood and the new birth; faith, repentance and restitution; assurance and the Word; the nine elements of prevailing prayer; the power of the Spirit and the life wholly given to God; heaven, and the urgency that the Chicago fire burned into him. It weighs honestly where his plainness could thin out the truth, where the methods of the enquiry room need care, and where his hope for the world ran low. And it ends each chapter as he ended nearly every sermon: with a call to act, and a prayer.

  Moody’s own writings are in the public domain and freely available, and every chapter here names them so the reader can go to the source; The Way to God, Prevailing Prayer, the devotional collection Thoughts for the Quiet Hour and eleven of his sermons are in the Ochorus library. This companion is offered only to open the door. It quotes Scripture from the Authorised (King James) Version, and it makes no claim to stand in for the warm, urgent voice of the man himself.

# Introduction

> Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins. 1 JOHN 4:10

Dwight Lyman Moody never finished school, never learned to spell with confidence, and was never ordained. He began his working life wrapping shoes in the back of his uncle’s shop in Boston, and when he first asked to join a church he was turned away because he could not explain what he believed. Twenty years later he was the best-known preacher in the English-speaking world. He filled the largest halls of London, Glasgow, New York and Chicago; he founded schools, conferences and a Bible institute; and by the time he died in 1899 he had spoken, by the most careful estimates, to millions of people, without a microphone.

It is tempting to explain him by his energy, his business sense or his gift for a story, and he had all three in abundance. But those who knew him best gave a simpler explanation. He believed that God meant what He said, and he could not rest until other people heard it.

## One message in a thousand forms

Moody was not a theologian, and he did not pretend to be. What he had was one message, and he preached it in a thousand forms for forty years. The message was this: God loves sinners, Christ has died for them, and the door is open now.

Each part of that sentence mattered to him. God loves sinners: Moody had grown up, as he later admitted, thinking of God as a stern judge, and when he discovered the love of the Father it changed his preaching for ever. In The Way to God he says that the great obstacle to faith is not doubt but fear: “The trouble is that men think God hates them; and so they are all the time running away from Him.” Christ has died for them: he never tired of the cross, the blood, the Substitute who stood where the sinner should have stood. And the door is open now: this was the note that made him a revivalist rather than a lecturer. He had learned, in a night of fire in Chicago, what it cost to let a congregation go home to think about it.

> Behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation. 2 CORINTHIANS 6:2

That is the thread that runs through every chapter of this book: the love of God, offered today. Moody’s teaching on sin, on the new birth, on faith and repentance, on assurance, on the Bible, on prayer and on the Holy Spirit all comes back to it in the end. Sin is the thing that keeps people from the love of God. The new birth is the love of God making a new creature. Faith is simply taking God at His word. Prayer is the child speaking to the Father. The power of the Spirit is given so that others may hear. And the urgency is there because love will not wait while the house burns.

## Why he still matters

Moody is worth reading for at least three reasons.

The first is his plainness. He wrote and spoke for shop girls and dock workers, farmers and clerks, and he assumed nothing. If you have ever wanted someone to tell you in ordinary English what it means to be born again, or how a person can know that he is saved, Moody is as clear as anyone who ever lived.

The second is his warmth. He is often remembered as a preacher of hellfire, and he did not flinch from judgement. But the dominant note in his sermons is tenderness. He wept easily. He preached the prodigal’s father running down the road. He told his hearers again and again that God was not waiting to punish them but waiting to receive them.

The third is his practicality. Moody measured religion by what it did on Monday. His chapters on repentance and prayer are full of men who returned stolen money, women who forgave old enemies, and church committees who could not pray until they were reconciled. He had no patience with a faith that did not straighten out a person’s life.

## How this book is arranged

After a chapter on his life, seventeen short chapters set out his teaching. They begin with the love of God, move through what Moody and his friends summed up as ruin by the fall, redemption by the blood and regeneration by the Spirit, then take up faith, repentance, assurance, the Word of God and the sufficiency of Christ. Four chapters follow his book Prevailing Prayer through the nine elements he believed true prayer must contain. The last chapters are on the power of the Holy Spirit for service, the life wholly consecrated to God, heaven, and the urgency of decision.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. Moody would have wanted it so. He almost never finished a sermon without pressing his hearers to do something before they left the building, and he regarded a truth that was merely admired as a truth wasted. Read a chapter a day, or one a week. Stop at the application points. Pray the prayer, or better, pray your own.

## Where he must be read with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and it is right to say plainly at the start where Moody must be weighed rather than simply received.

First, his simplicity was a gift that could become a weakness. He sometimes spoke as though doctrine and creeds were obstacles to be cut away, and his picture of the church as the pole that holds up the brazen serpent, true as far as it goes, leaves little room for the church as the body of Christ, or for the sacraments as more than reminders. He was right that no creed saves anyone; he was less right when he implied that careful thinking about God is a distraction. A faith that knows only one sermon can be thin when the storms come.

Second, the methods of mass revival, which Moody did more than anyone to shape, need care. He pressed for immediate decisions, invited the anxious into an enquiry room, and counted results. Much good was done that way. But the pressure of a crowded hall can produce decisions that do not last, and Moody himself was aware that not every profession was a conversion.

Third, his view of the world and its future ran low. He looked for the return of Christ at any moment and is remembered as likening the world to a wrecked ship, his own work to a lifeboat sent to save as many as he could. That gave him urgency, but it could also make the wider work of justice and culture look like rearranging furniture on a sinking vessel.

Finally, he was a storyteller, and many of his stories came to him second-hand. They are illustrations, not documents. Read them for the truth they carry, not as history.

None of this is said to diminish him. Moody himself would have been the first to say it. He knew he was an unlettered man, and he never asked anyone to follow him. He asked them to follow Christ.

# The Shoe Clerk from Northfield

> But God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise; and God hath chosen the weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:27

A fatherless farm. Dwight Lyman Moody was born on the fifth of February, 1837, at Northfield, a farming village in the Connecticut valley of western Massachusetts, the sixth of nine children. When he was four his father, Edwin, died suddenly, leaving debts the small farm could not meet; the creditors took almost everything that could be carried away. His mother, Betsey, refused to let the family be broken up and farmed out to neighbours, and she kept them together by hard work and stubbornness. The boys worked the fields and hired themselves out. Dwight had only a few years of schooling, and his spelling and grammar showed it for the rest of his life. What he did have was a strong body, a quick tongue, an enormous appetite for work and a mother who prayed.

Boston. At seventeen he went to Boston to make his fortune and talked his way into his uncle Samuel Holton’s shoe shop. The uncle set one condition: the boy must attend church and Sunday school. So Moody went to the Mount Vernon Congregational Church, where he understood little and sometimes slept through the sermons.

The back of the shop. His Sunday-school teacher there was a quiet man named Edward Kimball, who resolved to speak to each of his boys about Christ. On the twenty-first of April, 1855, Kimball walked into the shoe shop, found Moody in the back wrapping shoes, put a hand on his shoulder and spoke to him simply of the love of Christ. There, among the boxes, Moody gave himself to the Saviour. It was a real conversion, but a very unlearned one. When he applied for church membership soon afterwards, the examining committee found he could say almost nothing about what he believed and advised him to wait. He was received the next year. Kimball said afterwards that he had seldom met anyone whose mind was spiritually darker. Moody never forgot either the teacher who came looking for him or the lesson that God does not wait for people to become clever.

Chicago. In 1856 he moved to Chicago, a raw and booming city, and sold shoes with the same zeal he brought to everything else. He hoped to be worth a hundred thousand dollars. But his real energy went elsewhere. He rented pews and filled them with young men he gathered from the streets and boarding houses. In 1858 he began a Sunday school in one of the roughest districts of the city, meeting in an old market hall, and drew the children in with sweets, rides and sheer persistence. It grew to many hundreds. In November 1860 the president-elect, Abraham Lincoln, visited it and spoke briefly. That same year Moody gave up business altogether to give all his time to Christian work, with no salary and no certain support. His friends thought he had lost his senses.

War and marriage. He threw himself into the Young Men’s Christian Association, of which he later became president in Chicago, and when the Civil War came he worked among the soldiers in the camps around the city and went to the front with the United States Christian Commission. He was among the wounded after Shiloh; in The Way to God he tells of a dying man in a hospital at Murfreesboro who wanted him to help him die, and who died resting on two verses from the third chapter of John. In 1862 he married Emma Revell, a teacher in his Sunday school, whose calm judgement steadied him for the rest of his life. In 1864 the mission became a church of its own, the Illinois Street Church.

Two lessons. In 1867 Moody crossed to Britain for the first time. He wanted to hear Spurgeon and to see George Müller’s orphan houses at Bristol, and he did both. It was on that visit, as the story is usually told, that he heard the evangelist Henry Varley remark that the world had yet to see what God would do with a man fully consecrated to Him, and resolved in his heart to be that man. The following year a young Englishman named Harry Moorhouse, whom Moody had met in Britain, came to Chicago and preached in Moody’s church night after night on a single verse, John 3:16. Moody had preached a God who was angry with sinners. Moorhouse preached a God who loved them, and Moody never preached the old way again. In 1870, at a convention in Indianapolis, he heard a young man named Ira Sankey lead the singing and asked him, almost at once, to come and work with him.

The fire. On Sunday evening, the eighth of October, 1871, Moody preached in Chicago on Pilate’s question in Matthew 27:22, What shall I do then with Jesus which is called Christ? At the close he told the great congregation to take the question home, think it over, and come back next Sunday with their answer. Sankey began to sing. Before the hymn was done the noise of the fire bells drowned him out. That night the Great Chicago Fire destroyed much of the city, including Moody’s hall, his church and his home. Moody later said that he never saw that congregation together again, and that he would rather have his right hand cut off than give an audience a week to decide for Christ. The lesson runs through the last chapter of this book.

Power. Some months earlier two women in his congregation, Sarah Cooke and a Mrs Hawxhurst, had begun sitting on the front row and telling him that they were praying for him to receive the power of the Holy Spirit. He was irritated at first; he thought he had power. Then he began to pray with them, with increasing hunger. Late in 1871, while he was in New York raising money for the ruined work in Chicago, the answer came as he walked in the street. He seldom spoke of it afterwards, except to say that God revealed Himself to him and that he had such an experience of His love that he had to ask Him to stay His hand. He went back to preaching the same sermons, and far more people were converted.

Across the sea. In June 1873 Moody and Sankey sailed for Britain with their families and hardly any invitations. They began in York in a small way. At Sunderland and Newcastle the meetings grew; in Edinburgh that winter they became a movement, and cautious Scottish ministers found themselves supporting two American laymen with an organ and a hymn book. Glasgow followed, then Ireland and the great English cities, and finally London, from the spring into the summer of 1875, where the crowds were beyond counting. Britain did not know what to make of an uneducated man who spoke fast, told stories, and never pretended to be anything but a layman. He came home the most famous evangelist in the world.

The builder. For the rest of his life Moody went from city to city in America and, in the early 1880s, in Britain again, where his meetings at Cambridge in 1882 touched students who afterwards gave themselves to the mission field. But he also built. In 1879 he founded the Northfield Seminary for girls, and in 1881 the Mount Hermon School for boys, both for young people who could not otherwise afford an education. From 1880 he gathered Christians each summer at Northfield for conferences on the Bible, and at a student conference at Mount Hermon in 1886 a hundred young men pledged themselves to missionary service, the beginning of the Student Volunteer Movement. In Chicago he founded the training school that after his death took his name, the Moody Bible Institute, to prepare ordinary men and women for Christian work. And in 1894 he began a colportage association to put good books into the hands of people who could not afford them.

Home. Through it all he kept a farmer’s habits. He rose early for the Bible and prayer, and he loved nothing better than Northfield, his family and the hills. In November 1899, in the middle of a campaign in Kansas City, his heart began to fail. He was taken home to Northfield, and on the twenty-second of December he died at home there, among the hills where he had been born sixty-two years before. Those at his bedside remembered him saying that earth was receding and heaven opening, and that this was his coronation day. He was buried on Round Top, a small hill above the seminary he had built. The boy who had been turned away by a church committee had become, under God, a door through which a great multitude came home.

# Love That Passeth Knowledge

> And to know the love of Christ, which passeth knowledge, that ye might be filled with all the fulness of God. EPHESIANS 3:19

The Way to God, the little book in which Moody gathered the heart of his preaching, does not begin with sin, or the law, or the judgement. It begins with the love of God. That order was deliberate. Moody had learned, from his own heart and from twenty years of talking with anxious people, that most men and women keep away from God not because they have examined the gospel and found it wanting, but because they are afraid of Him. “The trouble is that men think God hates them; and so they are all the time running away from Him.”

He knew the fear from the inside. He confessed in the same chapter that for years he had thought more of Christ than of the Father, picturing God as a stern judge whose anger the Son had to appease. It was becoming a father himself that changed him. As he looked at his own boy, he began to think it must have required more love for the Father to give His Son than for the Son to die.

> For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. JOHN 3:16

That verse, he said, was so high that he could never climb to it; he would quote it and pass on. It was the verse Harry Moorhouse had preached on for night after night in Chicago, and it undid Moody’s old preaching. From then on the love of God was the first and last note of everything he said.

## Love that does not change

The first thing Moody insists on is that God’s love is unchangeable. Human love cools. Friends drift apart, and love can even turn to hatred. God’s love does not. He points to the night of the betrayal: Jesus knew that Judas would sell Him and that Peter would deny Him, and still He washed their feet.

> Now before the feast of the passover, when Jesus knew that his hour was come that he should depart out of this world unto the Father, having loved his own which were in the world, he loved them unto the end. JOHN 13:1

It was that love, Moody says, that broke Peter’s heart and brought him back. And then he draws the distinction that every anxious person needs to hear. God hates sin, and He hates it precisely because it ruins the people He loves. But the sinner and the sin are not the same. “God loves the sinner; but He hates the sin.” A mother does not stop loving her child because he is disobedient; she loves him still, and hates what is harming him.

## Stronger than a mother’s love

Moody’s favourite picture of the love of God was a mother’s love, because he thought it the strongest human love there is. He had watched his own widowed mother hold her family together, and he filled his sermons with mothers: the mother who sat through her son’s trial for murder and asked to be buried beside him, the mother who hung her picture on the wall of a mission hall in Glasgow in the hope that her lost daughter would see it. But he always ended in the same place. No mother’s love compares with God’s.

> Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. ISAIAH 49:15

> Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands; thy walls are continually before me. ISAIAH 49:16

It is worth dwelling on what that means. The love that holds a mother to a child who has disgraced her is only a reflection of the love of the God who made mothers. If we can hardly imagine a mother giving up her child, we should be ashamed to imagine that God has given us up.

## Loved before we loved

The second thing Moody insists on is that God loved us first. Many people, he says, assume that God will love them once they have become good, or once they have begun to love Him. The Bible teaches the opposite. God loved us while we were His enemies, and His love is the cause of our turning, not its reward.

> But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. ROMANS 5:8

He found the same order in a single phrase from the Revelation: unto him that loved us, and washed us (Revelation 1:5). It might have been expected, he says, that God would first wash us and then love us. But no; He loved us first. He compared it to a mother who finds her lost child ragged and filthy at the back of a meeting. She does not wait until he is washed and dressed before she runs to him. She takes him in her arms first, and washes him afterwards.

Why should God love us at all? Moody admits that he cannot say, except that it is God’s nature. “It is His nature to love; just as it is the nature of the sun to shine.” That is the ground of all hope. If God’s love depended on our deserving it, no one would be safe. Because it flows from what He is, no one is beyond it.

## Seen at the cross

Where is this love to be seen? Moody’s answer never varied: at Calvary. He asks his readers to stand at the foot of the cross and hear the prayer for the men who were driving in the nails, and then to say, if they can, that God does not love them.

> Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends. JOHN 15:13

Christ did more than that, Moody adds; He laid down His life for His enemies. The cross is the measure of the love of God, and it is a measure no one has ever reached the end of. That is why Paul could pray that the Ephesians might know a love that passes knowledge. Moody compares it to Columbus discovering America and dying without knowing much of what he had found. We may have discovered something of the love of God; there are heights and depths of it that eternity will not exhaust.

> Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins. 1 JOHN 4:10

## Love that can be refused

There is one more thing Moody says, and it is the reason his love is never sentimental. The love of God can be resisted. God does not love people into heaven against their will; He wants sons and daughters, not machines. “If any one is lost it will not be because God does not love him: it will be because he has resisted the love of God.”

That is where Moody always pressed his hearers. Having shown them the love of God, he asked what they would do with it. Would they trample it underfoot, or give themselves to Him that hour?

A word of balance is in order. Moody preached the love of God with such warmth that a hasty reader might think he made little of God’s holiness. He did not. The same book speaks plainly of judgement, and his next chapters are about sin and the blood. But he was sure that people will not come to a God they believe hates them. He would rather have them run toward the Father’s love, and learn His holiness on the way home, than stand at a distance, frightened and unforgiven.

> The LORD hath appeared of old unto me, saying, Yea, I have loved thee with an everlasting love: therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee. JEREMIAH 31:3

# Ruined by the Fall

> For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God. ROMANS 3:23

Moody’s preaching is often summed up in three phrases that his friends and followers used as a kind of motto: ruined by the fall, redeemed by the blood, regenerated by the Spirit. The next three chapters take them in turn. The first is the one nobody likes to hear, and Moody did not enjoy saying it. But he was sure that no one would value the remedy until he had understood the disease.

## Two kinds of enquirers

In The Way to God Moody divides all the people he met in the enquiry room into two classes. They had either the spirit of the Pharisee or the spirit of the publican. The publican already knew he was a sinner; he needed to be pointed straight to Christ. The Pharisee did not, and until he did, no amount of talk about the love of God would reach him.

> Two men went up into the temple to pray; the one a Pharisee, and the other a publican. LUKE 18:10

For the Pharisee, Moody had one passage he used more than any other: the third chapter of Romans, where Paul gathers the Old Testament’s verdict on the human race.

> As it is written, There is none righteous, no, not one: there is none that understandeth, there is none that seeketh after God. ROMANS 3:10–11

He tells of a man in a New England city, brought night after night to the meetings by a praying wife, who was so wrapped in his own righteousness that, Moody said, you could not get the point of a needle into him anywhere. Near the end of the campaign the man came to him trembling and asked him to step into a side room. He said he was the worst man in the state. Moody, alarmed, asked whether some particular crime was troubling him. No, said the man; his whole life had been a sin, because he had been a self-righteous Pharisee. Moody’s comment is the heart of this chapter: no man could have produced that conviction. The Spirit had.

## Not a matter of degree

Moody’s point about the fall is not that everyone is as wicked as possible. He knew perfectly well that some people are kinder, more honest and more useful than others. His point is that all have fallen short of the same standard, and that the difference between the respectable and the disreputable is a difference of degree, not of kind.

> If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. 1 JOHN 1:8

That is why he was glad that Christ spoke about the new birth to Nicodemus rather than to a thief or a harlot. If He had said, You must be born again, only to the notorious, respectable people would have assumed the words were not for them. Instead He said them to one of the best men in Jerusalem, a ruler and a teacher, with nothing on record against him.

The same conviction shapes Moody’s teaching on the law. In Weighed and Wanting, his addresses on the Ten Commandments, he holds the commandments up one by one so that his hearers might see themselves in them. His point, in effect, is that the law is like a mirror. It shows a man the dirt on his face, but no one ever washed his face with a mirror. The law was given to stop every mouth, not to save anyone.

> Now we know that what things soever the law saith, it saith to them who are under the law: that every mouth may be stopped, and all the world may become guilty before God. ROMANS 3:19

## The heart that cannot be trusted

Moody had a plain view of the human heart. He had seen too much of it in the slums of Chicago and the camps of the Civil War to be sentimental. When people told him they could not believe, he would answer that he could believe God better than he could believe his own heart, and quote Jeremiah.

> The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it? JEREMIAH 17:9

This is not gloom for its own sake. It is the ground of his whole gospel. If the heart were basically sound, people would need advice and encouragement. Because it is not, they need a new heart. That is why Moody had so little patience with religion that tried to patch up the old nature. You might as well, he said, try to make a leopard change its spots.

## Adam’s sin and ours

People sometimes objected to Moody that it was unjust for them to suffer for something Adam did thousands of years before. His answer was characteristically practical rather than philosophical. He did not argue the doctrine of original sin at length. He told a story.

Suppose, he says in The Way to God, that he was dying of consumption he had inherited from his parents. It was not his fault that he had it. But suppose a friend came with a remedy that had cured him, and then brought another and another who had been cured by it, and Moody dashed the medicine to the ground and died. Whose fault would his death be then? “If you are lost, it will not be on account of Adam’s sin.” We inherit the disease. We are condemned for refusing the cure.

> How shall we escape, if we neglect so great salvation. HEBREWS 2:3

It is a good sermon illustration, and it lands where Moody always wanted to land, on the hearer’s own responsibility. The reader should notice that it does not settle every question the Bible raises about Adam. Paul teaches in Romans 5 that in Adam all died, and that the one man’s disobedience affected the many in a way that is deeper than inheritance. Moody would not have denied it; he simply did not dwell on it. His concern was not to explain the fall but to get people out of it.

> Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned. ROMANS 5:12

## Good news in bad news

It is important to see why Moody preached the fall at all. He did not do it to crush people. He did it because, as he said of the Pharisee, the needle of conviction has to go in before the thread of the gospel can follow. The bad news is the necessary shadow of the good. A person who does not know he is lost has no use for a Saviour who came to seek and to save the lost.

> For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost. LUKE 19:10

A word of qualification is fair. Moody’s preaching could make the line between the two classes very sharp, and the crowded enquiry room could hurry people across it. Conviction of sin is not always a crisis in a side room at two in the morning. For some it is a slow dawning over years. Moody himself admitted elsewhere that some are converted so gradually that one cannot say when the light began. But he was right on the main point. Nobody comes to Christ as a good person needing a little help. Everyone comes as a sinner needing to be saved, and the sooner that is admitted the sooner the good news begins.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Are you, by temperament, more like the Pharisee or the publican? Where does the Pharisee in you show itself?
2. Read Romans 3:9–26 aloud, slowly. Which sentence do you find hardest to accept about yourself?
3. Moody said he could believe God better than his own heart. Where have you been trusting your feelings rather than God’s word?
4. Take one of the Ten Commandments this week and let it be a mirror. Do not excuse what you see; bring it to Christ.
5. Pray for someone you know who thinks himself too respectable to need saving, that the Spirit would do what argument cannot.

### A PRAYER

> Holy God, I have so often compared myself with others and felt safe. Show me myself as You see me.
> I confess that my heart is deceitful, and that I have fallen short of Your glory, not in one place only, but in all.
> I do not blame Adam, or my parents, or my circumstances. I have sinned, and I need a Saviour.
> Thank You that the bad news is not the last word. Lead me from the mirror to the fountain, and wash me in the blood of Your Son. Amen.

# Redeemed by the Blood

> Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed. 1 PETER 2:24

If the love of God was the first note of Moody’s preaching, the cross was its centre. He did not preach a vague benevolence in the sky. He preached a love that had done something, at a particular place, at a terrible cost. The love of God, he said in The Way to God, is to be learned at Calvary, and nowhere else so clearly.

## The old-fashioned gospel

In a sermon on the one word gospel, preached in England and now in the Ochorus library, Moody takes his hearers to the fifteenth chapter of First Corinthians, where Paul sets out what the gospel is.

> For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:3

That, says Moody, is the old-fashioned gospel, and he hoped the church would never get away from it. Some people in his day were urging preachers to say less about Christ’s death and more about His life and example. Moody would have none of it. A beautiful example, however beautiful, cannot take away a single sin. Christ’s life shows us what we ought to have been; His death deals with what we are.

He did not forget the rest of Paul’s summary. The same sermon rejoices that Christ was buried and rose again, that death could not hold Him, and that we do not worship a dead Saviour. But the resurrection matters because of what the death accomplished. It is God’s receipt for a debt paid.

## Our Substitute

The word Moody used most for Christ on the cross was substitute. He stood in our place, bore our sins, took the judgement that was ours.

> But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. ISAIAH 53:5

“The moment I believe on the Lord Jesus Christ as my substitute, as my Saviour, that moment I get light and peace.” It is a very simple sentence, and it is meant to be. Moody knew that anxious people do not need a lecture on the theories of the atonement; they need to know that their sins have been dealt with. So he pressed the fact again and again. The sins you have committed, and the ones you have forgotten, are not waiting for you at the judgement if you are in Christ. They were laid on Him.

In The Way to God he reaches for the Passover: when the children of Israel were placed behind the blood in Egypt, he says, that blood was their salvation. The picture repays thought. The destroyer did not ask whether the people in the house were good or bad, clever or simple. He asked only whether the blood was on the door.

> And the blood shall be to you for a token upon the houses where ye are: and when I see the blood, I will pass over you. EXODUS 12:13

## The burnt ground

Moody’s most memorable picture of the atonement came from the American prairies. In the autumn, he says, the long grass sometimes caught fire, and a strong wind drove the flames faster than a horse could run. Hunters caught in its path had one way of escape. They set fire to the grass around themselves, let it burn, and stepped onto the blackened ground. When the great fire came, it passed them by, because there was nothing left to burn.

Then he applied it. “There is one mountain peak that the wrath of God has swept over—that is, Mount Calvary, and that fire spent its fury upon the bosom of the Son of God.” The believer stands on burnt ground. The judgement has already fallen there.

> There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit. ROMANS 8:1

It is worth pausing on how seriously this takes the wrath of God. Moody’s preaching of love is sometimes caricatured as sentimental. But the prairie fire is not sentimental. It assumes that there is a fire, that it is coming, and that no one can outrun it. The love of God in Moody’s gospel is not a love that ignores judgement. It is a love that bears it.

## What God cannot see

In the same sermon Moody tells of a child in Ireland who was asked by a teacher whether there was anything God could not do. The child said yes: God could not see his sins through the blood of Christ. Moody’s comment was brief: “The blood covers them.”

> The blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin. 1 JOHN 1:7

That is the heart of redemption for Moody. It is not that God pretends our sins were never committed. It is that they have been paid for, and the payment covers them completely. Redemption is a word from the slave market; it means to buy back. We were not bought, Peter says, with silver and gold, which God could have made in any quantity, but with something infinitely more costly.

> Forasmuch as ye know that ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold, from your vain conversation received by tradition from your fathers; But with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot. 1 PETER 1:18–19

## More than a rescue

Moody knew that the cross was not only a transaction. In The Way to God he says that Christ is not only a Saviour but a Deliverer. He had little sympathy, he says, with the idea that God comes down to save us and then leaves us in prison, slaves to our besetting sins. The blood that frees us from guilt also sets us on the road out of Egypt.

That is an important balance, because Moody’s emphasis on substitution could, in careless hands, turn into something cheap: a transaction completed once, with no claim on the rest of one’s life. Moody did not mean that. The Christ who died for us died that we should live unto righteousness, as Peter says. The fire has passed over; now we are to walk on the burnt ground as those who owe everything.

It is also fair to say that Moody, preaching to crowds, rarely went further than the plain facts of the atonement. He was content to say that Christ died in our place, and to leave the deeper questions of how it was just and why it was necessary to others. The reader who wants to go further will find help in the older divines he loved and quoted. But no reader will find a more direct invitation to stand on the burnt ground than Moody’s.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is there any sin you still carry as though it were waiting for you at the judgement? Name it, and then read Romans 8:1 over it.
2. Moody insisted on Christ’s death, not only His example. Which has your own faith leaned on more?
3. Take the picture of the burnt ground into a moment of fear this week. Tell yourself where you stand.
4. Read 1 Peter 1:18–19 and consider what it means that you were bought at such a price. What part of your life are you still treating as your own?
5. Read Moody’s sermon One Word — Gospel, in the Ochorus library, in a single sitting.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You bore my sins in Your own body on the tree. I cannot add anything to what You finished there.
> I stand on the ground where the fire has already fallen, and I thank You that there is now no condemnation for me.
> Let me never make Your blood cheap by living as though I were still my own.
> You bought me back; now keep me, deliver me from every besetting sin, and let my life say that I was redeemed at a great price. Amen.

# Ye Must Be Born Again

> Jesus answered and said unto him, Verily, verily, I say unto thee, Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God. JOHN 3:3

Of all the subjects Moody preached on, none came back more often than the new birth. There are two versions of the sermon in the Ochorus library — The Gateway into the Kingdom, the second chapter of The Way to God, and a separate sermon called The New Birth — and they say the same thing with the same urgency. This, Moody said, is where everything begins. “It is really the A B C of the Christian religion.” A man who is wrong here, he added, will be wrong nearly everywhere else.

## Said to the best man in Jerusalem

Moody never tired of pointing out to whom Jesus first said these words. Not to the woman at the well, or to Zacchaeus, or to a thief, but to Nicodemus: a Pharisee, a ruler of the Jews, a teacher of Israel, one of the most respectable men in the city. If the Lord had kept this teaching for publicans and harlots, respectable people would have assumed it was not for them. Instead He said it to the best of them, and so He said it to everyone.

> Marvel not that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again. JOHN 3:7

## What it is not

Moody was a practical man, and he found that the quickest way to explain the new birth was to clear away what people mistook for it. He had met them all in the enquiry room.

It is not church-going. Many told him they were Christians because they went to church every Sunday. It is not trying to do right, or turning over a new leaf, or making a good resolution. It is not saying one’s prayers. It is not the Lord’s Supper. And it is not baptism. Here Moody is careful. Baptism, he says, is all right in its place, and God forbid that he should say anything against it. But if it is put in the place of the new birth, it is a terrible mistake. A person may be baptised into the church without ever being born of the Spirit.

> That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. JOHN 3:6

The reason all these things fall short is the same. They are things we do. The new birth is something God does.

## A new creation

This is the centre of Moody’s teaching. The problem with human nature is not that it needs improving but that it needs replacing. “God never mends, He creates anew,” he told one congregation; therefore do not try to patch up the old Adam nature. In The Way to God he makes the same point from Genesis. When God made the world, man was not there to help. When Christ redeemed the world, He was alone. So it is when a soul is born again. “Regeneration is a new creation; and if it is a new creation it must be the work of God.”

> Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:17

That is humbling, because it means we cannot make ourselves Christians. It is also hopeful, because it means that no one is too far gone. If the new birth depended on the raw material, the drunkard and the thief would have little chance. Because it depends on God, the worst have as much hope as the best. Moody filled his sermons with drunkards whose children had hidden from them in terror and who now sat at home singing Rock of Ages, and he offered them as evidence: not reformed only, but regenerated.

> Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost. TITUS 3:5

## The wind

People often told Moody that they could not understand the new birth and would not believe what they could not understand. He did not pretend to explain it. He pointed, as Jesus did, to the wind.

> The wind bloweth where it listeth, and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth: so is every one that is born of the Spirit. JOHN 3:8

No one, he said, can fully explain the wind. It may blow north here and south a hundred miles away. But a child who has had her umbrella blown out of her hands knows that there is such a thing as wind. “I have felt the spirit of God working in my heart, just as really and as truly as I have felt the wind blowing in my face.” We believe in the creation without understanding how God made something out of nothing; we may believe in the new birth without understanding how God makes a new creature.

## Why it is necessary

Moody gave a reason for the new birth that is easily forgotten. It is not only that God requires it; it is that heaven would be unbearable without it. Take a man whose heart is full of corruption, he says, and set him down among the redeemed, and he would not want to stay. He cannot spend one Sunday on earth among God’s people with pleasure; what would he do with an eternal one? “Heaven is a prepared place for a prepared people.”

That is a searching thought. The new birth is not an arbitrary entrance fee. It is the giving of a nature that can enjoy God. Without it, heaven would be a foreign country, and its joy a language one could not speak.

## How it happens

Having said that the new birth is God’s work, Moody immediately says what the sinner must do: look. He goes straight on in John 3 to the brazen serpent.

> And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: That whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life. JOHN 3:14–15

The new birth and faith are not two separate events, one God’s and one ours. When a person looks to Christ lifted up, the Spirit gives life. The next chapter takes up that look in detail.

A word of care is needed. Moody’s way of speaking — nearly always of a definite moment, often in a meeting — could leave the impression that a person must be able to name the day and hour of the new birth. He himself denied it. Some, he says in The Way to God, are converted so gradually that, like the morning light, it is impossible to tell when the dawn began. A child raised in a godly home may never remember a time when he did not trust Christ. What matters, Moody said, is not being able to prove when you were converted, but knowing that you have been. It is a good test for anyone. The question is not whether you had an experience like someone else’s. It is whether you have been born again.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Go through Moody’s list of what the new birth is not: church-going, good resolutions, prayers, sacraments. Which of these have you been tempted to rest on?
2. Nicodemus was a good man who still needed to be born again. Who in your life is in his position? Pray for them.
3. Ask yourself plainly, not whether you can name the day, but whether there is new life in you: a love for God and His people that you did not make.
4. If you are not sure, look today to Christ lifted up, and ask the Spirit to do what you cannot.
5. Read The Gateway into the Kingdom in The Way to God, or Moody’s sermon The New Birth, in the Ochorus library.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I cannot make myself new. I have turned over leaves and made resolutions, and my old nature is still there.
> Do in me what only You can do: give me a new heart and a new spirit, born from above.
> Let me not rest in going to church, or in anything I do, but only in what You have done.
> And make me fit for heaven, so that when I see Your face it will be home, and not a strange country. Amen.

# Look and Live

> Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth: for I am God, and there is none else. ISAIAH 45:22

Moody spent more hours than almost anyone of his century talking with people who wanted to be saved and did not know how. The question he heard most often was not whether they should believe, but what believing was. They had heard the words believe on the Lord Jesus Christ all their lives. They did not know what to do with them. Much of The Way to God, and several of the sermons in the Ochorus library, are his attempt to answer that question in words a child could follow.

## The bitten camp

His favourite picture of faith was the brazen serpent. He would describe the Israelite camp in the wilderness, with fiery serpents in the tents and graves along the road, and a mother bending over her dying son. Then the news runs through the camp: God has provided a remedy. Moses has set a serpent of brass on a pole, and whoever looks at it will live. The mother lifts the boy to the door of the tent, and he is too weak to see. Keep looking, she tells him. At last he catches the glint of the brass, and he is well.

> And the LORD said unto Moses, Make thee a fiery serpent, and set it upon a pole: and it shall come to pass, that every one that is bitten, when he looketh upon it, shall live. NUMBERS 21:8

Moody drew several lessons. The first was that looking is simple. It does not require education; a child who cannot read learns to look before he can walk. The second was that looking at the wound does not heal. Many anxious people, he said, are always examining their sins and their feelings. It was a Belfast surgeon, he says, who told his patients to take a good look at the wound and then fix their eyes on him and not take them off, and Moody turned it into counsel for the enquirer: “Sinner, take a good look at your wound; and then fix your eyes on Christ, and do not take them off.”

The third lesson was aimed at the clever. In Moody’s telling, a young man in the camp refuses to look until someone explains how brass on a pole can cure a snakebite, and dies demanding an explanation. His friend’s answer is Moody’s answer to every intellectual objection that is really an excuse: “Let your philosophy go: look and live.”

## Taking God at His word

When Moody defined faith, he kept it very plain. Faith is taking God at His word. We live by trusting testimony all the time, he said; commerce would stop in two days if no one believed anyone. If we receive the witness of men, the witness of God is greater.

> If we receive the witness of men, the witness of God is greater: for this is the witness of God which he hath testified of his Son. 1 JOHN 5:9

“Faith is a belief in testimony. It is not a leap in the dark, as some tell us.” God does not ask anyone to believe without giving him something to believe. That is why Moody sent anxious people to the Bible rather than into themselves.

> So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God. ROMANS 10:17

In The Right Kind of Faith he confesses that he used to pray for faith while all the time neglecting the Bible, and he sends his hearers to the Gospel of John, written, as John says, that ye might believe. Faith is not a mood that descends on us from nowhere. It grows where God’s word is heard.

## Knowledge, assent and appropriation

Moody was not content with a merely mental faith. He knew that the devils believe, and tremble. So in The Way to God he borrows an old threefold definition. “There are three things indispensable to faith—knowledge, assent, and appropriation.” We must know who Christ is and what He has done. We must agree that it is true. And we must take Him for ourselves.

It is the third that people miss. Moody compared it to bread: a hungry man may admit that there are a thousand loaves in the house and still starve if he never eats one. Or to a drowning man and a rope. He must not only see the rope; he must let go of everything else and hold on to it.

> He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life: and he that believeth not the Son shall not see life; but the wrath of God abideth on him. JOHN 3:36

## Faith is not a feeling

The commonest obstacle Moody met was feeling. People would not believe until they felt something, and because they did not feel it, they did not believe. His answer was blunt. “Feeling has nothing whatever to do with Believing.” The Bible does not say that he who feels has everlasting life. It says that he who believes has it. Feelings come and go with the weather and the digestion. God’s word does not.

He added a related counsel for those who kept examining their faith to see whether it was the right kind. “Faith is the hand that takes the blessing.” A beggar who has been given a thousand dollars does not worry whether he received it with the right hand or the left. What matters is not the quality of the hand but the gift in it. In the same way, he says, it is far more important to see that we have the right kind of Christ than to agonise over whether our faith is strong enough.

> And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand. JOHN 10:28

## A gift, not a purchase

In The Way of Salvation Moody puts the same truth another way. Suppose, he says, he offered a Bible to whosoever would take it, and a man came forward and insisted on paying a penny for it. The moment he paid, it ceased to be a gift. So it is with salvation. Many people want to give God something for it: tears, resolutions, a better life. God will not sell it. He gives it.

> For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord. ROMANS 6:23

Moody pressed this to its practical end. How long does it take to accept a gift? There is a moment when it is another’s and the next moment when it is yours. That is why he believed a person could come to Christ in a moment, in a meeting, or walking home.

## A caution

Moody’s simplicity here is a great gift to anxious people, and it has helped millions. But the reader should notice two dangers. The first is that take God at His word can shrink into agreeing to a proposition, without the turning of the whole person that Scripture calls faith. Moody guarded against that with his three elements and his insistence on repentance, which is the subject of the next chapter. The second is that the emphasis on a moment of decision can make people anxious about whether they believed correctly at that moment. Moody’s own answer to that is the right one: stop looking at your faith and look at Christ. It is not the look that saves. It is the One looked at.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Have you been waiting for a feeling before you will trust Christ? Read John 5:24 and ask what it says, not what you feel.
2. Moody prayed for faith while neglecting the Bible. How much of God’s word have you actually read this month?
3. Of knowledge, assent and appropriation, which is weakest in you? What would it mean to take Christ for yourself today?
4. Stop examining your hand. Spend ten minutes this week simply looking at Christ in one of the Gospels.
5. Read The Right Kind of Faith or The Way of Salvation, both in the Ochorus library.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have looked at my wound so long that I have almost forgotten there is a remedy.
> I lift my eyes to You, lifted up on the cross for me. I take You at Your word.
> I do not bring a penny to pay for what You give freely. I receive it as a gift, with empty hands.
> When my feelings fail, let my faith rest on what You have said, and keep my eyes on You until I see You face to face. Amen.

# Right About Face

> And the times of this ignorance God winked at; but now commandeth all men every where to repent. ACTS 17:30

Moody believed that repentance was one of the most misunderstood words in the Christian vocabulary. People had heard it from childhood, he said, and if you asked them for a definition you would get a strange one. So in the chapter of The Way to God called Repentance and Restitution he does what he did with the new birth: he clears away the mistakes first.

## What repentance is not

“Repentance is not fear.” Men at sea in a storm, he says, will cry to God for mercy and go back to their swearing when the weather clears. Pharaoh seemed to repent each time a plague fell, and hardened his heart each time it lifted.

Nor is repentance feeling. Moody preached for a time every Sunday to hundreds of convicts in the penitentiary at Baltimore. Nearly all of them felt miserable, especially in their first weeks inside. Most went back to their old ways when they were released. They were sorry they had been caught.

Nor is it remorse. Judas had remorse enough to hang himself, and it was not repentance. Moody believed that if Judas had gone to his Lord and confessed, he would have been forgiven. Nor is it fasting, or penance, or even deep conviction of sin, which a man may carry for months without turning. Nor, finally, is it giving up one particular sin. A drunkard may sign the pledge and stop drinking, and still not have repented. Breaking off one sin, Moody says, is like breaking one limb off a tree when the whole tree must come down, or stopping one leak in a ship that is holed in four places.

## What it is

Having cleared the ground, Moody gives his definition, and it is a soldier’s word of command: right about face. A man walking one way turns round and walks the other. He notes that repentance has also been described as a change of mind, and he takes his example from the parable of the two sons.

> But what think ye? A certain man had two sons; and he came to the first, and said, Son, go work to day in my vineyard. He answered and said, I will not: but afterward he repented, and went. MATTHEW 21:28–29

The son changed his mind, but Moody’s point is that he also went. He was not only convinced he had been wrong; he picked up his tools and walked into the field. That, says Moody, is Christ’s definition of repentance. It is a turning of the will that shows itself in the feet.

> Turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways; for why will ye die, O house of Israel? EZEKIEL 33:11

And because it is a turning, it need not take long. “It does not take a man six months to change his mind.” Moody tells of a vessel that went down off Newfoundland. There was a moment when the captain could have reversed the engines, and a moment after which it was too late. So there is a moment in every life, he says, when a person can halt and say, by the grace of God, I will go no further this way.

## The goodness that leads to it

It would be easy to read all this as though repentance were a feat of will that sinners must perform before God will look at them. Moody did not mean that, and he quotes a friend, Dr Brooks of St Louis, to guard against it. Some people, Brooks says, are not sure they have repented enough. If they mean they must repent in order to make God merciful, the sooner they give up that kind of repentance the better. God is already merciful, as the cross shows.

> Or despisest thou the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering; not knowing that the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance? ROMANS 2:4

It is not our badness but His goodness that leads us to turn. Saving repentance and saving faith, Moody’s friend says, always go together. We turn from sin because we have turned to Christ.

## Restitution

Here Moody becomes very practical, and many readers will find him uncomfortable. If repentance is real, he says, it will bear fruit, and one of its first fruits is restitution. “If we have done wrong to any one we should never ask God to forgive us, until we are willing to make restitution.”

He tells stories he had lived through. A well-dressed man came to him after a meeting and confessed that he had stolen about fifteen hundred dollars from his employers. He still had some of it and wondered whether he might invest it and pay them back from the profits. Moody told him that was a delusion of Satan. The man brought him what he had left, and Moody met the two employers in a side room of the church and asked them for mercy, not justice. They wept and forgave him, and the four of them knelt and prayed together. Another friend, newly converted, remembered that he had once taken advantage of the government in his business dealings, and sent a cheque to the Treasury. A man of thirty-two, grey before his time, confessed that at twenty he had set fire to his widowed mother’s failing newspaper office for the insurance money, and had been tormented for twelve years. Moody told him to calculate the sum with interest and pay it back.

> And Zacchaeus stood, and said unto the Lord; Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold. LUKE 19:8

“I believe a great many men are crying to God for light; and they are not getting it because they are not honest.”

## A necessary balance

Moody is right that repentance which keeps the stolen money is not repentance. He is right that many Christians are in darkness because there is something they know they should put right and will not. The reader should take him seriously.

But the reader should also notice that his language can be pressed too far. Forgiveness is not earned by restitution; it is received through Christ. Moody himself did not teach otherwise, but his wording can be heard as a condition that must be fulfilled before grace will act. The better way to say it is that a person who has truly turned to Christ will want to put things right, and will not be at peace until he has done what he can. Some wrongs cannot be undone: the person is dead, the harm is past repair. Moody’s counsel in Prevailing Prayer is to do what lies in our power, and the gospel covers the rest.

He added one more solemn note. Now, he said, is the only day of mercy you will ever have. He had searched his Bible carefully, and could find no second chance after death.

> Repent ye therefore, and be converted, that your sins may be blotted out, when the times of refreshing shall come from the presence of the Lord. ACTS 3:19

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Go through Moody’s list: fear, feeling, remorse, penance, conviction, giving up one sin. Which have you mistaken for repentance?
2. Is there a direction in your life in which you need to turn right about face? Name it, and turn today.
3. Ask God whether there is anything you have taken, damaged or misrepresented that you need to put right. Write it down.
4. Take one concrete step of restitution this week — a letter, a repayment, an apology — and do it before you lose your nerve.
5. Read Repentance and Restitution in The Way to God, in the Ochorus library.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have been sorry many times, and gone on the same way. I do not want remorse; I want to turn.
> Thank You that it is Your goodness, not my badness, that draws me back.
> Show me anything I have wronged and not put right, and give me the honesty and the courage to deal with it.
> I turn from my sin and toward You now, not in six months’ time, but today. Keep me walking the other way. Amen.

# That Ye May Know

> These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life, and that ye may believe on the name of the Son of God. 1 JOHN 5:13

Moody had no patience with the idea that a Christian must go through life hoping he is saved, and find out only at the judgement. He met it everywhere: in pious people who thought that certainty would be presumptuous, and in anxious people who had trusted Christ and still lived in fear. To both he said the same thing. God means His children to know.

The chapter of The Way to God called Assurance of Salvation is one of the most helpful things he wrote. It begins with a plain statement: “it is the privilege of every child of God to have beyond doubt a knowledge of his own salvation.”

## Not everyone should have it

Moody begins, surprisingly, by saying who ought not to have assurance. There are two classes. The first are those in the church who have never been born again; assurance for them would be a false peace. The second are those who are not willing to do God’s will, who want some other place than the one God has marked out for them. Assurance is not a comfort for people who are running from God. It is the birthright of those who have come home.

## Know, know, know

With that settled, Moody goes to the first letter of John. John wrote his Gospel, he says, so that we might believe; he wrote his letter so that we might know. In those five short chapters the word know occurs again and again, and Moody tells his readers to go through the letter and count it. The note of the whole epistle is not maybe but know.

In the third chapter he finds five things worth knowing. We know that Christ was manifested to take away our sins, and He did not fail in His mission. We know that we are of the truth. We know that we have passed from death to life, because we love the brethren. We know that He abides in us, by the Spirit He has given us. And, best of all, we know that we are the sons of God now, not only when we die.

> Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is. 1 JOHN 3:2

> We know that we have passed from death unto life, because we love the brethren. He that loveth not his brother abideth in death. 1 JOHN 3:14

## Where doubts come from

Moody was a good physician of doubt, and he names its causes plainly.

The first is looking at ourselves. “If you want to scatter your doubts, look at the blood; and if you want to increase your doubts, look at yourself.” You will get doubts enough for years, he says, by being occupied with yourself for a few days. When he was a boy walking in the snow, he could make a straight track only by fixing his eyes on a tree ahead; the moment he looked down, he walked crooked.

> Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee. ISAIAH 26:3

The second is wanting a token beyond God’s word. If Moody promised to meet a man tomorrow and the man asked for his watch as security, it would be an insult to his honesty. God has made promise after promise. To ask for a sign besides is to doubt Him.

The third is living as a servant rather than a son. Many Christians, Moody says, never get beyond thinking of themselves as God’s servants. A servant does not walk freely about the house; a son does. God has not only adopted us; we are His by birth. Moody’s little boy was as much his son at a day old as at fourteen. “The children of God are not perfect; but we are perfectly His children.”

## But I have sinned

The objection Moody heard most often was this: I believed, but I have sinned since. His answer was that there has never been a Christian who has not. God has made provision for it, and the provision is Christ.

> My little children, these things write I unto you, that ye sin not. And if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous. 1 JOHN 2:1

Moody makes a lovely point about confessed sin. When his son did wrong and asked forgiveness, he forgave him and kissed him. If the boy came back the next day to ask forgiveness for the same thing, it would grieve his father to be doubted. The past sins of Christians, confessed, are settled; they are not to be opened again.

He also distinguishes carefully between standing and growth. “Justification is instantaneous; but sanctification is a life-work.” A tree may be perfect in its first year without being mature. So a believer may be truly God’s child and still very unfinished. Assurance does not rest on how far we have grown, but on whose we are.

## Kept by the Shepherd

Underneath all of Moody’s teaching on assurance lies the keeping power of God. Many young converts, he says, are afraid they will not hold out. He answers that it is not the sheep’s business to keep the shepherd. “It is the Shepherd’s business to keep the sheep.”

> Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy. JUDE 1:24

He liked to quote Paul’s last letter: I know whom I have believed, and am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him against that day (2 Timothy 1:12). It was, he said, a grand persuasion.

Yet Moody was not careless about the danger of drifting. The last chapter of The Way to God is on backsliding, and it is full of warnings. Christians fall, he says, on the strongest side of their character, as Peter did, step by step: first boasting, then sleeping, then following afar off, then warming himself among the enemies of Christ. But the same chapter is full of the tenderness of God toward those who have wandered. The Lord never left them, Moody says; they left Him. And the way back is open: only acknowledge your iniquity (Jeremiah 3:13).

## A word of care

Moody’s teaching on assurance has comforted countless people. One sentence in it needs weighing. “No man is fit for God’s service who is filled with doubts.” It is true that settled peace makes a better worker than constant fear. But the Bible is full of servants who worked through dark nights of doubt, and some believers of tender conscience or troubled mind will find their assurance growing slowly over years. They should not be told they are unfit for service. They should be told, as Moody told most people, to stop looking inward and look at Christ. J. C. Ryle, whom Moody quotes in the same chapter, put it well: “Faith is the root, and Assurance the flower.” The root may be alive long before the flower opens.

1. Can you say that you know you have eternal life? If not, is it because you are not trusting Christ, or because you are looking at yourself?
2. Read 1 John through in one sitting and mark each time John says we know.
3. Is there a sin you have confessed and keep bringing back to God? Thank Him that it is settled, and stop asking again.
4. Look back over the last year. Is there any sign of Peter’s slide — self-confidence, prayerlessness, following afar off? Turn back today.
5. Read Assurance of Salvation and Backsliding in The Way to God, in the Ochorus library.

> Father, You have written so that I may know. Forgive me for dishonouring You with doubts I have fed by looking at myself.
> I look away to the blood of Your Son, and I rest on what You have said: whoever has the Son has life.
> Thank You that I am not a servant hoping for wages, but a child in my Father’s house.
> Keep me from drifting, and if I wander, bring me back. You are the Shepherd, and I trust You to keep Your sheep. Amen.

# The Old Book

> Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path. PSALM 119:105

Moody was not a scholar of the Bible. He could not read Hebrew or Greek, and he never pretended to weigh the arguments of the critics who, in his later years, were beginning to unsettle the churches. But he was a man of one book, and he read it with a hunger that shamed many who knew far more about it. He called it, with affection, the old Book, and he wanted every Christian to read it for himself.

## The book that came alive

In the chapter of The Way to God called Words of Counsel, Moody describes how the Bible changed for him. He remembered one night when it was the driest and darkest book in the world. The next day it was entirely different; he felt he had been given the key. The difference, he says, was that he had been born of the Spirit. But he adds a searching sentence: before he knew anything of the mind of God, he had to give up his sin.

> If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether I speak of myself. JOHN 7:17

This was Moody’s answer to many honest sceptics. God does not reveal His secrets to those who are determined to keep their sins. But if a man is willing to turn from sin, he will be surprised, Moody says, at how the light comes in. “I believe God meets every soul on the spot of self-surrender.”

> The secret of the LORD is with them that fear him; and he will shew them his covenant. PSALM 25:14

## Faith comes by hearing

The Bible was central to Moody’s gospel because it is where faith comes from. He sent anxious people not into their feelings but into the Word. In the enquiry room he was always turning to a verse: for the man who said he had no strength, Romans 5:6; for the one who said he could not see, the promise that Christ is the light of the world; for the one afraid he would not hold out, Psalm 121. It is dangerous, he wrote, for seekers to lean on other people’s experiences. The best thing for the anxious is to go straight to the Word of God.

> For ever, O LORD, thy word is settled in heaven. PSALM 119:89

He applied the same rule to himself. In The Right Kind of Faith he says that we are not to put our faith in any man, not even a prophet, if what he says is contrary to the word of God. “Let us cling to the word of God, and have faith in God.”

## What he did not understand

Moody was refreshingly honest about the parts of the Bible he could not explain. When he was first converted, he says in The Way to God, he thought he had to defend the whole Bible from beginning to end against all comers, until a Boston unbeliever demolished all his arguments at once. He learned from it. “There are many things in the Word of God that I do not profess to understand.” When he was asked what he did with them, he said he believed them. He compared those who start with the hard passages to people who begin eating a fish by picking the bones. Leave the bones, he says, until you have light on them, and feed on what is plain.

> The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law. DEUTERONOMY 29:29

There is great wisdom here. Many people never read the Bible because they cannot get past the first difficulty. Moody’s counsel is to read what you understand, obey it, and trust that the rest will become clearer. He said that things which had been dark to him five years before had since been flooded with light, and that he expected to go on discovering new things about God throughout eternity.

## Taking the Bible whole

Moody took the Bible as God’s word from Genesis to Revelation, and he was not ashamed of it. In his sermon on Noah he says that if he gives up the ark he will give up the whole Bible, because Christ Himself set His seal to the story of the flood.

> But as the days of Noe were, so shall also the coming of the Son of man be. MATTHEW 24:37

He was persuaded that once a man begins to pick the Bible to pieces, it does not take him long to tear it all to pieces. “I pity any man that is picking the old Book to pieces.”

This is the place for honest qualification. Moody’s instinct was right: Christ did treat the Old Testament as the word of God, and a Christian cannot simply set aside what his Lord received. But the argument that questioning one detail must lead to throwing away the whole can become a way of refusing to think. Some believing scholars in his own day wrestled carefully with hard questions of history and interpretation without losing their faith, and Moody, with his gift for friendship, invited some of them to Northfield. The better response to hard questions is not to stop asking them but to ask them humbly, on our knees, with the Book open, as Moody himself did with the passages he could not yet explain.

## Every Christian a reader

The practical heart of Moody’s teaching on Scripture is that it belongs to ordinary people. He founded the Northfield conferences largely for Bible teaching, and his school in Chicago to train lay men and women in the Word. In Pleasure and Profit in Bible Study he urges ordinary Christians to study for themselves, a book at a time and a subject at a time, and to mark their Bibles freely. He rose early to read it, and he wanted others to do the same. He had seen too many Christians who lived on sermons and never opened the Book themselves.

> Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me. JOHN 5:39

That verse gives the last word on Moody’s view of the Bible. He did not love it as a monument. He loved it because it spoke of Christ. The old Book was precious to him because it was the place where he met his Saviour, and where he sent everyone else to meet Him too.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is there a sin you are holding that may be keeping the Bible dark to you? Moody found the light came when he gave his up.
2. Choose one book of the Bible to read through this month, a chapter a day, with a pencil in hand.
3. Write down one passage you do not understand. Leave the bones for now, and feed on what is plain around it.
4. The next time you are anxious, go to a verse before you go to a friend. Keep a short list of promises to hand.
5. Read Words of Counsel in The Way to God, in the Ochorus library.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, thank You for Your word, settled in heaven and given to ordinary people like me.
> Forgive me for living on other people’s experiences and leaving the Book closed.
> Where it is dark to me, show me if there is anything I must give up; where I do not understand, teach me to trust and wait.
> Open my eyes to see Christ on every page, and let me meet Him there each morning. Amen.

# Christ All and in All

> Where there is neither Greek nor Jew, circumcision nor uncircumcision, Barbarian, Scythian, bond nor free: but Christ is all, and in all. COLOSSIANS 3:11

Moody preached on this text so often that it appears twice in the Ochorus library: as a chapter of The Way to God and as a separate sermon. Both are, in effect, a slow walk around the person of Christ, stopping at each of the names Scripture gives Him and asking the hearer whether he knows Christ in that way. It is Moody at his most devotional, and it shows that his gospel was never merely a plan of salvation. It was a Person.

## A small Saviour or a mighty one

The chapter begins with a sentence that needs careful reading. “Christ is all to us that we make Him to be.” Moody does not mean that we decide who Christ is. He means that we enjoy only as much of Him as we are willing to receive. “Some Christians have a very small Saviour, for they are not willing to receive Him fully,” and others have a mighty Saviour because they let Him be great in them.

He adds that there are three ways of knowing a man: by hearsay, by a slight introduction, and by long intimate acquaintance. So there are three kinds of people in and around the church: those who know Christ only by reading about Him, those who have a slight personal acquaintance, and those who long, with Paul, to know Him and the power of His resurrection.

> That I may know him, and the power of his resurrection, and the fellowship of his sufferings, being made conformable unto his death. PHILIPPIANS 3:10

## Saviour first

Moody’s walk around Christ always begins in the same place. Before anything else, Christ is Saviour from sin. “He did not come to save us in our sins, but from our sins.” The angels announced Him to the shepherds as a Saviour; Isaiah had long before heard God say that beside Him there is no Saviour. Other religions, Moody says, teach men to work their way up to God; the gospel is God coming down to lift men out of the pit. “So we start from the Cross, not from the cradle.”

> And thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins. MATTHEW 1:21

## Deliverer, Redeemer, Way

But Christ is more than a Saviour. A man may save someone from drowning and be able to do nothing further for him. Christ also delivers. When Israel was sheltered behind the blood in Egypt they were safe from the angel of death, but they would still have heard the crack of the slave-driver’s whip if God had not also brought them out. Moody had little sympathy with a gospel that saves people and then leaves them in prison to their besetting sins.

Then Christ is Redeemer, who bought us back. He is the Way, who has gone ahead and blazed the trail through the forest, as frontiersmen cut marks on the trees so that others could follow. And Moody gives a test for whether we are following Him: when someone slanders you, do you treat him as your Master would have done?

> Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me. JOHN 14:6

## The Light, and borrowed light

Christ is also the Light of the world. Moody says he once thought a good deal of a picture of Christ knocking at a door with a lantern in His hand, but came to feel he would rather turn its face to the wall: “you might as well hang up a lantern to the sun as put one into Christ’s hand.” Christ is not a small light in a dark place. He is the Sun of Righteousness.

> Then spake Jesus again unto them, saying, I am the light of the world: he that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, but shall have the light of life. JOHN 8:12

That has a practical consequence. Many people, Moody says, go hunting for light and peace and joy. We are nowhere told to seek these things directly. When he was a boy he tried to catch his shadow and could not; but when he turned his face to the sun, the shadow followed him. So peace and joy come when we are occupied with Christ. Once someone asked Moody how he felt, and he had to stop and think; it was so long since he had thought about his feelings.

And the Christian is to shine in turn, with light that is not his own. “If we have any light at all it is borrowed light.” The moon shines only because the sun shines on it.

## Keeper and Shepherd

Christ is our Keeper, the One who neither slumbers nor sleeps. He is the Shepherd, who in the tenth chapter of John says I will and I am again and again as He declares what He will do for His sheep. Moody loved the story of the Eastern shepherd who could not get his flock to cross a stream until he picked up two lambs, carried them over, and the sheep followed. A friend of his who had lost all his children to scarlet fever saw that happen in Syria and understood at last why the Shepherd had taken his lambs first.

> My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. JOHN 10:27

## Truly God

Behind all these names lies the conviction set out in another chapter of The Way to God, A Divine Saviour. Moody said that when he was a boy he thought of Jesus as a good man, perhaps the best man who ever lived. But he found that Christ claimed far more: to have come from heaven, to forgive sins, to be honoured as the Father is honoured, to receive worship. If Christ were a mere man, Moody reasoned, then either He was a deceiver or He was deluded, and worshipping Him would be idolatry. The only other possibility is the one the church confesses: He is God manifest in the flesh.

> Before Abraham was, I am. JOHN 8:58

Moody was not a sophisticated apologist, and the argument can be made with more care than he gave it. But its heart is sound. Christ cannot be kept as a good teacher. He must be worshipped or rejected. A small Saviour is no Saviour at all.

## A caution and an invitation

The danger of a chapter like this is that it becomes a list, admired and put away. Moody’s whole point was the opposite. He was not cataloguing Christ’s offices for the sake of theology. He was asking his hearers which of these they had actually received. Do you know Him as Deliverer, or are you still a slave to your temper? Do you know Him as Light, or are you groping in the fog? Do you know Him as Keeper, or are you trying to keep yourself?

> For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily. And ye are complete in him, which is the head of all principality and power. COLOSSIANS 2:9–10

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is your Saviour small or mighty? In what part of your life have you not been willing to receive Him fully?
2. Which name of Christ in this chapter do you know least from experience — Deliverer, Way, Light, Keeper, Shepherd? Ask Him to show you that side of Himself.
3. Apply Moody’s test: think of the last person who wronged you. Did you treat them as your Master would?
4. Stop hunting for peace and joy this week. Turn your face to Christ in the Gospels each morning, and see what follows.
5. Read Christ All and in All in The Way to God, or the sermon Christ All in All, in the Ochorus library.

> Lord Jesus, forgive me for making You small. You are the Saviour, the Deliverer, the Redeemer, the Way, the Light, the Shepherd and the Keeper of my soul.
> Where I am still a slave, deliver me. Where I am in darkness, shine on me. Where I have wandered, carry me over.
> I confess that You are not only a good man but God manifest in the flesh, and I worship You.
> Be all to me, and in all, and let whatever light I have be borrowed from You. Amen.

# Prevailing Prayer

> Elias was a man subject to like passions as we are, and he prayed earnestly that it might not rain: and it rained not on the earth by the space of three years and six months. JAMES 5:17

Moody’s book Prevailing Prayer, subtitled What Hinders It?, is not a treatise. Like most of his books, it grew out of addresses, and it is full of other people’s voices: Puritans, Scottish preachers, hymn writers, and men and women he had met in the enquiry room. But its argument is his own, and it is one of the most practical books on prayer ever written for ordinary Christians. The next four chapters follow it.

## Men and women of prayer

The book opens with a sweeping claim. “Those who have left the deepest impression on this sin-cursed earth have been men and women of prayer.” Moody runs through the Bible to prove it: Abraham, Jacob at Peniel, Hannah, Elijah on Carmel, Elisha at the bedside of a dead child, Daniel, Paul and Silas singing at midnight in the prison at Philippi. And he takes great comfort in the verse from James that stands at the head of this chapter. Elijah was a man subject to like passions as we are. The people who were mighty in prayer were not a different species. They lived in darker days than ours and had the same weaknesses. Their God still lives, and we have the same access to Him.

## The praying Christ

The heart of Moody’s opening chapter is Christ Himself. “Every great crisis in His life was preceded by prayer.” He prayed at His baptism, and the heavens opened. He prayed on the mount of transfiguration, and His face was changed. He spent the whole night in prayer before He chose the twelve and preached the sermon on the mount. He prayed at the grave of Lazarus before He called him out.

> And it came to pass in those days, that he went out into a mountain to pray, and continued all night in prayer to God. LUKE 6:12

Moody draws the lesson for preachers and workers. If our words are to reach the hearts of people, we must first get power with God. We so often fail, he says, because we try to win people without first speaking to the Father.

He notices too that the disciples asked Jesus to teach them to pray, not to preach. “I have often said that I would rather know how to pray like Daniel than to preach like Gabriel.” It was a remarkable thing for the most famous preacher of his age to say.

> And it came to pass, that, as he was praying in a certain place, when he ceased, one of his disciples said unto him, Lord, teach us to pray, as John also taught his disciples. LUKE 11:1

## Short in public, long in secret

Moody makes a practical observation that will be welcome to anyone who has sat through a long prayer meeting. The Lord’s public prayers were short. The longest recorded, in John 17, can be read in a few minutes. When He was alone with the Father He might pray all night. “My experience is that those who pray most in their closets generally make short prayers in public.” The publican prayed seven words; the woman of Canaan said, Lord, help me; the dying thief asked only to be remembered. Moody’s counsel: let our prayers be to the point, just telling God what we want.

## Nine elements

Having shown that prayer is the great secret of spiritual power, Moody sets out what he calls the nine elements essential to true prayer. They give the book its shape, and it is worth having them in one place.

Adoration comes first, because we cannot meet God on a level. Confession follows, because sin must be put out of the way. Restitution means making good the wrong wherever we can. Thanksgiving is gratitude for what God has already done. Forgiveness is letting go of every grudge against others. Unity is love among God’s people. Faith is confidence that God hears. Petition is actually asking for something. And submission is readiness to accept God’s will, whatever the answer.

Moody did not mean these as nine steps to be taken in order every time one prays. The woman of Canaan did not work through a list. They are nine things that belong to a life of prayer, and nine places to look when prayer has gone dry. That is the force of the subtitle. If prayer seems to go unanswered, Moody wants us to ask what hinders it.

## Adoration

The first element is adoration, and Moody treats it seriously. He turns to Daniel’s great prayer, which begins not with a request but with God.

> And I prayed unto the LORD my God, and made my confession, and said, O Lord, the great and dreadful God, keeping the covenant and mercy to them that love him, and to them that keep his commandments. DANIEL 9:4

“Daniel took his right place before God—in the dust; he put God in His right place.” So did Isaiah, when he saw the Lord high and lifted up and the seraphim covering their faces. So did Job, when God finally spoke and he laid his hand on his mouth. So did Peter, who fell at Jesus’ knees and said, Depart from me, for I am a sinful man. “Holiness belongs to God; sinfulness belongs to us.”

> Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory. ISAIAH 6:3

Moody saw that much prayer is selfish at the root. We come to God for what He can give us. Adoration corrects that by beginning with who He is. He quotes Newman Hall comparing selfish prayers to the raven that went out from Noah’s ark and never came back, and prayers for God’s glory to the dove that returned with an olive leaf. And he thought the irreverence of his own day a sign of evil times. He wanted Christians to hallow God’s name before they asked for anything.

## Weighing the book

Prevailing Prayer is a book of conditions, and that is both its strength and its danger. Its strength is that it refuses to treat prayer as magic. Moody is right that unconfessed sin, unforgiveness and division choke prayer, and right that the Bible says so. Its danger is that an anxious reader may come away thinking that every unanswered prayer is his own fault, and that he must get all nine elements right before God will listen. Moody’s own final chapters correct that impression: God sometimes delays, sometimes says no out of love, and sometimes answers after we are dead. And the Father who taught us to pray is not an examiner. He is a Father, who hears the stammering prayer of a child. The nine elements are not a gate God keeps shut until we qualify. They are the shape of a life lived near Him.

> The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much. JAMES 5:16

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Look at the last week of your prayers. How much was asking, and how much was adoring God for who He is?
2. Before your next time of prayer, read Isaiah 6:1–8 and spend five minutes simply worshipping.
3. Moody said he would rather pray like Daniel than preach like Gabriel. What would change in your priorities if you believed that?
4. Keep your prayers in public short this month, and your prayers in private longer.
5. Read the opening chapter of Prevailing Prayer, The Prayers of the Bible, in the Ochorus library.

> O Lord, the great and dreadful God, keeping covenant and mercy with those who love You: I bow before You.
> Holiness belongs to You; sinfulness belongs to me. Forgive me for coming to You so often only for what You can give.
> Lord Jesus, You prayed before every great crisis of Your life. Teach me to pray as You prayed.
> Make me one who prevails with You in secret before I speak to anyone in public. Hallowed be Your name. Amen.

# Search Me, O God

> Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: And see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting. PSALM 139:23–24

After adoration, Moody’s next three elements of prayer belong together. Confession, restitution and thanksgiving all concern the heart that comes before God: its honesty about sin, its willingness to put things right, and its gratitude for what has already been given. None of them is exotic. All of them, Moody believed, are commonly neglected, and their neglect explains a great deal of powerless prayer.

## Confession begins at home

Moody opens his chapter on confession with a sentence aimed not at the unconverted but at the church. “I think we, as Christians, have a good many sins to confess.” The men who lived nearest to God, he observes, were those who confessed most deeply. Daniel, against whom nothing is recorded, made one of the humblest confessions in the Bible. Isaiah, seeing the Lord, cried that he was undone. Job, whom God Himself called perfect, abhorred himself in dust and ashes.

> I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid. I said, I will confess my transgressions unto the LORD; and thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin. PSALM 32:5

Moody was convinced that revival had to begin here. “I firmly believe that the Church of God will have to confess her own sins, before there can be any great work of grace.” He sometimes thought, he said, that it was time to stop preaching to the ungodly and preach to those who professed to be Christians. A higher standard of life in the church would bring thousands into the kingdom. And he said something even sharper: that the lukewarmness and formality of the church were making more unbelievers than all the books unbelievers ever wrote.

## The verse people stop reading

In the same chapter Moody points to a verse that, he says, is quoted in the wrong place ninety-nine times out of a hundred. People quote the first half of Isaiah 59:1 and stop.

> Behold, the LORD’S hand is not shortened, that it cannot save; neither his ear heavy, that it cannot hear: But your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you, that he will not hear. ISAIAH 59:1–2

Of course God’s hand is not shortened. The question is what we have put between ourselves and Him. Moody had a list of messages from parents, as many as fifty in one week, asking him to pray for their unconverted children. He did not doubt their love. But he wondered aloud whether, as a rule, the fault did not lie at their own door: some secret sin that was keeping back the blessing.

> If I regard iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me. PSALM 66:18

“Unconfessed sin is unforgiven sin,” he says, and he asks his readers to learn by heart David’s prayer that heads this chapter, and to pray it every day. Notice the pronoun: search me, not my neighbour. “It is so easy to pray for other people, but so hard to get home to ourselves.” And there is a difference, Moody adds, between God searching me and my searching myself. I may examine my heart and pronounce it sound; when God searches it as with a lighted candle, things come to light I never knew were there. Among them he names jealousy for our own reputation, and the sectarian spirit that cannot rejoice when another church is blessed.

## Restitution in prayer

The third element is restitution, which we met in the chapter on repentance. Moody returns to it in Prevailing Prayer because he found that it was the one subject he could never raise without immediate results. When he spoke of it, people left meetings and took trains to distant cities to repay old employers.

He tells of a woman in the north of England who could not find peace. At last she told a worker that whenever she knelt to pray, a few bottles of wine kept coming before her mind. Years before, as a housekeeper, she had taken them from her employer. He was dead; she did not know what they had been worth. But she found his son, took him more than enough to cover them, and came back, Moody says, one of the happiest people he had ever met.

> Therefore if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath ought against thee; Leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift. MATTHEW 5:23–24

Moody was blunt about religion that stopped short of this. “I am getting so tired and sick of your mere sentimentalism, that does not straighten out a man’s life.” Hymns and prayers are an abomination to God, he says, unless we are willing to be straightforward in our daily dealings. Nothing would give Christianity such a hold on the world as believers acting like Zacchaeus.

## Thanksgiving

The fourth element is gentler, and it is the one Moody thought most neglected. Ingratitude, he says, is about the hardest thing we have to meet with. Every parent knows how little pleasure there is in doing things for a child who never says thank you.

> Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. PHILIPPIANS 4:6

“There is a great deal more said in the Bible about praise than prayer; yet how few praise-meetings there are!” He points to the dedication of Solomon’s temple: Solomon prayed well, but it was when the singers and trumpeters were as one, praising the Lord, that the glory filled the house. And he notes that Paul, who suffered more than any apostle, gave thanks more than any, writing his most joyful letter to the city where he had been beaten and imprisoned.

> It came even to pass, as the trumpeters and singers were as one, to make one sound to be heard in praising and thanking the LORD … that then the house was filled with a cloud, even the house of the LORD. 2 CHRONICLES 5:13

Even if nothing else called for thanks, Moody says, it would always be enough that Christ loved us and gave Himself for us. He tells of a farmer found kneeling at a soldier’s grave near Nashville. The soldier had taken his place in the draft, when the farmer’s family were all sick, and had died of his wounds. The farmer had travelled many miles to write over the grave: He died for me.

## A caution

Moody’s teaching here is bracing, and most of us need it. But his readers should hold it with care. The link he draws between parents’ secret sins and their children’s unbelief is a real warning, not a rule; children make their own choices, and many godly parents have wept over prodigals without any hidden sin to blame. Self-examination is healthy when it ends at the cross. It becomes morbid when it never ends at all. Moody’s own counsel elsewhere applies: take a good look at the wound, and then fix your eyes on Christ.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Pray Psalm 139:23–24 slowly every morning this week, and write down anything God brings to mind.
2. Is there something that comes before your mind whenever you kneel to pray, as the bottles of wine did for that housekeeper? Deal with it.
3. Is there a church or Christian group you find it hard to see blessed? Pray for their blessing by name.
4. Keep a list of answered prayers and daily mercies for a month, and give thanks for them aloud.
5. Read the chapters Confession, Restitution and Thanksgiving in Prevailing Prayer, in the Ochorus library.

### A PRAYER

> Search me, O God, and know my heart. Not my neighbour’s heart, Lord; mine.
> Bring to light whatever stands between us, and give me grace to confess it plainly and put right what I can.
> Forgive me for my ingratitude. You have loaded me with benefits, and I have complained.
> Above all, thank You that Christ died for me. Let my prayers be full of praise, and let Your glory fill the house. Amen.

# As We Forgive

> And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. MATTHEW 6:12

Moody called forgiveness perhaps the most difficult of all the elements of prayer to deal with, and he placed unity beside it because both concern the same thing: how we stand toward other people when we come to God. He was convinced that more prayers are blocked here than anywhere else. “I believe this is keeping more people from having power with God than any other thing—they are not willing to cultivate the spirit of forgiveness.”

## The only petition Christ explained

Moody noticed that of all the petitions in the prayer Jesus taught His disciples, only one is explained by Him afterwards. It is this one.

> For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you: But if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses. MATTHEW 6:14–15

He did not know, he said, how language could be plainer. “When you go into the door of God’s kingdom, you go in through the door of forgiveness.” And he asked his readers to let their minds run back over the past and through the circle of their acquaintance. Is there someone against whom you are nursing a hard feeling? Perhaps for months, perhaps for years? How can you then ask God to forgive the ten thousand sins you have committed against Him?

Moody went further, following Christ to the altar. It is not only a question of whether we have something against someone else. Has someone something against us? Perhaps they only think we have wronged them. Moody’s counsel was characteristically direct: go and see them before you sleep tonight, and settle it. If you are in the right, you may still win your brother.

## Stories from the enquiry room

As usual, Moody made the point with people he had met. A husband once asked him to speak to his wife, who seemed deeply anxious about her soul. The longer they talked, the darker she became. At last Moody asked her to repeat the Lord’s Prayer with him, phrase by phrase. When they reached forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive them that trespass against us, she stopped. There was one woman, she said, whom she would never forgive. Moody told her plainly that her prayers would go no higher than his head.

In another enquiry room he was talking with a young woman who could not find peace, and asked whether there was someone she had not forgiven. She pointed across the room to another young woman with whom she had not spoken for a long time. It was a hard struggle. But at last she went, and it turned out that the other was fighting the same battle at the same moment. They met in the middle of the floor and could not finish their sentences; they simply embraced. The two of them and the two workers knelt together.

And in one town Moody and Sankey preached for a week to no effect. Moody said one day that perhaps someone was cultivating an unforgiving spirit. The chairman of the committee rose and walked out in front of everyone. He had been at odds with another man for six months. He found him, asked his forgiveness, and came back in tears. That night, Moody says, the enquiry room was thronged.

> And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you. EPHESIANS 4:32

## Unity

The next element, unity, is forgiveness written large. “If we do not love one another we certainly shall not have much power with God in Prayer.” Moody noticed that the Spirit came at Pentecost when the disciples were all with one accord in one place, and he did not believe the blessing would have come if they had been quarrelling. His own experience confirmed it. When three churches in a town united in a campaign there was more blessing than when one stood alone, and when twelve united, more again.

> And when the day of Pentecost was fully come, they were all with one accord in one place. ACTS 2:1

He was grieved by the divisions of the church, and he had fun with them. Suppose Paul and Peter came back, he says, and were told about Churchmen and Dissenters, and then about the Dissenters’ own divisions, and then about the divisions within the Church of England. Paul, who had rebuked the Corinthians for saying I am of Paul and I of Apollos, would hardly know what to say.

> Now I beseech you, brethren, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that ye all speak the same thing, and that there be no divisions among you; but that ye be perfectly joined together in the same mind and in the same judgment. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:10

“What we want to do is to get these miserable sectarian walls taken away.” Moody lived it. His meetings drew Anglicans, Presbyterians, Methodists, Baptists and Congregationalists onto the same platform, and he refused to be claimed by any one of them.

> By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another. JOHN 13:35

## Weighing the chapter

Moody is right that forgiveness is not optional for those who have been forgiven, and right that Christ tied our forgiving and God’s forgiving together in words that cannot be softened. The reader should notice what he is not saying. He is not saying that we earn God’s forgiveness by forgiving others. He is saying that a heart which refuses to forgive has not yet understood what it has received, and cannot receive more.

Two cautions are nevertheless in order. The first concerns the woman who would not forgive. Moody adds that he later heard she had been taken to an asylum, and that he believed her unforgiving spirit drove her there. That may be so, but it is a hard thing to say, and no one should use it to frighten the grieving or the mentally ill. Forgiveness of deep wounds is often a long road, walked with God’s help a step at a time, and the Lord who commands it is patient with those who are trying.

The second concerns unity. Moody’s longing to tear down sectarian walls was noble, and much of the division he mocked deserved it. But unity can be bought at the price of truth. In his later years some of his friends worried that his generosity led him to share the platform at Northfield with teachers whose views on Scripture were far from his own. The unity Christ prayed for is unity in the truth, and it takes both love and discernment to keep it.

> Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity! PSALM 133:1

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Let your mind run, as Moody asked, through the circle of your acquaintance. Is there anyone you have not forgiven? Name them before God.
2. Is there anyone who thinks you have wronged them? Before you sleep tonight, decide how you will go to them.
3. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly, and stop at the petition on forgiveness. Can you pray it honestly?
4. Pray this week for the blessing of a church of a different tradition from your own in your town.
5. Read the chapters Forgiveness and Unity in Prevailing Prayer, in the Ochorus library.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You have forgiven me ten thousand debts. How can I hold a hundred pence against my brother?
> I bring before You the one I have found hardest to forgive. I cannot do it alone; forgive through me.
> Show me anyone I have wronged, and give me the humility to go and be reconciled.
> Tear down the walls between Your people, keep us together in the truth, and let the world know by our love that we are Yours. Amen.

# Ask, Seek, Knock

> Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you. MATTHEW 7:7

The last three of Moody’s nine elements of prayer are faith, petition and submission, and the book ends with a chapter of answered prayers. Together they make a single movement: we believe God hears, we ask Him plainly for what we need, and we leave the answer in His hands. It is the simplest part of Prevailing Prayer, and perhaps the most needed.

## Faith, the golden key

Moody begins his chapter on faith by repeating that Jesus taught His disciples to pray, not to preach. He wanted them to have power with God first, knowing that then they would have power with men. And the key to that power is faith: “faith is the golden key that unlocks the treasures of heaven.”

> Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them. MARK 11:24

> But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering. For he that wavereth is like a wave of the sea driven with the wind and tossed. JAMES 1:6

He adds a warning: we must be in earnest. The prayers God answers are rarely polite. They are the cry of a man hanging over a pit. Moody tells the story of a traveller trapped on the lip of a volcanic crater, who prayed as he had never prayed before and was pulled out by the guide he had ignored. Faith is not a calm opinion about God. It is the grip of someone who knows he has nowhere else to go.

## Actually asking

The next element is petition, and Moody begins it with a complaint many of us will recognise. “How often we go to prayer-meetings without really asking for anything!” Prayers, he says, go all round the world without anything definite being requested. Some are exhortations with the eyes shut; some are complaints. He tells of an eloquent man leading a meeting in a long prayer with not one petition in it, until a poor woman called out: “Ask Him summat, man.”

Moody’s point is that God invites us to ask, and to ask again. Some people think God does not like to be troubled by constant requests. “The only way to trouble God is not to come at all.”

> And he spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint. LUKE 18:1

He distinguishes three kinds of Christians: those who ask, those who seek, and those who knock. The highest is the one who keeps knocking until the door opens. He tells of a boy who asked his teacher why so many knock and are not admitted. The teacher reminded him of boys who knock at a door on a dark evening and run away before anyone can answer. Jesus has never promised to answer runaway knocks. Too often, Moody says, we knock at mercy’s door and then run away, as if we were afraid of the answer.

## Knowing what to ask

Not every request will be granted, and Moody is clear about why. Some prayers go unanswered because of something in our lives that God wants brought to light. Others because we ask for the wrong reasons.

> Ye ask, and receive not, because ye ask amiss, that ye may consume it upon your lusts. JAMES 4:3

The promise that we shall ask what we will and have it, he points out, begins with a very large if: if ye abide in me, and my words abide in you (John 15:7). The latter half is often quoted, he says, and the first half forgotten. If Christ is truly in the heart and His word abides there, we will not ask for what is against His will. So we need to search the Scriptures to find warrant for our prayers.

## Submission

That leads to the ninth element. “I would a thousand times rather that God’s will should be done than my own.” Moody says that he knows God’s mind about spiritual things: God wills our sanctification, so we may ask for it with confidence. But in earthly matters, what we ask may not be His purpose, and we must be content to leave it with Him.

He takes great comfort from the prayers of good men that God did not answer. Moses prayed to go over Jordan and was refused; yet fifteen hundred years later he stood on the mount of transfiguration with Christ in the promised land. Elijah prayed to die under a juniper tree, and God refused him because He meant to take him up in a chariot of fire. Paul prayed three times for his thorn to be removed, and was given more grace instead.

> And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:9

A parent does not give a child everything he asks for. Moody tells of a small boy who cried as if his heart would break because his father would not give him a razor to whittle with. “I am afraid that there are a great many of us who are praying for razors.”

## Answered prayers

Prevailing Prayer closes with stories of answers, and they are some of the most moving pages Moody wrote. George Müller showed a man the name of someone he had prayed for daily for twenty-four years without an answer yet, but with a settled assurance that it would come. A mother in Connecticut prayed day after day for a son who went to war unconverted and died in hospital; years later a stranger who had served with him told her that he had died in peace, trusting Christ. A Scottish boy separated from his drunken father in America and converted in Chicago prayed to find his mother, and found that a letter from her had been waiting for him for seven years at the place he had run away from.

> Ah Lord GOD! behold, thou hast made the heaven and the earth by thy great power and stretched out arm, and there is nothing too hard for thee. JEREMIAH 32:17

“Nothing is too hard for God; that is a good thing to take for a motto.” And for those whose prayers seem unanswered, Moody has a word of hope. “I think we shall find a great many of our prayers that we thought unanswered answered when we get to heaven.”

> Call unto me, and I will answer thee, and shew thee great and mighty things, which thou knowest not. JEREMIAH 33:3

## A caution

Moody’s stories of answered prayer are true to his experience and wonderfully encouraging, but they are stories of answers, and stories of long silences are harder to tell. A reader who has prayed for years for a child or a healing may find such chapters painful. It is worth remembering that Moody himself put submission before the answered prayers, and that the book’s last word is not that God always gives what we ask, but that He can be trusted with what we ask. Faith is not certainty about the outcome. It is certainty about the One who decides it.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Look at your last few prayers. Did you actually ask God for anything definite? Write down one specific request and bring it daily for a month.
2. Are you a runaway knocker? Choose one matter you have given up praying about, and begin knocking again.
3. Test your requests against John 15:7. Is there anything you are asking for that you know is not His will?
4. Is there an unanswered prayer that has made you bitter? Offer it back to God with the words, Thy will be done.
5. Read the chapters Faith, Petition, Submission and Answered Prayers in Prevailing Prayer, in the Ochorus library.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You have told me to ask, to seek and to knock. Forgive my vague prayers and my runaway knocks.
> I bring You now what I most need, plainly, believing that You hear me.
> Where my request is not Your will, give me the grace to say, Thy will be done, and to believe that Your no is love.
> Nothing is too hard for You. Keep me knocking until the door opens, in this life or the next. Amen.

# Power from on High

> But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth. ACTS 1:8

By 1871 Moody was already a successful Christian worker. He had a large Sunday school, a church, a leading place in the Chicago YMCA, and a name for tireless energy. Then two women on the front bench told him they were praying that he would receive the power of the Holy Spirit. He was annoyed. He thought he had power. The story of how that annoyance turned to hunger, and the hunger was answered on a street in New York, is told in the chapter on his life. This chapter is about what he taught afterwards.

## The same sermons, a different result

Moody rarely described what happened to him, and never made it a pattern for others to copy. What he did describe was the difference it made. He went back to preaching, he said, and the sermons were not different, and he did not present any new truths; yet where before a handful had been converted, now they came by the hundreds. He would not, he said, be placed back where he was before for all the world.

The lesson he drew was not that he had become a better man, but that there is a difference between working for God in one’s own strength and working in the power of the Spirit. He never tired of saying that sermons alone would not reach the masses. In Prevailing Prayer he puts it plainly: the reason we so often fail to move people is that we try to win them without first getting power with God.

> Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts. ZECHARIAH 4:6

## In and upon

Moody set out his teaching on the Spirit in a book called Secret Power, and its central distinction is simple. He believed that every true Christian has the Holy Spirit dwelling within him; no one is born again without the Spirit, and whoever has not the Spirit of Christ is none of His. But he also believed there is a further gift of the Spirit coming upon a believer for service, which not every Christian has sought or received. The Spirit within is for life and holiness. The Spirit upon is for witness.

> Now if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his. ROMANS 8:9

He found this distinction in the difference between the disciples on the evening of the resurrection, when Jesus breathed on them and said, Receive ye the Holy Ghost, and the same disciples at Pentecost, when the Spirit came upon them with power and three thousand were converted. He found it too in the Lord’s command to the apostles to wait in Jerusalem.

> And, behold, I send the promise of my Father upon you: but tarry ye in the city of Jerusalem, until ye be endued with power from on high. LUKE 24:49

The life of the Spirit, on this view, is not a reservoir to be kept but a stream to be passed on. The Lord Himself promised that it would flow outward from those who believe.

> He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water. JOHN 7:38

## Power for others

The most important thing about Moody’s teaching on the Spirit is its direction. The power he sought was not a private blessing to be enjoyed, still less a badge of superior holiness. It was power to reach others. That is why the promise in Acts 1:8 ends with witnesses to the uttermost part of the earth. The Spirit comes upon people so that they may speak of Christ with effect.

> For our gospel came not unto you in word only, but also in power, and in the Holy Ghost, and in much assurance. 1 THESSALONIANS 1:5

This gives his teaching a healthy practicality. Moody did not encourage people to chase sensations. He encouraged them to be useful. The test of the Spirit’s power, for him, was not how a person felt but whether anyone was being brought to Christ.

## How it comes

How is this power received? Moody’s answer, drawn from his own experience and from Scripture, was by asking, earnestly and persistently.

> If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children: how much more shall your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him? LUKE 11:13

He added the conditions that run through all his teaching on prayer: a heart emptied of known sin and self, a willingness to be used, and a desire for God’s glory rather than our own reputation. The women who prayed for him in 1871 had seen that he was ambitious, busy and successful, and that something was missing. The power came when he stopped wanting to be a great man and wanted only God.

## A caution

Moody’s distinction between the Spirit within and the Spirit upon was taken up by his friend and colleague R. A. Torrey, who taught it as a definite baptism with the Holy Spirit to be sought after conversion. The language has been much debated since, and the reader should hold it with care. Many careful Christians believe that the New Testament speaks of one baptism of the Spirit at conversion, followed by many fillings. The danger in Moody’s way of speaking is that it can divide Christians into two classes, the ordinary and the empowered, and send the ordinary looking anxiously for an experience.

Moody himself guarded against much of this by his reticence. He did not tell people to seek what he had felt. He told them to seek power for service, and he spoke far more about the fruit than the experience. That is the right emphasis, whatever terms one prefers. Every believer has the Spirit. Every believer is commanded to be filled with the Spirit. And every believer who is honest will admit that there is more power available than he has yet known.

> And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit. EPHESIANS 5:18

The women on the front row are perhaps the best lesson in the whole story. They did not preach to Moody. They prayed for him, told him so, and kept praying.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is your work for God — at home, at church, at work — done mainly in your own strength? Where do you most sense the lack of power?
2. Read Acts 1–2 and notice what the Spirit was given for. Ask for that power, for that purpose.
3. What in your heart might be hindering the Spirit’s work: known sin, ambition, a desire for your own reputation? Confess it.
4. Pray daily this month, by name, for someone who preaches or teaches, that they would be filled with the Spirit — and tell them you are doing it.
5. Look for Moody’s Secret Power, and read it alongside Acts.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Spirit, I thank You that You dwell in every child of God, and that You dwell in me.
> Forgive me for working so much in my own strength, and praying so little for Yours.
> I ask the Father, as He has promised, to give me the Holy Spirit in power — not for my name, but that others may hear of Christ.
> Empty me of self, fill me with Yourself, and let rivers of living water flow out to the people You have put around me. Amen.

# Wholly Consecrated

> I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. ROMANS 12:1

Almost every account of Moody’s life includes a sentence he heard in Britain in 1867. The evangelist Henry Varley remarked, as the story is usually told, that the world had yet to see what God would do with and through a man fully consecrated to Him. Moody went away turning it over. It was not, he reflected, a great man that was needed, or a learned one, or an eloquent one. It was simply a man wholly given to God. And he resolved, by God’s grace, to be that man.

Whether the words were exactly as the story remembers them matters less than what they did. They gave Moody a lifelong conviction that the limit on God’s work is not God’s power, nor the size of the task, nor the gifts of the worker, but the degree to which the worker is surrendered.

> For the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him. 2 CHRONICLES 16:9

## Not great, but given

This was good news for a man like Moody. He knew he was not great or learned or eloquent. He had seen more gifted men achieve less. What he had to offer was himself, and Varley’s remark told him that this was what God wanted.

It is good news for most of us too. The Bible is full of people God used not because of their gifts but in spite of their lack of them. Moses said he was slow of speech. Gideon said he was the least in his father’s house. When the council saw that Peter and John were unlearned and ignorant men, they marvelled; and they took knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus (Acts 4:13).

> Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God. 2 CORINTHIANS 3:5

Moody had a phrase for it in The Way to God: “Real true faith is man’s weakness leaning on God’s strength.” The trouble, he adds, is that we have too much strength and confidence in ourselves.

## Everything on the altar

What does consecration mean in practice? For Moody, it meant first the giving up of his own plans. In 1860 he left a promising business and the hope of a fortune to give his whole time to Christian work, with no salary and no guarantee. He did it again in smaller ways all his life.

It meant, second, giving up whatever stood between the soul and God. In The Way to God he tells of a friend who, after coming to Christ, wanted to consecrate himself and his wealth to God, but found that God would not seem to take it. His conscience kept reminding him of an old dishonest transaction with the government. Only when he had sent back what he had taken did he find peace and blessing. Consecration is not a grand gesture made over the top of unconfessed sin. It is the laying of everything on the altar, including the things we would rather keep hidden.

> What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own? For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God’s. 1 CORINTHIANS 6:19–20

## Confessing and following

Consecration also meant, for Moody, being openly on Christ’s side. In his sermon on the eight I wills of Christ he dwells on the promise to those who confess Him before men. He had known many Christians grope in darkness for years because they were ashamed to confess the Son of God. “Don’t be ashamed, Christians, to let your friends, and even your enemies, know that you are on God’s side.”

> Whosoever therefore shall confess me before men, him will I confess also before my Father which is in heaven. MATTHEW 10:32

And it meant service. The next I will in that sermon is the call to Peter and Andrew: Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men. Moody took it as a promise to every believer. “There is no Christian who cannot help to bring some one to the Saviour.” It is no use, he added, following this man or that man. Follow Christ, and Him only, and He will make the fisher.

> And he saith unto them, Follow me, and I will make you fishers of men. MATTHEW 4:19

This was Moody’s own practice. His biographers record that for a long period he made it his rule not to let a day pass without speaking to someone about Christ. He built a Bible institute to train ordinary men and women for the same work, and he believed that the church’s great need was not more ministers but more consecrated lay people.

## Kept humble

Moody knew the danger that lurks in consecration: pride. The man who gives himself to God and sees God use him is always tempted to think himself remarkable. In the chapter on backsliding in The Way to God, Moody warns that most men who fall do so on the strongest side of their character, and that if anyone gets self-conceited, even in the pulpit, he will be sure to fall. He noted that Moses did not know his face shone. And he drew a picture from home: a little brook near his house that roared after a storm and fell silent in dry weather, and a deep river that he never once heard, flowing steadily all year round. “It is not those who make the most noise who have the most piety.”

> He must increase, but I must decrease. JOHN 3:30

## Weighing the ideal

Varley’s sentence has inspired many, and it is worth weighing honestly. It can be heard as a call to spiritual heroism, as though God were waiting for one exceptional individual. That is not how the New Testament usually speaks. God’s work is done through a body, not a solitary champion, and Moody’s own ministry depended on Sankey, on his wife, on praying women, on thousands of unnamed workers. Consecration can also be misunderstood as a single dramatic transaction after which everything is settled. For Moody it was more like a direction of life, renewed again and again.

It is fair, too, to notice that Moody drove himself very hard, and that his heart gave out in the middle of a campaign at sixty-two. Consecration does not require us to ignore the body God gave us or the rest He commands. The living sacrifice of Romans 12 is still living.

But when every qualification has been made, the core of it stands. God is not looking for the gifted. He is looking for the given.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. What would it mean for you to be fully consecrated to God? Name one thing you are still holding back.
2. Is there anything unconfessed or unrestored that makes your consecration feel hollow, as it did for Moody’s friend? Deal with it first.
3. Are you ashamed to let anyone know you are a Christian? This week, tell one person plainly whose side you are on.
4. Ask God to show you one person you can help toward the Saviour, and speak to them before the week is out.
5. Ask yourself whether you have confused consecration with overwork. Is there rest you are refusing?

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I am not great or learned or eloquent, and You do not ask me to be. You ask me to be Yours.
> I lay on Your altar my plans, my money, my reputation and the things I would rather keep hidden.
> Make me unashamed of You before my friends and my enemies, and make me a fisher of men.
> Keep me humble when You use me, keep me resting when You bid me rest, and let my life be a living sacrifice to the end. Amen.

# Heaven, Our Home

> In my Father’s house are many mansions: if it were not so, I would have told you. I go to prepare a place for you. JOHN 14:2

Moody spoke about heaven the way a traveller speaks about home. He wrote a book on it, Heaven, gathered from addresses in which he asked where heaven is, who lives there and how to get there, and the subject runs through nearly everything else he preached. He had buried people he loved, and he had sat beside thousands of the dying, in hospitals during the war and in the homes of his congregations. Heaven was not a pious ornament to him. It was the place where the broken circle would be made whole.

## What makes heaven attractive

Moody had a clear answer to the question of what makes heaven desirable, and it was not the furniture. In the opening chapter of The Way to God he asks whether it is the pearly gates or the golden streets. No, he says. He tells of a little girl in Brooklyn who, after her mother’s death, ran from room to room of the house calling for her, and, when she could not find her, asked to be taken back to the neighbours’. The house without the mother was not home. “So what makes heaven attractive is the thought that we shall see Christ who has loved us and given Himself for us.”

> Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is. 1 JOHN 3:2

That is the centre of Moody’s hope. Heaven is where Christ is. He found it in the last of the eight I wills of Christ, from the prayer in the upper room.

> Father, I will that they also, whom thou hast given me, be with me where I am; that they may behold my glory, which thou hast given me: for thou lovedst me before the foundation of the world. JOHN 17:24

## The broken circle

Moody was at his tenderest when he spoke of heaven to the bereaved. When we lay our little children in the grave, he says in the same sermon, it is in sorrow but not without hope; the happy circle broken on earth will be completed again. Christ gave three specimens of His power over death, as a tradesman puts his best goods in the window: Jairus’s daughter, the widow’s son at Nain, and Lazarus. They were given so that every doubt might be swept from our hearts.

> And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. REVELATION 21:4

Of that country he said simply that death is banished from it. Sickness and pain and sorrow do not come there. God’s family will all be together.

## An enemy made a friend

Moody confessed that as a boy in Northfield death had terrified him. When someone died in the village the bell would toll out his age, and young Dwight would count the strokes, and shudder when the number was close to his own. In his sermon on the word gospel, he says that the gospel took that enemy out of his path. If a man is in Christ, he says, let death come. To be absent from the body is to be present with the Lord.

> O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? 1 CORINTHIANS 15:55

And he loved to tell of the thief on the cross, condemned in the morning by men as too vile for earth and received in the evening as fit for heaven. “Christ was not ashamed to walk down the crystal pavement of heaven with him.”

He died as he had preached. Those at his bedside in December 1899 remembered him saying that earth was receding and heaven opening, that this was his triumph and his coronation day. Months before, he is remembered to have told an audience that when they read in the papers that Moody was dead, they should not believe it, for he would be more alive than ever.

## Only by the new birth

Moody never spoke of heaven without speaking of the way there. In the chapter on the new birth in The Way to God, he says that you may see many beautiful countries, rivers, crowns and mansions in this world, but you will never see the river that flows from the throne of God, or the crown of life, or the mansions Christ has gone to prepare, unless you are born again. Heaven, he said repeatedly, is for those whom God has made fit for it.

> Jesus saith unto him, I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me. JOHN 14:6

## The coming King

Moody’s hope of heaven was joined to a lively expectation of Christ’s return. He believed that the Lord might come at any moment, that the world would not be converted before He came, and that the church’s task until then was to rescue as many as possible. In his sermon on Noah he says that the time is coming again when God will deal with the world in judgement. He is remembered for comparing the world to a wrecked vessel, and saying that God had given him a lifeboat and told him to save all he could.

> Watch therefore: for ye know not what hour your Lord doth come. MATTHEW 24:42

This expectation gave Moody’s preaching an edge that no one could miss, and it is a biblical expectation. The New Testament does teach that Christ will return, suddenly, and that we are to live ready. But the lifeboat picture needs careful handling. It can suggest that the world God made is only a wreck to be abandoned, and that caring for the poor, for justice, for good work and good learning is so much rearranging of deckchairs. Moody himself did not act that way: he built schools, raised relief for his burned-out city, and cared deeply for the education of the poor. His practice was wider than his picture. The better picture is the one Scripture gives: not a wreck to be abandoned, but a creation that groans, waiting for its redemption.

> Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God. ROMANS 8:21

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. What would make heaven heaven for you? If the honest answer is not Christ, what does that reveal?
2. Is there someone you have lost in the Lord? Read 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 and give thanks for the circle that will be completed.
3. Are you afraid of death? Take 1 Corinthians 15:54–57 and read it aloud until the fear is answered.
4. Moody’s lifeboat picture gave him urgency. How would your week change if you believed Christ might return before it ended?
5. Ask whether your hope of heaven has made you careless of this world. Do one concrete good this week for a neighbour’s body as well as his soul.

# Come Thou into the Ark

> And the LORD said unto Noah, Come thou and all thy house into the ark; for thee have I seen righteous before me in this generation. GENESIS 7:1

Every chapter of this book has ended with application points, because Moody never let a truth rest in the head. This chapter is about why. Behind the urgency that marked his whole ministry lay one night in Chicago that he never forgot.

## The night of the fire

On the eighth of October, 1871, Moody preached to the largest congregation he had yet gathered in Chicago on Pilate’s question: What shall I do then with Jesus which is called Christ? (Matthew 27:22). At the end he did something he had often done. He told the people to take the question home, think it over, and come back the next Sunday to decide. Sankey began to sing, and before he had finished, the fire bells were ringing across the city. By morning much of Chicago was in ashes, Moody’s hall and home among it. He said afterwards that he never saw that congregation together again, and that some of them, he believed, did not live to decide.

He told the story many times in later years, and always with the same resolve. He would never again give an audience a week to think about Christ. He would rather, he said, have his right hand cut off.

> Boast not thyself of to morrow; for thou knowest not what a day may bring forth. PROVERBS 27:1

## The door God shut

The sermon on Noah in the Ochorus library is Moody’s fullest treatment of this theme. He describes the hundred and twenty years in which Noah built and preached and was mocked: the scoffers who said there was no God, the clever men who said God was too merciful to judge, the practical men who said they could climb the hills if the water rose, the business men who signed long leases and laughed. Then the animals came in two by two, and still the people went back to their work. And then, Moody says, God Himself shut the door.

> And they that went in, went in male and female of all flesh, as God had commanded him: and the LORD shut him in. GENESIS 7:16

“God always precedes judgment with grace.” For a hundred and twenty years every stroke of Noah’s hammer was a warning and an invitation. But grace has a day, and the day ends. Twenty-four hours before the rain, Moody says, the ark would not have fetched the price of kindling at auction. Twenty-four hours after, it was worth more than the whole world.

> For he saith, I have heard thee in a time accepted, and in the day of salvation have I succoured thee: behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation. 2 CORINTHIANS 6:2

## Too late

Moody’s sermons are full of people who waited. There was the man he pleaded with for five years, who always said it had not struck him yet, and who died still waiting to be struck. There was the young soldier whose father had begged him to become a Christian, and who always said he would when the war was over. He was wounded and lay dying in hospital when a letter came from his sister asking him to accept her Saviour. A comrade read it to him. He sprang up on his cot, and then fell back saying it was too late.

> To day if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts. HEBREWS 3:15

Moody did not tell these stories to frighten people for the sake of it. He told them because he believed the Bible gives no second chance after death, and because he loved the people in front of him too much to let them go home undecided.

## All thy house

The text of the Noah sermon has a phrase Moody would not let his hearers miss: thou and all thy house. The call was not only to Noah but to his family. And Moody turned it on the parents in his congregation. Are your children in the ark? He had learned, from years of running a Sunday school for children from godless homes, how little can be done for a child whose parents are against the gospel. “What we want is godly homes.”

> But as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD. JOSHUA 24:15

He had no patience with the idea that children must grow up before they can be converted, and he warned against letting them drift until they were beyond reach. “Don’t rest day or night until you get your children in.” And he told of two fathers: one who had given his life to making money and could not pray when his dying son asked him to, and another whose little boy died saying he would tell Jesus that his father had prayed for him every day.

## Weighing the urgency

Moody was right that the gospel is urgent, and right that delay is one of the devil’s oldest tools. His great contribution was to insist that the invitation of Christ is for now, not for some more convenient season.

But the methods that grew from this conviction need care. Moody’s campaigns pressed for immediate decision, and the enquiry room gathered those who had been stirred. Much lasting fruit came of it. Yet the pressure of a crowded hall, a moving hymn and an earnest preacher can produce decisions that are more about the moment than about Christ, and Moody knew that not all his converts lasted. The urgency of the gospel is not the same as the urgency of a meeting. A person who needs a week to count the cost is not necessarily resisting God, and those who follow Moody’s pattern should take care not to hurry people past real repentance for the sake of a result. The door is God’s to shut, not ours.

With that said, the core of Moody’s urgency stands. God is patient, but His patience is for our salvation, not for our procrastination.

> The Lord is not slack concerning his promise, as some men count slackness; but is longsuffering to us-ward, not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance. 2 PETER 3:9

1. Is there anything God has asked of you that you have been taking home to think about for years? Decide it today.
2. If you have never given yourself to Christ, do not wait for next Sunday. Tell Him now, in your own words, that you are coming into the ark.
3. Are your children, grandchildren or others in your care in the ark? Pray for each by name, and speak to one of them this week.
4. Think of someone you have meant to speak to about Christ for a long time. Do it before this week ends.
5. Read Moody’s sermon Come Thou and All Thy House into the Ark, in the Ochorus library.

> Today I hear Your voice, and I will not harden my heart. I come into the ark.
> Bring my house in with me: my children and those I love. Give me the courage to speak before it is too late.

# Conclusion

> I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith. 2 TIMOTHY 4:7

Seventeen chapters can make it look as though Moody taught a great many things. He taught one thing, over and over, to anyone who would listen: God loves you, Christ has died for you, and the door is open now. Everything else was that message turned to the light at a different angle. The fall is why we need it. The blood is what it cost. The new birth is what it does. Faith is how we take it, repentance is the turning it requires, and assurance is the peace it brings. The Bible is where we hear it, Christ is its whole content, prayer is how we live in it, the Spirit is the power to pass it on, consecration is the life it asks for, heaven is where it ends, and urgency is what it feels like to believe it while other people do not.

## The danger of this book

Moody would have been uneasy about a book that summarised him. He distrusted religion that was admired rather than acted on, and a book like this one can easily become an agreeable way of reading about repentance, prayer and consecration without doing any of them.

> Be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

So the counsel given in the other volumes of this series applies here with special force. Go back to the chapter that made you least comfortable. For some it will be restitution; for others forgiveness, or the questions about their children, or the plain call to decide. Take its application points and live with them for a month. Moody measured a sermon by what people did afterwards, and so should we.

## Two things to carry

First, the love of God. Moody spent the first years of his Christian life thinking of God as a stern judge whom Christ had to pacify, and the rest of it undoing that picture in other people. If you take one thing from this book, let it be his conviction that God is not waiting to punish you but waiting to receive you. Everything else he taught rests on that.

Second, today. Moody learned in one night in Chicago that tomorrow is not ours. He could not bear to let anyone go home without a decision. You do not need to be an evangelist to carry that lesson. It applies to the apology you have not made, the restitution you have put off, the prayer you have not prayed and the conversation about Christ you have been meaning to have for years.

> Seek ye the LORD while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near. ISAIAH 55:6

## A last word about him

We have been honest about where Moody must be read with care. His simplicity sometimes made doctrine and the church look like obstacles rather than gifts. The methods of mass revival that he shaped could hurry people toward a decision that did not last. His expectation of Christ’s return, right in itself, could make the world look like a wreck to be abandoned rather than a creation waiting to be redeemed. His strong sentences on assurance, restitution and forgiveness can land heavily on tender consciences. And his stories, many of them heard second-hand, are illustrations rather than records.

None of this has been said to diminish him. He would have said it himself. He knew he was an unschooled man who had been turned away by a church committee, and he never asked anyone to follow him. What made him remarkable was not his gifts but his availability. He believed the Bible meant what it said, he gave himself to God without reserve, and God used him to bring more people to Christ than almost anyone of his century.

> For ye see your calling, brethren, how that not many wise men after the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble, are called. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:26

He is buried on a small hill at Northfield, above the school he founded for young people who could not afford one elsewhere. It is a fitting place for a shoe clerk who could not spell. The God who chose him has not changed, and is still looking for men and women who will simply be wholly His.

1. Of all the chapters in this book, which made you least comfortable? Return to it and work its application points for a month.
2. Write down what you now believe God feels toward you. Compare it with what you would have written before reading this book.
3. Name one thing you have been postponing, whether a decision, an apology or a conversation, and do it this week.
4. Choose one of Moody’s own books and read it through slowly, with the Bible open.
5. Pray daily for a month for one person you long to see come to Christ, and look for the moment to speak.

> Father, I have read of Your love, and I am capable of admiring it and doing nothing. Keep me from that.

# A Reader’s Guide to Dwight L. Moody

> Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. 2 TIMOTHY 2:15

Moody is one of the easiest Christian writers to read and one of the hardest to read well. Almost everything published under his name began as spoken addresses, taken down by reporters or edited by friends, and it keeps the rhythm of a man talking to a crowd: short sentences, questions, stories, appeals. That makes him wonderfully accessible. It also means that his books repeat one another, that his illustrations are often second-hand, and that he is at his best in small doses. Read him for warmth and clarity, not for system.

## Begin here

The Way to God, in the Ochorus library. The best single introduction, and the source of most of chapters three to eleven of this volume. Its chapters on the love of God, the new birth, repentance and restitution, and assurance are Moody at his clearest. Read Love That Passeth Knowledge first.

Prevailing Prayer: What Hinders It?, in the Ochorus library. The source of chapters twelve to fifteen. A practical book on the nine elements of prayer, full of quotations from Puritans and Scottish divines and stories from the enquiry room. Read the chapter on forgiveness slowly.

## His sermons

Eleven of Moody’s sermons are in the Ochorus library, among them The New Birth, Christ All in All, One Word — Gospel, Come Thou and All Thy House into the Ark, The Dying Thief and Eight I Wills of Christ. They are the best way to hear his actual voice: vivid, repetitive, tender and urgent. Many more were gathered in his lifetime into collections such as Twelve Select Sermons, Sovereign Grace and The Overcoming Life, which are widely available.

## On particular themes

Secret Power, on the Holy Spirit and power for service, the source of chapter sixteen. Heaven, on where heaven is, who is there and how to get there, drawn on in chapter eighteen. Weighed and Wanting, addresses on the Ten Commandments, useful for seeing how Moody used the law to convict. Pleasure and Profit in Bible Study, short and practical, on reading the Bible for oneself. To the Work! To the Work!, addresses urging ordinary Christians into personal work.

Thoughts for the Quiet Hour, in the Ochorus library, is a book of daily readings that Moody gathered, not wrote. Most of its entries are from other writers, some named and some not. It is a window into what fed him, and a good devotional companion, but it should not be quoted as Moody’s own words.

## About him

The Life of Dwight L. Moody by his son William R. Moody (1900) is the official life, full of letters and first-hand detail, and naturally admiring. Henry Drummond’s short tribute, published after Moody’s death, is a warm portrait by a close friend of very different temperament. R. A. Torrey’s little book Why God Used D. L. Moody (1923) is the testimony of the man he chose to lead his Chicago institute. Among modern lives, John Pollock’s Moody Without Sankey (1963) is lively and readable; James F. Findlay’s Dwight L. Moody: American Evangelist, 1837–1899 (1969) is the standard scholarly study, fair and thorough; and Lyle W. Dorsett’s A Passion for Souls (1997) is a sympathetic and careful Christian biography. Read at least one of the older lives and one of the modern ones. The first show what he meant to those who loved him; the second set him in his world.

## Approach with care

Moody’s addresses on the second coming, found in several collections, show the source of his urgency, but also the pessimism about the world that chapter eighteen weighs. His many stories should be read as illustrations; a number came to him second-hand and cannot now be checked. And his books, being edited talks, sometimes overstate for effect; a sentence that works from a platform can press too hard on the page.

## Three counsels for reading him

Read him with the Bible open. Moody’s whole method was to send people to the text, and he would want you to check every verse he quotes.

Read the stories for their point, not their details. They are there to open a door, not to be history lessons.

And read him with a decision in view. Moody never spoke without asking his hearers to do something before they left. One chapter acted on today will honour him more than the whole shelf admired.

> But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name. JOHN 20:31
