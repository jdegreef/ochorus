# Introduction

> I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing. JOHN 15:5

Andrew Murray spent almost his whole life at the southern end of Africa, in a church that most of the Christian world had never heard of, preaching in Dutch to farmers and villagers. And yet for more than a century his small books have been read on every continent. He wrote something like two hundred and forty books and tracts, and the best of them are still in print, still being translated, and still doing their quiet work in people who have no idea where Wellington is.

## One word beneath all the others

The reason is not his style; he circles a truth again and again, like a man walking round a building. The reason is that he had grasped one thing with unusual clarity, and he spent sixty years saying it. The thing is dependence.

A creature, Murray taught, has nothing in itself. It does not merely owe its beginning to God; it owes every moment of its continuance to Him. What is true of the body is true of the soul. The Christian life is not a stock of grace handed over at conversion and then managed by the believer. It is a life received moment by moment from Christ, as a branch receives its sap from the vine. Everything else he wrote follows from this. Humility is simply the creature taking its true place. Surrender is consenting to be what we already are. Waiting on God is dependence made into a habit. Prayer is dependence speaking. Holiness is the life of the Holy One flowing into an emptied vessel. Abiding is the whole of it gathered into a single word from the lips of Christ.

> For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen. ROMANS 11:36

That is the thread running through every chapter of this book, and it is the question to keep asking as you read: am I living as a branch, or as a tree of my own planting?

## Why he still matters

Murray is worth reading for at least three reasons. The first is that he takes the words of Christ at their plain meaning. When Jesus says, Without me ye can do nothing, Murray does not reach for a qualification. He asks what would happen if a believer actually believed it.

The second is that he wrote about prayer as a man who prayed. His books on it read like letters from someone who has come out of the room and is urging you to go in.

The third is that he is profoundly Christ-centred. For all his talk of surrender and waiting and consecration, his real subject is never the believer. It is Jesus Himself, the Vine, the Intercessor, the Holy One of God, who does in us what we cannot do.

## How this book is arranged

After a chapter on his life, eighteen short chapters set out his teaching. They begin with abiding in Christ and the vine, move through dependence, humility, surrender, the Spirit, holiness, obedience, love and healing, and end with the six themes nearest his heart: waiting on God, the inner chamber, the school of prayer, believing prayer, the sin of prayerlessness, and intercession for the world.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. Murray would have wanted it so. Nearly every chapter he ever wrote ends in a prayer, and during the years when he could not speak he dictated his meditations and prayed their closing lines aloud before they were written down. He did not think truth had done its work until it had driven the reader to his knees. Read a chapter a day, or one a week. Stop at the application points. Pray the prayer, or better, pray your own.

## Where he must be read with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and it is better to say plainly at the start where Murray must be weighed rather than simply received.

His book Divine Healing, which grew out of his own recovery in 1882, teaches that healing belongs to the redemption Christ purchased and presents healing without medical means as the higher way of faith. The same book gives thanks for doctors and allows that God often blesses remedies, and Murray never used his teaching as a stick to beat the sick. But parts of it go further than Scripture warrants, and chapter fourteen sets out what he taught and where it must be qualified. Please read that chapter with its cautions rather than without them.

Second, Murray preached for decisive surrender, and some of his addresses call for a single crisis in which everything is given up and a new kind of life begins. For many people that call has been the doorway to real freedom. For others — often the most conscientious — it has become a burden: an endless inspection of whether the surrender was absolute enough. His framework of carnal and spiritual Christians is also disputed by careful readers of the same passages. This book presents these teachings as he gave them, draws out what is true in them, and says where they need a gentler hand.

Third, Murray lived and worked in colonial South Africa, as a minister of a settler church, and on questions of race and empire he did not stand outside the assumptions of his time and place. It would be dishonest to make him either a prophet against the injustices of that society or their architect. He should be read as the man he was.

Why read him, then? Because on the central matter he was right. We are branches. We can do nothing without Christ, and with Him we can bear much fruit. The counsel of this book is the counsel of Scripture: Prove all things; hold fast that which is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Where Murray is faithful — on abiding, on humility, on prayer, on the all-sufficiency of Christ — receive it gladly and act on it. Where he presses beyond what is written, set it aside and lose nothing.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Before going further, ask honestly whether you live more like a branch drawing its life from the Vine or like a tree trying to grow on its own roots.
2. Choose a pace for this book — a chapter a day or a chapter a week — and decide now when you will read it.
3. Write down one area of your life in which you are relying on your own strength and calling it faithfulness.
4. Resolve to test everything in these pages against Scripture, keeping what is true and setting aside what is not.
5. Ask God to use this book to send you not to Murray but to Christ, and to your knees.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You are the true Vine, and I am a branch. Without You I can do nothing, though I have often lived as if I could do a great deal.
> Teach me what Your servant Andrew Murray learned: to depend on You moment by moment, to take the lowest place, and to pray.
> Keep me from admiring a teacher and missing his Master. Where he is faithful to Your Word, let me receive it; where he is not, give me discernment.
> And let every page I read end where he wished his pages to end, in fellowship with You. Amen.

# A Voice from the Veld

> The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much. JAMES 5:16

A praying household. Andrew Murray was born on the ninth of May, 1828, at Graaff-Reinet, a small town on the edge of the Karoo in the eastern Cape Colony. His father, also Andrew Murray, was a Scot who had come out to serve as minister of the Dutch Reformed congregation there; his mother, Maria Stegmann, was born at the Cape, of German and French Huguenot descent. The household was large, devout and disciplined. The elder Murray had known the revivals of Scotland, and it was his habit to read accounts of them aloud and to pray that God would pour out His Spirit upon South Africa. The boy grew up hearing that prayer. He could not have known that he would live to see it answered.

A boy sent across the sea. In 1838, when he was ten, Andrew and his elder brother John were sent to Scotland to be educated, and lodged with an uncle, a minister in Aberdeen. It was a long separation, and a hard thing for a family to do, though a common one for colonial families of that kind. Scotland was then alive with evangelical awakening, and the boys saw it at close quarters; the preaching of the revivalist William Chalmers Burns made a lasting impression on the younger brother. Both took their degrees at Marischal College, Aberdeen, in 1845, and went on to the University of Utrecht in the Netherlands to study theology, since they were destined for the Dutch Reformed ministry at home.

Assurance at Utrecht. Utrecht gave him more than a training. He found there a circle of students touched by the Réveil, a movement of evangelical renewal in the Dutch churches, and it was during those years that his own faith came to settled assurance. He had grown up religious and had first thought of the ministry as a natural calling for a minister’s son. Now he wrote home to his parents that he had cast himself on Christ. In May 1848, on his twentieth birthday, he and John were ordained at The Hague, and they sailed for the Cape.

Beyond the Orange River. His first charge was vast. He was sent to Bloemfontein, the first settled minister of his church beyond the Orange River, responsible for the scattered farming communities of the trekkers across an enormous territory. He was barely twenty-one. He spent much of his life in the saddle, riding for days between farms to preach, baptise, catechise, marry and bury, among people who might not see a minister for a year. It was a hard school for a young man with a scholar’s training. It taught him endurance, and it taught him how little a minister can do in his own strength.

Emma. In 1856 he married Emma Rutherford of Cape Town. It was a long and deeply happy marriage, and she became his closest partner in the work; in later years, when writing was a burden to him, it was often she or one of their daughters who took down his books from dictation. They had eleven children, and most of them lived to serve the church in one way or another.

Worcester, 1860. In 1860 Murray accepted a call to Worcester, a town in the Breede River valley, and there his father’s prayers were answered. The revival that broke out that year did not begin with him. It grew out of prayer meetings held by ordinary believers, and when it came it came with a force that alarmed him. The story is told that at one meeting the people broke out all together in prayer for mercy, and the young minister, anxious for good order, tried to quiet them, until he was warned that it was God who was at work. Murray learned the lesson. He did not manufacture the revival and he did not quench it. He shepherded it, and he spent the rest of his ministry caring for the kind of believers it produced: people newly awakened, earnest, and in danger of cooling into formality.

Abide in Christ. Out of that pastoral care came the books. The first of the famous ones, Abide in Christ, was written for young believers who had begun well and wondered how to continue; it appeared in Dutch and then in English, and it was followed by a steady stream of others. In 1864 he moved to the Groote Kerk in Cape Town, the mother church of his denomination, at a time of sharp controversy over liberal theology in the Dutch Reformed Church, and he stood firmly on the side of the older faith. In 1871 he accepted a call to Wellington, a small town in the wine country, and stayed for thirty-five years.

Wellington. It was at Wellington that Murray’s life widened. In 1874, inspired by the example of Mary Lyon’s seminary at Mount Holyoke in America, he founded the Huguenot Seminary for the education of girls and young women, and brought teachers from America to staff it. A training institute for mission workers followed. He served his church as moderator of its synod several times, and few men did more to turn it outward in mission, both to the African peoples of South Africa and northward beyond its borders. In 1889 he helped to found what became the South Africa General Mission. He spoke at conventions across the colony, and increasingly abroad — in Britain, at Keswick in 1895, and in America the same year, where the addresses later gathered as Absolute Surrender were first given.

The silent years. His own health was often poor, and in 1879 his voice failed. For about two years he could not preach. He was a preacher who could not preach, and he felt it. It was during this time that he began to seek the teaching on divine healing then spreading among evangelicals, and in 1882 he went to a healing home in London. He returned restored and convinced that healing belonged within the provision of Christ’s redemption, and his book Divine Healing followed. In 1895, while in England, he wrote a short note for his own comfort that has comforted thousands since, under the heading In time of trouble say. It sets out four things: that God brought him into the strait place, that God would keep him there in His love, that God would make the trial a blessing, and that in His own time God could bring him out again. A carriage accident in 1893 had left him with a permanent injury to his back, and in old age he often preached seated.

The war years. The Anglo-Boer War of 1899 to 1902 tore through the country he loved and divided people he had served all his life. His sympathies lay with the Afrikaner people among whom he had ministered since his youth, and he grieved over the war and its suffering. He was by then an old man. His books from these years are full of the call to intercession and to a deeper dependence on God.

The last years. He retired from the Wellington pulpit in 1906 but did not stop writing; some of his most searching books on prayer, including The Prayer Life, came after that. He died at Wellington on the eighteenth of January, 1917, in his eighty-ninth year. He left no great fortune and wanted no monument. He left schools, missions, a church far more missionary than the one he had been ordained into, and a shelf of small books that have taught believers in every part of the world to abide in Christ, to take the low place, and to pray.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Murray’s father prayed for years for a revival he did not live to lead. Name one thing you have stopped praying for because the answer was slow, and take it up again.
2. The Worcester revival began in the prayers of ordinary believers. Ask whether you are waiting for someone more gifted to pray what God has laid on you.
3. Murray learned not to quench a work of God because it was untidy. Where might your love of order be resisting God?
4. In his silent years he could not do the work he was trained for. If you are in a strait place, read the four sentences of In time of trouble say and apply them honestly.
5. Choose one of Murray’s own books in the Ochorus library and read its first chapter this week.

> Lord God, You heard a father’s prayers in a frontier town and answered them in his son. Teach me to pray long and not lose heart.
> You brought Your servant through loneliness, hard journeys, silence and pain, and You were faithful in them all.
> If You have brought me into a strait place, keep me there in Your love, teach me what You mean to teach, and bring me out in Your own time.
> Make me a branch that bears fruit I shall never see. Amen.

# Abide in Christ

> Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me. JOHN 15:4

If you asked Andrew Murray to put the Christian life into two words, he would give you the two words Jesus used on the night before He died: Abide in me. His first famous book was called Abide in Christ, and he wrote it for young believers after the Worcester revival who had begun well and did not know how to go on. He returned to the theme in The True Vine, in Absolute Surrender, in The Master’s Indwelling, and in nearly everything else. It is the root of all his teaching, and the best place to begin.

## Begun well, and then what

The problem Murray set out to answer is one every pastor knows. A person comes to Christ with joy. For a season everything is fresh. Then the freshness fades, sin returns in old forms, prayer becomes dutiful, and the believer concludes either that the beginning was not real or that the Christian life is simply a long struggle with occasional relief. Murray thought both conclusions were wrong. The trouble was not that such believers had received too little at the start. It was that they did not understand how the life they had received is meant to continue.

> As ye have therefore received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk ye in him. COLOSSIANS 2:6

We received Christ by faith, in weakness, trusting Him to do what we could not. Murray’s point was that we are to go on exactly as we began. The believer who was saved by trusting Christ is not then handed a set of resources and told to manage. He is to keep on trusting, keep on receiving, keep on coming. The life is in the Vine, not in the branch.

## What a branch does

In an address to Christian workers, printed at the end of Absolute Surrender under the title Ye are the Branches, Murray asks his hearers to consider how simple it is to be a branch. A branch, he says, “has no responsibility except just to receive from the root and stem sap and nourishment.” It does not strain to produce grapes. It does not worry about the soil or the rain. It stays where it is, joined to the vine, and the life of the vine flows through it and comes out as fruit.

> I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing. JOHN 15:5

Murray drew three lessons from this. The first is absolute dependence: the branch has nothing of its own. The second is deep rest: a branch that is simply receiving is not anxious. The third is fruitfulness: a branch that abides will bear fruit, not by effort but by union. He was not describing laziness. The branch is fully employed. But it is employed in receiving, and its whole activity is the outflow of a life that is not its own.

He applied this directly to Christian workers, and his words are still uncomfortable. Many who labour for Christ, he observed, complain that their work leaves them no time for communion with Him, and that busyness has dulled their prayer. “Sad thought,” he wrote, “that the bearing of fruit should separate the branch from the vine!” If our work has come between us and Christ, we have misunderstood what our work is.

## Abide in me, and I in you

In The True Vine Murray looks closely at the double command. Abide in me, he says, points chiefly to what we do: we trust, obey, turn from other supports, and cling to Christ. And I in you points to what He does: He dwells in the heart by faith and strengthens us by His Spirit. The two belong together. We cannot make Christ dwell in us, but we can keep coming to Him, and He has promised to do the rest.

> If a man love me, he will keep my words: and my Father will love him, and we will come unto him, and make our abode with him. JOHN 14:23

Murray was realistic about why believers do not experience this. Many long for the fulness of the Spirit and the indwelling of Christ, he observed, and wonder why they make no progress; often the reason is that they are asking for the second half of the command while neglecting the first. They want Christ in them without the daily, humble, obedient turning to Him that the word abide requires.

And yet he insisted that abiding is not a strenuous achievement. The branch does not hold on to the vine by clenching. It is held. The union is Christ’s work, and our part is to consent, to trust, and to stay.

> Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy. JUDE 1:24

## Every moment

The most distinctive thing in Murray’s teaching on abiding is the word moment. He did not think of abiding as a feeling to be recovered on Sundays or at conventions. The sap does not flow from the vine to the branch for a while and then stop. It flows continuously. So Christ’s life is available to the believer not in occasional supplies but moment by moment, in the ordinary hours of an ordinary day.

> In that day sing ye unto her, A vineyard of red wine. I the Lord do keep it; I will water it every moment: lest any hurt it, I will keep it night and day. ISAIAH 27:2–3

This is liberating when it is understood. It means you do not have to keep up a spiritual temperature. You do not have to feel close to Christ in order to be close to Him. You are asked to do the next thing in dependence on Him, trusting that His life is flowing even when you are not conscious of it. When you fail, you do not have to climb back up to where you were. You simply return, confess, and abide again.

## Where care is needed

Murray sometimes wrote as if a single act of faith could settle the matter once and for all, so that from that day the believer would abide without interruption. Some readers have taken this to mean that any lapse proves they never truly entered the abiding life, and have exhausted themselves trying to enter it again. That is not the best of Murray, and it is not the teaching of John 15, which is addressed to disciples who would fail that very night. Abiding is a relationship, not a technique, and relationships have their bad days. Christ’s command to abide carries within it His promise to keep, and the one who stumbles is still a branch.

> My little children, these things write I unto you, that ye sin not. And if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous. 1 JOHN 2:1

The heart of what Murray taught is simple, and it does not need the scaffolding. You are joined to Christ. His life is in you. Stay close. Keep coming. Let His life, not your effort, be the source of whatever fruit your life bears.

> He that saith he abideth in him ought himself also so to walk, even as he walked. 1 JOHN 2:6

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask whether you are trying to live the Christian life in the same way you began it — by trusting Christ — or by effort and management.
2. Identify the work, even good work, that most often comes between you and Christ. Bring it to Him deliberately this week before you begin it.
3. Several times a day, stop for a moment and say inwardly: I am a branch; Your life is in me. Notice what changes.
4. When you fail, resist the urge to prove yourself before returning. Confess at once and abide again.
5. Read one chapter of The True Vine in the Ochorus library slowly, with its closing prayer.

> Teach me to abide: to trust You, to keep coming, to stay where You have placed me.
> Let Your life flow into me moment by moment, in the ordinary hours as well as the holy ones.
> And when I wander, draw me back quickly, for You hold me more firmly than I hold You. Amen.

# The True Vine

> I am the true vine, and my Father is the husbandman. JOHN 15:1

The True Vine is one of his shortest books and one of his best: a series of brief daily readings that walk slowly through the first sixteen verses of John 15, one phrase at a time. He wrote it, he says in the preface, for young Christians, as a help to take up the position in which the Christian life must be a success. He lived in wine country, and as he wrote he could look out of his window on large vineyards. The parable was not a figure of speech to him. It was the landscape.

The previous chapter looked at abiding. This one looks at the other words of the parable: the Husbandman, the fruit, and the knife.

## The Husbandman

Before Jesus says anything about branches, He says, My Father is the husbandman. Murray thought this order mattered. Before we think about our fruitfulness we must look up and see who is caring for the vine. The Father planted it, tends it, waters it and prunes it. He is not a distant owner who expects a crop and punishes failure. He is the one who does the work.

> For we are labourers together with God: ye are God’s husbandry, ye are God’s building. 1 CORINTHIANS 3:9

Murray noticed that many Christians who have learned to trust Christ still leave the Father out of their thoughts, as though Christ had to protect them from Him. In The True Vine he puts it bluntly: “The great lack of the Christian life is that, even where we trust Christ, we leave God out of the count.” The Father who cared for the Vine with such delight cares in the same way for every branch. Your growth is not finally in your hands. It is in His.

## Fruit is the one thing

What is the branch for? Murray’s answer is uncompromising: fruit is the one thing the branch is for. A branch exists to carry the life of the vine out to where it can be gathered. And fruit, he says, is “something that the branch bears, not for itself, but for its owner.”

> Herein is my Father glorified, that ye bear much fruit; so shall ye be my disciples. JOHN 15:8

This led him to one of his sharpest warnings. Many Christians, he observed, arrange their priorities in a fixed order: first their own salvation, then their livelihood and families, and then whatever time and interest is left over for the kingdom and the saving of others. No wonder so little is left. Murray insisted that bearing fruit for others is not an optional extra to the Christian life but the reason for it. The Vine exists to carry God’s saving love to men, and so does every branch.

What is the fruit? Murray was clear that it is not simply activity. It is the character of Christ reproduced in us — love, joy, peace and the rest — and it is the blessing of others through that life. Both go together. A life that is busy but loveless has not borne the fruit of the Vine; a life that is pious but blesses no one has not either.

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law. GALATIANS 5:22–23

## The pruning knife

Every branch that beareth fruit, he purgeth it, that it may bring forth more fruit (John 15:2). Murray knew the vine as a plant that runs quickly to wild wood and needs the hardest pruning of any. Some trees can do without it. The vine cannot. Looking out on the vineyards, he wrote that the chief care of the vinedresser is the pruning.

Most Christians assume that the Father’s pruning knife is affliction. Murray disagreed, or at least he would not let affliction take first place. In The True Vine he argues that the knife is the Word of God, and he points to the next verse, where Jesus says that the disciples are already clean through the word which He has spoken to them.

> Now ye are clean through the word which I have spoken unto you. JOHN 15:3

> For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword, piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul and spirit, and of the joints and marrow, and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart. HEBREWS 4:12

Affliction, Murray says, becomes a blessing only when it drives us to the discipline of the Word; without that, suffering is often wasted. Even Paul’s thorn did him good only when Christ’s word about strength made perfect in weakness had shown him his danger. This is a wise and pastoral insight. It means that the believer who is spared great suffering is not spared pruning, and the believer who is suffering is not being pruned by the suffering alone. The knife is what God says. The question in every season is whether we are letting His Word cut away what is of self.

What does pruning remove? Not the bad wood only. The vinedresser cuts back healthy growth too — shoots that are vigorous and green and would produce leaves instead of grapes. That is the hardest part. God may cut away good things in us — interests, ambitions, even forms of service — not because they are sinful but because they draw off life that should go into fruit.

## Much fruit

The parable moves from fruit, to more fruit, to much fruit. Murray loved that progression. He believed God intends far more for His people than they expect, and that the reason for our barrenness is not that the Vine is poor but that the branch is not abiding.

> Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you, and ordained you, that ye should go and bring forth fruit, and that your fruit should remain. JOHN 15:16

Here a gentle qualification is needed. Murray sometimes writes as if the fruit of an abiding life will always be visible and abundant. Scripture is more reticent. Some of the most faithful servants of God have seen very little of the fruit of their labour in their own lifetimes, and some fruit — patience, gentleness, faith — is hidden even from the one who bears it. Much fruit is a promise, but its measure and its timing belong to the Husbandman. A believer should not conclude from a small harvest that he is not abiding. He should simply keep abiding, and leave the counting to God.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Consider whether you trust Christ but quietly fear the Father. Read John 15:1 and 15:9 together, and let the Father’s care reach you.
2. Be honest about your priorities. Does bearing fruit for others come first, or does it get what is left over?
3. When you read Scripture this week, ask the Father to use it as a pruning knife. Write down one thing it exposes.
4. Name one good thing in your life that may be drawing off strength from what matters most. Ask God whether it should be cut back.
5. If you see little fruit in your life, refuse discouragement. Abide, and leave the harvest to the Husbandman.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You are the Husbandman, and I am in Your hands. Forgive me for trusting Your Son and forgetting Your care.
> Prune me by Your Word. Cut away what is of self, even the good things that keep me from bearing fruit.
> Let the life of the Vine flow through me, not for my own sake, but for Your glory and the blessing of others.
> Give me fruit that remains, and let me be content to leave its measure to You. Amen.

# The Glory of the Creature

> Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power: for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created. REVELATION 4:11

Most books on the Christian life begin with sin. Murray’s most searching books begin somewhere earlier. Humility opens not with the fall but with creation, under the title The Glory of the Creature, and Waiting on God devotes one of its first readings to The True Place of the Creature. Before he says a word about what has gone wrong with us, Murray wants us to understand what we were made to be. His answer, in a single word, is dependent. Everything else in his teaching grows from that root.

## Not once, but every moment

The heart of the idea is simple and far-reaching. When God made the world, He did not make it as a watchmaker makes a watch, winding it up and leaving it to run. He made it as a fountain makes a stream: the stream exists only because the fountain is continually pouring. In Humility Murray writes that the life God bestows “is imparted not once for all, but each moment continuously, by the unceasing operation of His mighty power.”

> Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high. HEBREWS 1:3

This means that the creature’s relationship to God is not merely one of origin. It is one of unceasing dependence. We do not owe God our beginning and then carry on by ourselves. We owe Him this breath, this heartbeat, this thought. Murray took Psalm 104 as his text in Waiting on God: the young lions, the birds, the creatures of the sea — these all wait upon God, that He may give them their meat in due season.

> These wait all upon thee; that thou mayest give them their meat in due season. That thou givest them they gather: thou openest thine hand, they are filled with good. PSALM 104:27–28

## The creature as vessel

What, then, is a creature for? Murray’s answer is lovely and humbling. God created us, he says, to make us partakers of His own goodness and blessedness, and so to show forth His glory. But He did not do this by handing us a quantity of goodness to own and dispose of. He did it by making us vessels. The creature, Murray writes in Humility, must “present itself as an empty vessel, in which God can dwell and manifest His power and goodness.”

> But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:7

This reverses our instinct about dignity. We tend to think that to be great is to be independent: to have resources, to need no one, to be self-made. Murray thought that was exactly the lie of Eden. The serpent offered our first parents the chance to be as gods — that is, to stand on their own. The creature’s true glory is not independence but receptivity. A vessel is glorious when it is full, and it is full only of what is poured into it.

> For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it? 1 CORINTHIANS 4:7

## The root of every sin

If dependence is the creature’s glory, then the root of all sin is the refusal of dependence. Murray is unambiguous: pride, the loss of this humble dependence, is in his words “the root of every sin and evil.” The fallen angels looked on themselves with self-satisfaction. Our first parents reached for a life of their own. And every sin since has had the same shape: a creature acting as though it were not a creature.

> Pride goeth before destruction, and an haughty spirit before a fall. PROVERBS 16:18

This is why, for Murray, redemption is not merely forgiveness. It is restoration. Christ came to bring the creature back to its true place, and He did it by taking that place Himself. The eternal Son became a creature and lived in perfect dependence on the Father — the Son can do nothing of Himself, He said. In His life we see what a human being was meant to be. In His death we see dependence carried to the uttermost. And by His Spirit He puts that same dependent life within us.

> Then answered Jesus and said unto them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, The Son can do nothing of himself, but what he seeth the Father do. JOHN 5:19

## Why this is good news

At first all this can sound crushing. Nothing in ourselves; everything received; every moment dependent. But Murray insisted that when a believer really sees it, dependence becomes not a burden but a relief. If God is the one who works, then I do not have to be the source of my own goodness. If He maintains me every moment, then my weakness is not a threat to His supply. The vessel does not have to produce the water. It only has to stay open and turned toward the fountain.

In Waiting on God Murray says that the call to dependence is no arbitrary command, but simply our restoration to our original destiny and our highest nobility as creatures. It is not that God, being great, demands that we grovel. It is that God, being good, delights to give, and a creature is by definition the one who receives.

> Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning. JAMES 1:17

## A necessary balance

Murray sometimes pressed the creature’s nothingness in language drawn from William Law and the older mystical writers, and a careless reader can come away thinking that the human person is worthless, or that our wills and gifts count for nothing. That is not Scripture’s view, and it was not Murray’s at his best. God made us in His image and called His work very good. The point is not that we are nothing in value but that we are nothing in ourselves — that everything we are and have is received. A vessel made by a master potter is precious. It is precious as a vessel.

This distinction matters pastorally. People who already despise themselves do not need to hear that they are nothing. They need to hear that they are made, held and loved by a God who delights to fill them. That, in the end, is what Murray meant.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read Psalm 104 slowly, and notice how every creature in it waits on God. Place yourself among them.
2. At several points today, pause and acknowledge that your very breath is being given to you now.
3. Identify one area where you take pride in being self-sufficient. Confess it as a creature’s forgetfulness.
4. Ask whether your sense of your own worth depends on independence or on being held by God.
5. If you tend to despise yourself, meditate on 2 Corinthians 4:7: you are an earthen vessel, and God has put treasure in you.

### A PRAYER

> Creator God, You made me, and You hold me in being every moment. I have lived as though my life were my own.
> Forgive the pride that reaches for independence. Restore me to the place of a creature, open and dependent on You.
> Fill this earthen vessel with Your goodness, that the glory may be Yours and not mine.
> And teach me to find in my dependence not a burden but a rest, for You delight to give. Amen.

# Humility, the Root of Every Grace

> Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus: who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant. PHILIPPIANS 2:5–7

Of all Murray’s books, Humility is perhaps the one most often pressed into other people’s hands, and the one most often put down unfinished. It is short — twelve brief chapters — and it is merciless. It does not flatter the reader or let him hide. It asks him to look at Christ until his own pride becomes visible to him, and then to bring it to Christ to be dealt with. Many believers have found it the most uncomfortable book they have ever read, and one of the most healing.

## More than a virtue

The book’s central claim comes in its first chapter, and it is worth quoting exactly. “Humility,” Murray writes, “the place of entire dependence on God, is, from the very nature of things, the first duty and the highest virtue of the creature, and the root of every virtue.”

Notice what he does with the word. Most of us think of humility as one virtue among others — a pleasant modesty, a willingness not to boast. Murray defines it as a place: the place of entire dependence on God. It is the creature standing where a creature belongs, as the previous chapter described. That is why it is not one grace alongside others but the soil in which all of them grow. In his own words, “Humility is the only soil in which the graces root.” Love without it becomes patronage. Zeal without it becomes ambition. Knowledge without it puffs up. Prayer without it becomes a performance.

> God resisteth the proud, but giveth grace unto the humble. JAMES 4:6

This means, Murray says, that a lack of humility is a sufficient explanation for every defect and failure. When a Christian struggles for years with impatience, or irritability, or envy, the problem may not be that he has not tried hard enough at patience or kindness. It may be that the root is diseased, and the fruit cannot be healthy until the root is healed.

## The humility of Christ

The second chapter of Humility is called The Secret of Redemption, and it turns everything toward Christ. What is the incarnation, Murray asks, but His heavenly humility, His emptying of Himself to become man? What was His life but humility, the form of a servant? What was His death but humility, obedience unto death? And what is His exaltation but humility crowned? Christ is, in Murray’s phrase, the humility of God embodied in human nature.

> And being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross. PHILIPPIANS 2:8

This is why Murray could write that “His humility is our salvation and His salvation is our humility.” He did not mean that we are saved by being humble. He meant that the salvation Christ gives is nothing less than the communication of His own life, and His life is humble to its core. To be saved is to have the mind of Christ planted in us. The humble Christian is not a person who has achieved a virtue but a person in whom Christ’s own disposition is taking shape.

> Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. MATTHEW 11:29

## Tested in daily life

The most practical chapter of the book is Humility in Daily Life, and it closes every escape route. It is easy, Murray observes, to think we humble ourselves before God. But humility towards men is the only sufficient proof that our humility before God is real. The humility that is really ours is not the posture we adopt on our knees but what we carry with us into ordinary conduct. And he adds a sentence worth pondering: “It is in our most unguarded moments that we really show and see what we are.”

> Let nothing be done through strife or vainglory; but in lowliness of mind let each esteem other better than themselves. PHILIPPIANS 2:3

That verse raises an honest question, and Murray faces it. How can I esteem others better than myself when I can plainly see that some are less wise or less gifted than I am? His answer is that the question itself shows how little we understand humility. The humble person is not constantly comparing himself with others, whether to his advantage or his disadvantage. He has seen himself as nothing before God and has stopped measuring. He meets others as one who seeks nothing for himself, and so is free to serve them.

## Humility and sin

Murray made a distinction that many readers find freeing. Humility is often confused with penitence, he says, and so people try to grow humble by dwelling endlessly on their sins. But the angels are humble and have never sinned. Christ was humble and was without sin. Humility is not first a response to our sinfulness; it is the right posture of any creature before God. Sin deepens it — Paul never forgot that he had been a persecutor, and the memory kept him low — but sin is not its source.

> For I am the least of the apostles, that am not meet to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the church of God. But by the grace of God I am what I am. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:9–10

This matters because a humility built on self-reproach can become a subtle form of self-absorption. The person is still looking at himself, only with disgust instead of admiration. Murray’s humility looks away from self altogether, toward God, and finds rest there.

## Where the book needs a gentler reading

Humility is strong medicine, and it must be taken with care. Murray writes in places of the soul seeing itself as nothing and consenting to part with self altogether, and he appends passages from William Law that speak of pride with great intensity. A sensitive reader who already struggles with self-hatred can take this language as licence for more of the same. That would be a misreading. Murray’s humility is not self-contempt; it is self-forgetfulness in the presence of a loving God. It is also not passivity in the face of wrong. The humble Christ drove the traders from the temple and rebuked the Pharisees to their faces. Humility is compatible with strength, with conviction, and with saying no.

> Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he shall lift you up. JAMES 4:10

Murray’s last chapter is called Humility and Exaltation, and it ends not in the dust but in God’s lifting up. The way down is the way home.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think of a sin or weakness you have long struggled with. Ask whether pride may be its root, and bring the root to Christ rather than only the fruit.
2. Notice this week how you behave in unguarded moments — interrupted, contradicted, overlooked. Write down what they reveal.
3. Do one hidden act of service for someone who cannot repay you, and tell no one.
4. When you catch yourself comparing yourself with others, stop and turn your attention to Christ instead.
5. If you tend to self-contempt, meditate on James 4:10. Humility ends in being lifted up by God.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You were meek and lowly in heart, and I am proud in ways I cannot even see.
> Show me my pride, not to crush me but to free me. Take the root, and not only the fruit.
> Plant Your own humility in me, so that I may serve others gladly and seek nothing for myself.
> Keep me from mistaking self-contempt for lowliness. Let me forget myself in You, and in Your time lift me up. Amen.

# Absolute Surrender

> I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. ROMANS 12:1

The phrase most people associate with Andrew Murray is the title of his most famous collection of addresses: Absolute Surrender. He gave these addresses on his travels in the 1890s, and they were gathered into a small book that has been reprinted ever since. The title address takes an unlikely text — the words of the wicked king Ahab to the Syrian king who besieged him — and turns them into a confession for every child of God: My lord, O king, according to thy saying, I am thine, and all that I have.

> And the king of Israel answered and said, My lord, O king, according to thy saying, I am thine, and all that I have. 1 KINGS 20:4

It is a startling choice of text, and Murray knew it. But the words were exactly what he wanted: an unconditional yielding of everything, held back from nothing.

## Where the words came from

Murray tells us in the address where he first heard the phrase in its full force. In Scotland he had been in a company discussing the needs of the church, and he asked a godly man who trained Christian workers what he considered the great need. The man answered simply that absolute surrender to God was the one thing. Those workers who were sound on that point, he said, went on and improved even when they were slow; those who were not, often fell away. Murray never forgot it. The condition for obtaining God’s full blessing, he concluded, is absolute surrender to Him.

## God claims it

Murray’s first point is that surrender is not an extra that especially devoted Christians choose. It is rooted in who God is. God is the fountain of all life and goodness, and nothing good exists anywhere except what He works. The sun, the stars, the lilies — all are wholly yielded to Him, and He works in them as He pleases. How then could He work His work in His redeemed children if only part of them is given to Him?

He used a homely illustration. A pen, he said, must be wholly surrendered to the hand that writes with it. If someone else is holding it partly, it cannot write properly. So with the believer: God cannot do His full work in a life that is half given.

> Know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own? For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God’s. 1 CORINTHIANS 6:19–20

## God works it

Then comes the turn that makes this address gospel rather than law. Murray anticipated the objection that many hearts would raise: surrender implies so much, and I am afraid of what it will cost. His answer was that God does not ask us to produce a perfect surrender by the power of our own will. God is willing to work it in us.

> For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure. PHILIPPIANS 2:13

And he offered a prayer that has helped more timid believers than perhaps anything else he wrote: “My God, I am willing that Thou shouldst make me willing.” If you cannot say that you are willing for everything, you can at least say that. You can bring your reluctance to God and ask Him to deal with it. The longings you feel toward a deeper surrender, Murray told his hearers, are themselves the drawings of Christ.

## God accepts it

Murray’s third point is the one most often forgotten. When a believer brings his surrender to God, he may be painfully aware of how imperfect it is. He may ask, Is it really absolute? Murray pointed to the father of the demon-possessed boy, who cried out, Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief — and whose trembling faith was enough. So, Murray said, come just as you are, even with a trembling heart, even without feeling the power or the assurance. God accepts the surrender that is brought to Him and makes it good.

> And straightway the father of the child cried out, and said with tears, Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief. MARK 9:24

This is the part of the teaching that must be held on to most firmly, because the phrase absolute surrender can so easily turn into its opposite.

## The danger of the phrase

For many people, Murray’s call has been the door into freedom. They had held back some area of life from God for years, and his words gave them the courage to let it go. But there is another kind of reader. Conscientious, sensitive, prone to self-examination, this reader hears the word absolute and begins to inspect every motive. Was my surrender complete? Is there something I have held back without knowing it? Did I mean it enough? The call to rest in God becomes a new and endless law, and the believer who was meant to be set free is more anxious than before.

Murray did not intend this, and his own address guards against it. God claims the surrender, God works it, God accepts it, and God maintains it. It is not the perfection of our yielding that saves or sanctifies us; it is the faithfulness of the God to whom we yield.

> Faithful is he that calleth you, who also will do it. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:24

It is also worth saying plainly that surrender in Scripture is both a decisive act and a daily one. There may well be a moment when a believer, like Murray’s hearers, gives up to God something long withheld, and that moment can be a turning point. But Jesus also said, take up the cross daily. No single act exhausts our yielding, and no failure afterwards proves that the act was false. We give ourselves again tomorrow, not because yesterday’s gift was fraudulent, but because every day brings new things to give.

> And he said to them all, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me. LUKE 9:23

## The blessing on the other side

What did Murray expect to follow surrender? Not an easy life, but a life in which God is free to work. If our hearts are willing, he said, there is no end to what God will do. Surrender is not the loss of a life but the handing over of an instrument to the hand that made it — and a pen in the writer’s hand is at last able to do what it was made for.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name, honestly and specifically, one thing you have held back from God. Write it down.
2. If you are not willing to surrender it, pray Murray’s prayer: My God, I am willing that You should make me willing.
3. If you tend to anxious self-inspection, stop asking whether your surrender was absolute enough. Rest instead on the faithfulness of God.
4. Each morning this week, begin the day by giving yourself to God afresh in a single sentence.
5. Read the title address of Absolute Surrender in the Ochorus library, noticing the three movements: God claims it, works it, accepts it.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, You made me, bought me, and have every claim on me. I confess that I have held parts of my life back from You.
> I am not always willing, but I am willing that You should make me willing. Do in me what I cannot do.
> I bring You my surrender, trembling and imperfect as it is. Accept it, and make it good.
> And when I am tempted to measure my yielding, turn my eyes from my own heart to Your faithfulness. Amen.

# Possible with God

> And he said, The things which are impossible with men are possible with God. LUKE 18:27

The rich young ruler went away sorrowful. Jesus turned to His disciples and said how hard it is for those who have riches to enter the kingdom, and they were astonished: Who then can be saved? His answer is the text of one of the best addresses in Absolute Surrender, titled simply Impossible with Man, Possible with God. Murray loved this verse because it holds together the two things he believed every Christian must learn, usually slowly and often painfully. The first is that the Christian life is impossible. The second is that God does the impossible.

## Two lessons

Murray put it plainly: “It often takes a long time to learn the first lesson, that in religion man can do nothing, that salvation is impossible to man.” And some people, he added, learn the first lesson and never learn the second. They come to the end of themselves and stay there.

> Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God. 2 CORINTHIANS 3:5

He traced the way a believer typically travels. At first the young convert has no idea that the life is impossible. He is joyful and earnest and sure he can conquer, for God will help him. Then he fails where he did not expect to, and concludes that he was not watchful enough, and makes stronger resolutions, and fails again. Later he reaches a darker place. He begins to see that the life is impossible, but instead of turning to God he concludes that God never really expected it of him. Murray thought multitudes of Christians lived here — sincere, orthodox, resigned to a low life, doing their best and expecting little.

The way out is the third stage: the believer accepts the impossibility in its full truth and at the same time refuses to give up. He says, It is impossible for me, and yet it must be done. And in that tension he cries to God — and God answers.

## The gateway of Romans 7

Murray found the same pattern in Romans 7 and 8. In the address O Wretched Man that I am! he describes the man at the end of Romans 7 as regenerate — he delights in the law of God after the inward man — and yet impotent: to will is present with him, but how to perform that which is good he finds not. He is therefore wretched. But he is also, Murray says, “a man on the borders of complete liberty.” His cry is the gateway into the eighth chapter, where the Holy Spirit is named again and again.

> O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord. ROMANS 7:24–25

> For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus hath made me free from the law of sin and death. ROMANS 8:2

The point Murray pressed is that willing is not enough. A renewed heart that loves God’s law may still fail. What is needed is not a stronger will but a divine power working within the will. He set Romans 7 beside Philippians 2:13 — it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do — and said the difference is exactly this: in the one, a man wills and cannot perform; in the other, a man has learned that God works both.

## Peter learned it

Murray’s favourite example was Peter. He spent three years in Christ’s school, Murray says, and never learned that it was impossible — until he had denied his Lord and gone out and wept bitterly. Peter’s confidence was sincere. He meant it when he said he would die with Christ. But his sincerity was the problem. He believed he could.

> But he spake the more vehemently, If I should die with thee, I will not deny thee in any wise. MARK 14:31

In the address Peter’s Repentance, Murray traces how that bitter night became the turning point. The self-confident Peter had to be broken so that the Spirit-filled Peter could be born. After Pentecost, the same man who had denied Christ before a servant girl stood before the council and would not be silenced. What had been impossible with Peter had become possible with God.

## Where the impossible meets us

This teaching reaches into every part of life. The command to love an enemy is impossible. The command to be anxious for nothing is impossible. The command to forgive seventy times seven is impossible. The call to be holy as God is holy is impossible. Murray would not soften any of them. He did not want us to lower the standard to what we could manage. He wanted us to discover that God meant every word and would do what He commanded.

> Ah Lord GOD! behold, thou hast made the heaven and the earth by thy great power and stretched out arm, and there is nothing too hard for thee. JEREMIAH 32:17

This is the difference between Murray’s teaching and mere moral exhortation. The moralist says: this is hard, but try harder. Murray says: this is impossible, so stop trying in your own strength and trust God to work it.

## A caution about stages

Murray tended to describe these experiences as stages in a sequence, with Romans 7 as a place one passes through and leaves behind on the way to Romans 8. Many faithful interpreters read Romans 7 differently. Some think Paul is describing his experience before conversion; others think he is describing the ongoing experience of every believer, who lives in Romans 7 and Romans 8 at once until the resurrection. The debate is old and serious.

The pastoral danger in a strict stage scheme is that the believer who still finds in himself the struggle of Romans 7 may conclude that he has never truly entered the Spirit-filled life, and may exhaust himself seeking a crossing he thinks he has missed. Murray’s central truth does not depend on settling the debate. Whether we pass through Romans 7 once or revisit it often, the answer is the same: I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord. The impossible is still possible with God, and we still come to Him by confessing that we cannot.

> And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:9

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name one command of Christ you have quietly decided is impossible for you, and therefore not really meant.
2. Ask where you are in Murray’s sequence: confident in your own strength, resigned to failure, or crying to God for what only He can do.
3. The next time you fail at something you resolved, resist the reflex to make a stronger resolution. Instead confess your inability and ask God to work in you.
4. Read Romans 7:14–8:4 aloud, slowly, and thank God for the answer in 7:25.
5. If you have exhausted yourself seeking a spiritual crossing you think you have missed, rest in 2 Corinthians 12:9.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have tried to live for You in my own strength and have failed more times than I can count.
> I confess what I have been slow to learn: that without You it is impossible.
> Teach me the second lesson too: that what is impossible with me is possible with You. Work in me both to will and to do.
> When I fall, as Peter fell, do not let me despair, but bring me through weeping into Your strength. Amen.

# Carnal and Spiritual

> And I, brethren, could not speak unto you as unto spiritual, but as unto carnal, even as unto babes in Christ. 1 CORINTHIANS 3:1

The first address in The Master’s Indwelling is titled Carnal Christians, and it opens with a sentence that frames much of Murray’s teaching on the deeper life. In 1 Corinthians 3, he says, the apostle speaks of two stages of the Christian life, two types of Christians. The Corinthians were truly in Christ. They were not unbelievers. But Paul could not speak to them as spiritual, because they were carnal — babes still, when they should have grown.

Murray believed this distinction explained a great deal of what he saw in the churches: believers who were genuinely converted, sincere and active, and yet lived in continual defeat, bitterness and worldliness. The problem, he said, was not that they needed to be converted again. It was that they were living in the carnal state and had not yet yielded themselves wholly to the Spirit.

## Four marks of the carnal state

Murray drew four marks of the carnal Christian from the Corinthian letters, and they are worth examining honestly.

The first is prolonged infancy. The Corinthians should have been eating meat, and they could still only take milk. The same complaint is made in Hebrews: by this time they ought to have been teachers, and they needed someone to teach them the first principles again.

> For when for the time ye ought to be teachers, ye have need that one teach you again which be the first principles of the oracles of God; and are become such as have need of milk, and not of strong meat. HEBREWS 5:12

The second is continual sin and failure — especially, in Corinth, envying, strife and division. Murray urged his hearers not merely to confess individual sins but to recognise the pattern behind them: that repeated failure is a sign that the life is not healthy.

> For ye are yet carnal: for whereas there is among you envying, and strife, and divisions, are ye not carnal, and walk as men? 1 CORINTHIANS 3:3

The third mark is the one that makes this teaching so searching. The carnal state, Murray said, can exist alongside great spiritual gifts. The Corinthians came behind in no gift, and yet they were carnal. A minister, he warned, may preach beautifully, gather a large congregation and be used by God to bless others, and still be a carnal man. Gifts are not a measure of spirituality. That is a warning every person in Christian service needs to hear.

The fourth mark is that the carnal state makes a person unable to receive spiritual truth. This is why Paul could not give the Corinthians more: they were not fit to take it. Murray thought that much preaching of the deeper life fell on ears that could not hear it, because the hearers had not yet dealt with the carnal life itself.

## The root and the fruit

What is the remedy? Murray’s answer is characteristic. Many people, he said, go to Christ to have a hasty temper or a proud disposition taken away, and are puzzled when He does not do it. The reason, he suggested, is that “you wanted Him to take away the ugly fruits while the poisonous root was to stay in you.” They had not asked that the flesh itself should be nailed to the cross and that they should give themselves entirely to the power of His Spirit.

> And they that are Christ’s have crucified the flesh with the affections and lusts. GALATIANS 5:24

He used an illustration of a painter and a canvas. Would a painter labour over a canvas that did not belong to him, which anyone else might take and use? No more will Christ work His picture in a life that has not been given into His hands. The call, then, is to yield oneself wholly to the Spirit as an empty vessel. Murray ended the address with a prayer he urged his hearers to make their own: “Lord, I give myself an empty vessel to be filled with your Spirit.”

> This I say then, Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh. GALATIANS 5:16

## What is true here

There is a great deal of truth in this teaching, and churches that have rejected it outright have often lost something. It is true that Scripture distinguishes between believers who are maturing and believers who are stuck. It is true that Paul rebuked the Corinthians as carnal and expected them to grow. It is true that gifts, activity and success are not proof of spiritual health. And it is true that many believers live far below what Scripture holds out to them, and have settled for it. Murray’s insistence that God has more for them — that the Spirit is given not merely to regenerate but to rule — is a word that many need.

> That he would grant you, according to the riches of his glory, to be strengthened with might by his Spirit in the inner man. EPHESIANS 3:16

## Where the scheme needs care

But the framework of two types of Christians, carnal and spiritual, must be handled with care. Paul in 1 Corinthians 3 was rebuking a church for a condition, not dividing all believers into two fixed classes, one of which had entered a second experience and the other had not. Every believer is in some respects still carnal and in some respects spiritual, and growth is usually gradual as well as marked by moments of decision. When the distinction hardens into two castes, several harms follow. Some believers conclude that they belong to the upper class and look down on others. Some conclude that they belong to the lower class and must seek a crisis that never seems to come. And some, told that the blessing will certainly come if they yield wholly, blame themselves endlessly when they do not feel it.

Murray’s best counsel guards against this. He urged believers to abide in humble surrender and childlike trust — not to measure themselves, but to look to God. That is the right place to stand, whichever words we use for the stages of growth.

> But grow in grace, and in the knowledge of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ. 2 PETER 3:18

The honest question this chapter leaves is not, Which kind of Christian am I? It is, What in me is still ruled by the flesh, and will I give it to the Spirit today?

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Consider Murray’s four marks — prolonged infancy, repeated failure, gifts without spirituality, inability to receive truth. Which one describes you most honestly?
2. If you serve in any Christian work, ask whether you have mistaken usefulness for spiritual health.
3. Name a recurring sin you have asked God to remove. Ask whether you have brought Him the root as well as the fruit.
4. Pray Murray’s prayer, offering yourself as an empty vessel to be filled with the Spirit, and then leave the result with God.
5. Resist sorting other believers into carnal and spiritual. Apply this chapter only to yourself.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Spirit, I confess that much in me is still carnal: envy, strife, pride, and the desire to be seen.
> I have asked You to take away the fruit while I kept the root. Take the root.
> I give myself to You as an empty vessel. Fill me, rule me, and lead me into maturity.
> Keep me from measuring myself or others, and let me simply grow in grace and in the knowledge of Christ. Amen.

# The Indwelling Spirit

> Are ye so foolish? having begun in the Spirit, are ye now made perfect by the flesh? GALATIANS 3:3

Murray wrote a full book on the Holy Spirit, The Spirit of Christ, and it remains one of his weightiest. But his teaching on the Spirit runs through almost everything he wrote, and some of its clearest statements are in the addresses gathered in Absolute Surrender and The Master’s Indwelling. The heart of it is a single conviction. The Holy Spirit is not an occasional visitor, given for special moments of power. He is the indwelling life of Christ in the believer, given to rule and to work in every hour of every day. And the great failure of the church, as Murray saw it, is that believers who have received the Spirit go on trying to live by their own strength.

## Begun in the Spirit

In the address Having Begun in the Spirit, Murray takes Paul’s question to the Galatians and turns it on the church of his day. The Galatians had heard the gospel, believed it, and received the Spirit. Then they tried to complete by their own efforts the work the Spirit had begun. Murray thought that was the story of countless believers. He put it bluntly: “God has called the church of Christ to live in the power of the Holy Spirit, and the church is living for the most part in the power of human flesh, and of will and energy and effort apart from the Spirit of God.”

> This only would I learn of you, Received ye the Spirit by the works of the law, or by the hearing of faith? GALATIANS 3:2

He described young Christians who came to him puzzled. They had vowed with their whole hearts to serve God, and yet they failed. Why? His answer was always the same: “My dear friend, you are trying to do in your own strength what Christ alone can do in you.”

## The Spirit of Christ

For Murray the Spirit is always the Spirit of Christ. He does not bring a separate blessing alongside Christ; He brings Christ Himself. The whole purpose of His coming is to make the absent Lord present, to make the Vine’s life flow in the branch, to form Christ in the believer.

> Nevertheless I tell you the truth; It is expedient for you that I go away: for if I go not away, the Comforter will not come unto you; but if I depart, I will send him unto you. JOHN 16:7

This is why his teaching on the Spirit is so closely bound up with his teaching on abiding. To abide in Christ and to walk in the Spirit are not two different programmes. They are the same life described from two sides. The branch abides in the Vine; the sap flows into the branch. We turn to Christ; the Spirit makes Christ real in us.

> Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwelleth in you? 1 CORINTHIANS 3:16

## The director of the work

In the address Separated unto the Holy Ghost, Murray turns from the individual to the church and its mission. His text is Acts 13, where the Holy Spirit says to the praying church at Antioch, Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them. The great lesson, he says, is that the Holy Spirit is the director of the work of God upon the earth.

> As they ministered to the Lord, and fasted, the Holy Ghost said, Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them. ACTS 13:2

Murray was sharp about how the church often reverses this. We make our plans and then ask God to bless them, he said, instead of refusing to go unless God goes before us. He told how, at the opening of the Mission Institute at Wellington, the principal reminded the gathering that only the architect had known what the building would become; so only God knows what a work begun in obedience will grow into. The Spirit leads, and His servants wait for their orders and obey them.

That word — wait — links this teaching to Murray’s great theme of waiting on God. The church at Antioch was ministering to the Lord and fasting when the Spirit spoke. The instruction came to people who were waiting.

## Filled, not once, but continually

Murray pressed believers to be filled with the Spirit, and he used strong language about consecration. But he was careful about where the power lies. “I may consecrate myself a hundred times with all the intensity of my being,” he said, “and that will not help me.” What helps is that God accepts and seals the consecration. The believer’s part is to give himself up; God’s part is to fill.

> And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit. EPHESIANS 5:18

The Greek of that command, as commentators have long noted, suggests an ongoing filling rather than a single event: go on being filled. Murray’s emphasis on the Spirit’s daily, hourly rule fits it well.

## A hard saying, and a softer one

There is one line in Having Begun in the Spirit that needs a gentle hand. When young Christians protested that they had not been trusting themselves, Murray answered that they must have been, or they could not have failed; if they had trusted Christ, He could not fail. There is a truth in this — failure often does expose self-reliance we did not know was there. But pressed as a rule, it would make every stumble proof of unbelief, and it would lay a crushing burden on sensitive consciences. Scripture is kinder. The apostles themselves trusted Christ and still stumbled; the Spirit Himself helps our infirmities. We should take Murray’s warning to heart without turning it into a verdict on every failure.

> Likewise the Spirit also helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought: but the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered. ROMANS 8:26

The Spirit is patient with weak believers. That, too, is part of what it means that He dwells in us.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Identify one area where you began in dependence on God and have drifted into self-effort. Name it before Him.
2. Before your next task — work, conversation, service — pause and ask the Spirit to do through you what you cannot do.
3. In any plans you are making for Christian work, ask honestly whether you are waiting for the Spirit’s direction or asking Him to bless your own.
4. Pray Ephesians 5:18 daily this week as a request: fill me again today.
5. When you fail, do not conclude that you never trusted Christ at all. Confess, and let the Spirit help your infirmity.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Spirit, Spirit of Christ, You dwell in me, and I have lived as though You did not.
> I began in You and have tried to finish in my own strength. Forgive me.
> Fill me again today. Rule my thoughts, my words and my work. Make Christ real in me, and lead me where You would have me go.
> And when I stumble, help my infirmity, and bring me back to rest in You. Amen.

# Holy in Christ

> But as he which hath called you is holy, so be ye holy in all manner of conversation; because it is written, Be ye holy; for I am holy. 1 PETER 1:15–16

Holy in Christ is the longest of Murray’s books in the Ochorus library, and one of the least read. It is a sequence of thirty-one meditations, one for each day of a month, that trace the idea of holiness through Scripture from creation to the new Jerusalem. It is slower going than Humility or Absolute Surrender, and it repays the patience. Its subtitle speaks of the calling of God’s children to be holy as He is holy, and its argument can be put in a sentence: God calls us to be holy as He is holy, and He makes us holy by giving us Christ, who is our holiness.

## Called to be holy

Murray begins with the call. God has called believers to many things, but Scripture names none more often than this. They are called to be saints — that is, holy ones. They were chosen in Christ that they should be holy and without blame. God has not called us unto uncleanness, but unto holiness.

> According as he hath chosen us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before him in love. EPHESIANS 1:4

Murray thought many Christians had misunderstood their calling. They had heard that God called them to salvation, pardon, happiness or heaven, and had not noticed that all these were subordinate to holiness. And he traced a great deal of spiritual weakness to exactly this. The complaints of so many believers about lack of joy, lack of strength and lack of growth, he wrote, come down to this: the place God gave holiness in His call, they have not given it in their response.

## What holiness is

What does holy mean? Murray spent several chapters tracing the word through the Old Testament: the holy day in creation, the holy ground at the burning bush, the holy nation at Sinai, the holy place in the tabernacle. Holiness, he observed, is first of all what God is, and only then what He makes His people. It is His separateness, His majesty, His burning purity, His life.

> Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory. ISAIAH 6:3

And so our holiness cannot be something we manufacture. In one of the most important sentences in the book, Murray writes: “Holiness is not something we do or attain: it is the communication of the Divine life, the inbreathing of the Divine nature, the power of the Divine Presence resting on us.”

This is the same pattern we have met everywhere in his teaching. Humility is the creature receiving; abiding is the branch receiving; and holiness is the believer receiving the life of the Holy One. It is not first a code of conduct, though it will certainly show itself in conduct. It is a presence.

## Christ our sanctification

That presence has a name. Murray took his title from Paul’s words to the Corinthians, that Christ is made unto us sanctification. Holiness is not a thing Christ gives us to hold on our own. It is Christ Himself, living in us.

> But of him are ye in Christ Jesus, who of God is made unto us wisdom, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:30

“In Christ I see what God’s Holiness is, and what my holiness is,” Murray writes. The holiness of God, which in the Old Testament was a consuming fire, has in Christ come near in a human life of perfect love, obedience and humility. And that life is ours. We are holy in Christ — set apart in Him, accepted in Him, and progressively made like Him by His Spirit.

## Redeemed by blood

Murray would not let anyone think of holiness apart from the cross. Throughout the book he returns to the blood of Christ. At the Passover the blood of the lamb stood at the very threshold of Israel’s path to becoming a holy nation. In the tabernacle there was no entrance into the holiest except through blood. And the New Testament makes the same connection.

> Wherefore Jesus also, that he might sanctify the people with his own blood, suffered without the gate. HEBREWS 13:12

His counsel is simple and deeply evangelical: “Always think of holiness, or pray for it, as one redeemed by blood.” The believer who seeks holiness is not climbing towards God to earn a place beside Him. He is a sinner who has been brought near by the blood of Christ, and who lives, in another phrase of Murray’s, under the covering of the blood in its daily cleansing power.

> But if we walk in the light, as he is in the light, we have fellowship one with another, and the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin. 1 JOHN 1:7

Murray later gave this theme a book of its own, The Power of the Blood of Jesus. But the heart of it is here. Holiness grows only in forgiven people.

## The way of holiness

How then does a believer become holy in practice? Murray’s answer, spread across the book, is not a technique but a set of dispositions: faith, which receives Christ as sanctification; obedience, which does what He says; humility, which gives God the glory; separation, which turns from what is unholy; and meditation, which lingers in God’s presence until His holiness begins to shine through. Each of these has a chapter.

> Follow peace with all men, and holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord. HEBREWS 12:14

## A caution about perfection

Murray belonged to the broad holiness movement of the late nineteenth century, and he sometimes wrote in a way that raised expectations of a very high degree of victory over sin in this life. He was more careful than many of his contemporaries, and he never taught that the believer becomes sinless. But readers should keep in mind that Scripture places the completion of our holiness at the appearing of Christ, not before. We are holy in Christ now, truly and really; we are being made holy day by day; and we shall be wholly holy when we see Him. All three are true at once.

> Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is. 1 JOHN 3:2

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask whether you have given holiness the place God gave it in His call, or whether you have treated it as optional.
2. Meditate on 1 Corinthians 1:30. Thank Christ that He is your sanctification, not merely your helper toward it.
3. When you pray for holiness this week, begin by thanking God for the blood of Christ that has brought you near.
4. Choose one of the dispositions Murray names — faith, obedience, humility, separation, meditation — and practise it deliberately for a week.
5. Read one meditation from Holy in Christ in the Ochorus library each day for a week.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Father, You have called me to be holy as You are holy, and I confess I have often wanted Your blessings more than Your likeness.
> Thank You that Christ is made unto me sanctification, and that His blood has brought me near.
> Breathe Your life into me. Let the presence of the Holy One rest on me and shine through my ordinary days.
> Make me more like Your Son now, and complete the work when I see Him as He is. Amen.

# Obey and Abide

> If ye keep my commandments, ye shall abide in my love; even as I have kept my Father’s commandments, and abide in his love. JOHN 15:10

Murray is often read as a teacher of the inner life — of rest, dependence, waiting and surrender — and so he was. But no one who reads him carefully could mistake him for a quietist. Running through all his books is an insistence on plain obedience: doing what Christ says, today, in the things we already know. The reading on John 15:10 in The True Vine is titled Obey and Abide, and those three words could stand over a great deal of his teaching.

## The precept is a promise

Murray noticed that John 15:10 frightens some readers. Christ has just opened the door to His love — Continue ye in my love — and then seems to shut it again with a condition: If ye keep my commandments. Since we do not keep His commandments perfectly, does that mean we cannot abide in His love?

Murray’s answer was firmly no. The command to abide in His love, he said, could not be a command if it were not also a promise. “The precept is a promise.” The love that invites us to abide also reaches out its hand and enables us to obey. So we need not fear to give ourselves to the keeping of His commandments, trusting Him for the strength. And he summed it up beautifully: “Through His will, loved and done, lies the path to His love.”

> For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments: and his commandments are not grievous. 1 JOHN 5:3

## What obedience means

Murray was careful to define what he meant, and the care is important. Obedience, he said in the same reading, refers to our doing all that we know to be God’s will. There may be doubtful things about which we are not yet sure. There may be sins of ignorance, and involuntary stirrings of the flesh that rise up before we can control them. God will deal with these in His own time, by searching and humbling, and will give larger deliverance than we expect. But they are not what Christ is speaking of here. He is speaking of the known command, and the honest will to do it.

This distinction brings great relief to the anxious. Obedience in Murray’s sense is not a perfection of performance. It is a settled disposition: whatever I know Christ wants, I will do, trusting Him for the power.

In With Christ in the School of Prayer he makes the same point. “The Lord does not demand of us a perfect fulfilment of the law; no, but only the childlike and whole-hearted surrender to live as a child with Him in obedience and truth.”

> If ye love me, keep my commandments. JOHN 14:15

## Obedience and knowing

One of Murray’s most useful insights concerns the relationship between obedience and understanding. In The Inner Chamber, answering a young Christian who wanted rules for Bible study, he begins not with methods but with motive. “If your aim is simply to know the Bible well,” he warns, “you will be disappointed.” Knowledge of Scripture can be a blessing, a burden or even a curse, depending on the spirit in which it is sought. The first requirement is a great hunger after righteousness, a great desire to do all God’s will.

> If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether I speak of myself. JOHN 7:17

Murray took Jesus’ words here with full seriousness. Light comes to those who are willing to act on it. The person who reads the Bible to gain information will gain information. The person who reads it to obey will be given understanding. This is why Murray could say, in the same book, that the doer of the word is blessed in his deed. The blessing is not in the hearing but in the doing.

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

## Obedience and prayer

Murray also connected obedience directly to power in prayer. One of the lessons in With Christ in the School of Prayer is titled Obedience the Path to Power in Prayer. The promise is clear enough in the words of John: whatsoever we ask, we receive of Him, because we keep His commandments. The child who finds no pleasure in the father’s presence and will, Murray observes, and yet expects to ask and obtain whatever he likes, will be disappointed.

> And whatsoever we ask, we receive of him, because we keep his commandments, and do those things that are pleasing in his sight. 1 JOHN 3:22

This is not a mechanical bargain, as if obedience purchased answers. It is a relationship. The one who lives close to the Father’s will learns to ask according to it, and such asking is heard.

## As He kept His Father’s commandments

Murray never forgot the second half of John 15:10: even as I have kept my Father’s commandments, and abide in his love. Christ Himself lived this way. His whole earthly life was one of obedience, and in that obedience He abode in the Father’s love. What He asks of us is not a burden He never bore, but a fellowship in His own way of life.

> Though he were a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered. HEBREWS 5:8

## A word of balance

There is a danger in any teaching that links obedience to the experience of love, and Murray’s readers should see it clearly. We do not earn God’s love by obeying. We are loved first, freely, in Christ, before we have done anything. What obedience affects is not God’s love for us but our abiding in the enjoyment of it. A child who disobeys is still a child and still loved; but he cannot enjoy the father’s company while he is in rebellion. Murray knew this — he insisted that the precept is a promise — but it is worth saying plainly, because the conscientious are quick to hear any condition as a threat.

> We love him, because he first loved us. 1 JOHN 4:19

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name one thing you know Christ wants you to do that you have been postponing. Do it this week.
2. Separate in your mind the known commands you are resisting from the involuntary failings that trouble you. Bring the first to obedience and the second to God’s patient dealing.
3. Before you read Scripture tomorrow, ask not only What does this mean? but What will I do about it?
4. If your prayer life feels powerless, ask whether there is a point of known disobedience standing in the way.
5. If you tend to hear commands as threats, meditate on 1 John 4:19 before you meditate on John 15:10.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You kept Your Father’s commandments and abode in His love. Teach me to walk the same way.
> I will do what I know You want. Give me the strength I do not have, for Your precept is Your promise.
> Show me the thing I have been postponing, and make me willing to do it today.
> And keep me always mindful that You loved me first, before I had obeyed at all. Amen.

# The Fruit of the Spirit Is Love

> A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you, that ye also love one another. JOHN 13:34

It would be easy to read Murray’s teaching on the deeper life and come away thinking it is chiefly about private experience: surrender, filling, rest, the inner chamber. The second address in Absolute Surrender exists to correct exactly that impression. It is called The Fruit of the Spirit is Love, and in it Murray insists that the surest test of any claim to be filled with the Holy Spirit is not power, not feeling, not gifts, but love.

## A searchlight

Murray begins by distinguishing two ways of thinking about the Spirit. Under the Old Testament, he says, the Spirit often came upon people for revelation or for power to do God’s work, but did not dwell in them. Many Christians, he feared, want only that older gift — power for service — and know little of the New Testament gift of the indwelling Spirit, renewing the whole life. When God gives the Holy Spirit, Murray writes, “His great object is the formation of a holy character.”

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law. GALATIANS 5:22–23

So he offers the verse as a searchlight. Has it been our experience, he asks, that the more we have of the Holy Spirit the more loving we become? If our thoughts about the Spirit and our experiences of the holy life have not made us more loving, something is wrong with them. That is a test that cuts through a great deal of religious excitement.

## Because God is love

Why is love the first fruit? Because the Spirit is the Spirit of God, and God is love. Murray drew out what this means in one of his characteristic sentences: “God has no selfishness, God keeps nothing to Himself.” It is His nature to give. He pours life into every creature, glory into the angels, and His own love into His redeemed children. The Father gave all things to the Son; the Son gave Himself for the world; the Spirit is the living love of God overflowing into human hearts.

> And hope maketh not ashamed; because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us. ROMANS 5:5

If this is who the Spirit is, Murray argued, He cannot be less a Spirit of love when He comes to dwell in us than He is in God. He cannot change His nature.

## The badge

Murray noticed that before Jesus promised the Spirit in John 14, He gave the new commandment in John 13. The order is not accidental. The Spirit is promised to people who have been commanded to love one another as Christ loved them. And Christ gave them a badge by which the world would recognise them.

> By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another. JOHN 13:35

Murray asked a painful question. If the world were asked whether it had seen the church wearing the badge of love, what would it say? He feared it would say that what it had heard of the church was quarrelling and separation. He was not writing as an outsider. He had lived through bitter controversies in his own denomination and knew the divisions of the church at close quarters.

## A cracked vessel

One of the most memorable images in the address concerns unity. A vessel that ought to be whole, Murray said, cannot be filled if it has been broken into pieces. You can dip a little water into a fragment, but if you want the vessel full, it must be whole. He applied this to the church: one of the great reasons God cannot bless His people as He would is the want of love among them. If we want the fulness of the Spirit, we must be willing to be melted together.

> Endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. There is one body, and one Spirit, even as ye are called in one hope of your calling. EPHESIANS 4:3–4

This takes the pursuit of spiritual blessing out of the realm of private experience and plants it in the congregation, the family, and the difficult relationship. A Christian who seeks the fulness of the Spirit while nursing a grudge against a brother is, on Murray’s reading, asking God to fill a cracked vessel.

## Love conquers self

Murray’s deepest reason for insisting on love was that nothing else can defeat selfishness. Self, he says, is the great curse, whether in relation to God or to others. Sin made the human race selfish — Adam blaming the woman, Cain killing his brother — and selfishness cannot love. Christ came to restore love to the world, and the Spirit is sent to banish the selfishness and envy and pride that sin brought in.

> Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up. 1 CORINTHIANS 13:4

He was very practical about where this love is tested. It is tested in temper, in speech, in the way we treat those who irritate or oppose us. He called love the fruit of the Spirit all the day and every day. And he made a solemn link with prayer: if we want to pray with power and to see the Spirit poured out, we must first be at peace with one another.

## What this chapter guards against

This teaching is a safeguard for everything else in Murray’s writing. Any doctrine of surrender, filling or holiness that does not issue in love has gone wrong somewhere. It is possible to be earnest, disciplined and devout and still be hard, critical and cold. Murray knew that the people most eager for the deeper life are sometimes the least pleasant to live with, and this address is his warning to them.

> Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, and have not charity, I am become as sounding brass, or a tinkling cymbal. 1 CORINTHIANS 13:1

It also corrects a narrowness that can creep into any movement. Murray loved and worked with Christians of many traditions, and he believed the Spirit’s love reached across the lines that divide believers. The vessel is not a single denomination. It is the whole body of Christ.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Apply Murray’s searchlight: has your growth in religious knowledge and experience made you more loving? Answer honestly.
2. Identify one person you find hard to love. Pray for them by name every day this week, and do one kind thing for them.
3. If there is a broken relationship with another believer that you could help to mend, take one step toward it.
4. Watch your temper and your tongue for a day as a test of the Spirit’s fruit in you. Confess what you find.
5. Before you next pray for spiritual blessing, ask whether you are asking God to fill a cracked vessel.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Spirit, You are the love of God poured into my heart, and I confess how little of that love the world has seen in me.
> Search me. Show me where my religion has made me hard, critical or cold.
> Conquer my selfishness. Make me patient, kind, free of envy and pride, loving even those who oppose me.
> Melt Your people together, and let us wear the badge of Christ’s love before the world. Amen.

# The Lord for the Body

> Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. JAMES 5:14

This chapter must be handled with more care than any other in the book. Please read it to the end. The cautions are not an afterthought; they are part of the teaching.

## The silent years

In 1879 Murray’s voice failed. For about two years the most sought-after preacher in his church could not preach. It was a long trial for a man whose whole calling was to speak, and it led him to examine the teaching on divine healing then spreading among evangelicals in Europe and America. In 1882 he went to London, to a home where the sick were received for prayer, and he returned with his voice restored. He resumed his ministry and carried it on for another three decades.

Out of that experience came the book Divine Healing, first published in the 1880s and later in English. It is a series of short chapters, most of them expositions of a particular text, and it is written with his usual warmth and directness. It is also, of all his books, the one that most needs to be read with discernment.

## What he taught

Murray’s central claim is that healing for the body belongs within the redemption Christ purchased. He built this on Matthew’s use of Isaiah 53.

> That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying, Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses. MATTHEW 8:17

From this and from the plain command of James 5, Murray argued that the believer may look to Christ for the healing of the body as truly as for the forgiveness of sins. The body is the temple of the Holy Ghost, and the Lord is for the body. Sickness, he taught, is often connected with sin and used by God as chastening, and healing is to be sought with confession and faith. He urged the church to recover a practice it had largely abandoned: the elders praying over the sick and anointing them in the name of the Lord.

> And the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him. JAMES 5:15

## What he said about doctors

Murray’s view of medicine was more nuanced than is sometimes supposed, and it is only fair to set out what the book actually says. The chapter Jesus and the Doctors begins: “We may be thankful to God for having given us doctors.” Their calling, he writes, is one of the most noble, and some of them are zealous servants of Christ. Elsewhere, asked whether the use of remedies excludes the prayer of faith, he answers no — for, he says, the experience of a large number of believers testifies that in answer to their prayers “God has often blessed the use of remedies, and made them a means of healing.”

But the book goes further. Murray held that healing received directly from Christ, without medical means, was the better way — more spiritually fruitful, and more fully the fulfilment of James 5. He wrote that healing through physicians, even believing ones, can leave the patient further from God than before, and he encouraged the sick who had faith to look to Christ alone. In the chapter The Will of God he argued that a prayer for healing which adds if it be Thy will is praying with submission but is not the prayer of faith, because Scripture has already made God’s will for healing plain.

Even in this book he gives thanks for doctors, and nothing in it condemns the believer who uses them. It is sometimes said that in later life he moderated his position further; this companion will not claim more about his later views than can be shown. The book is what he left us, and the book is what we must weigh.

## What is genuinely biblical here

Much in Murray’s emphasis is plainly scriptural and has been lost in many churches. God heals, and nothing in Scripture retires Him from healing. James 5 is a standing instruction to the church, and a great many congregations ignore it. The body matters to God: He made it, His Son took one, and it will be raised. And Christ’s compassion for the sick fills the Gospels.

> What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own? 1 CORINTHIANS 6:19

Murray was also right that sickness can be an occasion for searching the heart. James himself links the prayer for healing with confession. A believer who is ill loses nothing by asking God whether there is anything He wishes to show him.

## Where it must be qualified

Now the necessary corrections, stated without softening, because people have been hurt here.

Scripture does not promise healing in every case. Paul left Trophimus sick at Miletus (2 Timothy 4:20). Epaphroditus was sick nigh unto death (Philippians 2:27). Timothy had often infirmities, and Paul’s counsel was a practical remedy, not a rebuke (1 Timothy 5:23). And Paul himself asked three times for his thorn to be removed, and was refused.

> And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:9

Sickness is not always tied to the sick person’s sin. Jesus explicitly denied it of the man born blind (John 9:3), and the book of Job exists partly to rebuke those who assume it. Any suggestion that continued illness proves unconfessed sin or weak faith is cruel, and it adds guilt to suffering.

Praying if it be Thy will is not unbelief. It is the prayer of the Son of God in Gethsemane, who had more faith than anyone who has ever lived.

> O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt. MATTHEW 26:39

And medicine is not a lesser way. Luke was the beloved physician. Isaiah applied a lump of figs to Hezekiah’s boil in the very story in which God added fifteen years to his life. Scripture nowhere sets treatment and trust against each other. Nothing in this chapter or in Murray’s book should lead any reader to refuse, delay or stop medical care. Pray, and see your doctor.

## How to hold it

Held with these corrections, the substance of Murray’s teaching is worth recovering. Pray boldly for the sick. Obey James 5. Expect God to act, and leave the outcome with Him. When He heals, give Him the glory. When He does not, do not conclude that He has not answered. The final healing of the body waits for the resurrection, when this mortal shall put on immortality.

> Who shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body. PHILIPPIANS 3:21

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Pray specifically and expectantly for someone who is ill this week, without promising them an outcome God has not promised.
2. If your church never practises James 5:14, ask your leaders why.
3. If you have been made to feel that your illness is a verdict on your faith or proof of hidden sin, reject that. It is not the teaching of Scripture.
4. Take your medicine and keep your appointments. Trust and treatment are not rivals.
5. Pray Christ’s Gethsemane prayer about the thing you most want God to change, and mean both halves of it.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You bore our sicknesses and carried our sorrows, and You are still moved with compassion for the suffering.
> Teach me to pray boldly for the sick, and to leave the outcome in Your hands.
> Where You heal, let me give You the glory. Where You do not, let me hear You say that Your grace is sufficient.
> Guard me from ever adding to another’s burden by suggesting that their sickness is their own fault. And keep my eyes on the day when You will raise these bodies in glory. Amen.

# Waiting on God

> Truly my soul waiteth upon God: from him cometh my salvation. PSALM 62:1

Waiting on God is a small book of daily readings for a month, each built round a verse of Scripture that uses the word wait. Murray wrote it in the mid-1890s, in his late sixties, and near its beginning he admits something surprising for a man who had been teaching the deeper life for decades: that he felt he was only beginning to see how the deepest truth about God and our relation to Him centres in this waiting. He had come, as he put it, to regard waiting on God as the keynote of life.

## What waiting is

What does Murray mean by waiting? Not idleness, and not simply patience in trouble, though it includes that. He means the settled posture of a soul that expects everything from God and nothing from itself. It combines, in his words from the reading on Jacob’s cry, the deep sense of our entire helplessness and the perfect confidence that God will work it all.

> I have waited for thy salvation, O LORD. GENESIS 49:18

We have already met the foundation of this in his teaching on the creature. A creature lives by receiving. Waiting is simply that dependence made into a habit of the heart. In the introduction to the book Murray draws the comparison with breathing: even the regenerate man, he says, has no power of goodness in himself, and “waiting on God is just as indispensable, and must be just as continuous and unbroken, as the breathing that maintains his natural life.”

## Praying and waiting

Is waiting on God different from praying? Murray thought it was, and his answer is one of the most useful things in the book: “that there may be much praying with but very little waiting on God.”

We can pray the way we fill in a form: state the request, sign off, and go about our business. Waiting is what happens when we linger. It is the pause in which we stop talking and turn our attention to God Himself — to His presence, His will, His timing. Murray thought much prayer was hurried and self-directed, and that it would be transformed if believers learned to spend even a few minutes simply waiting before God before they asked anything.

> Be still, and know that I am God: I will be exalted among the heathen, I will be exalted in the earth. PSALM 46:10

He was also frank about the church’s neglect of it. At all our conventions and gatherings, he observed, too little time is given to waiting on God. Meetings are filled with speaking, and the silence in which God might speak is crowded out.

## God waits too

One of the loveliest readings in the book turns the whole subject around. It is titled Who Waits on Us, and its text is from Isaiah.

> And therefore will the LORD wait, that he may be gracious unto you, and therefore will he be exalted, that he may have mercy upon you: for the LORD is a God of judgment: blessed are all they that wait for him. ISAIAH 30:18

The Lord waits to be gracious. Murray thought this changed everything. If we saw God in His love, he wrote, and believed that He waits to be gracious, waits to be our life and to work all in us, then waiting on Him would become our highest joy, the natural response of our hearts to His love. We do not wait on a reluctant God who must be persuaded. We wait on a God who has been waiting for us.

## Strength renewed

Murray drew out many of the fruits of waiting across the readings: supplies for every need, instruction, light in the heart, courage, hope in times of darkness, and the new song. The most famous is in Isaiah 40.

> But they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint. ISAIAH 40:31

He noticed the order: mounting up, running, walking. The spectacular comes first, the ordinary last, and the ordinary is the hardest. To walk day after day and not faint is the real test of a life renewed by waiting on God.

Murray also had a reading titled Patiently, and another titled Quietly, and he was honest that waiting often involves delay. God’s timing is not ours, and the soul that waits must learn to rest in not knowing when.

> It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD. LAMENTATIONS 3:26

## Continually, and only

The last readings of the book press two words. The first is continually: waiting is not an exercise for the morning only but the undercurrent of the whole day. The second is only. Its text is from Psalm 62.

> My soul, wait thou only upon God; for my expectation is from him. PSALM 62:5

Only is the hard word. Most of us are willing to wait on God as one support among several. We wait on Him and also on our plans, our contacts, our savings and our cleverness. Murray was not against plans or work; he founded institutions and raised money for missions. But he believed the heart must rest on God alone, so that every other support is held loosely and none is trusted in His place.

## Where care is needed

Waiting on God can be misunderstood as passivity, and some readers of Murray have used it as a reason not to act. That was never his intent. The same book that tells us to wait tells us to keep God’s ways, and Murray himself was one of the most active men of his generation. Waiting is not the alternative to obedience; it is the spirit in which obedience is done. And waiting is not a technique for extracting answers. It is fellowship with a Person. The blessing of waiting, Murray says, is that it is itself the highest salvation — to be near to God is already the answer.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Before you pray tomorrow, spend five minutes in silence simply turning your attention to God, asking nothing.
2. Notice this week how much of your praying is hurried. Try once to linger, and to wait.
3. Meditate on Isaiah 30:18. Let the thought that God waits to be gracious to you reshape how you come to Him.
4. Name one thing you are trusting alongside God. Hold it more loosely, and let your expectation be from Him.
5. Read one day of Waiting on God in the Ochorus library each morning for a week.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, my soul waits upon You. I have prayed much and waited little, and I have filled my life with noise.
> Teach me to be still and know that You are God. Teach me to linger in Your presence and to expect everything from You.
> Thank You that You wait to be gracious. Let my waiting be the answer of my heart to Your love.
> Renew my strength, that I may walk and not faint. Be my only expectation. Amen.

# The Inner Chamber

> But thou, when thou prayest, enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret; and thy Father which seeth in secret shall reward thee openly. MATTHEW 6:6

Murray’s phrase for the place of secret prayer was the inner chamber, taken from the older rendering of Jesus’ words in the Sermon on the Mount. He gave the phrase to a book in 1905, The Inner Chamber, and it runs through his writing on prayer from the beginning. In With Christ in the School of Prayer he calls the inner chamber Jesus’ schoolroom: the place where the pupil puts himself daily in the Master’s presence. Everything he taught about abiding, humility and waiting on God comes to a point here. It is in the shut room, alone with the Father, that the life is either sustained or lost.

## A place, and a time

Murray was practical. The first thing the Lord teaches His disciples about prayer, he says in the School of Prayer, is that they must have a secret place — some solitary spot where each one can be alone with God. Christ had already taught at Samaria that true worship is not tied to places. And yet He wants each disciple to choose a fixed spot where He can meet him daily. That spot may be anywhere, and it may change as our circumstances change, but there must be one, with a quiet time in which we place ourselves before the Master.

> And in the morning, rising up a great while before day, he went out, and departed into a solitary place, and there prayed. MARK 1:35

The Inner Chamber begins with a reading called The Morning Hour. Murray notes that believers of every generation have regarded the beginning of the day as the fitting time to seek God, whether they called it the morning watch, the quiet hour or the quiet time. He did not lay down a fixed length. What mattered was not the minutes but the purpose.

> My voice shalt thou hear in the morning, O LORD; in the morning will I direct my prayer unto thee, and will look up. PSALM 5:3

## Not an end in itself

That purpose is the most important thing Murray says about the inner chamber. “The morning watch must not be regarded as an end in itself,” he writes. It is not sufficient that it gives a pleasant time of prayer and Bible study and a measure of refreshment. It is a means to an end. “And that end is to secure the presence of Christ for the whole day.”

This transforms the morning hour. It is no longer a devotional duty to be completed and ticked off. It is the time in which the believer, in Murray’s image, fastens the link for the day between himself and Christ. What happens in the room determines what happens outside it. If the link is made firm in the morning, the day can be lived in His presence. If it is not, the day is lived without Him, however busy with religious activity it may be.

> I have set the LORD always before me: because he is at my right hand, I shall not be moved. PSALM 16:8

## A terrible danger

In the reading titled The Door Shut — Alone with God, Murray names a danger that every earnest Christian should hear. You are in danger, he says, “of substituting Prayer and Bible Study for living fellowship with God.” We can be so absorbed in our requests, or so interested in our Bible study, that the Word of God itself becomes a substitute for God Himself. We go out into the day having read and prayed, but without having met Him.

The one essential thing, Murray insists, is fellowship with God. Of more importance than all our requests, however urgent, and than all our earnestness to pray aright, is the childlike assurance that the Father sees us, that we have met Him, and that with His eye on us and ours on Him we are enjoying real communion.

> Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you. JAMES 4:8

This is why Jesus names the Father three times in those few verses of Matthew 6. The inner chamber is not a study or an office. It is the place where a child meets his Father.

## The day given away

Murray pictured the morning prayer as a kind of handing over. He wrote of the nobility that would come into life if secret prayer were not only an asking for comfort, light or strength, but the giving away of life for one day into the keeping of a mighty and faithful God. That is a beautiful way to begin a day: not with a list of demands, but with an offering. Here is this day, Lord. It is Yours. Go with me.

> Commit thy way unto the LORD; trust also in him; and he shall bring it to pass. PSALM 37:5

## The open reward

The next reading in the book is titled The Open Door — The Open Reward, from the last clause of Jesus’ saying. What is done in secret, Murray says, is rewarded openly. Where secret fellowship with the Father is maintained, the public life will carry the reward. He did not mean public recognition. He meant that the life lived before others will bear the marks of the hour spent before God — in peace, in patience, in power, in love.

> They that sow in tears shall reap in joy. PSALM 126:5

## Where care is needed

The morning watch was a strong emphasis of the student and holiness movements of Murray’s day, and some of the language surrounding it could make it sound like a condition of blessing that must be kept at all costs. For many people — parents of small children, shift workers, carers, the chronically ill — a fixed early hour is simply not possible, and they can be made to feel second-class. Murray’s own principle answers this. He said the spot may change, and the length is not fixed; what matters is the purpose. The inner chamber is wherever and whenever a believer shuts the door, even briefly, to meet the Father. God is not measuring the minutes. He is looking for the child.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Identify your inner chamber: a place and time where you can be alone with God. If you do not have one, choose one today.
2. Tomorrow, before asking for anything, spend a moment making sure you have actually met the Father.
3. Ask whether your Bible reading has become a substitute for fellowship with God. If so, read less and linger more.
4. End your morning prayer by giving the day away: This day is Yours; go with me.
5. If your circumstances make a fixed hour impossible, find the moment you can have, and refuse the guilt of the one you cannot.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You see in secret, and You wait for me behind the shut door. I have too often hurried past You.
> Teach me to keep the inner chamber, not as a duty but as a meeting. Let me know that You see me, and let my eyes be on You.
> Keep me from putting prayer or study in the place of Yourself.
> I give You this day. Go with me, keep me, and let the hour spent with You be seen in the hours spent with others. Amen.

# With Christ in the School of Prayer

> And it came to pass, that, as he was praying in a certain place, when he ceased, one of his disciples said unto him, Lord, teach us to pray. LUKE 11:1

If Andrew Murray is remembered for one book, it is With Christ in the School of Prayer, published in 1885. It is arranged as thirty-one lessons, one for each day of a month, and each takes a saying of Jesus about prayer and presses it home. The first lessons were also issued separately as a small book, Lord, Teach Us To Pray, and both are in the Ochorus library. The book has been in print ever since it first appeared, and it has taught a great many Christians, in many languages, to pray.

## The only Teacher

The book opens with the disciples’ request. They had watched Jesus pray. They had begun to see the connection between His astonishing public life and His hidden life with the Father. And so they asked, not for a lecture on prayer, but to be taught to do it.

Murray invites his readers to enrol in the same school. Christ, he says, is the only Teacher. Books, methods and examples may help, but only He can teach us to pray, and He does it by His Spirit, in the inner chamber, as we practise. The book is not a manual to be read through once. It is a set of lessons to be learned slowly, on our knees.

> Likewise the Spirit also helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought: but the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered. ROMANS 8:26

## So simple, and so high

In the first lesson Murray gives one of his most quoted descriptions of prayer. “Though in its beginnings prayer is so simple that the feeblest child can pray,” he writes, “yet it is at the same time the highest and holiest work to which man can rise.” It is fellowship with the unseen and most holy God. The powers of the eternal world are placed at its disposal. It is the channel of all blessing.

That double truth rescues prayer from two opposite errors. Some think prayer is too simple to need learning — you just talk to God. Others think it is too lofty for ordinary believers, a speciality of saints and mystics. Murray says it is both simple and high, and that is exactly why we need a Teacher. A child can begin; no one ever finishes.

## Our Father

The lessons on the Lord’s Prayer and on the Father’s giving are among the warmest in the book. Murray notes that in the Old Testament the saints had scarcely ever ventured to address God as their own Father. Jesus puts the word on the lips of His disciples from the first.

> After this manner therefore pray ye: Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name. MATTHEW 6:9

In the lesson titled The Infinite Fatherliness of God, Murray takes Jesus’ argument from earthly fathers. If sinful fathers know how to give good gifts to their children, how much more will the Father in heaven? The power of the promise, he says, lies in the relationship. And he draws a searching conclusion: “Live as a child of God, then you will be able to pray as a child, and as a child you will most assuredly be heard.”

> If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him? MATTHEW 7:11

Prayer, in other words, is not a technique detached from life. It flows out of a relationship, and its power depends on living in that relationship — in the Father’s house, in His love, in His will.

## God means prayer to have an answer

The book’s preface contains the conviction that drives it. Murray writes that we have grown so used to limiting the promises of God that we cannot read the plainest words of Jesus without adding qualifying clauses. If there is one thing the church needs to learn, he says, “it is that God means prayer to have an answer.”

> Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you. MATTHEW 7:7

This was not naïve. Murray’s lessons deal carefully with the conditions attached to Jesus’ promises: praying in His name, abiding in Him, obedience, love, faith, perseverance. But he refused to let the conditions swallow the promises. God hears. God answers. The believer who prays expecting nothing has not understood the Teacher.

## Christ the Intercessor

The later lessons rise from the disciple’s prayer to Christ’s own. Murray has lessons on Christ the Intercessor, Christ the High Priest and Christ the Sacrifice. Our praying, he says, is taken up into His. The One who taught the disciples to pray now ever lives to make intercession for them, and our prayers are heard because they are joined to His.

> Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them. HEBREWS 7:25

This is the deepest comfort in the book. Our prayers are weak, distracted and often confused. But they do not rise alone. The great High Priest carries them.

## Where care is needed

Murray urged his readers to take Jesus’ promises about prayer simply and literally, and his impatience with qualifying clauses is a healthy rebuke to unbelief. But Scripture itself supplies some qualifications, and they are not unbelief. John writes that we are heard when we ask according to God’s will. Paul asked three times and was refused. Jesus Himself prayed, not as I will, but as thou wilt. A reader who takes Murray’s boldness without Scripture’s balance may conclude, when a prayer is not answered as he hoped, that his faith has failed. Murray’s whole book points the other way: to the Father’s love and wisdom, which gives good things — sometimes the thing asked, sometimes something better.

> And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us. 1 JOHN 5:14

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask Christ, in your own words, to enrol you afresh in His school of prayer.
2. Read one lesson of With Christ in the School of Prayer each day for a month, and pray its closing prayer.
3. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly once this week, stopping at each phrase to make it your own.
4. Ask whether you pray expecting an answer. Choose one specific request and pray it expectantly.
5. When you feel your prayers are weak, remember that Christ ever lives to intercede. Thank Him for carrying them.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, as Your disciples asked, so I ask: Lord, teach me to pray.
> I have treated prayer as too simple to learn and too high to attempt. Take me into Your school, and teach me as a child.
> Let me live as a child of the Father, so that I may pray as a child and be heard.
> Take up my weak prayers into Your own intercession, and teach me to expect the answer You mean to give. Amen.

# The Faith That Takes

> Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them. MARK 11:24

The middle lessons of With Christ in the School of Prayer are the boldest things Murray ever wrote. They carry titles such as The Certainty of the Answer to Prayer, Prayer Must Be Definite, The Faith That Takes, The Secret of Believing Prayer and The Power of Persevering Prayer. Together they set out what he believed about the prayer of faith: that it is definite, that it rests on God’s word, that it receives before it sees, and that it does not give up. They have strengthened the prayers of many and troubled the consciences of some, and both effects deserve attention.

## Say what you want

Murray’s lesson on definite prayer takes the story of blind Bartimaeus. The man had been crying out for mercy, loudly and often. Jesus knew perfectly well what he needed. And yet He stopped and asked him: What wilt thou that I should do unto thee?

> And Jesus answered and said unto him, What wilt thou that I should do unto thee? The blind man said unto him, Lord, that I might receive my sight. MARK 10:51

Why ask? Murray’s answer is wise. Not because Christ does not understand our cry, but for our sake. “Our prayers must not be a vague appeal to His mercy, an indefinite cry for blessing, but the distinct expression of definite need.” Definite prayer searches us. It makes us find out what we really need, test whether our desires are honest, judge them by God’s word, and watch for the answer when it comes.

Murray noticed how vague most prayer is. People pray for deliverance from sin without naming any sin. They pray for revival without any particular place where they are looking for it. They pray for God to bless others without knowing what blessing they are asking. Such prayers can hardly be answered in any way that would be recognised.

## Believe that ye receive

The lesson titled The Faith That Takes is built on Mark 11:24. Murray begins by marvelling at the size of the promise: all things whatsoever. He will not let reason cut it down to what seems safe. Faith, he says, is not a mere conclusion drawn from premises. It is the ear that has heard God say what He will do. And so, in his phrase, “faith is the pledge and forerunner of the coming answer.”

> Jesus said unto him, If thou canst believe, all things are possible to him that believeth. MARK 9:23

The distinctive thing in this teaching is the tense. Jesus says, believe that ye receive — or in the older reading Murray used, believe that ye have received. Faith takes the answer from God’s hand before it is seen. It thanks Him for what He has promised as though it were already done, and then waits for it to appear.

## The secret: knowing His will

Here a reader must pay close attention, because Murray is often misrepresented at this point, sometimes by his admirers. He strongly resisted the habit of adding if it be Thy will to every prayer as a way of weakening Christ’s promise. But he did not teach that faith can make God do whatever we choose. In the same lesson he writes: “Ere we can believe we must find out and know what God’s will is.” Believing is the act of a soul surrendered to the Word and the Spirit. The prayer of faith is not a lever for our wishes; it is the confident taking of what God has shown us He wills to give.

> And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us: and if we know that he hear us, whatsoever we ask, we know that we have the petitions that we desired of him. 1 JOHN 5:14–15

This is the key to the whole teaching. Where God has plainly promised — forgiveness, the gift of the Spirit, wisdom, grace to endure, the spread of His kingdom — faith may take boldly and give thanks before it sees. Where God has not made His particular will known, faith seeks it humbly, and may well pray as Christ prayed in the garden.

## Do not faint

What if the answer does not come? Murray’s lesson on persevering prayer admits that this is one of the great mysteries of the prayer life. Why should a God who loves to bless need to be asked again and again, sometimes for years? He warned against the easy retreat that looks like piety: deciding that God must have secret reasons and ceasing to pray.

> And he spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint. LUKE 18:1

He used two images. Water must sometimes gather behind a dam before it comes down in force; so prayer may need to accumulate until the measure is full. And a ploughman must take ten thousand steps and sow ten thousand seeds before the harvest; so each persevering prayer is part of the preparation for the answer. Not one is lost.

> And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not. GALATIANS 6:9

The book closes with a study of George Müller of Bristol, who for half a century had asked God alone for the needs of his orphan houses, and in whom Murray saw the lessons of the whole book drawn together: definite petitions, grounded in the Word, sought for the glory of God alone, and held with unwavering trust.

## Where care is needed

Murray’s boldness is a gift, and the church needs it. But these lessons, read without their balancing lines, have hurt people. A believer who prays for a sick child, believes that he has received, and then watches the child die, may conclude that his faith was defective and his loss his own fault. That conclusion is false and cruel. Jesus believed perfectly and still prayed, not as I will. Paul prayed in faith and was told that grace would be sufficient instead. Faith rests on God, not on our certainty about an outcome He has not promised. When Murray says that true faith cannot be disappointed, the deepest truth in that sentence is that God never fails those who trust Him — even when He answers otherwise than they asked.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Take one vague prayer you often pray and make it definite. Write down exactly what you are asking God to do.
2. Find one promise in Scripture that clearly applies to you, and pray it with thanksgiving as though already received.
3. Name something you have stopped praying for through discouragement. Take it up again this week.
4. Before praying about a matter on which God has not made His will plain, ask Him first to show you His will.
5. If you have ever blamed your faith for an answer that did not come, bring that grief to God and lay the blame down.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You asked the blind man what he wanted, and You ask me too. Teach me to pray plainly and definitely.
> Where You have promised, give me faith to take what You have given and to thank You before I see it.
> Where I do not know Your will, make me humble enough to seek it and ready to say, not as I will, but as You will.
> And when the answer tarries, keep me from fainting. Let me pray on until the measure is full. Amen.

# The Sin of Prayerlessness

> Moreover as for me, God forbid that I should sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you. 1 SAMUEL 12:23

In the 1890s Murray travelled widely among the churches of South Africa, speaking at conventions and ministers’ meetings. Everywhere, he tells us in The Ministry of Intercession, the same confession came up: We pray too little! Ministers said it, missionaries said it, earnest lay people said it. And with the confession came something that troubled him even more — a kind of hopelessness, a sense that with the pressure of duty and the force of habit, no real change could be expected. He wrote The Ministry of Intercession, subtitled A Plea for More Prayer, partly to answer that despair. Years later, in The Prayer Life, he returned to the same burden with even greater force.

## We pray too little

Murray gathered a series of honest testimonies at the start of the book. He tells of a well-known Edinburgh minister, Dr Whyte, who had confessed to his fellow ministers that as a young man he had given his spare hours to study, and had since learned that prayer was of more importance. He tells of a London minister who protested that with a morning post of ten or twelve letters and a day of committee meetings, he could not see how more time could be given to prayer. And he tells of a district superintendent who described a devout routine — half an hour with God in the morning, prayers breathed through the day, evening devotions — and then admitted that of the intense, definite, importunate prayer of which Scripture speaks, he knew little.

> Ye have not, because ye ask not. JAMES 4:2

Murray’s answer to the busy London minister was simple. It was a question of whether God’s call on our time was of more importance than man’s. If God was waiting to meet us and give us power for His work, it was short-sighted to put other work in the place that belonged to Him.

## Restraining prayer as sin

The most searching chapter in the book is titled Restraining Prayer — Is It Sin? Murray takes his title from a hard word spoken to Job: thou restrainest prayer before God. And he takes as his main text the story of Achan, whose hidden sin brought defeat on all Israel at Ai.

> Therefore the children of Israel could not stand before their enemies, but turned their backs before their enemies, because they were accursed: neither will I be with you any more, except ye destroy the accursed from among you. JOSHUA 7:12

From this story Murray drew a sequence of lessons. The presence of God is the great privilege of His people and their only power. Defeat is always a sign that His presence has been lost. The loss of His presence is always owing to some hidden sin. God Himself must uncover the sin. And his conclusion was sobering: the lack of prayer is itself such a sin. It is not merely a weakness or a regrettable habit. It is unfaithfulness to our consecration to God.

What does prayerlessness reveal? Murray lists it plainly. It shows that we have little taste for fellowship with God; that our faith rests more on our own efforts than on His power; that we have little sense of the blessing He waits to give; that we are unwilling to sacrifice our ease; and that our abiding in Christ is too feeble to prevail. When the pressure of Christian work becomes our excuse for not seeking God’s presence, he says, it proves that we have no right sense of our absolute dependence on Him.

## The first step

Why call it sin? Not to add guilt, Murray insisted, but to open the way to deliverance. “To see that our prayer-sins are indeed sins,” he wrote, “is the first step to a true and Divine deliverance from them.” As long as we treat prayerlessness as an unfortunate circumstance — a busy season, a temperament, a lack of discipline — we will try to fix it by resolutions and schedules, and we will fail. When we see it as sin, we bring it to the cross, where sin is dealt with.

> If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. 1 JOHN 1:9

## Law and grace

The next chapter, Who Shall Deliver?, is where the book turns from diagnosis to cure, and it is some of the best pastoral writing Murray ever did. He tells of an earnest man who came to him in distress. He had no relish for the Word, his prayers felt heartless, and the more he sought the Spirit the farther away He seemed. Murray told him that the whole matter was simple: he was living under law and not under grace.

Then he spelled out the difference in a passage worth reading slowly. “Law demands; grace bestows.” Law commands but gives no strength; grace promises and performs. Law burdens, casts down and condemns; grace comforts and makes strong and glad. Law appeals to self to do its utmost; grace points to Christ to do all.

> For sin shall not have dominion over you: for ye are not under the law, but under grace. ROMANS 6:14

The man’s first step, Murray said, was not to strive harder against his failure but to accept it — to confess his helplessness and sink down before God, and let grace do what effort never could. This applies directly to prayer. The person who resolves to pray more, and fails, and resolves again, is living under law. The cure is not a stronger resolution but a turning to Christ, who will teach and enable us to pray.

> Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them. HEBREWS 7:25

## Where care is needed

Calling prayerlessness sin is strong language, and it can land badly on the weary. A mother with small children, a man working two jobs, a believer struggling with depression, may hear it as one more condemnation. Murray was speaking chiefly to ministers and Christian workers, who had chosen to fill their days with religious business and then complained they had no time for God. The charge fits them precisely. For others, the principle still applies, but it must be applied with the gentleness of the grace Murray himself preached. God knows our frame. He remembers that we are dust.

> Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust. PSALM 103:13–14

The point is not to be crushed. The point is to be honest, to confess, and to come.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Say the confession plainly to God, if it is true of you: I pray too little.
2. Ask what your prayerlessness reveals — about your trust, your priorities, your sense of dependence. Write down the answer.
3. If Christian work is your excuse for not praying, reduce the work before you reduce the prayer.
4. Instead of making another resolution to pray more, confess your helplessness and ask Christ to teach and enable you.
5. If you are weary rather than careless, receive Psalm 103:14 as God’s word to you, and offer Him what you can.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I confess that I pray too little, and that I have called it busyness when it is sin.
> My prayerlessness shows how little I depend on You, and how much I trust my own efforts. Forgive me.
> I cannot mend it by resolutions. I bring it to You. Take me out from under law and set me under grace.
> Teach me to pray, and give me the desire I do not have, for You ever live to intercede for me. Amen.

# God Seeks Intercessors

> And I sought for a man among them, that should make up the hedge, and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I should not destroy it: but I found none. EZEKIEL 22:30

Murray’s teaching on prayer did not end in the inner chamber. It went out from there to the whole world. He believed that the highest form of prayer is intercession — prayer for others — and that the church’s missionary task depended on it more than on money, organisation or even workers. The Ministry of Intercession ends with two chapters that gather this conviction together: God Seeks Intercessors and The Coming Revival. They are the natural end of this book too.

## The magnitudes

God Seeks Intercessors opens with an image from astronomy. A person who looks at the night sky without any sense of the size and distance of the stars cannot understand what he is seeing. So, Murray says, it is with intercession: everything depends on a right sense of the magnitudes.

He named three. There is a world, with needs beyond counting, waiting to be helped by intercession. There is a God in heaven, with an all-sufficient supply for every need, waiting to be asked. And there is a church, with its high calling and sure promises, waiting to be roused to its responsibility. Between the world’s need and God’s supply stands the intercessor.

> I have set watchmen upon thy walls, O Jerusalem, which shall never hold their peace day nor night: ye that make mention of the LORD, keep not silence, … and give him no rest, till he establish, and till he make Jerusalem a praise in the earth. ISAIAH 62:6–7

## Pray the Lord of the harvest

In With Christ in the School of Prayer there is a lesson titled Prayer Provides Labourers. Murray noticed that Jesus rarely told His disciples what to pray for, leaving that to their sense of need and the Spirit’s leading. But here He gave them one specific request.

> Then saith he unto his disciples, The harvest truly is plenteous, but the labourers are few; pray ye therefore the Lord of the harvest, that he will send forth labourers into his harvest. MATTHEW 9:37–38

Why should the disciples pray for this, Murray asks, when the Lord of the harvest already sees the need, and Christ could pray Himself? His answer is that Jesus spoke the deepest truth: He really believed their prayer was needed and would help. Prayer is not a form. It is one of the means God has appointed for the ingathering of the harvest and the coming of His kingdom.

This conviction shaped Murray’s whole ministry. He pressed his church to become a missionary church, founded a training institute for mission workers at Wellington, and helped establish missionary societies. He later wrote a book specifically on the subject, The Key to the Missionary Problem, arguing that the root of the church’s missionary weakness was spiritual, and its cure was prayer.

## The marks of an intercessor

In the chapter Because of His Importunity, on the parable of the friend at midnight, Murray drew out what makes a Christian an intercessor. He listed them in one sentence: “A sense of the need of souls; a Christlike love in the heart; a consciousness of personal impotence; faith in the power of prayer; courage to persevere in spite of refusal; and the assurance of an abundant reward.”

> Which of you shall have a friend, and shall go unto him at midnight, and say unto him, Friend, lend me three loaves; for a friend of mine in his journey is come to me, and I have nothing to set before him? LUKE 11:5–6

Notice the third mark: a consciousness of personal impotence. The man in the parable had nothing to set before his guest. That is the intercessor’s starting point. We cannot save anyone, heal anyone, or send anyone. We can only go to the One who can, and keep knocking.

## Anyone can do it

Murray was convinced that intercession was not a special calling for a few, but the privilege of every believer. There are tens of thousands, he wrote, who work with little prayer, and as many more who do not work because they do not know how, who might be won to swell the host of intercessors. The bedridden, the elderly, the unnoticed — all can take their place in this ministry.

And he added a word that every believer should test: “Nothing will so test and stimulate the Christian life as the honest attempt to be an intercessor.” It is easy to talk about prayer. It is humbling to try to pray daily, by name, for people and nations, and to persevere. The attempt reveals how little love and faith we have, and drives us back to Christ for more.

> I exhort therefore, that, first of all, supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men. 1 TIMOTHY 2:1

## The Intercessor behind the intercessors

All of this rests on Christ. The One who ever lives to make intercession is the pattern and the power of every intercessor. In the preface to With Christ in the School of Prayer Murray wrote that the believer, abiding in Christ, is taken up into His priestly work, and that it is in intercession that the church is to find and wield its highest power.

> But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people; that ye should shew forth the praises of him who hath called you out of darkness into his marvellous light. 1 PETER 2:9

## Where care is needed

Murray sometimes wrote as though God were held back from acting until His people prayed enough, and his missionary appeals used vast numbers to press the urgency. Both need a steadying hand. God is not a prisoner of our prayerlessness. He is sovereign, and He works in ways we do not see. What Scripture teaches is that He has chosen to work through the prayers of His people, and that He invites us into His purposes. That is a privilege, not a burden laid on us to carry the world’s salvation. The intercessor prays because God has asked him to and because love compels him, not because the fate of millions rests on the length of his prayer.

It should also be said that Murray’s missionary vision grew up in a colonial age, and some of its language reflects that. The heart of it does not. Christ’s compassion for the multitudes, and His command to pray for labourers, belong to every age and every people.

> For the love of Christ constraineth us. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:14

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Consider Murray’s three magnitudes — the world’s need, God’s supply, the church’s calling. Which have you underestimated?
2. Obey Matthew 9:38 this week: pray by name for a people, place or mission that needs labourers.
3. Choose three people who do not know Christ and pray for them daily for a month.
4. Measure yourself against Murray’s six marks of an intercessor. Ask God for the one most lacking.
5. If you are housebound, elderly or unnoticed, take up intercession as your ministry. It is one of the greatest there is.

### A PRAYER

> Lord of the harvest, the harvest is plenteous and the labourers are few. Send forth labourers into Your harvest.
> You sought for someone to stand in the gap. Make me one, though I have nothing to set before those in need.
> Give me a sense of the need of souls, a Christlike love, faith in prayer, and courage to persevere.
> And let my small prayers be joined to the intercession of Your Son, who ever lives to pray for us. Amen.

# Conclusion

> For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen. ROMANS 11:36

Eighteen chapters can make it look as though Andrew Murray taught a great many things. He taught one thing, and everything else is that one thing applied. The one thing is this: a creature lives by receiving, and the Christian life is Christ’s own life received moment by moment through faith.

Follow it through and the whole shape appears. Abiding is the branch receiving the life of the Vine. The Husbandman’s pruning makes room for more of that life. Humility is the creature consenting to its true place, and pride is the refusal of it. Surrender is giving up to God what already belongs to Him, and trusting Him to work what we cannot. The impossible becomes possible because God does it. The Spirit is Christ’s life within us; holiness is the presence of the Holy One; obedience is the path by which His love is enjoyed; love is the first fruit of it all. Waiting on God is dependence made into a habit. The inner chamber is where the link for the day is fastened. Prayer is dependence speaking, and intercession is dependence reaching out for others. One idea, one Person, one life.

## The danger of this book

If you have read this far, you are in some danger, and Murray would have named it first. The peculiar risk of teaching about the deeper life is that it can produce exactly the condition it was meant to cure. A reader sees the heights — absolute surrender, unbroken abiding, the prayer of faith, the Spirit-filled life — and sets out to reach them by effort. He makes a resolution, has a good week, falls back, and tries again. The teaching that was meant to lead him out from under law has become a new law.

Murray knew this danger well. It is why he told the anxious man who came to him that he was living under law and not under grace. It is why he taught that God claims our surrender, works it, and accepts it. It is why he prayed, and taught others to pray, that God would make them willing. The deeper life is not a summit to be climbed. It is a gift to be received, and it is received the way everything else is received from God: by coming empty.

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

So the plainest counsel this book can give is the same as in its companion volumes. Go back to the chapter that made you least comfortable, not the one you found most interesting. Take its application points and work them slowly over months. Then take the next.

## Two things to carry

First, stay a branch. Whatever else you forget from these pages, remember that you can do nothing without Christ, and that you are not asked to. You are asked to abide, to keep coming, to receive. When you fail, return at once. The Vine has not moved.

> I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing. JOHN 15:5

Second, pray. Murray would not be satisfied with any reader who finished this book admiring his teaching on prayer and praying no more than before. Find your inner chamber. Wait before you speak. Pray definitely. Persevere. Intercede for others by name. The Teacher still keeps His school open, and the only way to learn is to enrol.

## A last word about the man

We have been honest about his limits. His book on divine healing goes beyond what Scripture warrants in treating healing without medical means as the higher way and in its handling of sickness and the will of God, and it must be read with the corrections set out in chapter fourteen. His call to absolute surrender, and his scheme of carnal and spiritual Christians, have freed many and burdened some, and the conscientious need to hear the grace that he himself preached alongside them. His boldest words about prayer need Scripture’s own balance. And he was a minister of a settler church in a colonial society, who did not rise above all the assumptions of his time and place.

None of this has been said to diminish him. It has been said because his own teaching requires it. A man whose whole message was that the creature has nothing in itself and everything in God cannot be honoured by pretending that he was an exception. He would have been the first to say that whatever was good in him was received.

> Not unto us, O LORD, not unto us, but unto thy name give glory, for thy mercy, and for thy truth’s sake. PSALM 115:1

He died at Wellington in January 1917, in his eighty-ninth year, while the world was at war. He had lived to see the revival his father prayed for, to watch his church turn outward in mission, and to hear from readers across the world who had learned from his little books to abide and to pray. He left schools, missions and a shelf of books, and he left behind a prayer he had written for himself in a hard season: that God had brought him to the strait place, would keep him in it, would teach him in it, and would bring him out in His own time. It is not a bad prayer to end with.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Return to the chapter of this book that made you least comfortable. Read it again, and choose one application point to work on for a month.
2. Ask whether anything in these pages has become a new law to you. If so, bring it to God and receive it again as grace.
3. Establish one daily practice of dependence — a morning hour, a pause before work, a prayer at night — and keep it for thirty days.
4. Begin a list of people and places to pray for by name, and keep it where you will see it.
5. Choose one of Murray’s own books from the Reader’s Guide that follows, and begin it this week.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, I have read about abiding, surrender, humility and prayer, and I am capable of turning all of it into one more burden. Keep me from that.
> I am a branch. Let me simply receive Your life, day by day and moment by moment.
> Teach me to pray. Take me into Your school, and do not let me leave it.
> And as You were faithful to Your servant Andrew Murray in every strait place, be faithful to me, until I see You face to face. Amen.

# A Reader’s Guide to Andrew Murray

> Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. 2 TIMOTHY 2:15

Murray is easy to read and easy to find. He wrote for ordinary believers, in short chapters that usually end in prayer, and nearly all of it is now in the public domain. The difficulty is quantity. He wrote something like two hundred and forty books and tracts, many of them first in Dutch, and he repeats himself freely. The guide below is arranged to save the reader time.

## Begin here

With Christ in the School of Prayer (1885). His best-known book and the best single entry point: thirty-one short lessons on the sayings of Jesus about prayer, one for each day of a month. In the Ochorus library, as is Lord, Teach Us To Pray, which gathers its opening lessons.

Humility. Twelve brief chapters, and perhaps the most searching thing he wrote. Read it slowly and not all at once. In the Ochorus library.

Abide in Christ. His first famous book, thirty-one short readings for young believers on the life of abiding. Not yet in the Ochorus library, but widely available.

## On the deeper life

The True Vine. Short daily meditations on John 15:1–16, and the basis of chapter four of this volume. In the Ochorus library.

Absolute Surrender. Addresses given on his travels in the 1890s, including the title address, The Fruit of the Spirit is Love, and Impossible with Man, Possible with God. In the Ochorus library.

The Master’s Indwelling. Addresses on the carnal and spiritual life, the self-life, and Christ as our life. Read chapter nine of this volume alongside it. In the Ochorus library.

Holy in Christ. Thirty-one meditations tracing holiness through Scripture. Slower going, and rewarding. In the Ochorus library.

Jesus Himself. Two short addresses on knowing the risen Christ and His abiding presence. In the Ochorus library.

## On prayer and waiting

Waiting on God. Daily readings for a month on the word wait in Scripture; the gentlest of his books and one of the most useful. In the Ochorus library.

The Inner Chamber. Readings on the morning hour, secret prayer and Bible study, published in 1905. In the Ochorus library.

The Ministry of Intercession. A plea for more prayer, addressed chiefly to ministers and workers, and the source of chapters nineteen and twenty of this volume. In the Ochorus library.

The Prayer Life. A late and searching book on prayerlessness as sin, written after his retirement. Not yet in the Ochorus library.

## Other works worth knowing

The Spirit of Christ, his fullest treatment of the Holy Spirit; The Two Covenants, on the old and new covenants; The Power of the Blood of Jesus; The Holiest of All, a large devotional exposition of Hebrews; and The Key to the Missionary Problem, on prayer and missions. None of these is yet in the Ochorus library.

## Approach with care

Divine Healing. The fullest statement of the teaching treated in chapter fourteen of this book, and the one that most needs the cautions set out there. It gives thanks for doctors, but it treats healing without medical means as the higher way and discourages praying if it be Thy will. Read it alongside 2 Corinthians 12:7–10, 2 Timothy 4:20 and Matthew 26:39, and do not follow it in setting aside medical care. In the Ochorus library.

Murray admired William Law, and quotes him in the notes to Humility and Waiting on God. Law’s later writings drew on mystical sources many evangelicals would not share; take what is helpful.

## About Murray

J. du Plessis, The Life of Andrew Murray of South Africa (1919), is the standard life, written soon after his death by a younger colleague in the Dutch Reformed Church. It is long, detailed and admiring. W. M. Douglas, Andrew Murray and His Message, is a shorter and more popular account. Both were written by people who loved him, and it shows in what they leave out. The Ochorus author page carries a shorter biography.

## Three counsels for reading him

Read him with the Bible open. Murray claimed no authority of his own; his method was to take a text and press it. Check the texts.

Read him for the heart rather than the scheme. Where he presses a framework beyond what Scripture states — two classes of Christians, a crisis that settles everything, healing without means — take the pastoral burden and leave the scaffolding.

And read him on your knees. Nearly every chapter he wrote ends in a prayer. Pray it, or pray your own, before you turn the page. A chapter prayed will do more than a shelf admired.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21
