  This is not a book by Amanda Berry Smith. It is a companion to her — an independent work of exposition and appreciation, written by Ochorus, that gathers what she taught and testified into eighteen short chapters for the ordinary reader, and tells the story of the life that gave her words their weight. She was born enslaved in Maryland, earned her bread for years at the wash-tub, and became one of the best-known holiness preachers of her century on four continents.
  
  She wrote no treatise, but she was a theologian all the same: a theologian of lived holiness. Her one theme was God in you — a Saviour who does not only forgive, but comes to dwell in the heart, rule it, and set it free from sin, from fear and from self-will. This volume follows that thread through her conversion and her sanctification, her deliverance from the fear of man, her honest witness about race and the church, her life of prayer for rent and bread, her call to preach without ordination, and her work in India and West Africa. It weighs her fairly where she leaned too hard on impressions or pressed a rule too far, and it ends each chapter where she would have wanted it to end: in a few questions and a prayer.
  
  Amanda Smith’s Autobiography of 1893 is in the public domain and freely available, and every chapter here names the part of it that it draws on, so the reader can go to the source. This companion is offered only to open the door. It quotes Scripture from the Authorised (King James) Version, the Bible she read by lamplight and preached from on three continents, and it makes no claim to stand in for her own plain, vivid and searching words.

# Introduction

> For this is the will of God, even your sanctification. 1 THESSALONIANS 4:3

Amanda Berry Smith is usually remembered as a story. She was born into slavery in Maryland in 1837, and her father bought her freedom with years of extra labour by night. She had about three months of schooling in her whole life. She buried two husbands and four of her five children, and for years she kept herself and her daughter by taking in washing. Then, with no ordination, no salary and no society behind her, she became one of the best-known holiness preachers of her century, in America, Britain, India and West Africa.

But this book is not only about her story. It is about what she taught. She wrote no treatise and no book of sermons, only one large book, An Autobiography, published in 1893, which she called in its last pages just the simple story of God’s dealings with her. Yet the story is full of teaching, and the teaching is not naive. On conversion and assurance, on holiness and temptation, on prayer and guidance, on suffering and on race, she saw things clearly and said them plainly. Bishop James Thoburn of India, who knew her well, wrote in the introduction to her Autobiography that he had learned more of real use to him as a preacher from her than from any other one person he had met. She was a theologian of lived holiness. Her classroom was a wash-tub and a cellar floor, and her examinations were set by grief, poverty and prejudice.

## One thread

Her teaching is easy to summarise, because she learned it in one sentence on one Sunday morning, and spent the rest of her life unfolding it. In September 1868, in the Green Street Methodist church in New York, the only Black woman in the congregation, she heard John Inskip say that a believer need not fix any way for God to live in her; she had only to get God in her in all His fullness, and He would live Himself. The words that rang through her, as she tells it, were these: “God in you, God in you.”

> Christ in you, the hope of glory. COLOSSIANS 1:27

That is the thread through all of it. Salvation, for Amanda Smith, was not only pardon, though it began there. It was God Himself come to dwell in the heart, to rule it and fill it. God in you meant assurance, because the Spirit who lives within bears witness. It meant holiness, because He cleanses the house He lives in. It meant freedom from fear — the fear of white people, the fear of men and women, the fear of the ocean — because the perfect love of God casts fear out. It meant guidance, because He speaks. It meant daily bread, because He is a Father. It meant strength to bury a child and say Thy will be done. And it meant that a washerwoman might be sent anywhere in the world, because the One who sent her would go with her.

So the question to carry through this book is her question: is God in me, or only near me? Do I know Him as a doctrine, or as the One who lives here?

## How this book is arranged

After a chapter on her life, the book follows the order of her own experience: conversion, the witness of the Spirit, and the blessing she called sanctification. Then it follows that grace into a hard marriage and daily work, into her deliverance from the fear of man, and into her honest witness about colour and the church. Next come the inner lessons she learned the hard way — dark seasons, guidance, obedience, prayer for daily bread, bereavement and sickness. The rest follow her outward: her call to preach, her fellowship in the holiness movement, her work in India and Africa, temperance, children, and her plain and simple life.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. She never told a story for its own sake; every account in her book ends in a lesson, a hymn, or a Hallelujah. Read one chapter at a time. Stop at the application points and actually attempt them. Pray the prayer, or better, pray your own.

## Where she must be read with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and it is better to say at the outset where she must be read with care.

First, impressions. She lived by inward whispers, texts that seemed to leap from the page, and signs she asked of God. Many were plainly used by Him. But she tells us herself that she was once badly misled by what looked like the Lord’s will, and that she had to learn by painful experience to tell the Spirit’s voice from her own wishes and the tempter’s.

Second, holiness. She taught, with the Methodist holiness movement of her day, that entire sanctification is a second definite work of grace, received by faith after conversion. Many godly Christians read the same Scriptures and see a lifelong process of growth rather than a single crisis. This book notes where the disagreement lies.

Third, her era. She saw India and Africa as a nineteenth-century missionary, and wrote of heathen darkness and Christian civilisation in the language of her time. Some of her judgements will trouble a modern reader; she was also often more honest than her contemporaries about the failures of missionaries and churches. We will state both soberly.

Fourth, temperance. She held total abstinence from alcohol as a rule for every Christian, not as a wise choice for many.

Why read her at all, then? Because on the central matter she was right, and because she lived it at a cost few of us will ever pay. So the counsel of this book is the counsel of Scripture: Prove all things; hold fast that which is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Weigh every chapter against the Word of God. Where she is faithful — and on the reality of the new birth, the witness of the Spirit, the love that casts out fear, and the faithfulness of God to the poor, she is faithful indeed — receive it and act on it. Where she presses beyond what is written, mark it, set it aside, and lose nothing.

> Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts. ZECHARIAH 4:6

2. Decide now how you will read this book — one chapter at a time, with the application points actually attempted before you go on.

# Bought Twice, Set Free Twice

> If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed. JOHN 8:36

A father’s nights. Amanda Berry was born at Long Green, in Baltimore County, Maryland, on the twenty-third of January, 1837, the eldest daughter of Samuel Berry and his wife Mariam, whose maiden name was Matthews. Both her parents were enslaved, on neighbouring farms. Trusted with his mistress’s business, her father was allowed to buy his own freedom, and he did it by working through the night after his day’s work was done — making brooms and husk mats to sell in Baltimore, and walking miles to labour in other men’s harvest fields until the small hours. Having paid for himself, he set out to buy his wife and children. The way opened, as the family always told it, through the deathbed plea of his wife’s young mistress, who had been converted at a Methodist camp meeting and asked her family to let Samuel have Mariam and the children. Amanda’s grandmother, a woman of great faith, had prayed for years for her grandchildren’s freedom. Amanda was too small to remember slavery, but she never forgot what her freedom had cost. “I have been bought twice, and set free twice,” she liked to say, “and so I feel I have a good right to shout.”

Pennsylvania. Maryland law threatened free Black people who stayed out of the state too long, and when her father overstayed a visit to his brother in the North, the family moved to a farm in Pennsylvania on the Baltimore and York turnpike. Her father read the Bible to the children every Sunday morning. Amanda taught herself to read by cutting large letters out of the newspapers and asking her mother to put them together into words. Her whole formal schooling, walking miles to a white school where the Black children were taught only when the others had finished, came to about three months. At thirteen she went into domestic service, and for most of the next thirty years she earned her bread as a cook, a servant and a washerwoman.

A girl at the back. As a young servant she went forward at a Methodist revival, drawn by a white girl who knelt with her arm around her and prayed. She joined the church, but the class leader would never lead her in turn until all the white members had spoken, and she was scolded at home for coming back late. In the end she had to choose between her class and her place, and she gave up the class. For a time she drifted, and for a season a book of infidel arguments took hold of her.

Marriage and the cellar. In September 1854 she married Calvin Devine. He could talk sensibly about religion, but he drank. They had two children; the first died, and the second, her daughter Mazie, lived to grow up. In 1855 she fell so ill that the doctors gave her up, and in what she described as a vision she saw herself preaching at a great camp meeting. She recovered and resolved to be converted — but quietly, and in her own way. It took months of struggle, and the surrender of her pride, before, on the seventeenth of March, 1856, on her knees in a Quaker family’s cellar where she was in service, she told the Lord she would believe Him, and did. Her husband enlisted in the Civil War and never came home.

Frances. In 1862 she learned that her younger sister Frances, born free in Pennsylvania, had been sold into service in Maryland. Amanda borrowed fifty dollars from her employer, went down into Maryland alone in wartime, and bought her sister out for forty.

James Smith. In Philadelphia she married James Smith, a local preacher and ordained deacon in the African Methodist Episcopal Church. She married him partly because he told her he would enter the travelling ministry and she longed to be a minister’s wife in the work. He never meant to. The marriage was unhappy. They moved to New York in 1865, and she took in washing. Three more children were born, and all three died in infancy. James Smith died in November 1869. By her early thirties she had buried two husbands, and four of her five children.

Green Street. For twelve years after her conversion she hungered for what the Methodists called holiness, and no one could tell her how to find it. Then in September 1868 she went, against a string of excuses, to hear John S. Inskip preach at the Green Street Methodist Episcopal Church, and there she received the blessing of entire sanctification, as she believed and as she told it for the rest of her life. That morning, she said, the fear of white people that had always held her went too.

Salem. Not long afterwards she became convinced that God was sending her out, and the word that came was Salem. She went to Salem, New Jersey, a stranger with almost no money, and a revival broke out that spread for miles around. Other towns followed. She was soon in demand at the great holiness camp meetings — Oakington, Sing Sing, Ocean Grove, Knoxville — singing and speaking to crowds of white and Black hearers in her plain Quaker-style dress and poke bonnet. She had no ordination, and wanted none. She had received her ordination, she said, from the One who chose her.

Across the water. In 1878, after a long struggle with her fear of the sea, she sailed alone for England. What was meant as a short visit lasted years. She spoke at the Keswick Convention and at conferences and missions across England and Scotland. In 1879 she went on to India, where she worked for the better part of two years, much of it in Calcutta alongside James Thoburn, who later became a Methodist missionary bishop. In January 1882 she landed at Monrovia, and for about eight years she laboured in Liberia and along the West African coast, often sick with fever, preaching, leading revivals, organising temperance bands, and caring for African children, among them a small boy called Bob, whom she took as her own.

Harvey and Sebring. She came back to America, by way of England, around 1890, worn out in body. Friends urged her to write her story, and in 1893 An Autobiography: The Story of the Lord’s Dealings with Mrs. Amanda Smith, the Colored Evangelist was published. She settled near Chicago, and in 1899, at Harvey, Illinois, she opened an orphanage and industrial home for Black children, which she carried for years through chronic shortage of money and much trouble. In 1912 she retired to Sebring, Florida, where a friend provided a house. She died there on the twenty-fourth of February, 1915, aged seventy-eight.

Her book ends not with herself but with a hope for younger women: that some who had better opportunities than she ever had would take up the standard when she fell, bearing on it the words, Without holiness, no man shall see the Lord.

> The LORD hath done great things for us; whereof we are glad. PSALM 126:3

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Her father worked through the night to buy her freedom. Spend some time this week thanking God, specifically, for what your freedom in Christ cost.
2. She was turned away from class because of her colour and drifted for years. Is there someone at the edge of your church who is quietly being left last? Go and sit with them.
3. She married on a promise that looked like the Lord’s will and was not. Before your next large decision, ask what you would do if the attractive reasons were taken away.
4. She had three months of schooling and taught a bishop. What is the one thing you are waiting to be qualified for before you obey?
5. Read the first chapter of her Autobiography in the Ochorus library, on her birth and her family’s deliverance.

> Lord Jesus, You bought me with a price far greater than any father’s labour, and You have set me free indeed.
> Forgive me for how lightly I hold what cost You so much, and teach me to shout, as she did, for my freedom.

> And when my course is done, let it be seen that it was Your doing, and not mine. Amen.

# Pray Once More

> Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you. MATTHEW 7:7

Amanda Smith never tired of telling how she was converted, and she told it at length in the chapter of her Autobiography called Marriage and Conversion. It is not only her story. It is a small, exact picture of what she believed about how a soul comes to God: that the great obstacle is not the size of our sins but the stubbornness of our will, and that the whole thing turns, in the end, on a simple act of trust.

## I will be converted in my own way

She had wanted to be a Christian as long as she could remember. As a girl she prayed in the fence corner when she went for the cows. As a young servant she went forward at a revival and felt real peace, but, as she said, she did not know how to exercise faith, and when the class meeting became a place of humiliation she let it all go. 

Then, in 1855, she was desperately ill. She recovered, and she resolved to be converted, but quietly and on her own terms. She would never, she said, go forward to an altar. She had a strong will and was proud of it, and her pride held her. She reasoned that she was not so bad as some of the rough people in the town. She had always lived in first-class families.

> The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it? JEREMIAH 17:9

This is the first thing she teaches about conversion, and it is not comfortable. The chief hindrance is usually not ignorance but pride: the wish to be saved without being humbled, to come to God as a respectable person rather than as a sinner. She found herself, almost without knowing how, walking down the aisle of a Baptist church to the very altar she had sworn she would never kneel at. She threw herself down and cried for mercy until she was hoarse. A stillness came over her. But she did not yet have peace, because, as she said plainly, no one had taught her how to believe.

## Tell Jesus I am a poor sinner

She was in service with a Quaker family near Columbia, in Pennsylvania. She prayed, fasted, read her Bible and prayed again. In her ignorance, she says, she prayed to the sun and moon and stars. She reasoned that her sin was disobedience, and that the sun, moon and wind had always obeyed God, so she asked them to carry a message for her: “O, Sun, you never sinned like me, you have always obeyed God, and kept your place in the heavens; tell Jesus I am a poor sinner.”

She put it down to ignorance. But it is also profound. She had grasped that she needed someone who had never sinned to stand between her and God. She simply did not yet see that such a One had come. The Obedient One she was looking for was the Saviour she was praying to.

> For as by one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous. ROMANS 5:19

In the middle of this struggle she gave up a new spring outfit she had been saving for, which kept rising before her as she prayed; the story belongs to a later chapter. It was not yet conversion, but it was the loosening of a grip.

## Pray once more

On Tuesday, the seventeenth of March, 1856, she was sitting in the kitchen by her ironing table when the voice she afterwards knew to be the devil’s went over her case like a lawyer. She had prayed, been sincere, fasted and read her Bible; if God had meant to convert her He would have done it long ago. She agreed, and resolved to give up and submit to her fate.

Just then a voice whispered, “Pray once more.” Another said, “Don’t you do it.” And she said, “I will,” and felt the words go through her from the crown of her head. She set the table for the family’s tea, put everything on it but the bread and butter, and went down into the cellar to pray, determined to have salvation that afternoon or die.

> And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart. JEREMIAH 29:13

There in the dark she prayed her old prayers, and the accuser told her each time that she had said all that before. She came to the end of everything. And then she said something new. She looked up and said, “O, Lord, if Thou wilt help me I will believe Thee,” and then, she writes, “in the act of telling God I would, I did.”

That is the whole secret, and she spent the rest of her life explaining it. Faith is not a feeling to be worked up or a condition to be earned. It is the decision to take God at His word, and the decision is itself the believing. The burden rolled away. Light and joy flooded her. She ran up out of the cellar, looked at her hands, and said, “Why, I am new, I am new all over.” She even looked in the tall dining-room mirror to see whether her colour had changed, so wonderful was what had happened inside her.

> Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:17

## What she learned

Three lessons run through the rest of her teaching from that cellar. First, God is found by those who come to the end of themselves. She had tried every way of her own — quietness, respectability, good works, praying to the stars — and every one had to fail before she would simply trust.

Second, the struggle is not only with our own hearts. There is an enemy who argues that God will not hear, and she learned early to know his voice.

Third, saving faith is simple, but it must be clearly taught. She says more than once that if anyone had shown her how to believe, she might have found peace months earlier. It made her a patient guide to seekers, who told them not only to seek but how to trust.

> For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God. EPHESIANS 2:8

There is one thing to weigh with care. Her vow in the cellar — that she would have salvation that afternoon or die — was the vow of a desperate young woman, and God met her in it. But it is not a pattern to be pressed on everyone. Many come to Christ quietly, over time, without a single dramatic hour, and are no less truly His. What must be pressed on everyone is what lay beneath the drama: the end of self-reliance, and the simple trust that says, Lord, I will believe Thee.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is there some way in which you have wanted to be saved, or to grow, on your own terms — quietly, respectably, without being humbled? Name it to God.
2. Her spring outfit had to go before peace came. Is there something you love that stands between you and a whole-hearted surrender? Tell Him you will let it go.
3. If you have never done so, say to God in so many words: Lord, I will believe You. Then, in the act of saying it, do it.
4. Learn to recognise the accuser’s arguments: that it is no use, that God will not hear, that you have tried before. Write down one Scripture to answer each.
5. Think of someone who is seeking God but has never been shown how to trust. Pray for a chance to explain it to them simply.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You are the Obedient One I could never be, and You have stood where I should have stood.
> I have wanted to come to You in my own way and on my own terms. Forgive my pride, and bring me to the end of myself.
> I will believe You. I do believe You. Make me new all over, and let it show.
> And when others are seeking, give me the patience and the plain words to show them how to trust. Amen.

# The Witness of the Spirit

> The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God. ROMANS 8:16

A week after her conversion, Amanda Smith was singing over her work. She was so happy she hardly knew what to do with herself. And then, as she tells it, the enemy whispered to her: “There, you are singing just as if you had religion.” She answered that the Lord had converted her. The voice went on: how did she know? She did not want to be a hypocrite, did she? And then the decisive thrust: “But you have no evidence.”

She did not know the word. “Evidence, evidence, what is that?” she asked herself. Then she remembered that the old people used to speak of the witness of the Spirit, and she wondered whether that was what was meant.

## Holding the point

She decided she would not sing and would not pray, in her usual happy way, until she had the witness. Instead she prayed one prayer for a whole week: Lord, I believe Thou hast converted me, but the devil says I have no evidence; give me the evidence, so clear and definite that he will never trouble me on that line again. God, she says, helped her to hold the point. At the end of the week He gave it. And from that day, through many sorrows and much buffeting by Satan, she never again had a question about her conversion.

> Hereby know we that we dwell in him, and he in us, because he hath given us of his Spirit. 1 JOHN 4:13

This was not a technicality to her. “This witness of God’s spirit to my conversion has been what has held me amid all the storms of temptation and trial that I have passed through,” she wrote. When she later came under deep conviction for holiness, she was grateful that this one point was already settled, because otherwise, she said, the struggle would have been much worse. A soul unsure of its pardon cannot seek anything further with a quiet heart.

## Two kinds of conviction

One of the most careful passages in her Autobiography distinguishes two very different inward states. When she was convicted of sin, she was under condemnation; she felt herself a lost and wretched sinner. When God pardoned her, He took all condemnation away. Later, when she began to long for a clean heart, it was, she wrote, “a deep conviction of want—an indescribable want; not condemnation.” It was like the hunger of someone who has worked hard all day and longs for supper.

> There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit. ROMANS 8:1

That distinction matters more than it looks. Many earnest Christians who long for more of God mistake the longing for guilt, and conclude they were never saved at all. But hunger for God is not condemnation before God. A child who is hungry for supper does not doubt that she belongs to the family.

## A privilege, not a luxury

She taught that the clear witness of the Spirit is the normal privilege of every believer, not a rare luxury for the advanced. It is, she wrote, “their privilege to have the clear, distinct witness of the Spirit to both justification and sanctification.” She was realistic that it does not come to everyone in the same way. But she observed, from long experience at the altar, that those who never come to settled assurance “are unsettled in their Christian life, often waver and falter, and are more easily turned aside to new isms and doctrines.”

> These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life. 1 JOHN 5:13

That is sound pastoral wisdom. A believer who does not know whether she belongs to God is easy prey — for the accuser, for every new teacher with a scheme, and for a religion of anxious effort. The New Testament was written that we might know.

## Where to be careful

There is something here to be weighed. On one later occasion, when the devil told her she had no witness to her sanctification, she asked God for a sign, and the sign she was given was the conversion of a particular sick young woman she had prayed with. She prayed that the woman would be converted before Sunday, and on Sunday morning a friend crossed the church to tell her that the woman had received the blessing the afternoon before. Amanda Smith took it as God’s direct evidence, and she never doubted again.

We need not doubt that God answered her. But she was wise enough to add that everybody does not receive their witness in that way, and her readers should be wiser still. The witness of the Spirit, in Scripture, is the Spirit’s own testimony within the heart, confirmed by His fruit in the life and by the promises of the Word. It is not normally a matter of asking for outward signs, and a believer who makes her assurance hang on such signs has built on sand. If the sign had not come, would the promise have been any less true? Her own deepest assurance, in the cellar and the week after, rested on the plain word she had believed. That is where ours must rest too.

> He that believeth on the Son of God hath the witness in himself. 1 JOHN 5:10

## Settled once for all

What she wanted for every believer was a settled, humble certainty that could not be argued away. Satan might buffet her and feelings might come and go; but the question of whether she belonged to Christ had been answered, and she could say, as she later did in a very dark hour, that whatever else was true, she belonged to Jesus.

> For I know whom I have believed, and am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him against that day. 2 TIMOTHY 1:12

It is a strange thing that the Christians most afraid of assurance are often the most sincere. They fear presumption, and they are right to. But there is a deeper presumption in doubting what God has plainly said. She liked to close an account of God’s dealings with a phrase from Hosea: ye shall know, if ye follow on to know the Lord.

> Then shall we know, if we follow on to know the LORD. HOSEA 6:3

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Do you know that you belong to Christ? Write down, in a sentence, what your assurance rests on. If it rests on feelings, find a promise of Scripture to rest it on instead.
2. If you have never settled the question, do what she did: set apart time to ask God plainly for the witness of His Spirit, and hold the point until He answers.
3. Distinguish in your own heart between condemnation and hunger. If what you feel is longing for more of God, thank Him for it instead of fearing it.
4. Notice where your uncertainty has made you restless for new teachers or new methods. Return to the plain promises of 1 John 5:10–13.
5. Think of a believer you know who is tormented by doubt. Read 1 John 5:13 with them this week.

### A PRAYER

> Father, Your Word was written that I might know that I have eternal life. Let me not live in the fog when You have offered me the light.
> Let Your Spirit bear witness with my spirit that I am Your child, clearly enough that the accuser’s arguments lose their hold.
> Teach me the difference between the guilt You have taken away and the hunger You have given me, and let me follow on to know You.
> Settle me, keep me, and let me say in every storm: whatever else is true, I belong to Jesus. Amen.

# God in You

> And that ye put on the new man, which after God is created in righteousness and true holiness. EPHESIANS 4:24

The centre of Amanda Smith’s life and teaching was a single Sunday morning in September 1868. She had been converted for twelve years, and for almost all of that time she had been hungry for something she could not find or even name. The chapter of her Autobiography called The Blessing of Sanctification tells how she found it, and it is the key to everything else she believed.

## Twelve years in the wilderness

The hunger came soon after her conversion. She was leading a class, praying with the dying at midnight, seeing souls converted, and still there was an ache she could not explain. A minister’s wife told her that if she kept on so she would go crazy. Then one day she read the first verse of the forty-second Psalm, and found words for it at last. “Oh!” she said, “That’s what I wanted—God!”

> As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. PSALM 42:1

So she tried works. She would be immersed, and then, having done everything the Bible required, the Lord would be obliged to give her the blessing. When that fell through she proposed foot-washing with her band of praying sisters. That too was hindered again and again, and she came to see the hindrance as mercy: otherwise, she wrote, she might have gone about teaching that immersion and foot-washing were necessary to be sanctified. The Lord had saved her from seeking salvation by works.

> This only would I learn of you, Received ye the Spirit by the works of the law, or by the hearing of faith? GALATIANS 3:2

## Green Street

What finally led her to Green Street was, as she believed, an inward word: on Sunday morning go and hear John Inskip. She was argued with at every corner on the way — her husband would be angry, she should go somewhere nearer, Inskip was not even there — and went on. He was there, and took his text from Ephesians 4:24.

During the sermon she seemed three times to hear her baby screaming at home, and each time a quiet voice said, Trust the Lord. At the third she said she would trust the Lord with the child even if he were dead. Just then Inskip spoke of those who were troubled about how they could keep the blessing if they got it. She was sure he meant her. He gave an illustration: when you go to bed tired you do not arrange anything in order to breathe; you simply breathe all night. “You don’t need to fix any way for God to live in you,” she heard him say; “get God in you in all His fullness and he will live Himself.”

That was the moment. She felt herself, as she put it, sink down out of sight of herself and then rise, and words rang through her like a bell: God in you. She asked herself, doing what? And the answer came: ruling every ambition and desire, and bringing every thought into captivity to His will.

> I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me. GALATIANS 2:20

## A lamp in a dark room

Inskip gave another illustration that morning, and she never forgot it. The blessing of purity, like pardon, is received by faith; and if by faith, why not now? “How long is a dark room dark when you take a lighted lamp into it?” That is the heart of her doctrine of entire sanctification: that it is received, like justification, by faith, in a moment, as a gift.

> Faithful is he that calleth you, who also will do it. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:24

She came to understand the difference between her conversion and her sanctification with a picture that could only have come from a washerwoman who sewed at night. What is the difference, she asked the Lord, between the two? And the answer came as a question: what is the difference between sunlight and moonlight? She had often sewed by moonlight, and it was beautiful; that was her justified state. But the sun gets into every crack and nail-hole and leaves no room for frost. There were deep recesses in her heart that the moonlight did not reveal; the sunlight of sanctification searched them all.

## The second touch

She also asked, as many do, why God had not done it all at once. The question, she came to believe, was meant to make her think unkindly of God. The answer came by another question: why did Jesus not make the blind man at Bethsaida see clearly at the first touch? He had the same power in both. She concluded that it was none of her business why; it was enough that He had done it. She quit, as she put it, asking God questions about His own work.

> After that he put his hands again upon his eyes, and made him look up: and he was restored, and saw every man clearly. MARK 8:25

## Weighing the doctrine

Here the reader must weigh her carefully. Amanda Smith taught, with the Methodist holiness movement of Phoebe Palmer and John Inskip, that entire sanctification is a distinct second work of grace, received instantaneously by faith after conversion, which cleanses the heart from inbred sin and fills it with perfect love. Many sincere and careful Christians read the New Testament differently. They see sanctification as a lifelong work, never complete in this life, and fear that the language of a finished experience can lead to despair or self-deception.

Both sides should notice what she did not claim. She never said that a sanctified person does not sin, grow, or struggle. Her own book is full of her failures after 1868, and she insisted, “There is much of the human nature for us to battle with, even after we are wholly sanctified.” She described her experience not as arrival but as the inauguration, as she put it, of a perpetual May-day that would go on increasing in faith and light and strength. And the heart of what she received on Green Street is something every tradition should want: the believer ceasing to hold herself together and trusting God to live in her. Whether it comes once, or many times, or as a gradual dawning, that is the thing to seek.

> Now the God of peace … make you perfect in every good work to do his will, working in you that which is wellpleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ. HEBREWS 13:20–21

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Can you name the hunger in you as she did — not for a blessing or an experience, but for God? Pray Psalm 42:1 as your own prayer this week.
2. Notice where you are trying to earn from God, by some religious act or discipline, what He means to give by faith. Stop, and ask for it as a gift.
3. Take Inskip’s illustration to bed with you tonight. You do not arrange your breathing; ask God to live in you as simply as that.
4. Where you disagree with her doctrine of a second blessing, write down from Scripture why. Then ask whether you have the thing itself: a heart that has stopped holding back from God.
5. Ask God to shine into the dark corners of your heart that the moonlight has not reached. Wait for Him to show you one, and deal with it.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, my soul pants after You. Not after a blessing, not after a feeling, but after You.
> I have tried to hold myself together, and to earn from You what You mean to give. I stop now. Come and live in me, and live Yourself.
> Rule every ambition and desire, and bring every thought into captivity to Your will.
> Shine into every corner of my heart, and leave no room for frost, through Jesus Christ my Lord. Amen.

# Enduring Grace

> And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:9

What first sent Amanda Smith seeking holiness in earnest was not a sermon or a book. It was a bad night at home. Her second marriage had turned out badly; her husband, she wrote, “was one of those poor unfortunate dispositions that are hard to satisfy.” One morning, bent over the wash-tub after such a night, her heart sore, she prayed, Lord, is there no way out of this? And the Lord, she says, sent Mother Jones.

## Get sanctified, and then you have enduring grace

She could not help crying, and poured out all the good things she did to make home pleasant, and how unkind her husband still was. Mother Jones listened and said that her own husband used to treat her the same way, “but when God sanctified my soul He gave me enduring grace, and that is what you need; get sanctified, and then you have enduring grace.”

The phrase went straight to her heart. “My,” she thought, “is that what sanctification means? Enduring grace? That is just what I need; I have always been planning to get out of trials, instead of asking God for grace to endure.”

> Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. But let patience have her perfect work, that ye may be perfect and entire, wanting nothing. JAMES 1:3–4

That sentence contains a great deal of practical theology. Most of us, when life is hard, pray to be got out of it. There is nothing wrong with praying so; Paul asked three times for his thorn to be taken away. But the believer who only ever asks for escape has not yet learned what Paul learned, that the answer may be not the removal of the trial but the gift of grace sufficient to bear it. Amanda Smith’s holiness was never an escape from ordinary life. It was strength to live it.

## Not a nun

After her sanctification she found out, she said, “that it was not necessary to be a nun or be isolated away off in some deep retirement to have communion with Jesus.” Her hands might be in the wash-water, and her heart could still be with Him. “Many times over my wash-tub and ironing table, and while making my bed and sweeping my house and washing my dishes I have had some of the richest blessings.”

> And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men. COLOSSIANS 3:23

She told this to many mothers, and it cheered them to learn that the blessing of holiness did not mean isolation from the natural and legitimate duties of life. It meant God in you, supplying all your needs — including the need of patience, long-suffering and forbearance, “for we have to learn how not only to bear, but also to forbear with infirmities of ourselves and others as well.”

That is a working woman’s holiness, and it is deeply biblical. The New Testament does not call most Christians out of the kitchen and the workshop; it calls them to be holy in them. Holiness is not the absence of dishes. It is God present at the sink.

## Manna fresh every morning

She was realistic, too, about how such grace is kept. Soon after her sanctification she said to herself that she would not need to pray now as she used to. The answer came at once: “The children of Israel gathered manna fresh every morning.” She understood. As her body needed daily bread, so her soul needed daily prayer, daily reading of the Word, and daily appropriating of the promises. Without that, all else would avail nothing.

> Give us this day our daily bread. MATTHEW 6:11

## Returning good for evil

Her book is full of small, unglamorous tests of this grace. For two years a neighbour in her lodging house, a woman from her own church whom she had helped, refused to speak to her after a misunderstanding, sent spiteful messages, and threw greasy bread crumbs over the steps she had just scrubbed. Amanda often cried, but she said nothing. When another woman came to carry tales of what the neighbour said, she told her plainly never to bring such tales again: if the neighbour wanted her to know something, she must say it herself. Months later the tale-bearer was about to be put out on the street after falling out with the same neighbour, and Amanda took her in. It was, she said, a chance to return good for evil.

> Recompense to no man evil for evil. Provide things honest in the sight of all men. ROMANS 12:17

On another occasion a friend told a lie about her that caused a stir. She spent a whole morning walking from house to house trying to track it down, and found at each door only that someone said that someone else said that she had said. She came home ashamed and disgusted, went down to the cellar, and promised the Lord that she would never, while she lived, go after another lie. And, she says, she never did.

> Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves, but rather give place unto wrath: for it is written, Vengeance is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord. ROMANS 12:19

## The husband who did not change

The hardest thing to admit is that her home did not get better. A week after she was sanctified, the enemy pointed out that when people get sanctified everything around them gets better, and her husband was, if anything, worse. She agreed that it was so, and did not understand it. When she told him what God had done, he wanted to argue, and she would not. He tantalised her, she says, in every way; but God kept her still in her soul. She could only weep and pray.

That honesty is precious. Much Christian testimony promises that if we are right with God our circumstances will come right too. Hers did not. Her husband died within a year or so, unchanged as far as she tells us. What changed was her. Enduring grace does not always end the trial. It makes the believer able to stand in it without bitterness, and that, in the end, is the greater miracle.

> Blessed is the man that endureth temptation: for when he is tried, he shall receive the crown of life. JAMES 1:12

There is a caution here too. Her counsel to endure was counsel for trials, not for danger. Scripture does not require anyone to remain where they or their children are being harmed, and she herself later advised a friend in a frightening home not to leave, advice that grieved and estranged that friend. Endurance is a grace, not a law. But for the ordinary, grinding unkindnesses of family and neighbours and fellow church members, her counsel stands: ask not only to be got out, but to be given grace to endure.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. What trial are you currently praying to be got out of? This week, pray also for grace to endure it, and notice the difference.
2. Choose one ordinary task you do every day — washing up, commuting, a spreadsheet — and make it a place of communion with Christ.
3. Set a fresh time each morning this week to gather your manna: a passage of Scripture, a promise claimed, a short prayer.
4. Is there someone who has wronged you to whom you could quietly return good for evil? Do one concrete kind thing for them.
5. Resolve, as she did, never to go chasing a lie told about you. Leave your reputation with God.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have spent so much of my life planning to get out of trials. Teach me to ask You for grace to endure them.
> Meet me at the sink and the desk and the bedside, and let the ordinary work of my hands be a place where I know You.
> Give me my manna fresh this morning, and keep me from thinking I can live on yesterday’s.
> Where I have been wronged, give me the grace to return good for evil, and to leave my cause in Your hands. Amen.

# Perfect Love Casteth Out Fear

> There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear: because fear hath torment. He that feareth is not made perfect in love. 1 JOHN 4:18

On the morning Amanda Smith was sanctified, the thing that nearly stopped her was fear. Three times the Spirit came upon her in the Green Street church and she longed to shout Glory to Jesus, and three times she held it in. The voice at her side said: “Look, look at the white people, mind, they will put you out.” She was the only Black person in the building. She had been taught a keen sense of propriety. So she put her hands up to her mouth and kept still, and felt the Spirit leave her.

## A fear because they were white

She is remarkably honest about what that fear was. “Somehow I always had a fear of white people,” she wrote, “that is, I was not afraid of them in the sense of doing me harm, or anything of that kind— but a kind of fear because they were white, and were there, and I was black and was here!”

Anyone who has been made to feel small in a room full of people with more money, power or standing will recognise that fear. It is not quite cowardice. It is a deep, trained sense of not belonging, of being tolerated rather than welcomed. Slavery and its long aftermath had put it into her, and years of being led last at class meeting and sent to the gallery had kept it there. It governed where she sat, when she spoke, and whether she dared to praise God aloud.

> The fear of man bringeth a snare: but whoso putteth his trust in the LORD shall be safe. PROVERBS 29:25

At the end of the service, as they sang of the blood that cleanses from all sin, she stood up trembling and shouted at last, and John Inskip answered from the pulpit, “Amen, Glory to God.” And as she stood there, she heard words that seemed to come slowly and clearly from one corner of the church: There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female, for ye are all one in Christ Jesus. She had never understood that text before. Now, she said, the Holy Ghost had made it clear. As she looked at the white people she had always been afraid of, “now they looked so small. The great mountain had become a mole-hill.”

> There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither bond nor free, there is neither male nor female: for ye are all one in Christ Jesus. GALATIANS 3:28

## Not contempt, but freedom

It is important to see what happened and what did not. She did not come to despise white people, nor did she pretend that prejudice had disappeared; she met it for the rest of her life and named it plainly. What happened was inside her. The congregation had not changed; her place before God had become clear. She was one in Christ Jesus with every believer in that room. The mountain had become a molehill not because the people were smaller, but because God was larger.

## The man-fearing spirit

The deliverance was real, but it was not the end of the matter. Months later, in her own church, a new pastor opposed the doctrine of holiness from the pulpit and mocked the plain-dressed sanctified sisters, so that every eye turned to her and the congregation giggled. Kind friends advised her not to say anything more about sanctification. She began to be vague in her testimony, choosing words people would like. And a darkness settled on her that she could not explain.

She set apart a Friday to fast and pray about it, and in the afternoon the Spirit seemed to say, Read, and her Bible opened at the words of 1 John: perfect love casteth out fear. She saw at once what was wrong. It was fear — fear of her pastor and of the people. Her prayer is one of the most useful in her book: “Oh! Lord, take all the man-fearing spirit out of me. I thank Thee for what Thou hast done for me, but deliver me from fear. Take all the woman-fearing spirit out of me, and give me complete victory over this fear.”

> The LORD is on my side; I will not fear: what can man do unto me? PSALM 118:6

There was no special manifestation, she says, but a deep consciousness that God had done it. And then came the test. Would she go on Sunday to a church uptown where not one member believed in holiness, and testify there definitely? She went to the six o’clock love feast with her knees trembling, sat through the pointed remarks, and prayed the noisy meeting into silence. Then she stood and quoted the Scriptures on holiness and told what God had done. The ministers who had looked so critical when she came in ended by shouting for God to bless that sister.

## Lifting a black hand

One small story shows how deep the fear ran and how simply it was overcome. In a New York Methodist church one Easter Sunday, during the sermon, the Spirit prompted her to raise her right hand. The tempter answered at once: “Yes, you look nice lifting up your black hand before all the people.” She drew back. Then she remembered that she had told the Lord she would do whatever He told her. She asked for another chance, and when the prompting came again she lifted her hand — and, she says, the power of the Spirit fell on the whole congregation. He had not told her to shout, only to lift her hand, and the people did the shouting.

> For God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind. 2 TIMOTHY 1:7

The lesson she drew is the lesson of the whole chapter. Fear rarely asks us to do something wicked. It asks us to do nothing: to keep our hands down, our mouths shut, our testimony vague, our place small. And the cost of such nothing is often borne by others, who might have been blessed if we had obeyed.

## How the fear goes

She did not teach that believers would never feel afraid again. She trembled on platforms for the rest of her life. She was terrified of the ocean and had to be dealt with by God before she would sail to England. What she taught was that fear must not rule. The cure was not self-confidence, which she never had, but love — the perfect love of God filling the heart until there is no room for fear to govern.

> Fear thou not; for I am with thee: be not dismayed; for I am thy God: I will strengthen thee. ISAIAH 41:10

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Where do you feel, as she did, that you are here and they are there — that you take up too much room? Bring that feeling to Galatians 3:28.
2. Pray her prayer in your own words: Lord, take all the man-fearing and woman-fearing spirit out of me. Name the particular people you fear.
3. Is your testimony vaguer than it used to be because someone disapproved? Tell one person plainly this week what God has done for you.
4. Think of a moment when fear told you to do nothing. What would obedience have looked like? Ask for another chance, and take it when it comes.
5. If you are in a position of power, ask who in your church or workplace feels the fear she describes, and what you could do to make them welcome.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have been afraid of people — of their looks, their laughter, their power over me. I have kept my hand down and my mouth shut.
> Take all the man-fearing and woman-fearing spirit out of me. Fill me with Your perfect love until there is no room left for fear to rule.
> Show me that I am one with all Your people in Christ Jesus, and let the mountains in my mind become molehills.
> And when You prompt me to stand, to speak or to lift my hand, give me grace to obey at once. Amen.

# The Royal Black

> God that made the world and all things therein … hath made of one blood all nations of men for to dwell on all the face of the earth. ACTS 17:24–26

Amanda Smith wrote about race and the church more honestly than almost any Christian writer of her century, white or Black. She did it without bitterness and without flattery. She named what was done to her, she named who did it — including her own people — and she refused to let any of it define her.

## Just turn black

People sometimes told her how nicely she got on, and how kindly everyone treated her. “But if you want to know and understand properly what Amanda Smith has to contend with,” she said, “just turn black and go about as I do, and you will come to a different conclusion.” And she added, with a sharpness that still stings, that some people would understand the quintessence of sanctifying grace if they could be black for about twenty-four hours.

> Remember them that are in bonds, as bound with them; and them which suffer adversity, as being yourselves also in the body. HEBREWS 13:3

Her Autobiography supplies the evidence. As a girl in service she was led last at class meeting, week after week, whatever seat she took. When she arrived to preach at Salem, she had to ride on the roof of the omnibus, because Black passengers were not allowed inside, and was carried round for three-quarters of an hour until every white passenger had been set down. She spent a night in a railway waiting room in Texas because no hotel would take a Black woman. At a Bible reading in a New York church she was ordered out by a woman who waved her to the door. At a famous series of meetings in Philadelphia she was told, kindly, that the meeting that day was for wealthy ladies and she had better come another time; she stood at the door and received a blessing there. 

## The same in black and white

What makes her witness so trustworthy is that she spared no one. She saw prejudice in white churches, and she saw vanity, snobbery and hostility to holiness in Black ones. Human nature, she wrote, “is the same in black and white folks.” Her own pastor mocked her from the pulpit, and she was hurt as much by her own people as by anyone.

> My brethren, have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons. JAMES 2:1

She was not naive about the smiles, either. At a meeting in New York a lady fidgeted and fanned and fussed at having to sit beside her, until Amanda spoke and the Lord blessed the people; then the lady turned to her all smiles and said she felt highly honoured to have sat by Amanda Smith. Amanda looked at her, pitied her, and felt sick. “From all hollowness and sham,” she prayed in her heart, “Good Lord deliver us!”

## Common sense on the colour line

Against all this she set the kindness she received, recorded just as carefully — above all a minister’s family in Washington who invited her to lunch when there was not a restaurant in the capital that would serve her. She describes it with delight: the little girl who took her hand and asked if she was going to sit at the table with her, the easy introductions, the talk of India and Africa, the prayers. What was needed on the colour line, she said, was nothing heroic. “Thank God for real, practical, inright, outright, downright common sense; that is all I think people need on the color line.”

> Let love be without dissimulation. Abhor that which is evil; cleave to that which is good. ROMANS 12:9

## Royal stock

When a lady at Ocean Grove asked her, only half in jest, whether she would not rather be white than black if she could, she answered: “No, no,” and then, “as the Lord lives, I would rather be black and fully saved than to be white and not saved.” She said she was glad she had no choice in her colour, because as a girl she was passionately fond of pea-green and would probably have chosen that. God’s colour, she decided, was the best and most substantial. And she added the line that sums up her theology of race: it is the blood that makes whiteness — the blood of Christ, which cleanses black and white alike.

> Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow. ISAIAH 1:18

She did not pretend that colour made no difference in this world. After listing the humiliations of travel, she wrote one of her finest sentences: “Now, to say that being black did not make it inconvenient for us often, would not be true; but belonging to royal stock, as we do, we propose braving this inconvenience for the present.” She would pass on, she said, into the great big future, where all these little things would be lost because of their absolute smallness, and she prayed that the Lord would send that future to meet her. She belonged, she said elsewhere, to royalty, and was well acquainted with the King of kings.

> But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people. 1 PETER 2:9

## Not looking only at the wrongs

In Liberia she once heard a party of newly arrived emigrants from America give speeches that did little but rake over the hard things white people had done to them. She felt sorry for it — not because the grievances were unreal, but because of the spirit. “It is wrong in those who have caused these grievances,” she wrote, “but it does not help us any to forever keep looking at the wrongs, and never see any of the good, which has always gone along side by side with the wrong.”

That must not be misheard. She never excused injustice or told the wronged to be silent; her own book is a record of wrongs, named. What she refused was to let the wrongs become the whole story. She held together the plain truth about prejudice and the larger truth of a God who had made her royal.

A reader today will want to go further than she did in some directions. She lived under a system of legal and social exclusion that the church too often blessed, and her patience was sometimes the patience of someone who had no other option. Christians now have more power to change such things than she did, and more duty. But no one should read her and think that honesty about injustice requires bitterness, or that love requires silence. She had both honesty and love, and she kept both.

> For ye are all the children of God by faith in Christ Jesus. GALATIANS 3:26

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Take her challenge seriously: imagine going through one ordinary week as someone your church or town looks down on. What would be different? Write it down.
2. Is there anyone in your church who is, in effect, led last? Do something this week to change that.
3. Examine yourself for hollowness and sham: for kindness that depends on someone’s reputation. Pray her prayer against it.
4. If you have suffered prejudice, name it honestly to God. Then ask Him to show you the good that has gone alongside it, without denying the wrong.
5. Practise her common sense: invite someone of a different background from yours to share a meal, and treat it as the most natural thing in the world.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You have made of one blood all nations of men, and in Christ You have made us one.
> Forgive the church, and forgive me, for every time we have led some of Your children last and sent them to the gallery.
> Deliver me from all hollowness and sham. Give me real, practical, downright common sense in love.
> And where I have been wronged, keep me honest and keep me free from bitterness, until the great big future comes to meet us. Amen.

# I Belong to Jesus

> And I heard a loud voice saying in heaven … for the accuser of our brethren is cast down, which accused them before our God day and night. And they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb, and by the word of their testimony. REVELATION 12:10–11

No reader of Amanda Smith’s Autobiography can miss how often the devil appears in it. He argues with her in the kitchen, whispers in her right ear in church, and turns up on Sixth Avenue to ask where all her holiness has gone. Modern readers may find this strange. But it is one of the most practical parts of her teaching. She knew she had an enemy, she learned to recognise his voice, and she learned how to send him running.

## Let down after the mountain

For about three weeks after her sanctification, she says, God let her walk above the world. Then He began, by degrees, to let her down, and the tempter seemed to be let loose upon her. She described it as a man turning a hose on a pavement: temptation came at her in every direction and with such force that she could not pray or do anything; she could only say, Fire away, but I will trust in God, though He slay me. It was dark. Then light broke in.

> Beloved, think it not strange concerning the fiery trial which is to try you, as though some strange thing happened unto you. 1 PETER 4:12

Why does God permit such seasons? She gave two sound reasons: first, to develop the strength and muscle of the soul for greater service, and second, to bring the believer into sympathy with others who are tempted, so that she can help them. Not long after, at a camp meeting, she met a young Sunday-school teacher who had lived in the joy of holiness for a year and then fallen under a black cloud for three months; the accuser told her she must be a hypocrite. Everyone gave her advice. Amanda, a Black woman who did not like to push herself forward, waited three days, then leaned over and whispered to her that it was a temptation of the devil, and she should praise the Lord. The woman asked through her tears whether Amanda had ever been so since she was sanctified. Yes, she said; she had been shut up in prison for three weeks. The young woman sprang to her feet free.

> For in that he himself hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted. HEBREWS 2:18

## Tell the other side

The young woman’s torment came largely from a lie: that sanctified people never have a cloud. Amanda had believed it herself. In one long darkness after a painful estrangement from her closest friend, she went to a holiness meeting and asked the sisters whether sanctified people ever had such an experience. They shook their heads and looked solemn, and one after another joined the accuser in condemning her for small neglects. Only one English lady by the window said something different: “The Lord has a controversy with Amanda Smith, and it must be settled between her and Him alone.” In a flash she saw that she had been leaning on her friend more than on God, and she was set free.

Looking back, she wrote: “I think sanctified people ought to tell the other side, for it is no sign that you are backsliding when there comes a shadow over your Spirit, even after you are wholly sanctified.”

> Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him. PSALM 42:5

That is a word every Christian tradition needs. Wherever testimony is expected to be all sunshine, the believer in darkness hides it and suffers alone; she herself confesses that she talked only of the bright side, for fear people would think she had backslidden. The remedy is honesty. The saints of Scripture — David, Job, Jeremiah, Paul — told the other side, and so should we.

## Learning his voice

How did she know when it was the accuser? He argued that God would not hear. He told her she had said all that before. He pointed at her circumstances as proof that God had done nothing. He dug up small neglects — a night she fell asleep on her knees, a day she went out without her tracts — and made them the explanation of everything. And he spoke, often, in the voice of reasonableness and even of piety: she should not make her husband angry; she should look after her child; she should not cast pearls before swine. Satan, she wrote more than once, can transform himself into an angel of light.

> Lest Satan should get an advantage of us: for we are not ignorant of his devices. 2 CORINTHIANS 2:11

Her most useful test was this. When she confessed the little sins the accuser raised, she would feel a voice say, No, that is not it. The Spirit’s conviction is specific, and leads to confession and peace. The accuser’s condemnation is vague and endless, and leads only to despair. When confession brings no peace, it is often because the charge was never from God in the first place.

## Happiness or no happiness

The climax of that dark season came on the street. She had just been freed from her dependence on her friend, and was full of joy, when she saw the friend across the road turn her head away. A pang went through her, and the old voice said at once: You have no joy now. You are not happy either. Where is all your sanctification and holiness? And for the first time, she says, she clearly realised that it was Satan himself. Her answer is one of the best things in her book: “Ah! Ah! Mr. Satan, it is you, is it? Well, now look here, happiness or no happiness, joy or no joy, sanctification or no sanctification, I belong to Jesus!”

> Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us. ROMANS 8:37

Then she sang, quietly, a hymn about the cleansing blood of Christ, and felt the hateful influence break. From that day, she says, she seemed able to know him whenever he approached. Her counsel: if you keep close to the blood he soon leaves, and nothing will put him to flight so quickly as a song or a testimony of the power of the blood.

## Where to be careful

She saw the devil’s hand in many things, and a modern reader may think she saw it too readily. Not every dark mood is an assault; some are illness, grief or exhaustion. Nor is every inner voice that argues against a plan the devil’s; sometimes it is good sense. The reader should not learn from her to blame Satan for what calls for rest, a doctor, or repentance. But neither should the reader learn from modern scepticism to forget that the New Testament takes the adversary seriously, and tells us to resist him steadfast in the faith. On that she was entirely scriptural.

> Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. JAMES 4:7

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Are you in a season of darkness now? Say aloud, as she did: happiness or no happiness, joy or no joy, I belong to Jesus.
2. Learn to distinguish specific conviction from vague condemnation. If you have confessed and there is still no peace, ask whether the charge is from God at all.
3. Tell someone the other side of your Christian life this week — honestly, not dramatically — so that they know they are not alone in theirs.
4. Keep a song or a Scripture about the blood of Christ ready for the next time the accuser speaks. Use it.
5. Think of someone who is under a cloud. Do not heap advice on them; tell them quietly that you have been there too.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, the accuser has spoken to me in my own voice and in the voice of reason, and I have believed him.
> Teach me to know his tones, and to answer him not with my feelings or my record, but with Your blood and Your word.
> Happiness or no happiness, joy or no joy, I belong to You.
> And use my dark seasons to make me tender with others in theirs. Amen.

# A Word Behind Thee

> And thine ears shall hear a word behind thee, saying, This is the way, walk ye in it, when ye turn to the right hand, and when ye turn to the left. ISAIAH 30:21

Amanda Smith lived by the leading of the Spirit more consciously than most Christians whose lives are on record. Her Autobiography is full of whispers. On Sunday morning go to Green Street. Take the tracts. Speak to that young man. Go stand in the way. Salem. You are sitting in the draught. She asked God for signs, opened her Bible for guidance, and describes at least two visions. For some readers this will be the most attractive part of her story; for others, the most troubling. She was more careful about it than a quick reading suggests.

## A Father who speaks

The foundation of her teaching is simple and scriptural. God is a Father, and a Father speaks to His children; the Spirit who lives in the believer leads, prompts, checks and warns.

> For as many as are led by the Spirit of God, they are the sons of God. ROMANS 8:14

One evening she was given two tickets to a church fair. She was tired and did not want to go, and she disapproved of fairs anyway. But as she prayed it became clear that she should go and take some tracts. She went, felt out of place, and asked the Lord what He wanted. Go stand in the way, came the answer. She stood at the top of the stairs, and the Spirit singled out one of two cheerful young men coming up; she spoke to him, and he answered politely that there was time enough for him. On Saturday she heard he had been found dead in his bed. She went and looked at him. She was sad, and glad she had obeyed.

On another day she was running late for a washing job and the prompting came to take her tracts; she was afraid the lady would not pay her if she was late, and left them behind. When she arrived the lady said the washing was put off till next week. She saw that she had been hindered from a blessing, and wrote, “O, it is so safe to obey even though it may be dark.”

> My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. JOHN 10:27

## Misled

What makes her teaching trustworthy is that she did not hide the times she was wrong. The worst was her second marriage. She had prayed for guidance, and believed afterwards that God had given her light, but she did not walk in it. James Smith told her he was preparing to enter the travelling ministry, and she pictured herself as a minister’s wife working beside him. It looked like the Lord’s will. After she had given her consent, she went to the Lord to have it ratified, “but not a ray of light came.” She went ahead anyway, reasoning that everything would be clear once the work began. It was not. He had never intended to join the Conference, and the marriage brought her years of sorrow.

Her comment is searching: “At that very hour Satan had gained the victory over me and yet I did not know it was he.” And again: “O, the subtilty of Satan, how he can transform himself into an angel of light to deceive even to this day.”

> Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God. 1 JOHN 4:1

## Her own safeguards

From such failures she learned safeguards worth more than any number of dramatic leadings.

First, she tested what she heard. When the word Salem came to her she did not simply pack. She said, “O, Lord, don’t let Satan deceive me, make it very plain to me,” and asked again and again until she was sure. When a prompting concerned someone else, she often asked God to confirm it by some outward circumstance before she acted.

Second, she learned that God’s refusals are also His guidance. When she was drawn to leave her own church and join the Free Methodists, she prayed for the minister to invite new members, and he did not. She saw afterwards that it had been an answer: “For sometimes when the Lord denies a request, it’s as much an answer as when He grants it.”

Third, she tested impressions by their fruit and their direction. The accuser’s whispers, she found, pushed toward fear, delay, self-protection and despair. The Spirit’s whispers, though often costly, came with a quiet hush and peace.

Fourth, she became suspicious of her own desires. The marriage taught her that a wish can dress itself up as a leading. When a good man at a camp meeting offered her a comfortable home for life in his family, it seemed at first glance plainly right; she spread it before the Lord, and found it was not His will for her.

> In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths. PROVERBS 3:6

## Where she must be read with care

Even with all this, a careful reader must say plainly that some of her practices are not safe patterns. She often opened her Bible at random in prayer and took the first words her eye fell on as God’s answer. God is gracious and sometimes meets His children that way, as He met her. But Scripture was not given to be used as a lot, and the habit can make the Bible say things it never says. She asked God for signs — that a particular person be converted, that a gale drop before an evening meeting — and she believed He gave them. He may have. But the Lord who gave signs in Scripture also warned against a generation that demands them, and a Christian who cannot move without one has made God’s guidance hang on something other than His Word and His character.

Her visions call for the same care. She describes seeing the Lord Jesus in her room, and a great arm in the clouds, and she did not doubt that they were given to her. We need not scoff; nor should we seek such things. The New Testament places the believer’s confidence in the Word made flesh and in the written Word, not in private appearances. Where a leading contradicts Scripture, or sets aside plain duty, or cannot be tested, it must be refused, however vivid.

> To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them. ISAIAH 8:20

## What to keep

What should we keep? Almost everything that matters. The conviction that God speaks to His children. The habit of listening for Him through the day, in small things as well as great. The willingness to obey a quiet prompting to speak to a stranger, to give, to go. The honesty to admit when we got it wrong. And the safeguards she learned the hard way: test the spirits, suspect your own wishes, accept God’s refusals, look for peace rather than pressure, and do nothing contrary to His Word.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Spend part of tomorrow deliberately listening for the Spirit’s quiet promptings in small things — whom to greet, what to say, when to be silent — and act on one.
2. Recall a time you mistook your own wish for God’s leading. What were the warning signs you ignored? Write them down.
3. Before your next significant decision, apply her safeguards: pray for plain light, ask whether a refusal might be the answer, and test the leading against Scripture.
4. If you have a habit of opening the Bible at random for guidance, replace it this month with steady reading in context, and see how God speaks.
5. Is there a prompting you have been delaying? It is so safe to obey even though it may be dark. Do it this week.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You are not silent. Open my ears to hear the word behind me that says, This is the way.
> Deliver me from mistaking my wishes for Your will, and from the enemy who comes as an angel of light.
> Teach me to test every spirit by Your Word, to accept Your refusals as answers, and to walk in the light You give.
> And when You speak, give me the simple courage to obey at once. Amen.

# To Obey Is Better Than Sacrifice

> Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to hearken than the fat of rams. 1 SAMUEL 15:22

If one word sums up the practical side of Amanda Smith’s religion, it is obedience. Not the grim obedience of a servant, but the glad obedience of a child to a Father she trusted. After her sanctification, she says, the Lord’s Prayer opened to her as if for the first time. Thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven: and she asked herself, how do the angels do God’s will? Do they hesitate? Do they question? Do they shrink? No. “Swift, prompt, loyal obedience by angels, and I asked God that I may do His will on earth like angels do it in heaven.”

## Obedience in small things

Her book is striking for how small most of her tests were. Lift your hand in church. Give a tract to that lad on the streetcar. Put your two dollars in the collection for India. Go to that meeting although you are tired. Don’t go back and accept fifty cents for a three-weeks’ washing. All of it, she believed, mattered to God, because obedience is not measured by the size of the command but by the readiness of the heart.

> He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much: and he that is unjust in the least is unjust also in much. LUKE 16:10

And she found, over and over, that small obediences opened large doors. The two dollars she gave to Mrs. Butler for the women of India, which she had meant for her daughter’s shoes, were followed that evening by three dollars under her plate at a friend’s tent. The tract given in fear to a young gentleman on Sixth Avenue brought him, she later heard, to peace with God. She did not obey in order to be rewarded, but she came to believe that God is no man’s debtor.

## Confessed failures

What keeps this from being a boast is that she recorded her disobediences as carefully as her obediences. Once on a streetcar she had a tract in her hand and felt prompted to give it to a boy who got on. She hesitated, telling herself she would wait until some of the people got out, then until she got out. The car stopped, the lad jumped off and ran away, and she was left with words ringing in her: “His blood will I require at your hand.” She prayed all the way home for forgiveness.

> When I say unto the wicked, O wicked man, thou shalt surely die; if thou dost not speak to warn the wicked from his way, that wicked man shall die in his iniquity; but his blood will I require at thine hand. EZEKIEL 33:8

On a voyage home, and again on a ship in the Bay of Bengal, she felt clearly led to speak to the passengers, and let herself be talked out of it — once by the reasonable thought that there had been enough for one day, once by the devil’s pious reminder not to cast pearls before swine. Both times she saw her mistake too late and wept before the Lord. “The mistakes of my life have been many,” she wrote. She called herself a coward, and marvelled at the patience of God with such cowards.

That confession is itself a teaching. Holiness, as she understood it, did not mean a record without failure. It meant a heart that wanted God’s will above everything, and was quick to confess and try again when it failed.

## The ocean

The greatest test came in 1878. A friend invited her to England and offered to pay her way. Her first reaction was that England was for swell people, not for Amanda Smith, the coloured washerwoman. Then she prayed and became convinced, as surely as she knew her own existence, that God wanted her to go. And then she wrote a letter refusing — she was tired, it was so far, there was her daughter to think of — and posted it with a strange dread on her.

That evening an avalanche of darkness came over her. She locked the doors, turned down the lamp, and lay on the floor asking God what was wrong. At last the answer came, and it went straight to the point. “You are going about telling people to trust the Lord in the dark,” said the voice; and then, “you tell other people to do what you are not willing to do yourself.” She was afraid of the ocean. She was afraid to trust the Lord and go to England.

> Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. PROVERBS 3:5

What followed was an hour of facing every fear. She saw the ship wrecked in a storm, heard the timbers crack and the people scream. She saw the telegram reaching her daughter, and her daughter wild with grief. She imagined her daughter falling sick while she was abroad, and dying, and being buried before her mother could get home. She saw each of her six living brothers and sisters sink and die, and went, on her knees, to each funeral. And then she said the sentence that became her motto: “But,” I said, “to stay here and disobey God—I can’t afford to take the consequence, I would rather go and obey God than to stay here and know that I disobeyed.”

A flood of light and peace came, and she sang. She sailed alone a few weeks later, and the voyage opened twelve years of work on three continents.

## Counting the cost

That hour on the floor is the heart of her teaching on obedience. She did not pretend the cost was small. She looked at every possible loss, one by one, and let it pass before her. Only then did she say yes. That is exactly what Jesus asked of those who would follow Him: to sit down first and count the cost. The easy yes, given before the cost is faced, often collapses at the first storm. The yes given after the cost has been counted holds.

> For which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it? LUKE 14:28

One caution. Her strong sense of God’s direct commands made her obedience vivid and brave. It can also, in less careful hands, make every whim a command and every hesitation a sin. The chapter before this one has set out her own safeguards. Obedience is owed to God’s voice, not to every impulse, and the primary place where His voice is heard is His Word. But where His will is plain, her counsel is the counsel of Samuel: to obey is better than sacrifice.

> If ye love me, keep my commandments. JOHN 14:15

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly today, stopping at Thy will be done. Ask yourself her question: do I hesitate, question, or shrink?
2. Name one small, plain obedience you have been putting off — an apology, a letter, a gift, a word to a neighbour — and do it within three days.
3. Recall a time when, like her on the streetcar, you let the moment pass. Confess it, receive forgiveness, and ask God for the next opportunity.
4. If there is a large step you believe God is asking of you, sit down and count the cost on paper, one loss at a time. Then ask whether you can afford to disobey.
5. Tell someone about a failure of courage in your own Christian life. Honesty about failure encourages others more than stories of success.

### A PRAYER

> Father, teach me to do Your will on earth as the angels do it in heaven — swiftly, promptly, loyally, without shrinking.
> Forgive me for the moments I let pass, the tracts I did not give and the words I did not say. Give me another chance.
> Where You are calling me to something costly, help me count the cost honestly, and then help me say yes.
> Let me know that I cannot afford to disobey You, and that I can afford anything else. Amen.

# The Lord Will Provide

> But my God shall supply all your need according to his riches in glory by Christ Jesus. PHILIPPIANS 4:19

When Amanda Smith was a little girl in Maryland, her grandmother prayed for years that the children would be set free, and they were. Amanda quotes more than once an old song: in some way or other, the Lord will provide; it may not be my way, it may not be thy way, and yet in His own way, the Lord will provide. She lived most of her life on very little money, much of it without any fixed income at all, and she learned to pray for rent, shoes, bread, fares and passages with the same simplicity she prayed for souls.

## Thirteen cents

One of her barest stories is also one of her best. When she first began to feel that God was calling her away from service into His work, her washing dried up. The families she worked for went to the country, and she was reduced to thirteen cents. The tempter told her that with all her talk of trusting the Lord she would end up begging. “It is none of your business,” she answered, “if I do. I belong to the Lord, and if He wants me to beg I’ll do it.”

She went to the grocer on the corner, where she never took anything on credit, laid her money on the counter, and asked for a loaf of bread, a quart of potatoes, three slices of salt pork and a bundle of wood, adding, “this is every cent of money I have between me and death.” He looked at her, said it was not enough, and then went and got everything — and gave her back three cents.

> I have been young, and now am old; yet have I not seen the righteous forsaken, nor his seed begging bread. PSALM 37:25

## Shoes by faith

Before she went to Salem her shoes were broken in the sole. She told the Lord she was willing to go in them if He wished, but that if she got her feet wet she would be ill. Then she did something that sums up her whole approach. She claimed the shoes by faith, and, as she puts it, put them on as really as ever she put on a pair of shoes in her life; she got up and walked about feeling that she really had them. Three days later, after a meeting, an old man shook her hand and put something in it. At home she found five dollars, counted it three times, and thought she must be asleep. Then a voice reminded her: you prayed about your shoes.

> Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them. MARK 11:24

She took that promise literally. It must be said that some later teachers took the same promise and made of it a technique for getting whatever one wanted. She did not. Her prayers were for needs, not luxuries — shoes, rent, a funeral, a passage to where God had sent her — and her faith was always bound up with obedience. She asked because she was doing what God told her and needed what He knew she needed.

## I never tell people my need

Her rule, she said, was this: “I never tell people my need; I always make my needs known directly to God.” She gratefully accepted help from many friends, but she refused to hint or to beg. She tells with rueful humour of sitting in a sister’s kitchen at Salem, hungry, while a pot of cabbage boiled on the fire, hinting all she knew how and getting no supper. And she drew the lesson cheerfully: she found out that a good deal of praying fills you up pretty well when you cannot get anything else.

> Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. PHILIPPIANS 4:6

At her first great holiness camp meeting she could not afford to go at all, until a friend asked her why she did not trust the Lord and go. Trust the Lord about her rent? She had never heard of such a thing. She thought a moment and said, I will. She scraped together her fare with ten cents over, missed her train, found herself a quarter short, and had to borrow two dollars from a friend. On the train a gentleman took some of her holiness tracts for his pastor and pressed two dollars on her, and she repaid the loan on Saturday. It was there, she says, that she first began to learn the deep meaning of Ask, and ye shall receive.

## When the money did not come

Not every prayer was answered at once. In Liberia she saw native boys drifting about on Sundays and longed to repair a room where she could gather and teach them. She prayed that friends in America would send the money, naming them one by one before the Lord. Weeks passed; steamers came and went; no money came. At last she asked the Lord what it meant, and the answer she heard was blunt: “You are not trusting in Me, you are trusting in America.” She saw it at once, confessed it, and let go of every hope in America. Within a fortnight help began to arrive, from Ireland, from India, and in small sums from home, and she opened her room.

> Thus saith the LORD; Cursed be the man that trusteth in man, and maketh flesh his arm. JEREMIAH 17:5

That is an important corrective within her own story. Faith for provision is not a way of getting God to fund our plans, nor of leaning on friends while calling it faith. It is trust in a Father who knows what we need, and who sometimes withholds until our trust has come back to Him. She held both: a bold expectation that God would meet every real need, and a humble willingness to be corrected.

## Glory for nothing

The spirit of all this is best caught in a moment at the Sing Sing camp meeting. She had given her last two dollars for the women of India, and was left with fifty cents. When the collection basket came round for the water tanks, the whisper came to give that too, and the tempter said she would be a fool. She shut her eyes and turned away, but the collector held the basket right under her face. She threw in the fifty cents and said, “Glory to God for nothing, and hallelujah for everything, for I have got Jesus yet.” That evening she found three dollars under her plate.

> As sorrowful, yet alway rejoicing; as poor, yet making many rich; as having nothing, and yet possessing all things. 2 CORINTHIANS 6:10

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Make a list of your real needs this month, as distinct from your wants. Bring them to God one by one, specifically, as she did.
2. Try her rule for a season: tell God your needs before you tell anyone else, and do not hint.
3. Is there a gift you have been prompted to give that feels too costly? Pray over it, and if the prompting holds, give it with glory to God for nothing.
4. Where have you been trusting in people, reputation or savings rather than in God? Confess it, even if the provision is real.
5. Write down one time God provided for you when you had nothing between you and need. Tell someone about it this week.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You know what I need before I ask. In some way or other, in Your own way, You will provide.
> Teach me to bring You every real need, the rent and the shoes as well as the soul, and to trust You with the answer.
> Deliver me from trusting in people and reputation, and from using faith to fund my own plans.
> And when I have nothing, let me say with her: glory to God for nothing, and hallelujah for everything, for I have got Jesus yet. Amen.

# Thy Will Be Done

> And he went a little further, and fell on his face, and prayed, saying, O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt. MATTHEW 26:39

Amanda Smith had prayed the Lord’s Prayer every night since she was a small child. After her sanctification the words Thy will be done took on a meaning she had never understood, and a few months later she learned what they cost. The chapter of her Autobiography called Learning Thy Will Be Done is one of the most painful things she ever wrote, and one of the most important.

## More than all burnt offerings

She describes what it is to say those words from the heart. It is to stand by your dear ones dying, “with not two dollars for funeral expenses,” with a husband and father away who might have come and did not, with no one to go to, when the heavens seem brass and the earth iron, and your own body is exhausted from work and watching and too little food. To say Thy will be done then, from the heart, “is more than all burnt offerings and sacrifice.”

> And to love him with all the heart … and to love his neighbour as himself, is more than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices. MARK 12:33

She knew what she was talking about. She had buried a baby daughter, and a baby son, in the years before. Now came her little Will. When he was three days old she had knelt by her bed and consecrated him to God, promising to train him for the ministry. She admits there was selfishness in it: she had not done this with the others, and she half thought that if she gave him to the Lord, the Lord would let her keep him.

## Five weeks

At five months old he had a convulsion. For five weeks she nursed him night and day, never taking off her clothes except to change them, doing her washing in between. It never entered her head that he would die. Then one morning she found his little foot swollen, and knew what it meant. She tried to say Thy will be done, and could not.

She took him to a doctor who told her not to worry, and spent all but fifty cents of what she had on the visit and the prescription. Still she could not say it. So she resolved that she would neither eat nor drink until she could say, from her heart, the will of the Lord be done. It took from Thursday to Friday afternoon.

> Though he slay me, yet will I trust in him. JOB 13:15

Then, alone, her heart seemed to sink into a deep quiet, and she could say it. It was sweet, she says, as honey. And then came a strange test. A voice seemed to say that she could have her choice: if she asked for the life of her child, she could have it as easily as turning her hand. She answered, “Lord, Thy will is so sweet, I only want Thy will.” It came again, and she answered again. Then joy sprang up in her, and she went out of the room saying, Victory, victory, thanks be to God.

About two o’clock the next morning little Will fell asleep in Jesus in her arms. She washed the body and laid it out herself. There were no tears; God, she said, seemed to dry them up with joy.

## Sinking into the will of God

What are we to make of that? It would be easy to fear that it teaches a heartless piety. It does not. Later that same morning she broke down in a flood of tears, felt she must sink, had to lie down three times before she could get dressed, and set out across the city to beg help with the burial. Her husband sent word he was sick and had no money. Her friends were busy. What she had been given was not an absence of grief but a peace beneath it that did not give way.

Nor was it fatalism. She did not believe that everything that happens is good. She believed that God is good, and wise, and that His will — even when it includes what we would never choose — is safer than ours. She learned the same thing in those years about her father, who had grown bitter and fallen away from God. She prayed for him with all her might, and was brought to the point of asking herself whether she could submit if he were lost, or crippled in a fall from the building he worked on. At last, as she put it, “I let go of father and took hold of God.” Two years later her father died at peace, sweetened, as she said, and brought back to the Lord. “What else ought we to do,” she asked, “when we bring our friends, but to sink into the will of God, and put them into His hands, and trust Him?”

> Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you. 1 PETER 5:7

## And I saw God

Then came a small mercy that she never forgot. On her way out to beg help for the funeral, a messenger insisted she come first to a friend in Clinton Court. The friend said simply that she had heard the baby was dead, and that if twenty dollars would help, she could have it. “And I saw God,” Amanda wrote, “and wept!”

> Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. PSALM 103:13

## Weighing it

One caution is worth making. Her experience of being offered the child’s life, and choosing God’s will instead, should not become a pattern that grieving parents feel they must reproduce. Most Christian parents who have lost a child did not have such an experience, and did not fail God by praying desperately to the end for their child to live. Jesus Himself prayed that the cup might pass. Submission and pleading are not enemies.

But having said that, what she found is what every believer must in some way find: that there is a place beneath grief where the will of God is not a sentence but a refuge. She called it sinking into the will of God, and she found it sweet.

> Blessed be God … the God of all comfort; who comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble. 2 CORINTHIANS 1:3–4

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly and stop at Thy will be done. Is there anything you are not willing to say it about? Tell God honestly.
2. Is there someone you love for whom you have been trying, as she said, to drive God? Let go of them and take hold of God, in prayer, today.
3. If you are grieving, do not force a peace you do not feel. Pray as Jesus prayed in Gethsemane, honestly, and ask for the refuge beneath the grief.
4. Be someone else’s twenty dollars. Is there a family near you facing a death or a crisis without means? Help them this week, simply and without fuss.
5. Read her chapter Learning Thy Will Be Done in the Autobiography, slowly, and pray for parents who have lost children.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I have prayed Thy will be done since I was a child, and I have not always meant it. Teach me to mean it.
> Where I am trying to drive You, help me to let go and take hold of You. Where I am grieving, meet me beneath the grief.
> Let Your will be not a sentence I fear but a refuge I run to, sweeter than my own.
> And make me, to someone in their darkest hour, a sign that You see them. Amen.

# Common Sense and the Great Physician

> Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord: and the prayer of faith shall save the sick. JAMES 5:14–15

In the late nineteenth century a movement for divine healing grew up among holiness Christians, and many of Amanda Smith’s friends were part of it. Some of its leaders taught that Christ’s atonement provides for the healing of the body as surely as for the pardon of sin, and that the use of medicine or doctors shows a lack of faith. For a time she practised it more strictly than many of its advocates. What she concluded, after long experience, is one of the wisest things in her book.

## A year and eight months

It began with a child’s physiology book fished out of an ash-box. Reading it one Monday morning in the autumn of 1868, soon after her sanctification, she marvelled at how fearfully and wonderfully the body is made, and reasoned that the God who made it could reach its troubles directly, without medicine going all the way round. Alone in the room, she promised the Lord she would never take another bit of medicine without His telling her to, and threw out the few simple remedies she had. For a year and eight months she used nothing.

> I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made: marvellous are thy works. PSALM 139:14

Then she took a severe cold that grew worse for three days and nights, with fever and a racking cough. She prayed as before, and grew worse. On the fourth morning she knelt by a chair and asked the Lord what she had done. The answer came three times, gently, in the form of a question: if you knew it was God’s will for you to take medicine, would you be willing? Three times she said no. It took an hour, she says, before her will went down. When it did, she was willing to go and swallow the whole drug store if He told her to. What He told her was to use a simple remedy she had used a thousand times before, and in twenty-four hours she was well.

Her conclusion was startling: “God showed me. I was worshiping my will.”

## Will-worship in the name of faith

She came to see the same thing in others. She had no controversy with anyone, she said, but she saw in many of those who professed divine healing the same spirit of will-worship she had had, and did not think they knew it. What troubled her most was the teaching that all Christians should trust God to heal them without means, even when their common sense told them to use means, and must “close their eyes, ignore all symptoms, and by the force of will, which they must call ‘faith,’ ride over everything.” That, she said, was where the tug of war came in.

> Wherefore be ye not unwise, but understanding what the will of the Lord is. EPHESIANS 5:17

And she put her finger on its cruelty. My neighbour prays and is healed; I pray and am not; someone tells me it is a lack of faith, or something wrong in my consecration. “Now comes the question: ‘How do you know that? Who told you so?’” Either I must stand judged, or I must judge, and where is the authority for either? She would not consign a poor, weak child of God to perdition for not being healed.

## Means are God’s gift

Her positive teaching is balanced and simple. The days of miracles are not past; God has healed without means of any kind, as well as with them. But she wrote: “I do not believe in calling the doctor for every little thing, or making a drug store of one’s self; but I believe it right when you need medicine or doctor, to use both, prayerfully, and with common-sense.” And she gave common sense its proper dignity: it is, she said, “as much God’s gift to us as any other blessing.”

> A merry heart doeth good like a medicine: but a broken spirit drieth the bones. PROVERBS 17:22

She illustrated it with a story. A good minister had been going deaf in one ear for a year, praying and believing all the while and growing worse. His wife gently insisted he see a doctor. He went, and came back two hours later clear as a bell: the doctor had found the wax very dry and taken out about a thimbleful. Amanda’s comment was dry too: “Some people would have teased the Lord to have Him clean out their ears, when they might do it themselves, or get someone to do it to whom God had given the sense and ability.”

## Tested again

The lesson was tested again, twenty years later, when she landed in Liverpool from Africa, broken in health. Dear friends in England were convinced of healing without means and pressed it on her with great love. She tried honestly to see it as they did. Two ladies anointed her with oil and prayed; she held on by faith for ten days, longing for an assurance of healing like the assurance she had received for her conversion and sanctification, and it never came. On Christmas Day her friends told her the pain was only a temptation, and she endured Christmas dinner in agony and then walked the floor of her room, finding that in spite of her will and faith the pain “would not be ignored a bit!”

Meanwhile, when she cried out in the night, she heard a quiet word telling her to put cotton wadding on her aching arm, and on another night to drink beetroot tea for her cough. She did both, and was helped. Her friends thought she should have ignored such means. She concluded that God was teaching her not to ignore the use of means, and that He is honoured as much when He tells her to do something and she obeys as when He tells her not to and she obeys. She had prayed sincerely for light, she said, and believed God had saved her from bondage on these points.

> Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights. JAMES 1:17

## Weighing it

Her position will satisfy neither those who teach that healing is guaranteed in the atonement nor those who doubt that God heals today. It is, for that reason, close to the balance of Scripture. The same apostle who saw miracles told Timothy to take a little wine for his stomach, left Trophimus sick at Miletum, and called Luke the beloved physician. James tells the sick to call for the elders and pray; he does not tell them to throw away their medicine. Amanda Smith prayed for healing, was anointed, and believed God had often healed her without means; she also used simple remedies and doctors when she needed them, and thanked God for both.

> Luke, the beloved physician, and Demas, greet you. COLOSSIANS 4:14

The deepest thing she saw was not about medicine at all. It was about the will. Faith that is really self-will dressed in religious language is not faith, and it can hurt people terribly. True faith bows to God’s way, whatever it is, and is not ashamed of a thimbleful of wax.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is there an area of your life where you have been, in her phrase, worshipping your will and calling it faith? Ask God to show you.
2. If you are ill, pray for healing, and also use the means God has given — doctors, medicine, rest — prayerfully and with common sense.
3. Have you ever implied that someone was not healed because of weak faith? If so, confess it, and resolve never to lay that burden on a suffering believer.
4. Thank God specifically this week for a doctor, nurse or medicine that has helped you or someone you love.
5. Read James 5:13–16 and pray for someone who is sick, by name, asking God to heal them in His way.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You made me fearfully and wonderfully, and You are my Physician. I bring You my body and all its weakness.
> Deliver me from worshipping my own will, even when it calls itself faith. Bow my will to Yours, whether You heal by a word or by a remedy.
> Thank You for common sense, and for the doctors and medicines You have given. Teach me to use them prayerfully.
> And keep me from ever judging a sick brother or sister whom You have not yet healed. Amen.

# Chosen and Ordained

> Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you, and ordained you, that ye should go and bring forth fruit, and that your fruit should remain. JOHN 15:16

Amanda Smith preached for over forty years on four continents, to crowds of thousands and to handfuls in thatched chapels, and she was never ordained by any church. She never asked to be. When the question was raised, and it was raised often, she gave the same answer: she had received her ordination from the One who said, Ye have not chosen me, but I have chosen you, and ordained you. It is one of the simplest and strongest defences of a woman’s call to preach ever made, and it came not from a treatise but from a life.

## A preacher woman

She did not set out to preach. For years after her conversion she prayed with the sick, led a class and the women’s prayer meeting, and spoke to people at the altar. When the call to go out came, it came as a word: Go, and I will go with you. The word she heard next was Salem. She went, and at Salem the old presiding elder who had been her pastor years before met her on the boat. When she told him her story, he said something she never forgot: “Well, Sister Smith, you know I don’t believe in women preaching. But still, honey, I have got nothing to say about you. You go on. The Lord bless you.”

> And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy. JOEL 2:28

## Nashville

The fullest account of the question in her Autobiography is her visit to the General Conference of the African Methodist Episcopal Church in Nashville. She longed to go, and asked one of the elected delegates how much it would cost. He looked at her plain poke bonnet with half disgust and told her it would cost money, and if she had not plenty, there was no use going. Behind her she heard the others: “What does she want to go for?” “Woman preacher; they want to be ordained.” “I mean to fight that thing.”

She went anyway, by faith, and felt very much alone among the fine dresses and the suspicious looks. The thought of ordination, she wrote, had never once entered her mind, “for I had received my ordination from Him, Who said, ‘Ye have not chosen Me, but I have chosen you, and ordained you.’” On an outing to Fisk University the leader of the famous Jubilee Singers spotted her plain bonnet in the crowd, brought her to the platform and asked her to sing. The Spirit fell on the people, the preachers wept and shouted, and several of them came to ask her to come and hold meetings on their circuits. The question of women’s ordination never came up in the Conference.

> But God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise; and God hath chosen the weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:27

Twenty years later she noted how things had changed, that most of her brethren now believed in the ordination of women and some had been ordained. She was glad. “But I am satisfied,” she wrote, “with the ordination that the Lord has given me.”

## Let your men keep silence

She did not usually argue the question. “I never argue with anybody,” she said, “just say my say and go on.” But in Bangalore, when some Plymouth Brethren wrote to the papers and to her, bombarding her with texts against women preaching, she announced that she would speak on the subject. The church was packed. She read out as her text, Let your men keep silence in the church, pointing to the verse in the fourteenth chapter of First Corinthians where Paul, giving directions so that there should be no confusion, tells a speaker to keep silence if there is no interpreter, and has the prophets speak one at a time while the others listen. She does not record the rest of what she called her version of it — only that the meeting was excellent, and that afterwards the newspaper articles and the letters stopped.

In Liberia a minister who treasured Paul’s words about women asking their husbands at home was opposed to her taking any public part. She noted drily that as she had no husband at home to ask, she took her orders from the Master.

> For ye may all prophesy one by one, that all may learn, and all may be comforted. 1 CORINTHIANS 14:31

## Weighing the question

Christians remain divided on this question, and a companion like this is not the place to settle it. Some churches read the apostle’s restrictions as permanent limits on the teaching office; others read them, as she did, as addressed to particular disorders, and point to Deborah, Huldah, Anna, Philip’s daughters and Priscilla, and to Pentecost’s promise to sons and daughters alike. Readers on every side should notice three things about her.

> And the same man had four daughters, virgins, which did prophesy. ACTS 21:9

First, she never sought office or power. She was indifferent to ordination for herself and never pushed herself forward; she often held back when others urged her to sing or speak, for fear of seeming to intrude. Her authority was that of a witness, and she knew it.

Second, she respected those who disagreed. She took the old elder’s blessing gratefully, worked happily under ministers who did not share her view, and never made the question a banner.

Third, the fruit was real. Bishop Thoburn, who worked alongside her for months, said he had learned more of real value as a preacher from her than from anyone else. Whatever one’s view of church order, it is hard to say that God was not in it.

## For the younger women

The very last paragraph of her Autobiography is a prayer for women. She hoped that many of her own people would be led to fuller consecration, and that the Spirit would come upon some of the younger women “who have talent, and who have had better opportunities than I have ever had, and so must do better work for the Master,” so that when she had fallen in the battle they might take up the standard and carry it on.

> The Lord gave the word: great was the company of those that published it. PSALM 68:11

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Whatever your view of women preaching, ask yourself honestly whether it comes from Scripture or from habit. Read 1 Corinthians 14 and Acts 2:17–18 in full this week.
2. What has God called you to do that no church office would give you? Stop waiting for permission and begin.
3. If you hold authority in a church, think of someone, perhaps a woman, perhaps someone poor or unschooled, whose gift is plain and unused. Make room for them.
4. Practise her way with disagreement: say your say, bless those who differ, and go on.
5. Pray, as she did, for younger believers with better opportunities than you had, that they would do better work for the Master.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You chose me, I did not choose You. You have ordained me to go and bear fruit that remains.
> Forgive me for waiting for a title before I obey, and for despising gifts You have given to people I did not expect.
> Pour out Your Spirit on Your sons and daughters, on the young and the old, the schooled and the unschooled.
> And raise up after us a generation who will carry the standard further, with holiness written on their hearts and lives. Amen.

# No Sects in Heaven

> After this I beheld, and, lo, a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations, and kindreds, and people, and tongues, stood before the throne, and before the Lamb. REVELATION 7:9

Amanda Smith’s ministry was carried on almost entirely outside the ordinary structures of any church. She had no appointment, no circuit, no salary, no board. She moved between the African Methodist churches of her own people, the white Methodist churches of New York and Philadelphia, the great interdenominational holiness camp meetings, the Quaker drawing rooms of Philadelphia and Dublin, the Keswick tent, the Anglican and Presbyterian missions of India and West Africa. And yet she was not a free-floating individualist. She had a strong and thoughtful view of Christian fellowship, and of the church, that is worth hearing today.

## The camp meeting

Her first national holiness camp meeting was at Oakington, Maryland, in July 1870, and it changed her life. She had never heard such testimonies or such preaching on holiness. At the Sunday morning love feast she told how she had found the great salvation, and the Lord blessed the people. When the leaders appealed for funds and she longed to give, a stranger rose and pledged ten dollars on her behalf. She had gone to that meeting fearing she would not be allowed to eat at the boarding tent, and found that she was, and the secret of it, she said, was that they were earnest Christians.

> Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity! PSALM 133:1

## Keswick and beyond

In England she found the same fellowship under another name. The Keswick Convention, a friend wrote to her before she sailed, answered to the holiness camp meetings at home, but they called it a convention for the deepening of spiritual life. She spoke there and at the conferences in Perth and elsewhere, and was received, as she says, with such warmth that by the third meeting in one Scottish town you would have thought they had been used to women preaching, and to Black women, all their days. She stayed with Quakers, Anglicans, Brethren and Presbyterians. In India she worked chiefly with the Methodists, but spoke wherever a door opened. In Liberia she held meetings in Methodist, Baptist, Presbyterian and Episcopal churches.

> I am a companion of all them that fear thee, and of them that keep thy precepts. PSALM 119:63

Her theology of all this is summed up in a cheerful aside. Remembering a well-known preacher who had left the Methodist Church for another denomination before he died, she said she had no objection to his going to heaven when his work was done, though she felt he might have gone as safely through the dear old Methodist Church; “but praise the Lord, anyhow there are no sects in Heaven.” The city, she said, will be full of blood-washed souls out of every kindred, tongue and people.

## And yet she stayed

What is striking is that this wide fellowship never made her restless in her own church. She was a member of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, and she stayed one all her life, though it gave her plenty of reasons to leave. Her pastor mocked her from the pulpit. Many of her people opposed holiness. She was a speckled bird, as she said, among her own. She was drawn to the Free Methodists for their plainness and zeal, and found prejudice and harshness there too. She thought of walking to Brooklyn every week to sit under a pastor she respected.

In the end she prayed for three weeks for guidance, and the word she was given was, Fear not, stand still, and see the salvation of God. She stayed. “I never left the church,” she wrote, “but I have seen sad results of many who have left and gone away.” She had watched young people in New York leave one church and join another, and then another, and fail badly in their lives. The Lord, she said, had kept her steady.

> Not forsaking the assembling of ourselves together, as the manner of some is. HEBREWS 10:25

This is a lesson badly needed in an age that moves churches as easily as it changes shops. She did not stay because her church was perfect. She stayed because she believed God had put her there, and because leaving would not escape human nature, which she found the same everywhere. At the same time, her loyalty did not make her narrow. She was at home wherever Christ was loved.

## The communion of saints

Her book is full of names. Page after page she stops to remember a band sister, a class leader, a Quaker hostess, an old man who prayed with power. It can seem like padding. It is not. It is her theology of the church. She knew herself carried by a great company of people, most of them obscure, who had fed her, housed her, prayed with her, lent her a quarter, or spoken one right word at the right time. She would not let them be forgotten.

> I thank my God upon every remembrance of you. PHILIPPIANS 1:3

She was especially tender toward the small praying bands in which she was formed: four or five women, meeting weekly, reading Scripture, telling each other their joys and sorrows, their victories and defeats, advising and praying for one another. Such bands, she believed, were the bone and sinew of a church. In Monrovia one of the first things she did was to reorganise such a band, and it outlived her stay.

## Where she must be read with care

The holiness movement she loved had its dangers, and she saw some of them. Camp-meeting fervour could become an end in itself; testimonies could become formulaic; a doctrine of the second blessing could harden into a badge that divided the sanctified from the ordinary believers in the pew. Her own occasional impatience with dressed-up worshippers and fair-going churches sometimes shows this edge. But her instinct was to keep the fellowship wide and the heart humble, and to keep her own place in her own church. That is a sound instinct.

> Endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. EPHESIANS 4:3

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think of believers from another tradition than yours whom you have learned from. Thank God for them by name, and tell one of them.
2. If you are restless in your church, ask honestly whether God is moving you or whether you are running from something He wants you to endure.
3. Consider joining or starting a small band of three or four believers who meet to read Scripture, confess, and pray for one another.
4. Write down the names of the obscure people who have carried you in your faith. Pray for the living and give thanks for the dead.
5. Is there a Christian meeting where someone like her would feel unwelcome? Do what you can to make it a place where earnest Christians make room.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You are gathering a multitude no one can number, from every nation, kindred, people and tongue. There are no sects in heaven.
> Make my heart as wide as Yours, at home wherever Christ is loved, and quick to learn from those who are not like me.
> Keep me steady where You have planted me, and save me from running from the trials You mean me to endure.
> And thank You for every obscure saint who has carried me. Bless them, and make me one of them for someone else. Amen.

# Into All the World

> And he said unto them, Go ye into all the world, and preach the gospel to every creature. MARK 16:15

The seed of Amanda Smith’s missionary life was sown in a tent at the Sing Sing camp meeting, where a missionary’s wife spoke about the women of India and set out on a table the images of their gods. Amanda had never heard a missionary address in her life. She wept, and thought that if she could only go and sing I am so glad that Jesus loves me, they would all be converted right off. “But O, how little I knew,” she added long afterwards, “about heathen superstitious and customs.” She gave her last two dollars, went up to the speaker sobbing, and asked, “Can I go to India?” Nine years later she did.

## India

She landed at Bombay in November 1879 and worked in India, with a visit to Burma, for the better part of two years. She travelled from mission to mission, speaking in churches, schools, orphanages, open squares and once in a Calcutta theatre. James Thoburn, then a missionary pastor in Calcutta and later a bishop, wrote in his introduction to her Autobiography that in seventeen years he had never known anyone who could draw and hold so large an audience in that city as she did.

He told one story that says a great deal. They were holding an open-air meeting in a Calcutta square at a tense time, when a mob that had just broken up another missionary’s meeting came rushing toward them with threatening cries. Thoburn would have climbed onto the box to command the crowd. Amanda knelt on the grass and began to pray, her face lifted to the evening sky, and the crowd, he says, became perfectly still, and they had as orderly a meeting as if they had been inside a church.

> And when they had prayed, the place was shaken where they were assembled together; and they were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and they spake the word of God with boldness. ACTS 4:31

## Africa

In January 1882 she landed at Monrovia, and for about eight years she worked in Liberia and along the West African coast, from Sierra Leone to Old Calabar. She had no mission board behind her; she worked alongside Methodist, Episcopal, Baptist and Presbyterian churches, and for part of the time with William Taylor, the Methodist missionary bishop for Africa, whose self-sacrifice she deeply admired. She held revival meetings, Bible readings and temperance meetings; she visited the sick and the dying; she organised praying bands. She suffered repeatedly from African fever and more than once thought she would die. She took African children into her care and paid for them from what she was given.

> Be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life. REVELATION 2:10

Her reflections on the work are often acute. She saw that many mission schools declined when the founding missionaries died or went home, and asked why. She protested that no church or society ever paid a coloured missionary, however efficient, as much as it paid a white one, however inexperienced, and expected more work of him besides; she believed this had hastened the death of the finest Black missionary she ever knew. She criticised Liberian traders who went up country and abandoned their families. She warned that many of the emigrants sent out from America were ill-prepared, sick and destitute, and argued, against much opinion, that the colonisation scheme should be wound up and Black Americans assured that there was a place for them in the United States. She was not a romantic about Africa or about Liberia. She saw its failures and said so, and told the Liberians that speaking of them was not a betrayal.

> For there is no respect of persons with God. ROMANS 2:11

## Where she must be read with care

Here the reader must be honest. Amanda Smith saw India and Africa as a nineteenth-century missionary, and she wrote about them in the language of her time. She speaks of heathen darkness, of idols and superstition, of the need to civilise and Christianise. She describes customs she found in the royal yards of Old Calabar — chiefs with many wives, girls married at fourteen, newborn twins abandoned to die, trial by poison for witchcraft — with horror, and sometimes without much attempt to understand the societies in which they occurred. She could write of Indians bound by caste, or of Africans in the interior, in terms that now sound condescending, and she generally assumed that the culture of the Christian West was the standard toward which others should rise.

It would be easy, and cheap, to sneer at this from a later century. She was a poor Black woman who had been born enslaved, working without pay among peoples she had never seen, and many of the things she opposed — the abandonment of twins, the sale of girls, the poison ordeal, the deadly liquor trade — were cruel by any measure, and were opposed by many Africans too. She also treated the Africans she lived with as brothers and sisters: she wept over their sufferings, took their children as her own, honoured African preachers and native Christian women, and did not spare white missionaries, foreign merchants or mission boards. But charity to her does not require agreement with all her judgements. The gospel is not Western civilisation, and a Christian reading her today should separate the one from the other more carefully than she always did.

> Who then is Paul, and who is Apollos, but ministers by whom ye believed, even as the Lord gave to every man? 1 CORINTHIANS 3:5

## Faith for the world

What she teaches most deeply about missions is not a strategy but a spirit. Thoburn noticed it first: she was always cheerful and hopeful. Others looked at the strongholds of the old religions and despaired. She believed that the Saviour she loved really meant to save and transform India and Africa, and she worked on that assumption with whatever she had. And she did it without a salary, a board or a guaranteed passage home, trusting the God who had sent her to keep her.

> The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Christ; and he shall reign for ever and ever. REVELATION 11:15

It is worth adding that the churches of India and West Africa are now among the most vigorous in the world. She would not have been surprised.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. She gave her last two dollars at a missionary meeting and asked, Can I go? Ask God what your part in the gospel reaching the nations is, and do one concrete thing about it this month.
2. Read about the church in one country she worked in — India, Liberia, Sierra Leone or Nigeria — as it is today, and pray for it by name.
3. Examine your own assumptions: where do you confuse the gospel with your own culture? Ask a believer from another culture to help you see it.
4. Think of those who serve cross-culturally and are underpaid or overlooked. Find one, and support them this week.
5. Practise her hope: name a place or a person you have privately written off, and begin to pray for them as if Christ really meant to save them.

### A PRAYER

> Lord of the harvest, You sent a washerwoman to India and Africa, and You are still sending whom You will.
> Give me her hope, that will not despair of any people or any place, and her willingness to give the last two dollars.
> Forgive the church for every time it has confused Your gospel with its own culture, or paid Your servants by the colour of their skin.
> Let Your kingdom come among every nation, kindred, people and tongue, and let me have some part in it. Amen.

# A Temperance Woman

> Wine is a mocker, strong drink is raging: and whosoever is deceived thereby is not wise. PROVERBS 20:1

Amanda Smith’s first husband was a man who could talk sensibly about religion when he was sober, and was profane and unreasonable when strong drink got the better of him, which, she says, was quite often. She never dwelt on it. But it is not hard to see why, for the rest of her life, wherever she went — New York, Calcutta, Monrovia, the villages of the Liberian interior — she preached temperance alongside holiness, and pressed people to sign the total abstinence pledge. “You know I am a temperance woman,” she told an African king who offered her wine, and it would have served as a description of her whole ministry.

## Gospel temperance

Her temperance work was not a separate campaign bolted onto her preaching. She called it Gospel Temperance, and saw it as part of the same message. In Liberia she organised Gospel Temperance bands in town after town, and explained that it was meant “to help Christian men and women on to a higher platform of Christian character and Christian life.” The pledge was not a substitute for conversion; it was a fruit of it, and a protection for the weak.

> It is good neither to eat flesh, nor to drink wine, nor any thing whereby thy brother stumbleth, or is offended, or is made weak. ROMANS 14:21

She saw at first hand what she was fighting. In the Liberian settlements she watched merchants flooding the country with cheap gin, and one foreign trader ordering, she was told, a hundred thousand cases to get it in before a new duty took effect. “The merchants are flooding the land with this accursed fire,” she wrote, “and men and women are being devoured by it.” In Old Calabar she looked into a king’s palace and saw the rulers of the town around a table covered with bottles of champagne and brandy, while the women of their households lived in misery. When the President of Liberia vetoed a bill to take the duties off imported gin and whisky, she wrote in her diary that it was a great triumph and an act of courage.

## Ministers who would not sign

What grieved her most was not the drinkers but the ministers. Again and again she notes that superintendents, local preachers, class leaders and pastors refused to sign the pledge or to speak against the trade. “Oh, what hindrances they are in the work,” she wrote after one Sunday school where the children signed and the officers would not. She was ashamed, she said, when a Black bishop she had admired, who knew the suffering of the Liberian and native people from strong drink, did not give the temperance work even his sympathy. She longed for church leaders to lead.

> Woe unto them that are mighty to drink wine, and men of strength to mingle strong drink. ISAIAH 5:22

## The bottle of champagne

The most vivid temperance story in her book is a story about herself. In India, a kind colonel and his wife had given her, as a gift, a bottle of wine and a bottle of champagne. She had promised to speak on temperance that very evening. The tempter suggested that no one would know, and that since her hosts, who had been so kind, did not see it as she did, it would be better not to say anything about it. She resolved, by God’s grace, to refuse the gift and to speak as she had intended; she spoke strongly, and no one was offended. But she still had the bottles. She carried them for days hidden in her lunch basket, terrified that someone would see them and she would not be able to explain. At last, alone in a railway compartment, she asked the Lord to help her get rid of the champagne, and threw it out of the window into a deep cutting.

> Abstain from all appearance of evil. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:22

## Weighing total abstinence

Here the reader must weigh her with care. She held total abstinence to be the duty of every Christian, and she could speak of those who did not sign the pledge as hindrances to the work. Scripture does not go so far. It condemns drunkenness in the plainest terms, warns against the snare of strong drink, and calls believers to give up even lawful things for the sake of a weaker brother. But it also records that the Lord Jesus made wine at a wedding, that Paul advised Timothy to take a little wine for his stomach’s sake, and that the Lord’s Supper was instituted with the cup. Many faithful Christians, then and now, drink moderately and are not thereby worse Christians. To make abstinence a condition of full discipleship is to add to the Word.

> Let not him that eateth despise him that eateth not; and let not him which eateth not judge him that eateth. ROMANS 14:3

And yet we should be slow to dismiss her. She lived among people for whom drink was not a pleasant accompaniment to a meal but a destroyer of homes, bodies and whole communities; she had married into its misery; she watched it devour Liberian settlements and African kingdoms while foreign merchants grew rich. In such places total abstinence was not legalism. It was love in action, and it was costly. Where drink is ruining lives around us, her example still speaks: the strong should be willing to give up their liberty for the sake of those it is destroying, and the church should be willing to speak against those who profit from it.

> Be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit. EPHESIANS 5:18

That last verse was, for her, the heart of the matter. She did not merely want people to stop drinking. She wanted them filled with something better. The remedy for strong drink, she believed, is the Holy Spirit.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Consider honestly whether alcohol, or anything else, has more hold on you than it should. If it does, deal with it this week, with help if you need it.
2. Is there someone near you for whom your liberty could be a stumbling block? Be willing to lay it down in their presence.
3. Find out what a local recovery ministry or support group is doing, and how you might pray for or support it.
4. Think about who profits from what is destroying people in your community. Is there a way for your church to speak or act?
5. Where you disagree with her rule of total abstinence, make sure you still agree with her love. Ask God to fill you with the Spirit rather than with anything else.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, strong drink and many other things have devoured homes and bodies and whole peoples. Have mercy on all who are enslaved.
> Keep me from being mastered by anything but You. Where my liberty could make a weaker brother stumble, give me grace to lay it down.
> Give Your church courage to speak against those who grow rich on the ruin of others.
> And fill me with Your Spirit, so that I need nothing else to make me glad. Amen.

# Suffer the Little Children

> But Jesus called them unto him, and said, Suffer little children to come unto me, and forbid them not: for of such is the kingdom of God. LUKE 18:16

Amanda Smith was a child whom the world counted as property. She was being got ready for market, as she later wrote, without knowing it, until her father bought her. She bore five children and buried four. She raised her surviving daughter largely alone, by washing and ironing through the night. In Africa she took two children who were not her own and raised them as hers. And in her old age she spent the last of her strength, and much of her peace, on a home for Black children whom no one else would take. The care of children runs through the whole of her story.

## Teaching a child to pray

Her daughter Mazie was converted during a revival in her early teens, and her mother at once set about helping her to grow. Every morning they read the Bible, verse about, sang a verse of a hymn, and prayed. A few days after the conversion, Amanda said to her: “Now, Mazie, the Lord has converted you, and you are very happy; and now if you want to be a real, growing and strong Christian, you must learn to pray.” Mazie answered honestly that she said her prayers but did not know how to pray. Her mother told her that if she asked the Lord, He would teach her, and that from now on, when they knelt together, and at their little Saturday-night prayer meeting, Mazie would pray first.

> Train up a child in the way he should go: and when he is old, he will not depart from it. PROVERBS 22:6

The first morning was hard. Mazie sighed, and sighed again, and at last began, in her childish way, to thank the Lord for what He had done and to ask Him to teach her to pray. Her mother, kneeling beside her, knew the heavy cross had been taken up.

There is a tender detail her mother only reveals afterwards. The real reason she had Mazie pray first on Saturday nights was that the child had been carrying heavy baskets of washing all day, and her mother knew she would probably fall asleep on her knees while the others prayed. So she let her pray first, and never told her why. That is a small picture of wise Christian parenting: expectation and tenderness together, a real cross laid on the child, and a mother quietly making it bearable.

> And ye, fathers, provoke not your children to wrath: but bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord. EPHESIANS 6:4

## Bob

In 1887, at Cape Palmas in Liberia, a small boy and his playmate used to hang on the gate of the house where she stayed, shouting good morning to her, and she would give them scraps from her table. One day the father of one of them came and asked her to take his boy and teach him about God, so that he would not grow up as ignorant of God as his father felt himself to be. She was sick with fever and had no money, and at first she told him to find a Liberian family instead. She explained to him that if she took the boy she must be his mother, just as if she had borne him herself; her heart would have to be as big as his own mother’s.

Then she prayed. She told the Lord that she had no money to support the child and did not want the responsibility with nothing to help herself or him, but that if He made it clear it was His will, she knew He would help her. It became clear. The father and mother came and signed an agreement giving their son Bob to Mrs. Amanda Smith to raise and educate as her own child, with Bishop Taylor as a witness. She took him to England, where one morning, kneeling together for their daily worship, he was converted, and then to America. She also cared for a little African girl, Frances, who was too sick to travel, and made provision for her before she left.

> And whoso shall receive one such little child in my name receiveth me. MATTHEW 18:5

## Other people’s children

Her book is full of children who were not hers. In Monrovia she worried over the African boys who worked as servants in Liberian families and drifted about on Sundays with no one to care for them. She rejoiced when some were converted in her meetings; these, she said, were to her the most interesting cases, because no one thought much about them. In Old Calabar she saw a missionary take in a newborn twin that had been left to die, wash it, and try in vain to feed it; she saw the young wives of chiefs, some of them girls of fourteen. She wrote of these things with grief.

> Defend the poor and fatherless: do justice to the afflicted and needy. PSALM 82:3

## Harvey

Her Autobiography ends in 1893, but the work that crowned her life came later. In 1899, in Harvey, Illinois, near Chicago, she opened an orphanage and industrial home for Black children — at a time when few institutions in the state would take them. She put into it the proceeds of her book and much of her savings. It was a hard work, always short of money and troubled by difficulties with staff, neighbours and inspectors; it cost her health and, by most accounts, a good deal of her peace. She kept at it until she could no longer carry it, and in 1912 she retired to Florida.

It would be easy to tell that story as a triumph, and it was not quite that. It was faithfulness. The woman who had trusted God for passages across oceans found the daily grind of feeding and clothing children heavier than any voyage. She did it anyway. She had been a child that the world counted worthless, and she would not let others be counted so.

> Pure religion and undefiled before God and the Father is this, To visit the fatherless and widows in their affliction, and to keep himself unspotted from the world. JAMES 1:27

## What she teaches

Three things stand out. First, children are to be brought to Christ early and helped to grow, not merely kept respectable; she expected her daughter to pray aloud and to lead. Second, the care of children is a matter for prayer and costly obedience, not only for sentiment; she counted the cost of taking Bob before she said yes. Third, the children the world overlooks — servants, orphans, the children of other races and nations — are precisely the ones Christ calls to Himself, and His people must make room for them.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. If you have children, find one simple way this week to help them pray for themselves rather than only listening to you.
2. Think of a child or young person in your church who has no one taking particular notice of them. Take notice.
3. Find out who is caring for orphaned, fostered or vulnerable children near you. Ask how you could help — with money, time, or a home.
4. If you are weighing a costly commitment to a child, pray as she did: tell God honestly what you lack, and ask Him to make His will clear.
5. Pray for Christian workers who care for children through long, unglamorous years without much recognition.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You called the little children to come to You and would not let anyone forbid them. Make my heart like Yours.
> Help me to teach the children in my care to pray, with expectation and with tenderness.
> Give me eyes for the children no one notices, and a heart big enough to receive them in Your name.
> And bless all who care for orphans and the fatherless. Supply their need, and give them strength for the long, hard days. Amen.

# The Plain Bonnet

> Whose adorning let it not be that outward adorning … But let it be the hidden man of the heart, in that which is not corruptible, even the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price. 1 PETER 3:3–4

For most of her public life Amanda Smith was recognised at a distance by her clothes: a plain dress in the style of the Quakers, a drab shawl, and a big poke bonnet with not a flower or a feather on it. At the General Conference in Nashville the preachers eyed it with disdain. At Fisk University it was the plain bonnet in the crowd that the choir leader spotted. In England the Friends asked her, very kindly, why she wore their garb when she was not one of them. Her dress was a small thing, but the way she thought about it tells us a good deal about the holiness she taught.

## The spring suit

The story begins before her conversion, with the spring outfit she had been saving for: the white straw bonnet with broad pink ties, the tucked muslin dress, the black silk mantilla, the light gaiter boots. It kept rising before her as she prayed, until she told the Lord that if He would take the burden off her heart she would never buy any of it. She did not buy it, and the desire left her. Years later she wrote that all such things were surrendered when she was converted, “though the love of them was deep in my heart,” so that when she came to seek holiness she had no difficulty on the dress question.

> Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world. If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him. 1 JOHN 2:15

She chose plain dress for two reasons. She admired it; many of the most devout women in the Black churches of Philadelphia, New York and Baltimore dressed like the Friends and were known as band sisters, noted for their deep piety, and she loved them for it. And it was cheap. She was a poor woman, and plainness was, she said, a matter of economy. The height of her ambition, she wrote, was “to be a consistent, downright, outright Christian.”

## Societies and show

The same instinct led her out of the secret societies she had joined in New York to get to know the right people. She had paid dues to several, including a very high-toned order for the wives of Master Masons, with regalia and parades and money spent on show. After her sanctification, as she prayed about it, God made it clear to her, she said, “that all these secret societies are the mothers of selfishness, pride and worldliness.” She withdrew from every one, though friends told her she was just coming into office and would lose everything she had paid, and some of them treated her coldly afterwards.

> For all that is in the world, the lust of the flesh, and the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life, is not of the Father, but is of the world. 1 JOHN 2:16

## Bondage to clothes

What makes her teaching on this so valuable is that she saw how simplicity itself can become a snare. For a time after her sanctification, she confessed, “I was in bondage to my clothes; in bondage to other people’s clothes.” If other people’s clothes were not made as she thought they should be, it troubled her; she did not care to hear them pray, and would rather not kneel at communion with the dressed-up people. She thought it wrong to fan herself in church. She would walk two miles to meeting rather than ride the streetcar on Sunday.

Then she went to a Free Methodist class, and saw her own spirit from the outside. A class leader with a stylish shawl and a bright steel pin and chain gave a gentle sister a public raking over a small bow on her fifty-cent bonnet, and Amanda thought there was more of self in his manner and his swell shawl than in the sister he rebuked. She began to see how legalism about dress can sit comfortably alongside pride. By the time she wrote her book she could say that on the matter of Sunday travel she was “not in bondage even in this as I once was.”

> Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage. GALATIANS 5:1

## The English bonnet

The last episode is gently comic. In England she found that no one wore Friends’ dress unless they were Friends, and that her bonnet was taken as a claim to be what she was not. That troubled her deeply, for she hated sham of every kind. But if she took it off, her American friends would say that Amanda Smith had dropped her plain bonnet as soon as she got to England. She was, as she put it, between two fires. In the end, back in Liverpool from Africa, cold, in her thin African clothes, she let two kind ladies buy her warm things and the plainest small bonnet they could find. Then in America everyone asked what she had done with her plain bonnet, until she felt like starting a new style and wearing no bonnet at all.

Her conclusion is the wisest thing she says on the whole subject: “Scores of people have asked me about my bonnet that have never thought of asking me how my soul prospered, and this, after all, is more important in God’s sight than though I wore a hundred plain bonnets.”

> For the LORD seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart. 1 SAMUEL 16:7

## What to keep

The holiness movement often made dress into a test of consecration, and some of its churches made rules about bows and feathers that became, in time, a burden and a badge. Amanda Smith lived inside that world, and for a while shared its scruples. But she came to see further than many of its leaders. She kept her simplicity, out of love and economy, and let go of the bondage. She never stopped believing that a Christian should be free of the love of display, the pull of fashion and the money spent on keeping up. But she refused to measure anyone’s soul by their bonnet, including her own.

That is a balance worth recovering. Simplicity of life is a real Christian grace, and an age of endless spending needs it badly. But simplicity that looks down on others, or that makes its own rules into God’s, has become another kind of vanity. The question she would ask is not what you are wearing. It is how your soul prospers.

> Beloved, I wish above all things that thou mayest prosper and be in health, even as thy soul prospereth. 3 JOHN 1:2

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is there a possession or a style you love more than you should, as she loved her spring suit? Offer it to God, and see whether He asks you to let it go.
2. Where are you spending money chiefly to impress people or keep up with them? Choose one such expense to give up, and give the money away.
3. Ask whether you are in bondage to other people’s clothes, or habits, or ways of worship. Confess any judging you find.
4. Examine the groups and memberships in your life. Do any of them feed selfishness, pride or worldliness in you?
5. This week, when you meet a fellow Christian, ask not about their appearance or achievements but, gently, how their soul prospers.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You look on the heart and not on the outward appearance. Search mine.
> Free me from the love of display and the need to impress, and give me the ornament of a meek and quiet spirit.
> Where my simplicity has become pride, and my rules have become Your rules, forgive me and set me free.
> Let my soul prosper in You, and let me care more for the souls of others than for how they look. Amen.

# Conclusion

> Follow peace with all men, and holiness, without which no man shall see the Lord. HEBREWS 12:14

Eighteen chapters can make it look as though Amanda Smith taught a great many things. She taught one thing, and everything else is that one thing lived out. The one thing is this: God in you. Follow it through and the whole shape appears. Conversion is the moment a proud and weary soul stops arranging its own salvation and says, Lord, I will believe Thee. Assurance is the Spirit within bearing witness that it is so. Holiness is God filling the house He has entered, ruling every ambition and desire, living Himself. And because He lives there, the rest follows: enduring grace at the wash-tub, love that casts out fear, a Father who speaks and must be obeyed, bread and rent from His hand, a will that can bend over a dying child, courage to preach without a title, a heart wide enough for every nation, and a life simple enough to care more for a soul than a bonnet.

## The danger of this book

If you have read this far, you are in some danger, and she would have named it at once. The danger is that you enjoy her. It is easy to be moved by the story of a formerly enslaved washerwoman who preached on four continents, and to close the book exactly as you opened it. She had no interest in being admired. She wrote her whole Autobiography, she said, in the hope that it would bring readers nearer to God and to a deeper consecration, and she would be sadly disappointed if it did not.

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

So the plainest counsel this book can give is the counsel she gave at every altar. Go back to the chapter that unsettled you most — not the one you enjoyed. Take its application points and do them. Then take the next.

## Two things to carry

First, let God in. Not a technique, a feeling or more information about holiness — she once longed to tell a woman who had read all the books that it was not in the books — but God Himself, received by faith, now. She believed that no one was too poor, too unschooled or too despised to be filled. She had been all of those.

Second, obey. The great test of her life was not whether she could feel God but whether she would do what He said: lift her hand, give the tract, go to Salem, cross the ocean. She confessed many failures, and she kept getting up. She reckoned that she could afford almost any loss except the loss that comes from disobeying God. Carry that arithmetic with you.

## A last word about her

We have been honest about where she must be read with care: her reliance on impressions, signs and random texts; a doctrine of a second blessing that serious Christians dispute; judgements about India and Africa shaped by her era; and a rule of total abstinence laid on every conscience. None of this has been raised to diminish her. It has been raised because she herself hated hollowness and sham, and a portrait that left out the shadows would be exactly that.

> But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:7

And the light is very bright. She was bought out of slavery by her father’s sleepless nights and set free again at the cross. She buried four children and two husbands and kept her faith. She washed and ironed through the night and found Christ at the ironing table. She walked into a white church afraid and walked out free. She preached without ordination. She crossed the ocean she feared and carried the gospel to three continents with no salary but the promises of God. She took other people’s children as her own. And when she wrote her story, she ended it not with herself but with a prayer for the younger women who would come after her.

## Without holiness

That last page is the right place to end. She hoped that others, with better opportunities than she ever had, would take up the standard when she fell, bearing the inscription deeply engraved on heart and life: Without holiness, no man shall see the Lord. She did not mean a holiness of rules and bonnets. She meant the holiness she found on Green Street: God in you, living Himself.

> Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy. JUDE 1:24

The standard is still there to be taken up.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name the one chapter of this book that unsettled you most, and the one application point from it you have not yet done. Do it this week.
2. Ask God plainly to come and live in you fully, and to rule every ambition and desire. Do not wait for a feeling; believe Him.
3. Write down one act of obedience you have been postponing, and a date by which you will do it.
4. Where you disagree with her, write down why, from Scripture. Where you agree, write down what it will cost you.
5. Read her Autobiography for yourself, a chapter at a time, as a conversation with a sister who will not let you settle for less than God.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, I have read about a woman who wanted nothing but You, and I am quite capable of admiring her and changing nothing.
> Do not let me. Come and live in me, and live Yourself. Rule every ambition and desire, and cast out every fear.
> Give me her obedience, her simplicity, her courage and her joy, and forgive me for the moments I have let pass.
> Make me holy, for without holiness no one shall see You; and let me see You, through Jesus Christ. Amen.

# A Reader’s Guide to Amanda Berry Smith

> Come and hear, all ye that fear God, and I will declare what he hath done for my soul. PSALM 66:16

Amanda Smith left one book, and it is a large one. Her Autobiography runs to some five hundred pages in its first edition, and it reads as she spoke: warm, direct, rambling, full of hymn verses, names, prayers and Hallelujahs. It is not polished. It is alive. Read it with patience and it will repay you as few spiritual autobiographies do.

## Begin here

An Autobiography: The Story of the Lord’s Dealings with Mrs. Amanda Smith, the Colored Evangelist (1893). In the Ochorus library. Do not try to read it straight through at first. Begin, using the chapter titles of the Ochorus edition, with the chapters on her early life and conversion — Birth, Parentage, and Deliverance from Slavery; Marriage and Conversion — then go straight to The Blessing of Sanctification, the heart of the book. After that, read His Presence and My Obedience and Learning Thy Will Be Done, which contain much of her best teaching, and The Call to Go to England, her great struggle with fear.

Read also Bishop Thoburn’s introduction, which gives a rare outside view of her ministry in India from a man who worked beside her.

## The later chapters

The Autobiography becomes a travel diary in its second half. The chapters on England and Scotland show her among the holiness circles of Keswick and Perth. The chapters on India and Africa are long and uneven, full of names, dates, fevers and meetings, but they contain some of her sharpest observations — on temperance, on missionary pay and practice, on Liberia and emigration — and some of the passages about African and Indian peoples that call for the careful reading described in this book. The chapter Return to Liverpool, and Faith Healing sets out her mature judgement on healing and the use of means, and includes the story of her bonnet.

## For younger readers

Ochorus also hosts retellings of her life for teenagers and for children, based on the Autobiography. They are a good way to introduce her to a family. For her teaching in her own words, adults should go to the original.

## About Amanda Smith

Marshall W. Taylor’s The Life, Travels, Labors, and Helpers of Mrs. Amanda Smith (1886) is an early account written while she was in Africa, useful for the way she was seen in her own time. Adrienne M. Israel’s Amanda Berry Smith: From Washerwoman to Evangelist (1998) is the standard modern biography: careful, sympathetic, and good on her place in the holiness movement and in Black church history, and on the later years that the Autobiography does not cover. A modern reprint of the Autobiography appeared in the Schomburg Library of Nineteenth-Century Black Women Writers (1988), which helped bring her back to wide attention.

The older account is admiring and uncritical; modern treatments are sometimes more interested in her as a pioneer than in the God she preached. Let her own words have the last say.

## Approach with care

Her accounts of inward voices, signs and visions. Read them as the testimony of a sincere woman whom God used, not as a method. Test them, as she learned to test them, by Scripture.

Her teaching on entire sanctification. Receive her hunger for God and her confidence in His power to cleanse. Where she describes holiness as a single second crisis, weigh it against the wider witness of Scripture and the church.

Her descriptions of Africa and India. Read them with charity for a woman of her era, and with discernment about where she confused the gospel with the culture she came from.

## Companions

Hannah Whitall Smith, whose Bible readings Amanda heard at the door of a Philadelphia meeting and whom she later called her dear friend, wrote The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life, in the Ochorus library, which comes from the same holiness world and can be read alongside her. Catherine Booth, whose closest friend was Amanda’s hostess in London, taught many of the same things from a very different life; Ochorus has a companion to her in this series.

## Three counsels for reading her

Read her slowly and aloud; she was a speaker and a singer, and her sentences come alive in the voice. Read her for the lesson in each story; she almost never tells an incident without drawing one, and the lessons are where her theology lies. And read her with the Bible open, as she always did, checking her against it as she would have wanted.

> Let the redeemed of the LORD say so, whom he hath redeemed from the hand of the enemy. PSALM 107:2
