# Introduction

> The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much. JAMES 5:16

E. M. Bounds died in 1913 in a small town in Georgia, known to a few friends and to the older members of the churches he had served. He had published one short book in his lifetime. He left a pile of manuscripts and a reputation for getting up at four in the morning to pray. More than a century later his books on prayer are among the most widely read devotional writings in the English language, translated many times over and still in print. Very few writers have had so much of their influence arrive after their death.

## One conviction beneath all the others

The reason is not his style. Bounds repeats himself, piles up his phrases, and drives the same nail again and again. The reason is that he had grasped one thing with unusual force and never let it go. The thing is this: God does His work in the world through people who pray.

He said it most famously on the first page of the book we now call Power Through Prayer: “Men are God’s method. The Church is looking for better methods; God is looking for better men.” Everything else he wrote is that sentence worked out. If God works through praying people, then the preacher’s first task is to pray, and the real sermon is made on his knees. Faith is what gives prayer its grip, and the Word is what gives faith its ground. Importunity is prayer that will not let go; the early hour is prayer given the best of the day; holiness is the life that makes prayer honest. Trouble is where prayer is tested, revival and missions are where it is multiplied, and the praying saints of the Bible are its proof. And behind all human praying stands the one Intercessor who never stops.

> For the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him. 2 CHRONICLES 16:9

That is the thread running through every chapter of this book, and it is the question to keep asking as you read: do I actually believe that my praying makes a difference to what God does?

## Why he still matters

Bounds is worth reading for at least three reasons. The first is that he takes the promises of Jesus about prayer at their plain meaning. Ask, and it shall be given you. Men ought always to pray, and not to faint. He does not hurry to soften them. He asks what would happen if a believer took them at their word.

The second is that he is a physician for a very modern disease. He lived in an age that was learning to run the Church like a business, with committees and campaigns and statistics, and he saw that busy organisation could quietly replace dependence on God. Our age has more machinery than his ever dreamed of. His warning is more pointed now than when he wrote it.

The third is that he wrote about prayer as a man who prayed. The friends who knew him in his last years describe someone who spent the first three hours of the day on his knees. He does not write like a professor describing a subject from outside. He writes like a man who has just come out of the room and is urging you to go in.

## How this book is arranged

After a chapter on his life, thirteen short chapters set out his teaching. They begin where he began, with the claim that men are God’s method and that the preacher’s power is made in the closet. Then come the partners of prayer that he treated in The Necessity of Prayer: faith, the Word of God, and importunity. The next chapters take up the early morning, holiness and obedience, and prayer in trouble. Then come the larger horizons: what God does in answer to prayer, revival and missions, and the praying men of the Bible and of history. The last two teaching chapters turn to the intercession of Christ Himself and to the hindrances that choke our praying.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. Bounds would not have wanted it otherwise. He thought that most talk about prayer was a substitute for praying, and he had no patience with it. Read a chapter a day, or one a week. Stop at the application points. Pray the prayer, or better, pray your own.

## Where he must be read with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and it is better to say plainly at the start where Bounds must be weighed rather than simply received.

First, his intensity can crush. He writes in superlatives. Prayer is the one thing, the only thing, the sole condition. Little praying is worse than none. Unanswered prayer is almost always our fault. For a reader who is already anxious, or worn out, or grieving, some of his pages can land like a sentence of condemnation. His aim was to wake up comfortable Christians, not to break bruised reeds, and the chapters that follow will try to keep both his fire and the gentleness of Christ.

Second, he belongs to his age. He writes of men where he often means people, and sometimes he really does mean men, insisting that praying men matter in a way praying women do not quite, even while praising Hannah as one of the great intercessors of Scripture. He speaks of the heathen and of foreign lands with the confidence of his century. He served as a chaplain in the Confederate army, a cause fought to preserve slavery, and nothing he later wrote about prayer alters that.

Third, most of what we read under his name was prepared for the press after his death by his friends Claudius Chilton and Homer Hodge. They loved him and they worked hard on the manuscripts, but the books bear the marks of that process: paragraphs that appear word for word in more than one book, chapter epigraphs written by the editors themselves, and stories gathered from other preachers. We cannot always be sure where Bounds ends and his editors begin.

Why read him, then? Because on the central matter he was right. The Church does not advance by cleverness, and God does delight to work through those who ask. The counsel of this book is the counsel of Scripture: Prove all things; hold fast that which is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Where Bounds is faithful, which is most of the time, receive it gladly and act on it. Where he presses beyond what is written, set it aside and lose nothing.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Before going further, ask honestly whether you believe that your praying changes anything, or whether you pray mainly out of habit or duty.
2. Estimate how much time you actually spent in prayer last week. Write the number down and keep it for the end of this book.
3. Choose a pace for reading — a chapter a day or a chapter a week — and decide now when and where you will read it.
4. Resolve to test Bounds against Scripture as you go, keeping what is true and setting aside what is only intensity.
5. Ask God to use this book to send you not to Bounds but to your knees.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You taught Your disciples that men ought always to pray, and not to faint. I have fainted often, and prayed seldom.
> Teach me what Your servant Edward Bounds learned in the dark hours before morning: that You work through those who ask, and that no busyness can take the place of prayer.
> Keep me from admiring a man of prayer while remaining a man of little prayer myself. Where he is faithful to Your Word, let me receive it; where he is not, give me discernment and a gentle heart.
> And let this book do its work by bringing me into Your presence, day by day. Amen.

# Before the Town Was Awake

> And in the morning, rising up a great while before day, he went out, and departed into a solitary place, and there prayed. MARK 1:35

A Missouri boyhood. Edward McKendree Bounds was born on the fifteenth of August, 1835, in Shelby County, in the north-east of Missouri. He was named after William McKendree, one of the pioneer bishops of American Methodism, and the name tells us something of the household he came from. Missouri was then a frontier state, and its churches were planted by circuit riders who covered great distances on horseback. His father died when he was still a boy, and the family’s circumstances narrowed. Like many young men of his generation he went west in the years of the gold rush, and like most of them he came back without a fortune.

A young lawyer. Back in Missouri he read law and was admitted to the bar while still very young. By every ordinary measure he had a career in front of him. Then, in his early twenties, he came under the preaching of a Methodist revival and was converted, and with conversion came a call to preach that he could not argue away. He gave up the law. In 1859, at the age of twenty-four, he was received into the ministry of the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, and sent to a small charge in Missouri. It was the first of several choices in his life that looked, from the outside, like a step down.

## War and prison

A matter of conscience. Two years later the country broke apart, and Missouri, a slave state that did not secede, was torn between the two sides. In 1861 Bounds, then a young pastor, was arrested by Union authorities along with a large number of other men suspected of Southern sympathies. He was required to take an oath of allegiance or to pay a heavy bond. He refused both, on grounds of conscience rather than politics, and was held as a prisoner for well over a year, first at Jefferson City and then at St Louis. It was a costly scruple, and a revealing one: he would not be compelled to swear.

A chaplain. When he was at last released in an exchange of prisoners, he went south and became a chaplain to Confederate regiments from Missouri. It was grim work: preaching to exhausted men, sitting with the wounded, burying the dead, writing to families. At the Battle of Franklin, Tennessee, in November 1864, one of the bloodiest afternoons of the war, he was taken prisoner again, and he was not freed until the war was over.

It must be said plainly that the cause he served was fought to preserve slavery. Bounds, like most white Southern ministers of his generation, did not stand outside the assumptions of his time and place, and a book about his teaching on prayer should not pretend otherwise. What can be said alongside it is what he did next.

## Franklin

A few men on their knees. After the war he went back to Franklin, the town where so many had died, and became pastor of its Methodist church. The town was wrecked and bitter, and the church was weak. Those who have written about his life record that he gathered a small group of men who were willing to meet regularly and pray — for themselves, for the church, for the town and for a revival of religion in a defeated country. It was not a programme. It was a handful of people asking God to do what they could not do. The pattern stayed with him for the rest of his life, and it is the pattern of everything he later wrote.

> Except the LORD build the house, they labour in vain that build it: except the LORD keep the city, the watchman waketh but in vain. PSALM 127:1

The editor’s desk. In the years that followed he served churches in Alabama and Missouri, and his gifts as a writer were noticed. He became editor of the St Louis Christian Advocate, a Methodist weekly, and later an associate editor of the Nashville Christian Advocate, the official paper of his denomination. For a time he held one of the more influential desks in Southern Methodism. Homer Hodge, who knew him well, later remarked that through twelve years of editing a Christian Advocate he remained little known outside his own church.

A household and a grief. In 1876 he married Emma Barnett of Washington, Georgia. She died ten years later, leaving him with young children, and in 1887 he married her cousin, Harriet Barnett. There were children from both marriages. The man who wrote so insistently about hours of prayer was not a monk in an empty house. He was a husband and father who knew the ordinary pressures of a large family, and he had known the particular sorrow of burying a young wife.

## The hours before daylight

A quieter life. In the mid-1890s Bounds left his editorial post and settled with his family in Washington, Georgia, his first wife’s home town. He held no prominent pulpit again. He travelled when he was invited, preached and held meetings, and wrote. But the centre of his life now was prayer, and he treated it as work.

Four in the morning. The detail everyone remembers is the hour. He rose at four and gave the first hours of the day to God, often until seven. Hodge wrote in his introduction to Prayer and Praying Men that at seventy-five Bounds could still pray for three hours on his knees in the early morning. He was not performing for anyone. Almost nobody was watching.

> O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is; PSALM 63:1

The books. Out of those mornings came the books. In 1907 he published a short work for preachers under the title Preacher and Prayer, which circulated widely in Britain and was later retitled Power Through Prayer. It is the book by which most readers still know him. Almost everything else remained in manuscript. He kept writing and revising into his last years, and in 1912, when he was old and frail, Hodge gave up his own pulpit in Brooklyn to let him preach. Hodge recalled that his voice was feeble and the sermon short. In his letters from those years Bounds asked his friend to pray for him, in words Hodge preserved: “Pray for me that God will give me new nerves and new visions to finish the manuscripts.”

## After his death

The last word. Bounds died at home in Washington, Georgia, on the twenty-fourth of August, 1913, nine days after his seventy-eighth birthday. Not long before, he had written to Hodge a sentence his friend quoted in the introduction to Prayer and Praying Men: “When He is ready I am ready; I long to taste the joys of the heavenlies.”

The friends who would not let the manuscripts die. The manuscripts could easily have been lost. Instead Claudius Chilton, and after him Homer Hodge, took up the long labour of editing them and finding publishers. Purpose in Prayer appeared in 1920 and Prayer and Praying Men in 1921, and the others followed through the 1920s. Hodge’s hand is visible throughout them, in his forewords and in the epigraphs he set at the head of many chapters. Bounds saw none of them in print.

> By it he being dead yet speaketh. HEBREWS 11:4

It is worth pausing over the shape of that life. He gave up the law for the ministry. He lost his liberty rather than swear against his conscience. He saw the worst of war, served a defeated cause, and spent his most productive years in obscurity. His best work lay unread in a drawer when he died. And yet the mornings were not wasted. The prayers he prayed in the dark have been read by millions who never heard his name while he lived. If his own life proves anything, it is what he spent those mornings writing: that God keeps accounts differently from us, and that what is done in secret is rewarded openly.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Bounds gave up a promising career when God called him. Ask whether there is anything you are holding on to that God has asked you to loosen.
2. He refused an oath he believed was wrong, at great cost. Consider where your own conscience is being pressed, and pray for courage to be faithful.
3. Reflect honestly on how his service of the Confederate cause should shape the way you read him. Pray for the grace to see the blind spots of your own time as clearly as you see his.
4. Choose one hour of the day, however small, that you will give to God before you give it to anything else, and keep it for a week.
5. Remember that his best work was hidden while he lived. Ask God to make you content to labour and pray where no one is watching.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You see in secret, and You reward openly. Thank You for Your servant Edward Bounds, who rose while the town was dark to seek Your face.
> Forgive me for how easily I give the first and best of my days to lesser things. Teach me to rise early, or to pause at any hour, and to meet You before I meet anyone else.
> Where he was blind, as all Your servants are, keep me humble about my own blindness. Where he was faithful, make me faithful.
> And let whatever I do in secret for You bear fruit in Your time, even if I never see it. Amen.

# Men Are God’s Method

> There was a man sent from God, whose name was John. JOHN 1:6

Power Through Prayer opens with a complaint and a correction. The complaint is that the Church of Bounds’s day was always straining to devise new plans, new organisations and new methods for advancing the gospel. The correction is one of the most quoted sentences he ever wrote: “Men are God’s method. The Church is looking for better methods; God is looking for better men.”

It is the foundation of all his teaching, and it is worth understanding exactly what he meant by it, and what he did not.

## The man behind the message

Bounds noticed how often Scripture moves from a message to a man. The way for Christ was prepared, not by a campaign, but by a man sent from God whose name was John. The salvation of the world came through a Son who was born and laid in a cradle. When Paul defended his ministry, he pointed not to his techniques but to the character of the men who had brought the gospel.

> For the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to shew himself strong in the behalf of them whose heart is perfect toward him. 2 CHRONICLES 16:9

For Bounds, this verse settled the matter. God is looking for people. He is not looking for talent, or money, or organisation, but for hearts wholly His, through which He can show Himself strong. In a passage that has become famous, he wrote that what the Church needs “is not more machinery or better, not new organizations or more and novel methods, but men whom the Holy Ghost can use—men of prayer, men mighty in prayer.” And he went on: “The Holy Ghost does not flow through methods, but through men. He does not come on machinery, but on men. He does not anoint plans, but men—men of prayer.”

> And I sought for a man among them, that should make up the hedge, and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I should not destroy it: but I found none. EZEKIEL 22:30

## Why methods are not enough

It would be easy to read this as a kind of anti-intellectual suspicion of all planning. It is not. Bounds was an editor and a church administrator for years; he knew that the Church needed order and organisation. His point was about where power comes from. A plan can be good, but a plan cannot pray. Machinery can carry power, but it cannot generate it. The danger he saw was that the Church, pleased with its structures, would forget that the structures were empty until God filled them, and that God fills them through people who ask.

In Purpose in Prayer he pressed the same point more sharply. An age of organisation, he warned, is rarely an age of strong personal holiness, because machinery looks for engineers to run it rather than for saints. Numbers, money and activity can become substitutes for the one thing God is looking for.

> Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts. ZECHARIAH 4:6

This is a warning that has only grown more urgent. We have far more tools than Bounds’s generation had. Most of them are good. None of them can do what only God can do, and none of them can take the place of a person who has been alone with Him.

## Prayer makes the man

If men are God’s method, what makes the man? For Bounds the answer was always the same. The man God uses is made in secret. He is shaped by long hours of communion, by honest confession, by pleading for others, by waiting on God. Character is not a by-product of ministry; it is the ministry. The preacher, he wrote, is the golden pipe through which the divine oil flows, and the pipe must be not only golden but open and flawless.

That is why he was so insistent that God cares about the person more than the product. In Purpose in Prayer he quoted God’s word through Isaiah, turning away from costly sacrifices to look instead at the one who is poor and of a contrite spirit, and who trembles at His word.

> But the LORD said unto Samuel, Look not on his countenance, or on the height of his stature; because I have refused him: for the Lord seeth not as man seeth; for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart. 1 SAMUEL 16:7

## Where this must be read with care

Two cautions belong here.

The first concerns the word men. In most places Bounds uses it as his century did, to mean human beings, and the reader loses nothing by hearing it that way. But in one chapter of Purpose in Prayer, commenting on Paul’s instruction that men should pray everywhere, he insists that men as distinguished from women have a particular duty, and writes: “The first of beings, man, should also be first in prayer.” The concern behind it is real; he feared that men had handed prayer over to their wives and mothers. But the principle as stated goes beyond Scripture, and Bounds himself undercuts it whenever he tells the story of Hannah, whose prayers gave Israel a prophet. The Bible is full of praying women, and the Church has always depended on them.

> Pray without ceasing. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:17

The second caution concerns the weight this teaching can lay on a person. If God is looking for better men, it is a short step to thinking that God is disappointed with me, and that His whole work waits on my improvement. That is not what Bounds meant, and it is not the gospel. God’s search for faithful people is not the search of a hard employer but of a Father. He does not wait for us to become strong before He uses us; He makes us strong by drawing us to Himself. Paul, whom Bounds admired above all men, called himself the chief of sinners and said that he was what he was by the grace of God.

> But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:7

That, in the end, is the heart of this teaching. God works through people because He chooses to, not because He needs us. The treasure is in earthen vessels precisely so that everyone can see the power belongs to Him. What He asks is not that we be impressive, but that we be available — open pipes, not golden ones only, through which His life can flow. And the way to become that kind of person is the way Bounds walked every morning: to pray.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. List the plans, programmes or tools you are relying on in your work, your family or your church. Ask honestly which of them you have prayed over, and which you have simply trusted.
2. Read 2 Chronicles 16:9 slowly and ask God to show you whether your heart is wholly His.
3. Think of one person whose life has shaped you more than any method or programme. Thank God for them, and pray for them by name today.
4. If you lead anything, spend as much time this week praying for the people as planning for the work.
5. If Bounds’s language about men has troubled you, read the story of Hannah in 1 Samuel 1 and give thanks for the praying women God has placed in your life.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, Your eyes run to and fro through the whole earth, looking for hearts that are wholly Yours. Let mine be one of them.
> Forgive me for trusting in plans and methods more than in You, and for being busy about Your work while neglecting Your presence.
> Make me an open vessel, however ordinary, through which Your power can flow. I do not ask to be impressive; I ask to be available.
> And raise up in Your Church, among men and women, old and young, a generation of people mighty in prayer. Amen.

# The Real Sermon Is Made in the Closet

> But we will give ourselves continually to prayer, and to the ministry of the word. ACTS 6:4

Power Through Prayer was written for preachers, and it is one of the most uncomfortable books a minister can read. Its original title, Preacher and Prayer, states its subject exactly. Its argument is that the power of a sermon is not made in the study, still less in the pulpit, but on the preacher’s knees. “The real sermon is made in the closet,” Bounds wrote. “Prayer makes the man; prayer makes the preacher; prayer makes the pastor.”

This chapter is about ministers, but not only for them. Everything Bounds said about the preacher applies, in its measure, to every Christian who speaks for God to anyone — a parent, a teacher, a friend.

## The man is the message

Bounds believed that a sermon could never rise higher than the person who preached it. The preacher is not a loudspeaker for a message that exists apart from him. What he is flows into everything he says. “It takes twenty years to make a sermon,” he wrote, “because it takes twenty years to make the man.”

> Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God; Who also hath made us able ministers of the new testament; not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life. 2 CORINTHIANS 3:5–6

He took that text as the title of one of his chapters. There is preaching, he said, that gives life, and there is preaching that kills. The preaching that kills may be orthodox, orderly and eloquent. It may even move people to tears. But it comes from the preacher’s own resources rather than from God, and it leaves hearts as hard as it found them. The difference is not in the words but in what lies behind them: a man who has been broken before God and emptied of self-reliance, or a man who has not.

## Unction

The word Bounds used for the difference is unction, the anointing of the Holy Spirit on the preacher and the message. He devoted three chapters to it and admitted it was almost impossible to define. You cannot manufacture it. You can counterfeit it, with emotion or performance, but the counterfeit leaves no lasting mark. Where it is present, the same truths that were dull in another man’s mouth break hearts and change lives.

> But ye have an unction from the Holy One, and ye know all things. 1 JOHN 2:20

His conclusion was blunt: “Prayer, much prayer, is the price of preaching unction.” It is not a gift received once and kept forever. It is renewed as it was first given, by continual asking. He compared unction kept without prayer to the manna the Israelites tried to store overnight: it spoiled.

> And my speech and my preaching was not with enticing words of man’s wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power: 1 CORINTHIANS 2:4

## The apostles’ priority

Bounds loved to point to Acts 6. When the young Church in Jerusalem faced a crisis over the care of widows, the apostles did not dismiss the need; they appointed others to meet it. But they refused to let even good and urgent work crowd out their first calling. They would give themselves continually to prayer, and to the ministry of the word — and prayer came first. Bounds observed that the apostles made a business of praying, putting time and fervour and perseverance into it.

He was sharp about how little many ministers of his day actually prayed. He pictured the preacher whose only praying was a few words at the bedside before sleep and a few hasty snatches in the morning, and he set that beside the long hours of the great men of God. He thought such thin praying was hardly better than none, and in one of his harder sentences he said that little praying might even be worse than no praying, because it salves the conscience while achieving nothing.

> Brethren, pray for us. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:25

## The preacher must be prayed for

One of the most humane chapters in Power Through Prayer turns from the preacher’s praying to the congregation’s. Bounds noted how often Paul, the greatest of apostles, begged the churches to pray for him — for utterance, for boldness, for deliverance, for an open door. If Paul needed the prayers of ordinary believers, how much more does any minister today?

> Now I beseech you, brethren, for the Lord Jesus Christ’s sake, and for the love of the Spirit, that ye strive together with me in your prayers to God for me; ROMANS 15:30

He set two propositions side by side that he said must never be divorced: the preacher must pray, and the preacher must be prayed for. And he asked the reader directly whether he prayed for his own minister in secret. Those who do, he said, are to the preacher what Aaron and Hur were to Moses, holding up his hands in the battle.

> And they took a stone, and put it under him, and he sat thereon; and Aaron and Hur stayed up his hands, the one on the one side, and the other on the other side; and his hands were steady until the going down of the sun. EXODUS 17:12

The book ends with a line that sums up its hope: “A praying pulpit will beget praying pews.” People learn to pray from those who lead them. A church rarely prays more than its ministers do.

## Where this must be read with care

Bounds is a physician, and his medicine is strong. Three cautions will help it do good rather than harm.

First, he sometimes writes as if prayer were the only thing a minister needs, and study almost a distraction. That is not quite his view — he quotes with approval Luther’s saying that he who has prayed well has studied well — but the reader should not take him to despise learning. Paul told Timothy to give attendance to reading as well as to prayer. The answer is not less study but study bathed in prayer.

Second, he speaks as though unction were always visible and results always follow. They do not. Faithful, praying ministers have laboured for years with little outward fruit, and others with little prayer have drawn crowds. God’s timing is His own, and the measure of a ministry is faithfulness, not success.

Third, many ministers who read Bounds already feel they fail at everything. For them his pages can become one more burden. The answer to that is not to stop praying but to remember that the One to whom we pray is the Good Shepherd, who knows the weakness of His under-shepherds and does not break the bruised reed. The closet is not a court where we are judged. It is the place where we are fed.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. If you preach or teach, keep a record this week of how much time you spend preparing your material and how much praying over it and over the people. Let the comparison speak to you.
2. Before your next time of speaking for God, however informal, pray first for the hearers by name, and then for yourself.
3. Pray for your own minister or pastor by name every day this week — for his or her soul, family and preaching.
4. Tell your pastor, briefly and without fuss, that you are praying for them.
5. If you are weary in ministry, read Isaiah 42:3 and bring your weariness to God rather than your performance.

### A PRAYER

> Lord of the harvest, You have given Your Church preachers and teachers, and You have told us to pray for them. Forgive me for criticising them more than I have prayed for them.
> Anoint those who speak Your word. Let them come to the pulpit from the closet, with their hearts warmed by Your presence and their lips touched by Your Spirit.
> And whenever I speak for You, to one person or to many, let it not be the letter that kills but the Spirit that gives life.
> Make our pulpits praying pulpits, and our pews praying pews, for the glory of Your name. Amen.

# Believe That Ye Receive

> Have faith in God. MARK 11:22

The Necessity of Prayer is Bounds’s most orderly book. It takes prayer and sets it beside the things it cannot live without — faith, trust, desire, fervency, importunity, obedience, vigilance and the Word of God — and it begins, as it had to, with faith. “In any study of the principles, and procedure of prayer,” he wrote, “first place, must, of necessity, be given to faith.” This chapter follows that opening, and his three chapters on faith and trust.

## Faith that asks and takes

Bounds drew the heart of his teaching from the words of Jesus beside the withered fig tree.

> Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them. MARK 11:24

He called this saying the keystone of the arch of faith and prayer. It describes, he said, a faith that realises, appropriates and takes — not a vague belief that God is good and able, but a confident expectation that He will do the particular thing we ask. He put it memorably: “Prayer projects faith on God, and God on the world. Only God can move mountains, but faith and prayer move God.”

Faith and prayer, for Bounds, cannot be separated. Faith gives birth to prayer, and prayer in turn feeds faith. He wrote that faith goes ahead of prayer to clear the way to the mercy-seat, walks beside it as its companion, and follows it into a life of trust. Remove faith and prayer becomes a string of words. Remove prayer and faith withers: “when faith ceases to pray, it ceases to live.”

> But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him. HEBREWS 11:6

## Faith rests on a Person

It would be easy to hear all this as a technique: work up enough certainty and you will get what you want. Bounds guarded against that reading. The faith that prays powerfully, he said, is the faith that centres itself on a powerful Person. It is not faith in faith, nor faith in a formula, but trust in God Himself and in Jesus Christ.

He illustrated it from the Gospels. The leper did not say, I believe in healing. He said, Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean. The blind men were asked, Believe ye that I am able to do this? Martha believed in the resurrection at the last day, but Jesus lifted her faith from a doctrine to Himself: I am the resurrection, and the life. Trust in a fact may be passive, Bounds observed, but trust in a person is alive and loving.

> And the Lord said, Simon, Simon, behold, Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat: But I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not: and when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren. LUKE 22:31–32

He noted that when Jesus foresaw Peter’s fall, it was Peter’s faith He prayed for. Faith is the foundation, and when it gives way the whole structure of spiritual life falls with it. That is why it must be guarded and nourished, and why, he said, we should keep praying the disciples’ prayer: Lord, increase our faith.

## Faith that waits

The most pastorally valuable part of these chapters is what Bounds said about delay. Faith, he wrote, is often called on to wait. It does not grow disheartened when prayer is not answered at once. It takes God at His word and lets Him take what time He chooses.

He found his example in Bethany. When Lazarus was ill, his sisters sent word to Jesus, and Jesus did not come. While He waited, Lazarus died. It looked like disaster. Yet the delay served a greater good, and the sisters saw the glory of God.

> Jesus saith unto her, Said I not unto thee, that, if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God? JOHN 11:40

“Delay is often the test and the strength of faith,” Bounds wrote. And he added a searching observation about Jacob, who prayed to be delivered from his brother Esau. Before that prayer could be answered, God had to deal with Jacob himself. “Jacob had to be converted to God, before Esau could be converted to Jacob.” Sometimes the delay in an answer is God doing a deeper work in the one who prays.

## Faith for today

In a passage of quiet good sense, Bounds tied faith to the present. When we pray Give us this day our daily bread, he said, we are shutting tomorrow out of our prayer. Faith lives in the present, trusts God today, and leaves the morrow with Him. We cannot store up tomorrow’s grace or do tomorrow’s praying. He wrote elsewhere that “Trust always operates in the present tense.”

> Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus. PHILIPPIANS 4:6–7

This is one of the most practical things in all his writing. Much anxiety is simply faith trying to live in next week. Prayer brings it back to today.

## Where this must be read with care

Bounds pressed the promise of Mark 11 hard, and it must be read alongside the rest of Scripture.

He taught that faith must be definite and specific, and that God commits Himself to do the very things that faith and persevering prayer name. Taken alone, this can sound as if the believer sets the terms and God must fill the order. In the century since Bounds wrote, some teachers have built exactly that doctrine, promising health and wealth to anyone who believes hard enough. Bounds would have been appalled; his faith was always tied to obedience, humility and the glory of God. But his words can be bent that way, and they need the balance of other texts.

> And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us: 1 JOHN 5:14

The faith that pleases God is not certainty that I will get what I want. It is confidence in God Himself — that He hears, that He is good, and that He will do what is best, whether that is the thing I asked, or something better, or grace to bear His refusal. Jesus prayed with perfect faith in Gethsemane, and the cup did not pass. Paul prayed three times about his thorn, and it stayed. Neither lacked faith.

So pray boldly and specifically, as Bounds urged. Expect God to act. But let your faith rest on the Person, not on the outcome, and it will not be shaken when the outcome is not what you hoped.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Write down one specific request you have been praying vaguely. Pray it this week plainly and definitely, and expect God to answer.
2. Ask whether your faith rests more on God Himself or on getting a particular result. Tell Him honestly.
3. Think of a prayer that seems delayed. Read John 11 and ask God what He may be doing in you while you wait.
4. Notice where anxiety is pulling you into tomorrow. Bring today’s need to God and leave tomorrow with Him.
5. Pray every day this week the disciples’ prayer: Lord, increase our faith.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You told us to have faith in God, and You promised that those who ask believing shall receive. I confess how small my faith is, and how often I pray without expecting anything.
> Increase my faith. Let it rest not on my own certainty but on You — on Your power, Your goodness and Your word.
> When You delay, teach me to wait without losing heart. When You say no, teach me to trust You still.
> Give me today my daily bread, and the faith to leave tomorrow in Your hands. Amen.

# The Word and the Promiser

> If ye abide in me, and my words abide in you, ye shall ask what ye will, and it shall be done unto you. JOHN 15:7

Bounds is often thought of as a man of one idea, and in a sense he was. But he never thought of prayer as a thing that stood by itself. In The Necessity of Prayer he gave two chapters to prayer and the Word of God, and in The Possibilities of Prayer two more to prayer and the promises. Taken together they make one of his most balanced teachings: prayer lives on Scripture, and Scripture is brought to life by prayer.

## Prayer feeds on the Word

Bounds called the Bible a book of prayer. It is full of praying men and their answers, of commands to pray and promises to those who do. He did not think anyone could pray well who did not know it well.

“The Word of God is the fulcrum upon which the lever of prayer is placed,” he wrote, “and by which things are mightily moved.” Prayer needs something to stand on. Our feelings shift, our merits are worthless, our reasons run out, but God’s word does not change. So he urged his readers to store the promises in their hearts, like fuel laid up for winter. Unless prayer is supplied by the Word, he said, it may be earnest and even loud, but it will be thin and empty.

> So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God. ROMANS 10:17

This is simple and deeply practical. Many people find their prayers going round in the same small circle of worries. The remedy is not to try harder but to read more. Scripture gives us words to pray, reasons to hope, and a far larger picture of what God cares about than we would ever invent for ourselves.

## The Word needs prayer

The relationship runs the other way too. Bounds loved Paul’s request to the Thessalonians that they pray for him, so that the word of the Lord might have free course. The gospel, he said, depends on prayer to run. He had a vivid phrase for it: prayer puts wheels under God’s Word.

> Finally, brethren, pray for us, that the word of the Lord may have free course, and be glorified, even as it is with you: 2 THESSALONIANS 3:1

He applied this to the parable of the sower. Why does so much seed fall on hard ground, or among thorns? Partly, he thought, because hearers come to God’s word without having prepared their hearts by prayer, and leave without watering what was sown. Prayer before hearing softens the soil; prayer after hearing keeps the seed alive.

He applied it too to the ordinary reader. The psalmist who loved God’s law was also a man who prayed Open thou mine eyes. “No man loves the Bible, who does not love to pray,” Bounds wrote, and the reverse was equally true: no one loves to pray who does not delight in God’s law.

> Open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law. PSALM 119:18

## Taking hold of the Promiser

The most striking thing Bounds said about the promises is that prayer takes hold not only of the promise but of the One who made it. “Jacob wrestled, not so much with a promise, as with the Promiser.” A promise can remain a dead letter on the page. Prayer lays hold of the living God who stands behind it and asks Him to do as He has said.

> Behold, the LORD’s hand is not shortened, that it cannot save; neither his ear heavy, that it cannot hear: ISAIAH 59:1

He illustrated this from Elijah. God had said plainly, Go, shew thyself unto Ahab; and I will send rain upon the earth. Elijah had the promise. Yet he still climbed Carmel and bowed himself to the ground and prayed, seven times, until the little cloud appeared. The promise did not make prayer unnecessary. It made prayer confident.

> And Elijah went up to the top of Carmel; and he cast himself down upon the earth, and put his face between his knees, And said to his servant, Go up now, look toward the sea. And he went up, and looked, and said, There is nothing. And he said, Go again seven times. 1 KINGS 18:42–43

In the same way, he pointed out, Daniel read in Jeremiah that the captivity would last seventy years, and his response was not to wait passively but to set his face to seek God with prayer and fasting. Knowing God’s purpose moved him to pray for it. The promise and the prayer went together.

## Knowing the will of God

Bounds tied the Word to one of the hardest questions in prayer: how can we know what God wants? His answer was that God has written His general will in Scripture, and that the Spirit helps us apply it. By studying the Word, hiding it in our hearts and letting it dwell in us richly, we come to pray more and more in line with what God desires.

> Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee. PSALM 119:11

He also noted a wonderful little verse from Paul, that every good gift of God is sanctified by the word of God and prayer. Prayer is not only for getting things from God; joined to Scripture, it makes holy what we already have.

> For it is sanctified by the word of God and prayer. 1 TIMOTHY 4:5

## Where this must be read with care

Two cautions are worth making.

First, Bounds sometimes suggests that importunate prayer can obtain more than God has promised — that the saints of old, through faith, obtained promises they had not been given. There is truth in this; Abraham and Moses pleaded where they had no specific word. But it can be pushed too far, until prayer becomes a way of getting round Scripture rather than living within it. The safer path is the one he himself usually walked: to pray the promises God has actually made, and to leave the rest to His wisdom.

Second, claiming promises can become careless. Not every promise in the Bible was made to every reader in every situation. Some were spoken to Israel, some to particular people, some for particular times. To pray the Word well means reading it well — in context, with humility, and with the whole of Scripture in view. Bounds’s own advice to store the Word richly in the heart is the best guard against using it carelessly.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Choose one psalm and pray through it slowly this week, turning each verse into a prayer of your own.
2. Before you next read Scripture or hear a sermon, pray for an open heart. Afterwards, pray over one thing you heard.
3. Find one promise of God that speaks to a real need in your life. Check its context, then take hold of the Promiser and ask Him to fulfil it.
4. Memorise Psalm 119:11 or John 15:7, and use it to begin your prayers for a week.
5. Pray daily for the preaching of God’s word somewhere in the world, that it may have free course and be glorified.

### A PRAYER

> Heavenly Father, You have given us Your word, and You have told us to ask in Jesus’ name. Forgive me for neglecting both, and for praying in small circles because I have read so little.
> Open my eyes to behold wondrous things out of Your law. Let Your words abide in me, so that I may learn to ask what You delight to give.
> Teach me to take hold not only of Your promises but of You, the faithful Promiser, who cannot lie.
> And let Your word run swiftly and be glorified, in my heart, in my church and among the nations. Amen.

# Pray and Never Faint

> And he spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint; LUKE 18:1

If there is one note Bounds sounded more often than any other, it is importunity: prayer that keeps asking, presses its case, and will not give up until the answer comes. He wrote two chapters on it in The Necessity of Prayer, returned to it in Purpose in Prayer under the title Pray and Never Faint, and wove it through nearly everything else. He had learned it, he believed, from Jesus Himself.

## The parables of persistence

Bounds noticed that Jesus told two parables whose whole point was persistence. In one, a widow keeps coming to an unjust judge until he gives her justice simply to be rid of her. In the other, a man knocks on his friend’s door at midnight, asking for bread, and keeps knocking after he has been refused.

> I say unto you, Though he will not rise and give him, because he is his friend, yet because of his importunity he will rise and give him as many as he needeth. LUKE 11:8

If these parables do not teach the need for importunity, Bounds said, they have no point at all. Jesus was not comparing God to an unjust judge or a reluctant friend. He was arguing from the lesser to the greater: if persistence can move even them, how much more will it move a loving Father? And He told the parables precisely because He knew that we grow faint-hearted and stop asking.

> And shall not God avenge his own elect, which cry day and night unto him, though he bear long with them? I tell you that he will avenge them speedily. LUKE 18:7–8

## Ask, seek, knock

Bounds saw a deliberate progression in Christ’s command to ask, seek and knock. Asking is putting our request into words. Seeking is the settled attitude of one who goes after what he has asked for. Knocking is the urgent, repeated effort of one who will not go away. They are, he said, ascending rounds on the ladder of prayer.

> And I say unto you, Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you. LUKE 11:9

He described importunate prayer as “not an incident, but the main thing, not a performance but a passion, not a need but a necessity.” It is made up of intensity, perseverance, patience and persistence. It holds on, presses on and waits. And it is not the energy of the flesh, but a strength the Holy Spirit stirs up in us.

## The Syrophenician woman

His favourite example was the Gentile mother who came to Jesus for her tormented daughter. Bounds traced every obstacle she met: Jesus seemed to ignore her, the disciples wanted her sent away, she was told she was outside His mission, and she was compared to a dog. She accepted it all and kept asking, and Jesus answered with a word of wonder.

> Then Jesus answered and said unto her, O woman, great is thy faith: be it unto thee even as thou wilt. And her daughter was made whole from that very hour. MATTHEW 15:28

To Bounds this showed that importunity is not rudeness but faith refusing to be discouraged. The woman’s persistence was clothed in humility; she did not argue that she deserved help, only that even the dogs eat the crumbs. What won the day was a faith that would not let go.

## Desire and fire

Importunity, Bounds thought, springs from desire. He gave a chapter each to desire and to fervency, and they belong here. Desire is not a passing wish but a deep longing, and prayer is desire put into words. Without it, prayer is, in his phrase, a meaningless mumble.

> As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. PSALM 42:1

He was honest that desire is sometimes absent. When that happens, he said, we should pray anyway — and pray for the desire to pray. He had no time for the idea that we should only pray when we feel like it. Feelings follow faithfulness more often than they lead it.

And he loved the image of fire. Prayer must be fervent, he said, because the effectual prayer that avails much is the fervent prayer. He added a characteristic line: “Heaven is too busy to listen to half-hearted prayers or to respond to pop-calls.” By fire he did not mean noise. Fervency, he wrote, is intensity rather than fuss, a glow rather than a show.

## Why God makes us persist

Why should a loving Father require us to keep asking? Bounds gave several answers. Persistence tests and strengthens faith. It deepens desire and makes us value the answer. It changes the one who prays, as Jacob was changed in his night of wrestling. And sometimes God is doing things we cannot see before the answer can come.

> And he said, Let me go, for the day breaketh. And he said, I will not let thee go, except thou bless me. GENESIS 32:26

He also warned against giving up too soon. “Too often we get faint-hearted and quit praying at the point where we ought to begin.” He quoted, with approval, an old saying about Abraham’s intercession for Sodom: that Abraham left off asking before God left off granting.

## Where this must be read with care

Importunity is a precious truth, but it is easily bent.

First, it is not a way of wearing God down. The parables contrast God with the unjust judge; they do not compare Him to one. We persist not because God is reluctant but because we are, and because prayer is a relationship, not a vending machine.

Second, Bounds sometimes wrote as if persistence always gains the very thing asked. It does not. There are prayers to which God says no, and there comes a point where faithful persistence means yielding the request to Him, as Jesus did in the garden and Paul did over his thorn. Knowing when to keep knocking and when to rest is part of wisdom, and it is learned in prayer itself.

Third, a tender conscience can hear all this as an accusation: my prayers are not answered because I have not prayed hard enough. Sometimes that is true. Often it is not. The widow in the parable was weak and friendless; her case was won not by her strength but by her refusal to give up hope in a judge who would act. Our hope is in a Father far better than that judge. Keep asking — but keep asking as a child, not as a slave.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name one prayer you have given up on. Take it up again this week and pray it daily.
2. If you lack desire to pray, tell God so honestly, and ask Him to give you the desire.
3. Read the story of the Syrophenician woman in Matthew 15:21–28 and notice every obstacle she overcame. Which of them do you face?
4. Keep a short list of long-term requests — for a person, a situation, a need — and pray through it regularly, noting any answers.
5. Ask God for wisdom to know whether a long-unanswered prayer calls for more persistence or for surrender.

### A PRAYER

> Father, Your Son told us that men ought always to pray and not to faint. I confess how quickly I faint, and how soon I stop knocking.
> Give me the persistence of the widow and the faith of the Syrophenician mother, who would not be turned away.
> Kindle in me a real desire for You and for the things You love, and where my heart is cold, warm it by Your Spirit.
> And when You say no, give me grace to rest in You as Jesus did, trusting that Your will is better than my asking. Amen.

# Early on Their Knees

> My voice shalt thou hear in the morning, O LORD; in the morning will I direct my prayer unto thee, and will look up. PSALM 5:3

No sentence Bounds wrote has been quoted more often than the one that opens the ninth chapter of Power Through Prayer: “The men who have done the most for God in this world have been early on their knees.” It is a sentence he lived. For the last two decades of his life he rose at four in the morning and gave the first hours of the day to prayer. This chapter is about the early hour, and about the larger truth behind it: that prayer needs time, and the best of our time.

## Seeking God first

Bounds’s argument for the morning was not superstition about a particular hour. It was about priority. “If God is not first in our thoughts and efforts in the morning,” he wrote, “he will be in the last place the remainder of the day.” The first hours set the course. Whoever gives them away to other things will find God crowded to the edges.

> And in the morning, rising up a great while before day, he went out, and departed into a solitary place, and there prayed. MARK 1:35

He pointed to Jesus, who rose a great while before day to pray, and to David, who sought God early. He noticed that the disciples, waking late and ashamed, knew where to find their Master. And he went through a long line of Christians who had done the same. Charles Simeon gave the hours from four to eight to God; Wesley rose at four for two hours of prayer; Samuel Rutherford rose at three; Joseph Alleine prayed from four until eight. Bounds did not offer these as rules to copy mechanically, but as a witness that those whom God used most had taken the matter seriously.

## Desire that breaks the chains of sleep

What lay behind the early rising, Bounds said, was desire. The saints did not get up early because a rule told them to. They got up because they wanted God more than sleep. “Morning listlessness,” he wrote, “is the index to a listless heart.” A desire for God that cannot get out of bed, he said, will do little good once it is up.

> O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is; PSALM 63:1

He also saw that the practice feeds the desire. Getting up gives desire room to grow; staying in bed smothers it. Habits are not the opposite of love. They are one of the ways love is kept alive.

He quoted at length the resolution of Robert Murray McCheyne, who confessed that when he slept long or met others early it was often late in the morning before he began secret prayer, and called this a wretched and unscriptural system. It is far better, McCheyne wrote, to begin with God and see His face first, before seeing anyone else.

## Much time with God

The morning hour was part of a larger conviction: that real prayer takes time. “Much time spent with God is the secret of all successful praying,” Bounds wrote. He allowed that many prayers must be short, and that public prayers should be brief. But he insisted that our short prayers draw their power from long ones. “Our short prayers owe their point and efficiency to the long ones that have preceded them.”

> And it came to pass in those days, that he went out into a mountain to pray, and continued all night in prayer to God. LUKE 6:12

His best-known phrase on this subject is also his most homely: “God’s acquaintance is not made by pop calls.” Friendship grows through time spent together. We cannot know God deeply by brief and hurried visits. Much time alone with Him, Bounds said, is the secret of knowing Him and of having influence with Him.

He was careful to add that the value of prayer is not measured by the clock. His point was not that God counts minutes, but that a faith which never takes time to be alone with God is shallow.

## Morning, noon and night

Bounds did not think prayer should be confined to the morning. He admired Daniel, who knelt three times a day, and David, who prayed evening, morning and noon. In Purpose in Prayer he wrote warmly of Christians who learned to turn their thoughts to God throughout the day, so that the heart returns to Him whenever the mind is free, as naturally as a bird returns to its nest.

> Evening, and morning, and at noon, will I pray, and cry aloud: and he shall hear my voice. PSALM 55:17

The morning hour is the root; prayer through the day is the branches. One without the other withers.

## Where this must be read with care

This teaching has helped countless people. It has also burdened many, and some care is needed.

First, circumstances differ. Bounds rose at four in his later years, when he had no congregation to serve and had made prayer his principal work. A mother with a newborn, a shift worker, someone with chronic illness, or a person whose mind is clearest at night may not be able to follow his pattern, and should not feel condemned. The principle is to give God the first and best of our attention, not necessarily the first hour on the clock. For some, the best time will be late at night or in the middle of the day.

Second, Scripture itself warns against turning early rising into a badge of merit.

> It is vain for you to rise up early, to sit up late, to eat the bread of sorrows: for so he giveth his beloved sleep. PSALM 127:2

God is not impressed by exhaustion. He gives His beloved sleep. Bounds’s long hours were the overflow of love, not a way of earning it, and they are not the measure of anyone else’s devotion.

Third, the lists of saints who prayed for hours can discourage the ordinary believer who struggles to pray for ten minutes. Begin where you are. Ten minutes given to God before anything else is a real offering, and He is glad to receive it. Faithfulness in little grows into more.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Look honestly at how your day begins. What do you give your first attention to? Decide on one change that would put God first.
2. Try for one week to pray before you look at your phone or read any messages.
3. Set aside one longer time of prayer this week — thirty minutes or an hour — and spend it unhurriedly with God.
4. Choose three moments in the day — morning, midday and evening — and pause briefly at each to turn your heart to God.
5. If your circumstances make early rising impossible, identify the best hour you do have and offer it to God without guilt.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You rose a great while before day to pray to Your Father. I confess how often I give my first hours to lesser things and leave You the scraps.
> Give me a desire for You that is stronger than my desire for sleep, comfort or news. Draw me to seek You early.
> Teach me that Your acquaintance is not made by hurried visits. Make me willing to spend time with You, and to return to You throughout the day.
> And where my circumstances are hard, let me offer You what I can, trusting that You receive it gladly. Amen.

# Lifting Up Holy Hands

> I will therefore that men pray every where, lifting up holy hands, without wrath and doubting. 1 TIMOTHY 2:8

Bounds never allowed prayer to float free of life. In The Necessity of Prayer he gave three chapters to prayer and character, conduct and obedience, and in Purpose in Prayer he returned to the theme under the blunt title Prayerless Praying. His conviction was simple: the way we live and the way we pray cannot be separated. “We pray feebly because we live feebly,” he wrote. “The stream of prayer cannot rise higher than the fountain of living.”

## Character and conduct

Bounds began with a distinction. Conduct is what we do; character is what we are. Conduct is the fruit, character the root. And prayer, he said, has to do with both. It helps to form character and fashion conduct, and both depend on prayer to keep going.

> Who shall ascend into the hill of the LORD? or who shall stand in his holy place? He that hath clean hands, and a pure heart; who hath not lifted up his soul unto vanity, nor sworn deceitfully. PSALM 24:3–4

He was convinced that the chief business of the Church is not to gather numbers or raise money but to produce righteous men and women, and that prayer is the means by which God does it. “Prayer and sinning cannot keep company with each other,” he wrote. One or the other must stop. Get people praying, and they will begin to hate sin; let them stop praying, and sin will soon feel at home.

## The prayer of the obedient

For Bounds, the clearest statement of the link between prayer and life was a single verse from John.

> And whatsoever we ask, we receive of him, because we keep his commandments, and do those things that are pleasing in his sight. 1 JOHN 3:22

He loved the confidence it describes. The obedient child, he said, comes to his father boldly; the disobedient one comes timidly, or not at all. Obedience does not earn answers, but it removes the barriers that make us hesitant and ashamed. “The difficulty in prayer is not with faith,” he wrote, “but with obedience, which is faith’s foundation.” Where the will is surrendered to God, faith comes almost of itself.

He had a sharp word for those who wanted to pray for great things while living carelessly. The penitent sinner may come to God with tears and be heard, he said; that is the prayer of repentance, and God delights in it. But the ongoing life of prayer belongs to those who are walking with God. “We must live for God out of the closet if we would meet God in the closet.”

> If I regard iniquity in my heart, the Lord will not hear me: PSALM 66:18

## A holy life and a praying life

In Purpose in Prayer Bounds put the relationship in a sentence that is worth memorising: “A holy life does not live in the closet, but it cannot live without the closet.” Holiness is lived out in the home, the workplace and the street. But it is sustained in secret. Cut off prayer and holiness dies; keep up prayer while living carelessly and prayer becomes a sham.

He applied this ruthlessly to himself and his readers. Beautiful theories, he said, are marred by ugly lives. Many would pray better if they lived better. So much of our time in prayer is spent settling old accounts — confessing the same sins, repairing the same damage — that we have little left for intercession. He had a vivid picture for it: our closets are too often like a shop with a sign in the window saying Closed for Repairs.

> Behold, the LORD’s hand is not shortened, that it cannot save; neither his ear heavy, that it cannot hear: But your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you, that he will not hear. ISAIAH 59:1–2

## Prayer as the way to holiness

It would be a misreading of Bounds to think he wanted people to clean up their lives before they dared to pray. He was clear that prayer is also the means of becoming holy. “Prayer promotes righteous living,” he wrote, and it is the great aid to uprightness of heart and life. The fruit of real praying is right living. We do not become holy and then pray; we pray, and God makes us holy.

> Watch and pray, that ye enter not into temptation: the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak. MATTHEW 26:41

This is the gospel order. The tax collector who could only cry God be merciful to me a sinner went home justified. Prayer begins in need, not in achievement.

## Where this must be read with care

Bounds belonged to the Methodist tradition, with its strong teaching on holiness, and some of his statements need careful weighing.

He argued in The Necessity of Prayer that God never commands what we cannot perform, and that the regenerate person, enabled by grace, can keep all of God’s commandments and please Him in everything. There is great encouragement in this: God does not mock us with impossible demands, and grace is real power, not only pardon. But Scripture also teaches that sin remains in the believer, that we all stumble in many things, and that the best of saints still pray Forgive us our trespasses every day. A reader who takes Bounds too literally may conclude either that he is not a Christian at all because he still sins, or that he has reached a perfection he has not. Both are dangerous.

> My little children, these things write I unto you, that ye sin not. And if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous: 1 JOHN 2:1

He also wrote at one point that a person who does not pray cannot be called a Christian at all. As a description of the healthy Christian life this is true; prayer is the breath of the soul. But as a test to apply to a struggling believer it is too severe. Many true Christians go through seasons when they can scarcely pray, and the Spirit Himself intercedes for them with groanings that cannot be uttered.

The balance is this. Take seriously Bounds’s insistence that life and prayer belong together, and let it search you. But when it exposes your failure, run to your Advocate, not away from Him. The holy hands we lift are not hands that have never been dirty. They are hands that have been washed.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask God to show you one area of disobedience that is making you hesitant or ashamed in prayer. Confess it, and take one concrete step to put it right.
2. Read 1 John 3:18–22 and ask what it would mean for your heart not to condemn you when you pray.
3. Consider whether your prayers are spent mostly on repairs. Ask God for grace to live in a way that frees you for intercession.
4. Think of one relationship where you are harbouring anger. Remember Paul’s words, without wrath, and seek to be reconciled.
5. When you fail this week, go straight to Christ your Advocate rather than avoiding prayer out of shame.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Father, You have told us to lift up holy hands when we pray. I confess that my hands are often soiled, and my life often contradicts my prayers.
> Search me and show me what stands between us. Give me grace to obey You, not to earn Your favour but because I love You.
> Let my praying make me holy, and let my holiness make my praying honest.
> And when I fall, keep me from running away. Bring me to Jesus Christ the righteous, my Advocate with You, in whose name I pray. Amen.

# Call upon Me in the Day of Trouble

> And call upon me in the day of trouble: I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify me. PSALM 50:15

Bounds had known trouble at close quarters. He had been imprisoned for his conscience, had buried young soldiers and a young wife, had seen his town and his cause ruined, and had lived his last years in obscurity. When he wrote about prayer and trouble in The Essentials of Prayer, and about prayer and providence in The Possibilities of Prayer, he was not writing from a comfortable study. These chapters are among the most tender he wrote.

## Trouble is common to all

He began with realism. Trouble belongs to life in a fallen world. It comes to rich and poor, learned and ignorant, saint and sinner. Anyone who expects nothing but sunshine, he said, has a false view of life and will be bitterly surprised.

> Man that is born of a woman is of few days, and full of trouble. JOB 14:1

He was equally clear that trouble is not proof of God’s anger. Job was the most upright man of his day, and he suffered more than anyone. Trouble, Bounds wrote, is no evidence whatever of divine displeasure. It is not punishment, which belongs to the world to come, but discipline — the chastening of a Father who loves His children.

> Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby. HEBREWS 12:11

## God’s hand is in it

The heart of his teaching is that nothing touches the believer outside God’s knowledge and control. Some trouble, he said, we bring on ourselves; some comes from other people; some, as in Job’s case, comes from Satan. But none of it escapes God. He does not approve of sin or cause every evil, but He permits what comes and takes hold of it for good.

> And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose. ROMANS 8:28

Bounds drew on Joseph, who told his brothers that though they had sold him, it was God who had sent him ahead to save many lives; and on Job, who said The LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away. The one who prays, he said, learns to see God’s hand in trouble, even when he cannot explain it. “Prayer does not interpret God’s providences,” he wrote, “but it does justify them and recognise God in them.”

## What prayer does in trouble

Bounds was honest that prayer does not always remove trouble. “Prayer often delivers out of trouble,” he wrote, “and still oftener gives strength to bear trouble.” It brings comfort, begets patience, and saves us from the bitterness that would turn trouble into a curse.

> This poor man cried, and the LORD heard him, and saved him out of all his troubles. PSALM 34:6

He noticed that the same trouble can soften one person and harden another. Pharaoh was hardened by the plagues until they no longer moved him; the prodigal son was brought to himself by hunger. “The same sun softens the wax and hardens the clay.” What makes the difference, he believed, is whether trouble drives us to God or away from Him.

> When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee; and through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee: when thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned; neither shall the flame kindle upon thee. ISAIAH 43:2

## The cup and the garden

For the deepest trouble Bounds turned to Gethsemane. In The Reality of Prayer he described Jesus in the garden, sorrowful unto death, praying three times that the cup might pass, and yet adding each time, Not my will, but thine, be done. The cup did not pass. But an angel came and strengthened Him, and He rose from His knees ready to drink it.

> Saying, Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done. LUKE 22:42

Bounds drew a precious lesson from this. It is no sin, he said, to cry out against pain, to ask for relief, even to plead with God about His way, provided the heart remains submitted to Him. God often answers such prayers not by taking us out of the furnace, but by sending strength to endure it. Paul learned the same over his thorn.

> And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:9

## Where this must be read with care

This teaching is full of comfort, but a few of Bounds’s sentences can wound.

He wrote that if trouble fails in its good purpose, “it is either because of prayerlessness or unbelief, or both.” There is truth in the warning, but it can land cruelly on someone whose suffering has not yet produced visible fruit. Healing is often slow. Some wounds are carried to the grave. The psalms of lament show that God’s people may pray honestly for years in darkness without being charged with unbelief.

He also argued, in The Possibilities of Prayer, that no child ever dies without God’s direct permission, and that such a death takes place in His providence for wise ends. He meant it as comfort, and many grieving parents have found comfort in knowing that their child’s life was never out of God’s hands. But others have found such words hard to bear in the rawness of loss. It is wise to hold this truth quietly, to speak it gently if at all, and to remember that Jesus wept at the grave of Lazarus even though He knew what He was about to do.

> But though he cause grief, yet will he have compassion according to the multitude of his mercies. For he doth not afflict willingly nor grieve the children of men. LAMENTATIONS 3:32–33

The last word on trouble is not an explanation. It is a Person. God does not always tell us why. He does promise to be with us. Bounds’s best counsel was the simplest: whatever the trouble and wherever it comes from, take it to God in prayer.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name the trouble that weighs on you most at present. Tell God about it plainly, holding nothing back.
2. Ask whether this trouble is driving you towards God or away from Him, and what it would take to turn towards Him.
3. Read Psalm 34 slowly, and underline every promise to those who are in trouble.
4. Think of someone who is suffering. Pray for them, and then find one practical way to show them care this week.
5. If you are in deep grief, give yourself permission to lament. Pray one of the psalms of lament, such as Psalm 13 or Psalm 88, as your own.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You have told us to call upon You in the day of trouble. I call upon You now, with what I understand and with all that I do not.
> Thank You that nothing reaches me outside Your hand, and that You are able to work even this for good. Where I cannot see Your purpose, help me to trust Your heart.
> If it be possible, take this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but Yours be done. And if You do not take it away, send me strength to drink it.
> Keep me from bitterness. Let this trouble soften me and draw me nearer to You, until I see You face to face. Amen.

# The Hand That Moves the World

> Call unto me, and I will answer thee, and shew thee great and mighty things, which thou knowest not. JEREMIAH 33:3

The Possibilities of Prayer is Bounds’s boldest book. Its argument is that prayer is not a pious exercise that makes us feel better, but a real force in the world, because it moves God to act. “Prayer moves the hand that moves the world,” he wrote. This chapter gathers his teaching on what God does in answer to prayer, and it is the chapter where his boldness most needs to be weighed.

## Prayer affects God

Bounds had no patience with the idea that prayer only changes the one who prays. The notion that we are to answer our own prayers he dismissed as a half-infidel declaration, and he called the whisper that prayer does not affect God a specious scepticism. Prayer, he insisted, is addressed to God and aims at God. It changes us, certainly, but it does so by first moving God to act for us. “It lays its hand on Almighty God and moves Him to do what He would not otherwise do if prayer was not offered.”

He found this everywhere in the Old Testament. Abraham prayed for Abimelech, and God healed his household. Moses prayed for forty days after the golden calf, and God turned from His anger. Nineveh repented and cried to God, and He spared the city. Hezekiah was told to set his house in order, because he would die; he turned his face to the wall and wept and prayed, and God added fifteen years to his life.

> I have heard thy prayer, I have seen thy tears: behold, I will heal thee: 2 KINGS 20:5

> And the LORD repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people. EXODUS 32:14

A chapter of Purpose in Prayer even carries the title Prayer Changes the Purpose of God. Bounds meant that God has chosen to make His actions depend in real ways on the prayers of His people. We are not spectators of a closed plan. We are invited to take part.

## No limit but God

From there Bounds moved to the breadth of the promises. Jesus said whatsoever, and anything, and all things. What, Bounds asked, is left out of whatsoever? The only limits to prayer, he wrote, are the promises of God and His ability to fulfil them — and His ability has no limit.

> Now unto him that is able to do exceeding abundantly above all that we ask or think, according to the power that worketh in us, EPHESIANS 3:20

He was insistent that this includes ordinary life. Prayer, he said, takes in food and clothing, business and health, as well as the soul. To leave daily needs out of prayer is to leave God out of most of our lives. Give us this day our daily bread belongs in the same prayer as Hallowed be thy name.

> Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. PHILIPPIANS 4:6

And he gathered stories to prove it: Wesley praying on a becalmed ship and the wind changing, George Müller trusting God for a chair for his wife’s voyage, Hudson Taylor and three others praying for a breeze as their ship drifted towards a reef. Some of these stories were gathered by his editors, and they vary in weight, but together they make his point: God answers prayer, and not only in spiritual things.

## Real answers

Bounds refused to let the idea of answered prayer dissolve into vagueness. The child who asks for bread, he said, gets bread, not a stone, and not something vaguely like bread. Jesus promised that the very door we knock at will be opened. He was impatient with the comfortable notion that God always answers, but usually by giving us something else or by making us better in some invisible way.

> If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children: how much more shall your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him? LUKE 11:13

There is a rebuke here that many modern Christians need. We can become so skilled at explaining why prayers are not answered that we stop expecting them to be. Bounds wanted his readers to ask for real things and look for real answers.

## Where this must be read with care

This is the teaching that most needs balance, and Bounds himself provides some of it.

First, on unanswered prayer he sometimes went too far. He wrote that unanswered prayers are training schools for unbelief, and that “The millions of unanswered prayers are not to be solved by the mystery of God’s will.” The whole explanation, he said, is wrong praying. Yet in the same book he admitted that Scripture records prayers that God did not grant: David’s for his dying child, Paul’s for the removal of his thorn, and Christ’s own in Gethsemane. He called them exceptions. They are more than that. They are windows onto a God who sometimes says no out of a love and wisdom we cannot yet see. A believer whose prayer for healing or rescue has not been answered should not conclude from Bounds that the fault must be theirs.

> For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:8

Second, the language of prayer changing God’s purpose must be held alongside Scripture’s teaching that God does not change and that His counsel stands for ever. Bounds was not a careful systematic theologian, and he did not try to reconcile the two. One of the voices gathered into Purpose in Prayer, the Bible teacher S. D. Gordon, offered a wiser way of putting it: that prayer does not so much change God’s purpose as give Him the opportunity to work it out. God has ordained not only the ends but the means, and our prayers are among the means He has chosen.

> And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us: 1 JOHN 5:14

Third, the stories of dramatic answers, however encouraging, can create expectations that God has not promised. For every becalmed ship that caught the wind, there were faithful believers whose ships were lost. God is not less loving to them.

The lasting gift of this teaching is its expectancy. Bounds wants us to believe that prayer matters, that it reaches God, and that God acts. Keep that. Hold it together with humility about how and when He will act, and trust in His goodness when He does not do what we ask.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself whether you really expect God to act when you pray. If not, confess it and ask Him to renew your expectancy.
2. Bring one ordinary, practical need to God this week — money, work, health, a household matter — and watch for His answer.
3. Keep a simple record of prayers and answers for a month. Note what God does, including answers that differ from what you asked.
4. If you carry a prayer that God has not granted, read 2 Corinthians 12:7–10 and ask for the grace Paul received.
5. Pray one large prayer this week for something beyond your own life — a nation, a church, a lost friend — and ask God to do exceeding abundantly above all you can think.

### A PRAYER

> Almighty God, You have invited me to call upon You, and promised to show me great and mighty things. Forgive me for asking so little and expecting less.
> Teach me that my prayers reach You, that You hear them, and that You act. Give me the faith to ask for real things and to look for real answers.
> Where You say no, keep me from bitterness and from blaming myself without cause. Let Your grace be sufficient for me.
> And do among us, through the prayers of Your people, exceeding abundantly above all that we ask or think, to the glory of Your name. Amen.

# Pray Ye the Lord of the Harvest

> Then saith he unto his disciples, The harvest truly is plenteous, but the labourers are few; Pray ye therefore the Lord of the harvest, that he will send forth labourers into his harvest. MATTHEW 9:37–38

Bounds was a man of the closet, but his closet had windows that looked out on the whole world. He wrote a chapter on prayer and missions in The Essentials of Prayer, another on prayer born of compassion, and in Purpose in Prayer a chapter called Revivals Are Born in Prayer. Together they show that for him prayer was never a private hobby. It was the engine of the Church’s mission.

## Compassion that prays

Bounds began where Jesus began: with compassion. When Jesus saw the crowds, weary and scattered like sheep without a shepherd, He was moved, and His first instruction to His disciples was not Go, but Pray.

Compassion, Bounds wrote, has eyes. It sees the sin and misery of people and is moved by them. It stands at the opposite pole from indifference. And true compassion always turns to prayer. Where it cannot relieve the need itself, it can at least cry to God. Compassion may not always move people, he said, but it always moves towards them, and it can move God towards them.

> Brethren, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for Israel is, that they might be saved. ROMANS 10:1

He drew attention to Paul, who carried such sorrow for his own people that he could wish himself cut off for their sake. That, Bounds said, is the root of real intercession: a heart so burdened for others that it must pray.

## The key in the home church’s hands

The chapter on missions contains some of his sharpest and most lasting words. “The key of all missionary success is prayer,” he wrote. “That key is in the hands of the home churches.” Money is needed, he admitted, but money without prayer is powerless. The churches of his day, he complained, gave great attention to raising funds and little to raising prayer, as if prayer would follow as a matter of course. The truth, he said, is the reverse. Get the church praying and the giving will follow. “He who enters his closet to God, will also open his purse to God.”

> As they ministered to the Lord, and fasted, the Holy Ghost said, Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them. And when they had fasted and prayed, and laid their hands on them, they sent them away. ACTS 13:2–3

He noticed that the first missionary journey began in a praying church at Antioch. God called and sent; the church fasted and prayed. That, he believed, is the pattern: missionaries are called by God in answer to the prayers of His people. He ended the chapter with a longing that a great wave of prayer would sweep through the Church, and with a line from Isaiah that summed up the need.

> And he saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor: ISAIAH 59:16

“The one great need in the modern missionary movement,” Bounds wrote, “is intercessors.”

## Revivals born in prayer

Bounds had seen revival in Methodism, and he read its history eagerly. In Purpose in Prayer he gathered accounts of awakening — the great communion at the Kirk of Shotts in Scotland, the ministry of Whitefield, stories from Charles Finney — and drew one conclusion from them all: every true revival has been born in prayer. God alone can revive His Church, but He does it in answer to the cries of His people.

> O LORD, revive thy work in the midst of the years, in the midst of the years make known; in wrath remember mercy. HABAKKUK 3:2

He told, with evident delight, the story of how D. L. Moody preached in a London church on a Sunday morning to no effect, and on the Sunday evening saw hundreds respond. The difference, it later emerged, was that an invalid woman in the congregation had spent the afternoon shut in her room praying for him. The story is one of many that Bounds or his editors gathered, and its details cannot now be checked. But its point is sound, and it is one Scripture makes again and again.

> And when they had prayed, the place was shaken where they were assembled together; and they were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and they spake the word of God with boldness. ACTS 4:31

## Ask of me

Behind all this lay Psalm 2, which Bounds quoted again and again. God says to His Son, Ask of me, and I shall give thee the heathen for thine inheritance. If even the Son is told to ask, Bounds reasoned, how much more must His Church. The advance of Christ’s kingdom is bound up with prayer.

> Ask of me, and I shall give thee the heathen for thine inheritance, and the uttermost parts of the earth for thy possession. PSALM 2:8

## Where this must be read with care

Three cautions belong here.

First, Bounds sometimes made the spread of the gospel depend so entirely on human praying that God’s own initiative is obscured. He wrote that if the Church had prayed as it ought, the light of the gospel would long ago have girdled the world, and he quoted with approval the saying that God cannot save the world alone. The concern is right — the Church is indeed called to pray and to go — but Scripture is clear that salvation is of the Lord, and that the Spirit blows where He wills. Our prayers are real means, not the power behind the power.

Second, his language about the heathen, foreign lands and dead churches belongs to his age. The Church he knew was chiefly in the West; today most Christians live in Africa, Asia and Latin America, and many of the prayers for revival now rise from there. We should hear his burden for the unreached without adopting his assumptions about who is sending and who is receiving.

Third, revival cannot be manufactured, even by prayer. Some later teachers turned Bounds’s conviction into a technique: pray in a certain way for a certain length of time, and revival will come. Bounds was closer to the truth when he spoke of God’s sovereign visitation in answer to the cries of a burdened people. We pray, and God comes when He wills.

With these cautions, his challenge stands. The harvest is still plenteous, the labourers still few, and the command still the same: Pray ye therefore the Lord of the harvest.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Choose one country or people group where the gospel is little known. Learn a little about it and pray for it every day this week.
2. If you give to missions, give prayer as well: pray regularly by name for at least one missionary or worker you support.
3. Pray Christ’s own prayer, that the Lord of the harvest will send out labourers — and be willing to be one of the answers.
4. Ask God to give you compassion for someone near you who does not know Christ, and pray for them by name.
5. Pray for revival in your own church, beginning with yourself: Lord, revive Your work, and begin in me.

### A PRAYER

> Lord of the harvest, You looked on the crowds with compassion, and You told us to pray. Give me eyes to see the people around me and across the world as You see them.
> Send out labourers into Your harvest. Call men and women, young and old, and equip them by Your Spirit. And if You would send me, make me willing.
> Revive Your work in the midst of the years. Do in our day what You have done before, and more.
> Let the nations become the inheritance of Your Son, for whom all things were made, and in whose name I ask it. Amen.

# Behold, He Prayeth

> Elias was a man subject to like passions as we are, and he prayed earnestly that it might not rain: and it rained not on the earth by the space of three years and six months. JAMES 5:17

Bounds liked to teach by example. Power Through Prayer is crowded with the names of praying men and women from church history, and Prayer and Praying Men, published in 1921, is almost entirely a gallery of portraits: Abraham, Moses, Elijah, Hezekiah, Ezra, Nehemiah, Samuel, Daniel and, at length, Paul. It is the easiest of his books to read straight through. This chapter walks through that gallery and asks what Bounds wanted us to learn from it.

## Men of like passions

Bounds’s favourite text about the saints was James’s remark that Elijah was a man subject to like passions as we are. Elijah seems larger than life — a prophet who shut the heavens and called down fire. But James insists he was ordinary flesh and blood, with the same fears and moods as anyone. Bounds took that as an invitation. The point of the praying saints is not that they were a different species, but that they show us what God does through ordinary people who pray.

> And he prayed again, and the heaven gave rain, and the earth brought forth her fruit. JAMES 5:18

He noticed how Elijah’s prayer on Carmel was brief, simple and bold, and how his prayer for rain was persistent, sending his servant seven times to look for a cloud. He noted too that Elijah’s prayers were not only public. Before he ever stood before Ahab, he had learned to pray in secret.

## The gallery of Scripture

Each of Bounds’s biblical portraits draws out one aspect of prayer.

Abraham, he said, built an altar wherever he pitched his tent, and was among the first to gather his household for prayer. His intercession for Sodom showed the boldness and humility of a friend of God.

> And Abraham answered and said, Behold now, I have taken upon me to speak unto the Lord, which am but dust and ashes: GENESIS 18:27

Moses was the mighty intercessor, who stood between God and a rebellious people and would not let go. Bounds was struck by the thought that Moses’ face shone most brightly not after receiving the law, but after his forty days of pleading for Israel. Intercession changes the intercessor.

> Yet now, if thou wilt forgive their sin—; and if not, blot me, I pray thee, out of thy book which thou hast written. EXODUS 32:32

Samuel was the child of his mother’s prayers and became a man of prayer himself, who counted it a sin to stop praying for his people. Hezekiah turned every crisis of his reign — the Assyrian army, his own mortal illness — into an occasion for prayer. Ezra wept and prayed over the sins of the returned exiles, and Nehemiah fasted and prayed over the ruined walls of Jerusalem before he set about rebuilding them. Daniel prayed three times a day with his window open towards Jerusalem, as he did aforetime, and would not stop even when it meant the lions’ den.

> Moreover as for me, God forbid that I should sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you: but I will teach you the good and the right way: 1 SAMUEL 12:23

## Paul, the teacher of prayer

Bounds gave five chapters to Paul, more than to anyone else. He loved the moment when the Lord told the frightened Ananias to go to Saul of Tarsus, and gave as His reason three words: Behold, he prayeth. That, Bounds said, was the sign of Paul’s conversion and the key to his whole life.

> And the Lord said unto him, Arise, and go into the street which is called Straight, and enquire in the house of Judas for one called Saul, of Tarsus: for, behold, he prayeth, ACTS 9:11

He traced Paul’s praying through his letters: his constant thanksgiving, his prayers for the churches to grow in knowledge, love and holiness, and above all his repeated requests that the churches pray for him. Paul was not ashamed to ask for help, and he treated the prayers of ordinary believers as essential to his work.

## Praying men of history

In Power Through Prayer Bounds widened the gallery to the whole history of the Church. He quoted David Brainerd, the young missionary to the Native Americans of the colonial frontier, who wrote: “I love to be alone in my cottage, where I can spend much time in prayer.” He told how Edward Payson’s knees had worn grooves into the hardwood floor where he knelt. He wrote of John Welch in Scotland, who thought a day ill spent if he did not give many hours to prayer; of John Fletcher, whose walls were said to be stained by the breath of his prayers; of Adoniram Judson in Burma, whose counsel was to withdraw from business and company seven times a day to pray. He quoted Luther, Wesley and McCheyne, Bishop Andrewes and Henry Martyn.

> Remember them which have the rule over you, who have spoken unto you the word of God: whose faith follow, considering the end of their conversation. HEBREWS 13:7

His purpose was not to make heroes but to hold up a mirror. These men, he said, prayed not because they watched the clock, but because prayer was so important to them that they could hardly stop. And he drew the moral sharply: we build their tombs and write their epitaphs, but are careful not to follow their examples.

## Where this must be read with care

Galleries of heroes can inspire, but they can also mislead.

First, many of Bounds’s historical examples are drawn from admiring biographies written in an age that liked its saints spotless, and some of the details — stained walls, grooved floorboards, hours counted exactly — have the character of pious tradition. They are worth reading for their spirit rather than as precise records.

Second, the gallery is almost entirely male. Bounds gave Hannah a warm place, and elsewhere he praised praying mothers, but a reader today should know that the history of prayer is full of women — from Anna in the temple to Susanna Wesley, from praying grandmothers to women missionaries — whose stories are largely missing from his pages.

Third, the lives of great intercessors can make ordinary believers feel small. Bounds meant the opposite. James chose Elijah precisely because he was a man of like passions. The saints of the Bible included Jacob the schemer, Moses who once refused God’s call, and Peter who denied his Lord. God did not wait for them to be impressive before He heard them. He will not wait for you.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Choose one of the biblical figures Bounds wrote about — Abraham, Moses, Samuel, Daniel, Nehemiah — and read their story this week, watching for their prayers.
2. Read Paul’s prayers in Ephesians 1:15–23 and 3:14–21, and pray them for someone you love.
3. Think of a person, living or dead, whose example of prayer has helped you. Give thanks for them, and consider what you can learn from them.
4. Ask whether anyone could say of you what the Lord said of Paul: Behold, he prayeth. What would need to change?
5. If the saints make you feel small, read James 5:17 again and take courage: they were people of like passions with you.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, You heard Abraham and Moses, Samuel and Daniel, Elijah and Paul. They were men of like passions with me, and You heard them because they prayed.
> Thank You for the great cloud of witnesses who have gone before, men and women who prayed and were answered. Let their faith stir mine.
> Keep me from admiring them without imitating them, and from building their tombs while neglecting their example.
> Make my life such that it might be said of me also, in heaven if not on earth: Behold, she prays; behold, he prays. Amen.

# He Ever Liveth to Make Intercession

> Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them. HEBREWS 7:25

Behind all of Bounds’s urging there is a Person. He did not think of prayer chiefly as a human technique, but as a sharing in what Jesus Christ has always done and is doing still. The Reality of Prayer gives most of its pages to Christ as the teacher and example of prayer, to His prayer in John 17 and His agony in Gethsemane, and then to the Holy Spirit as our helper in prayer. This chapter gathers that teaching, which is the deepest thing Bounds wrote and the ground beneath everything else.

## The praying Christ

Bounds never tired of pointing out how much Jesus prayed. He prayed at His baptism, and the heavens opened. He prayed all night before choosing the twelve. He prayed on the mountain and was transfigured. He rose early to pray, withdrew into the wilderness to pray, and prayed at the grave of Lazarus. In Gethsemane He prayed with strong crying and tears, and on the cross He prayed for His murderers.

> Who in the days of his flesh, when he had offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears unto him that was able to save him from death, and was heard in that he feared; HEBREWS 5:7

To Bounds this was astonishing. If the Son of God, who was one with the Father, needed to pray, how could anyone else think prayer optional? Jesus, he said, is not only our Saviour but our teacher and pattern in prayer. When the disciples asked Lord, teach us to pray, they were asking the one person who could.

## The High Priest’s prayer

Bounds gave a whole chapter to the prayer of John 17, which he called the sacerdotal prayer, the prayer of Christ as priest. He was moved by its calm and majesty, so different from the anguish of Gethsemane a few hours later. Here Jesus prays for His disciples: that they may be kept from the evil one, sanctified through the truth, made one as He and the Father are one, and brought at last to be with Him and see His glory.

> I pray not that thou shouldest take them out of the world, but that thou shouldest keep them from the evil. JOHN 17:15

What struck Bounds most was the burden of that prayer for unity. He grieved over the divisions of the Church, the walls of separation, the warring parties, and he saw in them a wound to the heart of Christ. Christ prayed for His people to be one, and those who share His prayer will pray for the same.

> Neither pray I for these alone, but for them also which shall believe on me through their word; JOHN 17:20

That verse means that on the night before He died, Jesus prayed for you by name, in advance.

## Christ at the right hand

The prayer of John 17 did not end when Jesus rose from His knees. Bounds drew attention again and again to the New Testament teaching that the risen Christ continues to intercede for His people at the right hand of God. In The Possibilities of Prayer he asked whether the earthly Jesus was the Christ of prayer, who sought solitude and wrestled in agony; and whether He does not ever live, enthroned above at the Father’s right hand, there to pray for us.

> Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us. ROMANS 8:34

This is the deepest comfort in all of Bounds. Our prayers are weak, broken and often confused. But they are not the only prayers offered for us. The Son of God prays for us continually, and He is heard. In The Essentials of Prayer Bounds spoke of the comfort and hope of knowing that one in heaven ever lives to intercede for us, whose compassion does not fail. And he drew a remarkable conclusion: “The benefits of His intercession flow to us through our intercessions.” When we pray for others, we are not acting alone. We are joining our small voices to the great intercession already going on in heaven.

## The Spirit who helps

Bounds taught that believers have not one intercessor but two. Christ prays for us in heaven; the Holy Spirit prays in us on earth. In The Reality of Prayer he wrote warmly of Romans 8, where Paul admits that we do not know what to pray for as we ought, and says that the Spirit helps our weakness.

> Likewise the Spirit also helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought: but the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered. ROMANS 8:26

The Spirit, Bounds said, takes hold with us as we struggle, adds His wisdom to our ignorance and His strength to our weakness, and shapes our praying according to the will of God. We do not pray by our own resources. We pray, as Jude says, in the Holy Ghost.

## Where this must be read with care

Bounds wrote about Christ’s prayers with devotion but not always with theological precision, and a few of his phrases need care.

First, he sometimes spoke of the Holy Spirit as another Christ, or the other Christ. He meant, rightly, that the Spirit continues Christ’s work and brings His presence to us. But the Spirit is a distinct Person of the Godhead, not a second Christ, and Scripture calls Him the Spirit of Christ rather than another Christ. The phrase is best read as a devotional figure.

Second, in Purpose in Prayer he wrote that Jesus gained His transfiguration and His mighty works by importunate prayer, as though the Son had to earn His Father’s gifts as we do. Jesus truly prayed, and His prayers truly mattered; but He did not pray from lack. He prayed from perfect communion, as the beloved Son.

Third, Bounds wrote that in Gethsemane Jesus prayed, on that one occasion, against the will of God. It is better to say that He expressed with perfect honesty the shrinking of His human nature from the cup, while submitting wholly to His Father. There was no rebellion in Gethsemane, only the costliest obedience in history. Bounds himself saw that clearly: he said the prayer was made in declared and inviolable submission.

With those cautions, his central point is one of the most encouraging truths in Scripture. You do not pray alone. The Son prays for you in heaven, and the Spirit prays in you on earth. When you cannot find words, they have not stopped.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read John 17 slowly, remembering that verse 20 includes you. Give thanks that Christ prayed for you before you were born.
2. When you feel your prayers are too weak to matter, read Romans 8:26 and 34 and rest in the two intercessors who never stop.
3. Pray this week for the unity of Christ’s Church, especially with believers from traditions different from your own.
4. When you intercede for someone, consciously join your prayer to Christ’s prayer for them, asking what He is asking.
5. Ask the Holy Spirit each day to help you pray, especially about the things you do not know how to put into words.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You prayed in the days of Your flesh with strong crying and tears, and You ever live to make intercession for us. Thank You that You prayed for me before I knew You, and that You pray for me still.
> Holy Spirit, help my weakness. When I do not know what to pray for as I ought, pray in me according to the will of God.
> Let my small intercessions be joined to Yours, and let Your blessings flow through them to those I love.
> Make Your people one, as You and the Father are one, that the world may believe. Amen.

# Prayerless Praying

> Ye have not, because ye ask not. Ye ask, and receive not, because ye ask amiss, that ye may consume it upon your lusts. JAMES 4:2–3

One of the most searching chapters Bounds ever wrote has one of the strangest titles: Prayerless Praying. It stands in Purpose in Prayer, and it asks a question every Christian should face: “Why do we not pray? What are the hindrances to prayer?” His answers, gathered with what he wrote about vigilance in The Necessity of Prayer and about devotion in The Essentials of Prayer, make a map of the things that choke the life of prayer.

## Not asking and asking amiss

Bounds found the two great evils of prayer in a single verse of James: not asking, and asking wrongly. Of the two, he thought the second might be worse, because it has the appearance of prayer without the reality. It deceives the one who prays into thinking a duty has been done when nothing has happened at all.

He described prayerless praying with a string of phrases: a heartless performance, a senseless routine, a dead habit, a hasty and careless exercise. It may be beautiful in words and correct in form, like the long prayers of the Pharisees. But it has no desire behind it, no faith in it and no expectation of an answer. “Prayerless praying stakes nothing on the issue,” he wrote, “for it has nothing to stake.”

> But when ye pray, use not vain repetitions, as the heathen do: for they think that they shall be heard for their much speaking. MATTHEW 6:7

He told of a preacher who, after the congregation rose from prayer, asked them sharply what they had just prayed for. Most could not say. The words had passed their lips without touching their hearts.

## Crowded out

The first hindrance Bounds named is one every modern reader knows: busyness. Other duties press in and crowd out prayer, not by open rebellion but by slow suffocation. “He who is too busy to pray will be too busy to live a holy life.”

He traced the process with painful accuracy. First prayer is hurried. Then it is shortened. Then it is pushed into a corner and done only when there are scraps of time. Then it loses its value, falls out of the habits of life, and dies. “Satan had rather we let the grass grow on the path to our prayer-chamber than anything else.”

> Martha, Martha, thou art careful and troubled about many things: But one thing is needful: and Mary hath chosen that good part, which shall not be taken away from her. LUKE 10:41–42

What makes this hindrance so dangerous is that the things that crowd out prayer are usually good. “One of Satan’s wiliest tricks,” Bounds wrote, “is to destroy the best by the good.” In The Essentials of Prayer he warned that activity is not strength, and that a church can be full of movement while empty of devotion. To be too busy with God’s work to talk to God about it, he said, is the highway to backsliding.

## Living against our prayers

A second hindrance is the life we live outside the closet. We have met this already, but Bounds returned to it here with force. We cannot pray for humility while nurturing pride, or for self-denial while indulging ourselves. Scripture names particular things that hinder prayer: an unforgiving spirit, and in marriage, a husband’s failure to honour his wife.

> And when ye stand praying, forgive, if ye have ought against any: that your Father also which is in heaven may forgive you your trespasses. MARK 11:25

> Likewise, ye husbands, dwell with them according to knowledge, giving honour unto the wife, as unto the weaker vessel, and as being heirs together of the grace of life; that your prayers be not hindered. 1 PETER 3:7

## Asleep at the post

A third hindrance is carelessness. In The Necessity of Prayer Bounds pictured the Christian as a soldier on picket duty, facing an enemy who never sleeps. The armour of God in Ephesians 6, he noted, ends not with another weapon but with prayer, and with the command to watch. Without watchfulness, prayer grows dull and the soul grows drowsy.

> Praying always with all prayer and supplication in the Spirit, and watching thereunto with all perseverance and supplication for all saints; EPHESIANS 6:18

He added that a soldier who prays only for himself has misunderstood the war. Selfish praying, he said, dries up the springs of spiritual life. The soldier prays for the whole army.

## The simplicity of a child

The last hindrance Bounds named is surprising: cleverness. The simplicity of prayer, he said, is itself an obstacle. We grow up, learn a great deal, and lose the directness of a child at his mother’s knee. Intellect gets in the way of the heart. To pray well, a grown man must become a child again, and that is no easy thing.

> Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. MATTHEW 18:3

## Where this must be read with care

This is a chapter to take seriously, but two of Bounds’s statements need tempering.

First, he wrote that it is better not to pray at all than to pray prayerless prayers, and that the worst of sinning is to sin on our knees. His target was hypocrisy — prayer used as a cover for a life that contradicts it. But a discouraged believer may hear it as permission to stop praying because his prayers feel dead. That would be the opposite of what Bounds wanted. Dry, halting prayer offered honestly is not prayerless praying. It is the prayer of a weak child, and God hears it. The cure for cold prayer is not less prayer but more honest prayer.

Second, some of his lists of worldly hindrances belong to his time. In The Essentials of Prayer he named the theatre, the card table and the dance as places where no one could pray. His underlying point — that we should not go where we could not pray — remains wise. But the particular list reflects the Methodist customs of his day, and each generation must ask honestly what its own crowding distractions are. For many of us they are no longer the dance hall, but the screen in our pocket.

The hindrances are real, but none of them is stronger than the invitation of God. The way back from prayerless praying is simply to begin to pray again — truly, briefly if need be, and from the heart.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Next time you finish praying, ask yourself what you actually asked for. If you cannot say, try again more simply.
2. List the good things that most often crowd out your prayer. Choose one to limit this week for the sake of the best.
3. If you are holding something against anyone, forgive them before you next pray, as Jesus commands in Mark 11:25.
4. Identify the modern distraction that most often keeps you from prayer, and set a boundary around it for a week.
5. Pray once this week as simply as a child would, in a few plain sentences, telling your Father exactly what you need.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I confess that much of my praying has been prayerless — words without desire, forms without faith. Forgive me.
> I confess too how easily the good crowds out the best. Keep the path to my place of prayer from becoming overgrown.
> Show me what hinders my prayers — unforgiveness, carelessness, pride, or simple distraction — and give me grace to put it away.
> Make me a child again before You, direct and trusting, and teach me, as You taught Your disciples, to pray. Amen.

# Conclusion

> Pray without ceasing. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:17

Thirteen chapters can make it look as though E. M. Bounds taught many things. He taught one thing, and everything else is that one thing applied. The one thing is this: God does His work in the world through people who pray.

Follow it through and the whole shape appears. Men are God’s method, and the real sermon is made in the closet. Faith gives prayer its grip, and the Word gives faith its ground. Importunity is prayer that will not let go, and the early hour is prayer given the best of the day. Holiness is the life that makes prayer honest. Trouble is where prayer is tested and deepened. What prayer can do is limited only by what God has promised and what He is able to perform. Revival and missions are prayer multiplied through a burdened Church, and the praying saints of the Bible and of history are its witnesses. Behind all our praying stand the Son who intercedes in heaven and the Spirit who intercedes within. And the hindrances, however many, are overcome in the same way — by beginning to pray again.

## The danger of this book

If you have read this far, you are in some danger, and Bounds would have named it first. He thought that reading about prayer could become a substitute for praying. A person can admire a chapter on the early hour and stay in bed. He can agree with every word about importunity and give up his prayers the same week. The peculiar risk of Bounds is that his fire warms the reader without ever reaching the reader’s knees.

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

There is a second danger, the opposite of the first. His intensity can crush. A reader who takes every superlative literally may conclude that his own feeble praying is worthless, that God is disappointed in him, and that every unanswered prayer is his fault. That is not the gospel, and in his better moments it was not Bounds either. He wrote that prayer is the helpless and needy child crying to a Father’s heart. The Father who invites us to pray is not a taskmaster. He is the God who knows our frame and remembers that we are dust.

> Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust. PSALM 103:13–14

So the plainest counsel this book can give is the same as in its companion volumes. Go back to the chapter that made you least comfortable, not the one you found most interesting. Take its application points and work them slowly over months. Then take the next.

## Two things to carry

First, begin. Bounds’s own counsel, repeated through all his books, was that there is no substitute for actually praying. Do not wait until you feel ready or until you can pray for an hour. Take the time you have, the best of it you can give, and give it to God. Ten honest minutes will teach you more than ten chapters.

Second, do not pray alone. Bounds lived at a time of great religious activity, and he feared that the Church would trust its organisation more than its God. Our danger is the same. Find others to pray with — a friend, a small group, your church’s prayer meeting if it has one. Pray for your minister. Pray for the work of God in places you will never see. Remember that the Son and the Spirit are already praying, and join them.

> Rejoicing in hope; patient in tribulation; continuing instant in prayer; ROMANS 12:12

## A last word about the man

We have been honest about his limits. He wrote in superlatives that can wound the weary. He made unanswered prayer too simply our fault. He belonged to an age that spoke of men where it meant people and of the heathen where we would speak of neighbours, and he served, as a chaplain, a cause that fought to preserve slavery. Much of what bears his name was shaped by devoted friends after his death, and we cannot always tell where his voice ends and theirs begins.

None of this has been said to diminish him. It has been said because his own teaching requires it. A man whose whole message was that God does the work, and that we can do nothing without Him, cannot be honoured by pretending that he was more than a man of like passions with us.

> Not unto us, O LORD, not unto us, but unto thy name give glory, for thy mercy, and for thy truth’s sake. PSALM 115:1

He died in the summer of 1913 with his best work unpublished, having told a friend that when God was ready, he was ready. He did not live to see what came of those dark mornings in Georgia. But the prayers he prayed and the pages he wrote have gone on doing their work for more than a century, in languages he never heard, among people he never met. His life is the best argument for his message. What is done in secret, God rewards openly.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Return to the chapter of this book that made you least comfortable. Read it again, and choose one application point to work on for a month.
2. Look again at the number you wrote down at the start of this book — the time you spent in prayer in a week. Decide, realistically and without guilt, what you want it to be, and ask God for help.
3. Find at least one other person to pray with regularly, even briefly.
4. Choose one person, one church and one nation to carry in prayer for the next year.
5. Choose one of Bounds’s own books from the Reader’s Guide that follows, and begin it this week.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, I have read much about prayer, and I am capable of reading more and praying no more than before. Keep me from that.
> Draw me to my knees. Give me the desire to seek You, the faith to expect You, the persistence to keep asking, and the humility to accept Your answers.
> Where Your servant Edward Bounds was faithful, make me faithful. Where I am weak, remember that I am dust, and hold me up.
> Let my life, in its small measure, be one through which You work, until the day when prayer gives way to sight. Amen.

# A Reader’s Guide to E. M. Bounds

> Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. 2 TIMOTHY 2:15

Bounds is easy to find and easy to read. He wrote in short chapters, in plain if rhetorical English, and everything he wrote is now in the public domain. The difficulty is not quantity but repetition. His books circle the same themes, and whole paragraphs reappear from one to another. The guide below is arranged to help the reader choose.

## Begin here

Power Through Prayer. The book by which most readers know him, first published in 1907 as Preacher and Prayer. Twenty short chapters, each opening with a quotation from an earlier man of prayer, pressing one argument: that the preacher’s power is made in the closet. Written for ministers, searching for everyone. The basis of chapters three and four of this volume. In the Ochorus library.

Prayer and Praying Men. The gentlest way in: a gallery of biblical intercessors from Abraham to Paul, published in 1921. It opens with Homer Hodge’s introduction, one of the few first-hand portraits of Bounds that we have. In the Ochorus library.

## The teaching on prayer

The Necessity of Prayer. His most orderly book, setting prayer beside faith, trust, desire, fervency, importunity, obedience, vigilance and the Word of God. The source of much of chapters five, six, seven and nine of this volume. In the Ochorus library.

Purpose in Prayer. Published in 1920, and the most quotable and least systematic of his books. It includes the chapters Prayer Is a Trade to Be Learned, Prayerless Praying and Revivals Are Born in Prayer, alongside many stories gathered from others. In the Ochorus library.

The Essentials of Prayer. Prayer joined to humility, devotion, praise, trouble, compassion and missions. The two chapters on prayer and trouble are among the most tender he wrote. In the Ochorus library.

The Reality of Prayer. The best of his books on Christ as the teacher, example and great high priest of prayer, with chapters on John 17, Gethsemane and the Holy Spirit’s help. The basis of chapter fourteen of this volume. In the Ochorus library.

The Possibilities of Prayer. His boldest case for what God does in answer to prayer, full of promises and stories. Read it for its expectancy, and with the cautions of chapter eleven of this volume. In the Ochorus library.

## Other works

The Weapon of Prayer, the last of the prayer books to be published, did not appear until 1931 and is not yet in the Ochorus library. Hodge also published writings of Bounds on heaven and on Satan. Several publishers have gathered the prayer books into a single collected volume, which is convenient but makes the repetitions more obvious.

## About Bounds

Much of what we know comes from his friends. Homer Hodge’s introduction to Prayer and Praying Men and Claudius Chilton’s foreword to The Necessity of Prayer are short, admiring and close to the man. Among modern studies, Lyle W. Dorsett’s E. M. Bounds: Man of Prayer (1991) combines a biographical essay with selections from his writings, and Darrel D. King has written a short popular life. The Ochorus author page carries a biography.

## Approach with care

Read the stories with discernment. Many of the anecdotes in Purpose in Prayer and The Possibilities of Prayer were gathered from other preachers and from the religious press, and some may have been added by his editors. They illustrate his point; they are not all equally reliable.

Read the superlatives as a preacher’s rhetoric. When Bounds says prayer is the only thing, or that unanswered prayer is always our fault, he is shouting to wake the sleeping, not stating a measured doctrine. Chapters nine and eleven of this volume set out where he must be qualified.

## Three counsels for reading him

Read him a little at a time. His chapters are short and dense, and his force is best taken in small doses. A chapter a day will do more than a book in a weekend.

Read him with the Bible open. He claimed no authority of his own. He wanted to send his readers to the promises of God. Check his texts, and let Scripture have the last word.

And read him on your knees. There is no point in reading Bounds without praying. He would have been the first to say that one hour of prayer is worth more than every book he wrote. Put the book down when it stirs you, and pray.

> Lord, teach us to pray, as John also taught his disciples. LUKE 11:1
