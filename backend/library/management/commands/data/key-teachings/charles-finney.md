  This is not a book by Charles G. Finney. It is a companion to him — an independent work of exposition and appreciation, written by Ochorus, that gathers the heart of his teaching into nineteen short chapters for the ordinary reader, with a chapter on his life, a conclusion and a guide to his books.

  Finney’s one great insistence was that the gospel is to be obeyed, now. A lawyer converted in the woods above Adams, New York, he spent fifty years pressing on churches and sinners alike that religion is something to do and not something to wait for: that a revival is a church returning to obedience, that prayer must be definite and believing, that the Spirit is given to those who ask, and that no sinner may shelter behind his inability. This volume follows that thread through his Lectures on Revivals of Religion, his Memoirs and his best-known sermons. It also says plainly where he must be read with great care — above all in his denial of inherited sin, his governmental view of the atonement, his account of human ability and his confidence in measures — because those departures have shaped the church, for good and ill, ever since.

  Finney’s own writings are in the public domain and freely available, and every chapter here names them so the reader can go to the source; his Lectures on Revivals of Religion, his Memoirs and seven of his sermons are in the Ochorus library. This companion is offered only to open the door and to help the reader weigh what is found there. It quotes Scripture from the Authorised (King James) Version, and it makes no claim to stand in for the man’s own urgent, argumentative, unforgettable pages.

# Introduction

> O LORD, revive thy work in the midst of the years, in the midst of the years make known; in wrath remember mercy. HABAKKUK 3:2

Charles Grandison Finney was a country lawyer who turned into the most famous preacher in America. He had no college degree and almost no theological training. He was converted in a patch of woods one October morning in 1821, walked out of his law practice the next day, and spent the next half-century pleading the cause of Christ as he had once pleaded cases before a jury. Towns in upstate New York were turned upside down under his preaching. He built a tabernacle in New York City, taught theology in a forest clearing in Ohio, and became president of the college that grew up there. By the time he died in 1875 his Lectures on Revivals of Religion had been read on both sides of the Atlantic, and the way most evangelical churches thought about revival, invitation and decision had been changed for good.

He is also one of the most disputed figures in the history of the church. Some regard him as the greatest evangelist since Whitefield. Others regard him as the man who turned revival into a technique and grace into a human achievement. Both judgements have evidence behind them. This book tries to set out what he actually taught, in his own words where they can be quoted, and to weigh it fairly.

## One thread through everything

Beneath all Finney’s teaching lies a single conviction: that God’s commands are to be obeyed, now, by the person who hears them. Finney could not bear the thought that anyone, church member or unbeliever, should sit still under the gospel and wait for something to happen to them. He opened his Lectures on Revivals of Religion with words that summed up his whole ministry: “Religion is the work of man. It is something for man to do.”

Everything else follows from this. A revival, he said, is simply a church returning to obedience. Prayer is to be definite, fervent and believing, because God has bound Himself by promises. The fulness of the Spirit is a command, and therefore within reach. The sinner is commanded to make a new heart, and therefore is able to do it. Preaching should be plain, direct and aimed at a verdict. Measures that bring people to decide are not to be despised because they are new. Christians are to aim at holiness without known sin, and to set their faces against the sins of their nation, including slavery. The question to keep asking as you read is his question: what does God require of me, and why am I not doing it?

> Choose you this day whom ye will serve … but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD. JOSHUA 24:15

## Why he still matters

There are good reasons to read Finney. He refused to let anyone hide behind a doctrine. Many in his day used God’s sovereignty as a reason to do nothing, and told anxious sinners that they could not repent until God made them. Finney thought this cruel and unbiblical, and much of the time he was right.

He took prayer with terrible seriousness. His lectures on prayer are among the most searching ever written, and he always insisted that his revivals were carried by the hidden intercession of people whose names were never famous.

He expected the Holy Spirit to be present in power. His own conversion ended in what he called a mighty baptism of the Holy Ghost, and he never stopped believing that ministers without that anointing were trying to do the work of God without God.

And he held that faith must show itself in public righteousness. At a time when most American churches were silent about slavery, he excluded slaveholders from the Lord’s Table and helped build a college that took in Black students and women.

## How this book is arranged

After a chapter on his life, nineteen short chapters set out his teaching. The first six follow the Lectures on Revivals: what a revival is, the breaking up of fallow ground, prevailing prayer, the spirit of prayer, the prayer of faith and the fulness of the Spirit. A chapter follows on power from on high. Then come three chapters on the doctrines at the centre of the storm: the sinner’s duty to make a new heart, Finney’s view of depravity and the will, and his account of the atonement. Five chapters take up the work of conversion — the directions he gave to sinners, repentance, soul-winning, preaching and the new measures — and one asks why revivals cease. Three more close the book: holiness, the law and the gospel, and the Christian’s obligation to reform.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. Finney would have wanted it so. His own lectures nearly always ended by turning on the hearers and asking, in effect, Will you do it? Read a chapter a day, or one a week. Stop at the application points. Pray the prayer, or better, pray your own.

## Where he must be read with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and on Finney more than most it is necessary to say plainly where he must be weighed rather than simply received.

First, and most important, Finney rejected the historic Protestant teaching on original sin. He denied that the guilt of Adam’s sin is reckoned to his descendants, and he denied that we are born with a nature that is itself sinful; sin, for him, consisted only in voluntary choices. He taught that people are fully able to obey God, and that the Spirit’s work is not to give power but to persuade the unwilling. Many careful readers have judged that this leans strongly towards the teaching of Pelagius, which the church rejected long ago. Chapter eleven sets out what he taught and where it falls short of Scripture.

Second, he held what is called a governmental view of the atonement: that Christ’s death did not pay the penalty of sinners’ sins but upheld the honour of God’s law so that God could safely forgive. He rejected the imputation of Christ’s righteousness to the believer. Chapter twelve weighs this.

Third, his confidence that revival follows the right use of means, and his defence of measures designed to bring people to decide, opened the door to something he himself warned against: the engineered revival, in which technique does the work and emotional pressure is mistaken for the Spirit. The region where he laboured became known as the Burned-over District, and in the years after his revivals it produced not only lasting churches but a crop of sects, fads and disillusionment. Chapters seventeen and eighteen look at this honestly.

Fourth, his teaching on holiness at Oberlin, which held that Christians may live without known sin, has helped some and burdened others, and was criticised by friends as well as foes.

Why read him, then? Because on much that matters most he was right, and because the church has rarely had a more urgent voice calling it to obey what it already knows. The counsel of this book is the counsel of Scripture: Prove all things; hold fast that which is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Where Finney is faithful — on prayer, on repentance, on the Spirit, on the duty to respond to God now — receive it gladly and act on it. Where he presses beyond what is written, set it aside and lose nothing.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name one thing you know God requires of you that you have been waiting for Him to do instead. Write it down.
2. Choose a pace for this book — a chapter a day or a chapter a week — and decide now when you will read it.
3. Ask whether you have ever used a true doctrine, such as God’s sovereignty, as a reason for doing nothing.
4. Resolve to test everything in these pages against Scripture, especially where Finney departs from the teaching of the church.
5. Ask God to use this book to send you not to Finney but to Christ, and to obedience.

> Revive Your work in my own heart first, and then in Your church. Amen.

# A Retainer from the Lord Jesus Christ

> Then shall ye call upon me, and ye shall go and pray unto me, and I will hearken unto you. And ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart. JEREMIAH 29:12–13

A frontier boyhood. Charles Grandison Finney was born on the twenty-ninth of August, 1792, at Warren, in Litchfield County, Connecticut. When he was about two his family moved west to Oneida County, New York, then largely wilderness, and later further north again, to the country near Sackets Harbor on Lake Ontario. His parents were not professing Christians. He recalled in his Memoirs that he seldom heard a sermon, and that the few he heard from wandering preachers were so ignorant that the people laughed at them on the way home. He went to the common schools, taught school himself, and spent some years in New Jersey and New England teaching and studying. He thought of going to Yale, and was talked out of it. He never went to college.

The law office at Adams. In 1818 he entered a law office at Adams, in Jefferson County, as a student. There, for the first time, he sat under a settled, educated ministry. The Presbyterian pastor, George W. Gale, had trained at Princeton and preached a strict Calvinism. But his law books kept quoting the Bible, and so he bought one, the first he had ever owned, and began to read it alongside Blackstone. He attended the church’s prayer meeting, and was troubled that the people prayed for revival week after week and confessed that nothing came. When they asked whether he wanted them to pray for him, he told them no, because he did not see that God answered their prayers. Yet he kept reading, and slowly became convinced that the Bible was the Word of God.

The woods above the village. In the autumn of 1821 he resolved to settle the question of his soul. For two days he read and prayed in secret, so ashamed of being seen that he stopped up the keyhole of his door and threw his law books over his Bible when anyone came in. On the morning of the tenth of October, on his way to the office, he seemed to hear the question put to him whether he was trying to work out a righteousness of his own. He saw, he wrote, that salvation was an offer to be accepted, full and complete in Christ. He turned aside into a wood north of the village, crept into a hollow among fallen trees, and knelt. He had vowed, “I will give my heart to God, or I never will come down from there.” At first he could not pray at all. Then the sound of rustling leaves showed him his pride — that he was ashamed to be found on his knees before God — and it broke him. A promise came to his mind, the words of Jeremiah above, and he took God at His word. He came back to the village by dinner time, so quiet in mind that he feared he had grieved the Spirit away.

Waves of liquid love. That evening, alone in the law office, he seemed to meet the Lord Jesus face to face and fell weeping at His feet. Then, as he turned back to the fire, he received what he called “a mighty baptism of the Holy Ghost.” It came, he said, “in waves and waves of liquid love,” until he cried out that he would die if it went on. The next morning, when a client arrived to remind him of a case to be tried at ten, Finney answered, “Deacon B——, I have a retainer from the Lord Jesus Christ to plead his cause, and I cannot plead yours.” He never practised law again.

The young evangelist. His conversion shook Adams. Within days many of the young people he had led in the choir were converted, and his own parents soon followed. He studied theology under Gale, and their disagreement was fierce and long; Finney would not accept Gale’s teaching on the atonement, original sin or human inability, and said so. The presbytery licensed him to preach in March 1824, and he began work as a missionary in the rough settlements of northern New York. In October that year he married Lydia Andrews. A few days after the wedding he set out to fetch their household goods, found revival breaking out at Evans Mills and Perch River, and did not go back for her; he sent someone else.

The western revivals. From 1824 to 1827 revival followed him through Evans Mills, Antwerp, Gouverneur, De Kalb, Western, Rome, Utica, Auburn and Troy. He preached without notes, in the language of the street and the courtroom, addressed his hearers as “you”, and pressed for immediate decision. He was helped by men of prayer, above all the old minister Daniel Nash, known as Father Nash, who often went ahead of him and gave himself to intercession. At a cotton factory near Utica the workers were so moved when he walked through the mill that the owner ordered it stopped, saying it was more important that souls should be saved than that the factory should run. But his methods and his theology alarmed many in New England, and in July 1827 a convention met at New Lebanon, New York, where Lyman Beecher and Asahel Nettleton confronted him. It settled little, but the opposition lost much of its force.

Rochester. After labours in Wilmington, Philadelphia, Reading and New York, Finney went in September 1830 to Rochester, a booming town on the Erie Canal, and stayed six months. Lawyers, merchants and their families came forward at what was later called the anxious seat. The effects were felt through the town’s business and social life, and the awakening spread through the region. Years later Lyman Beecher, once his opponent, told him it was the greatest work of God in so short a time that the world had seen.

New York and the lectures. In 1832 Finney became pastor of a Free Presbyterian church meeting in the Chatham Street Chapel in New York City, a former theatre. His health was failing, and in 1834 he took a voyage to the Mediterranean. On his return he gave, on Friday evenings through the winter, the Lectures on Revivals of Religion. They were reported in the New York Evangelist and published as a book in 1835. His friends built the Broadway Tabernacle for him, and in 1836 he left the Presbyterian Church for the Congregationalists.

Oberlin. In 1835 he accepted a call to teach theology at the new Oberlin Collegiate Institute in Ohio, on condition that it admit students without regard to colour. The college was poor, surrounded by forest and widely hated for its abolitionism. There, with Asa Mahan and others, he developed the teaching on sanctification that became known as Oberlin perfectionism, and was accused of heresy on every side. He published his Lectures on Systematic Theology in the 1840s. He served as pastor of the church at Oberlin for many years and as president of the college from 1851 to 1866, while still travelling to preach, including two long visits to Britain between 1849 and 1860.

Loss and the last years. Lydia died in 1847. He married twice more, and was widowed again. In 1867 and 1868, at the urging of the Oberlin trustees, he wrote his Memoirs, frank about his opponents and defensive of his measures and doctrine. He died at Oberlin on the sixteenth of August, 1875, less than a fortnight before his eighty-third birthday. The Memoirs appeared the following year.

He left an extraordinary legacy. Tens of thousands had been converted under his preaching. He had shaped American evangelicalism and its habit of calling for public decision more than any man of his century. He had also left a theology that many of the heirs of his revivals could not follow, and a region, the Burned-over District, that had been scorched as well as warmed. He would have wanted to be judged by one thing: whether people obeyed the gospel. On that measure, the lawyer who took the Lord’s retainer never stopped pleading.

2. He was converted when he stopped trying to feel something and took God at His word. Find one promise that fits your need and act on it.
3. He had to leave a profession he loved. Is there anything you are holding back from Christ because you have invested so much in it?
4. Behind his preaching stood people like Daniel Nash, praying unseen. Whose ministry could you carry in hidden prayer?
5. Read the chapter of his Memoirs on his conversion in the Ochorus library, slowly, and mark what struck you.

# Not a Miracle

> Wilt thou not revive us again: that thy people may rejoice in thee? PSALM 85:6

Finney’s Lectures on Revivals of Religion begin with a definition that startled his first hearers and still divides his readers. A revival of religion, he said, is not a miracle, falling like a hailstorm on one town and passing by the next. It is, in his words, “a purely philosophical result of the right use of the constituted means.” To understand Finney at all, we have to understand what he meant by that, what he did not mean, and why it has provoked so much argument.

## A new beginning of obedience

Start with what he said a revival is. It is, he wrote, “the renewal of the first love of Christians, resulting in the awakening and conversion of sinners to God.” It presupposes that the church has declined. It begins with Christians, not with the unconverted. Backslidden believers are brought under conviction of their own sin, driven to repentance, renewed in faith, and filled again with love for God and for the souls around them. Only then, as a rule, do sinners begin to be awakened and converted.

> Remember therefore from whence thou art fallen, and repent, and do the first works. REVELATION 2:5

His shortest definition is his best: “A revival is nothing else than a new beginning of obedience to God.” There is nothing mysterious in that. A church that has been disobeying God starts to obey Him again. It confesses its sins, prays in earnest, seeks the lost, and lays aside its quarrels and its worldliness. That, for Finney, is revival, and every believer can see at once what it requires of him.

Almost every Christian can receive this gladly. Scripture never tells a declining church to sit still until God acts. It tells it to remember, to repent and to return.

> Rend your heart, and not your garments, and turn unto the LORD your God: for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness. JOEL 2:13

## The farmer and his field

Finney’s favourite picture was the farmer. Suppose, he said, someone went among farmers and preached that God is sovereign and will give a crop only when He pleases, so that to plough and sow as if expecting a harvest is to interfere with His sovereignty. If the farmers believed it, they would starve the world. Just so, he argued, the church had persuaded itself that revival is so purely a matter of divine sovereignty that there is no connection between means and results, and had sat waiting while generations perished.

He did not deny that God gives the increase. “Means will not produce a revival, we all know, without the blessing of God,” he wrote. But neither will grain grow without that blessing, and nobody concludes that farmers should stop sowing. God has connected means and ends in grace as in nature, and He expects His people to use them.

> Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap. GALATIANS 6:7

There is a great deal of truth here. Scripture commands the means and never presents God’s sovereignty as a reason for idleness. Paul, who taught election more plainly than anyone, became all things to all men that he might by all means save some.

## Three agents and one instrument

Finney was careful to say who is at work in a revival. There are, he said, ordinarily three agents in the conversion of a sinner and one instrument. The agents are God, the person who brings the truth, and the sinner himself. The instrument is the truth. God acts by His providence, arranging events so that the truth reaches the sinner, and by His Spirit, who sets the truth home with such power that the sinner yields. The Christian acts by speaking, praying and living in a way that makes the truth plain. The sinner acts by obeying it.

> For as the rain cometh down, and the snow from heaven … so shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void. ISAIAH 55:10–11

He illustrated the power of a Christian’s very presence with a story he told in the first lecture, and again in his Memoirs. A man of solemn spirit walked through a factory. A young woman at a loom whispered a joke to her friend and laughed. He simply looked at her with grief. Her thread broke; she could not mend it; she sat down overcome. Conviction ran through the building, the owner stopped the works for prayer, and within days nearly everyone there was converted. The lecture does not name him, but the Memoirs make clear that the man was Finney himself, at a cotton mill near Utica.

## Where the definition goes wrong

So far, so good. But Finney’s definition carried more weight than it could bear, and it needs honest qualification.

First, the word philosophical. Finney meant that revival follows natural laws of the mind as reliably as a crop follows sowing. He went so far as to say that there is nothing in religion beyond the ordinary powers of nature, and that when people become religious they only use the powers they had before in a different way. That is consistent with his view of human ability, which we will weigh in later chapters, but it sits uneasily with Scripture’s account of the new birth as something only God can do.

> The wind bloweth where it listeth, and thou hearest the sound thereof, but canst not tell whence it cometh, and whither it goeth: so is every one that is born of the Spirit. JOHN 3:8

Jesus did not say that the Spirit’s work is lawless. He said it is not ours to command. The farmer can plough and sow and must; but he cannot make it rain, and a wise farmer knows the difference. Finney knew it too, in his better moments, and insisted that means are useless without the Spirit. But his language often suggested that if the church did its part, revival would follow as surely as a chemical reaction. Many who took up his lectures believed exactly that.

Second, the confidence. Finney seems to have thought that where revival did not come, the church had failed to use the means. Sometimes that was true. But Scripture also knows of faithful servants who laboured long with little visible fruit, and of seasons when God withheld His blessing for reasons of His own. Isaiah was sent to a people who would not hear. Jeremiah preached for forty years and saw the city fall. Their faithfulness was not measured by results.

> So then neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth; but God that giveth the increase. 1 CORINTHIANS 3:7

Third, the excitement. Finney taught that a sluggish church must be roused by powerful excitements, though he said, rightly, “Not that excited feeling is religion, for it is not.” The revivals that followed him show how hard it is to raise excitement without mistaking it for grace.

## What to keep

Keep his definition of revival as a new beginning of obedience, because it is true and it strips away every excuse. Keep his refusal to let sovereignty become a pillow. Keep his insistence that revival begins with the church, not with the world. And hold all of it inside the humility of the psalmist who asked God to revive His people, knowing that only God could.

> Turn us again, O God, and cause thy face to shine; and we shall be saved. PSALM 80:3

4. Pray for your own church by name, asking God to renew its first love — and ask what part He wants you to play.
5. Resolve to use the ordinary means God has given — His Word, prayer, fellowship, witness — without supposing that they work by themselves.

# Break Up Your Fallow Ground

> Sow to yourselves in righteousness, reap in mercy; break up your fallow ground: for it is time to seek the LORD, till he come and rain righteousness upon you. HOSEA 10:12

Of everything Finney said, the lecture on breaking up the fallow ground has probably done the most good. It was the third of his Lectures on Revivals of Religion, and Ochorus also carries it as a sermon. It is practical, blunt and uncomfortable, and it asks one thing: that before the reader looks for revival anywhere else, he should plough up his own heart.

## Ground that has gone hard

Fallow ground, Finney explained, is land that was once tilled but has been left to lie waste. It has grown matted and hard, and before it can bear a crop it must be broken up and turned over.

The heart of a Christian can go the same way. It was once tender. The Word once went in. But over months and years it has hardened, and now sermons run off it. Finney said plainly that it will do no good to preach to people whose hearts are in this state; the farmer might as well sow his grain on a rock. The more the rain of preaching falls on a fallow field, the thicker the turf grows.

> For thus saith the LORD to the men of Judah and Jerusalem, Break up your fallow ground, and sow not among thorns. JEREMIAH 4:3

## Not by trying to feel

The first thing Finney said about how to do it is the most surprising. It is not done, he insisted, by direct efforts to feel. “No man can make himself feel in this way, merely by trying to feel,” he wrote. Feelings are not under the direct control of the will. We cannot summon sorrow for sin by straining after it, any more than we can summon grief by deciding to be sad.

But feelings can be reached indirectly, by attention. If you want to feel about something, you think about it. So the way to break up the heart is to look steadily at the truth about your own life. And by self-examination Finney did not mean peering inward at the state of your emotions. He compared that to a man shutting his eyes and trying to see the image on the back of his own eye. In his Memoirs he confessed that in his early days, when he fasted and examined his own feelings and motives, the day would close with no advance at all, because he had turned his eyes away from Christ. Self-examination, he said, means looking at your life: your actions, your words, your omissions, what you have actually done and left undone.

> Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to the LORD. LAMENTATIONS 3:40

## One by one

Then comes the heart of the lecture. Take your sins, Finney said, one by one. Take a pen and paper and write them down as they come to mind. Go over them “as carefully as a merchant goes over his books.” “General confessions of sin will never do,” he wrote. Your sins were committed one at a time, and so far as you can reach them they should be reviewed and repented of one at a time.

He gave two long lists to work through. Under sins of omission he put ingratitude, want of love to God, neglect of the Bible, unbelief, neglect of prayer, neglect of the means of grace, heartless performance of duty, want of love for the souls of others, neglect of family duties, failure to watch over one’s brethren and lack of self-denial. Under sins of commission he put worldly-mindedness, pride, envy, censoriousness, slander, levity, lying, cheating, hypocrisy, robbing God of time and money, bad temper, and hindering others from being useful.

> Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: and see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting. PSALM 139:23–24

Some of his definitions are sharper than ours. Lying is any designed deception for a selfish reason, by words, looks or actions. Slander need not involve a lie; to tell the truth with the design to injure is slander. Very few readers come through that list unscathed.

## Confess and make it right

Finney did not stop at confession to God. Where you have wronged another person, he said, go and confess it to him at once. If he is too far away, write a letter, and pay the postage, or you will only add to the injury. If you have defrauded anyone, send the money back in full. Confess to God the sins committed against God, and to men the sins committed against men.

> Therefore if thou bring thy gift to the altar, and there rememberest that thy brother hath ought against thee; leave there thy gift before the altar, and go thy way; first be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift. MATTHEW 5:23–24

Then, he said, go over the whole ground a second time, and a third. Each sin you write down will bring others to mind. And as you go, resolve at every point on present and entire reformation. Examining yourself without resolving to amend does no good at all. “You need not expect that God will work a miracle for you to break up your fallow ground,” he wrote. It is to be done by means, and the means are attention, honesty and resolve.

## Where the plough needs guiding

This lecture is strong medicine, and like all strong medicine it needs to be taken with care.

First, it can turn morbid. A tender conscience, set to list every failure in its history, can dig and dig and never find the bottom. Finney meant the exercise to end in relief — in a heart made soft and ready for the seed — but he said much more about the digging than about where the burden goes. The gospel answer is not a longer list. It is the blood of Christ.

> If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. 1 JOHN 1:9

Every sin written on the paper should be carried to the cross and left there. The purpose of the list is not to earn forgiveness by thoroughness but to stop hiding from forgiveness by vagueness.

Second, some of Finney’s particulars belong to his time rather than to Scripture. He counted tea, coffee and tobacco among the hurtful superfluities a devoted Christian should deny himself, and he was sharp about novels and fashion. He was right that self-indulgence dulls the soul, but readers should distinguish his applications from God’s commands, and not bind their consciences, or anyone else’s, where Scripture has not.

Third, beware of turning repentance into a technique for producing revival. Repentance is owed to God because sin is sin, whether or not a revival follows.

## Drive the plough through

With those cautions, the lecture remains a gift. Most of us confess our sins in general and keep them in particular. We admit that we are sinners and avoid admitting what we have actually done. Finney will not let us. Do not balk the ground, he said, as the farmers say; do not turn aside for little difficulties. Drive the plough right through them, beam deep, and turn the ground all up, so that it may be mellow and fit to receive the seed.

> The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise. PSALM 51:17

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Set aside an hour this week with pen and paper. Work slowly through Finney’s two lists and write down specific instances, not general admissions.
2. Take each item you write down to God by name, confess it, and thank Him for the blood of Christ that cleanses it.
3. Identify one person you have wronged. Put it right this week — by a visit, a letter or repayment.
4. Watch for morbid introspection. If the exercise leaves you staring at yourself rather than looking to Christ, stop and read 1 John 1:7–9.
5. When you have finished, destroy the paper. What has been confessed and forgiven is not to be kept.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, my heart has gone hard. Your Word falls on it and runs off, and I have called that stability.
> Search me and show me my sins, not in general but one by one. Give me the honesty to name them and the courage to put right what I have done to others.
> As I confess, let me look to the cross and not only at my list. You are faithful and just to forgive; let me believe it.
> Break up the fallow ground, and then sow Your Word in it, and rain righteousness upon me. Amen.

# Prevailing Prayer

> Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed. The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much. JAMES 5:16

Finney’s fourth lecture on revivals, which Ochorus also carries as a sermon, gave the church a phrase: prevailing prayer. By it he meant prayer that obtains what it asks — not good wishes or a daily recitation, but prayer that takes hold of God for a definite thing and does not let go until the answer comes.

## Two kinds of means

Finney began with a sentence that sums up his whole approach to revival. There are two kinds of means, he said: one to influence men, the other to influence God. “The truth is employed to influence men, and prayer to move God.”

He was careful to explain what he meant. Prayer does not change God’s mind or His character. God was always willing to give the blessing. But prayer produces a change in us and fulfils the conditions on which God has promised to act, so that it becomes fitting for Him to do what it would not have been fitting for Him to do otherwise. As a sinner’s repentance makes it proper for God to forgive, so the church’s prayer makes it proper for God to pour out His Spirit.

> Ye have not, because ye ask not. JAMES 4:2

He insisted that both kinds of means are needed. Some preach and distribute tracts with great zeal and pray little, and wonder at their poor success. Others pray and use no truth. “To expect the conversion of sinners by prayer alone, without the employment of truth, is to tempt God,” he wrote. The two belong together.

## The marks of prevailing prayer

Finney then set out what such prayer requires, and his list is worth working through slowly.

It must be for a definite object. Many Christians, he said, go into their closets because it is time to say their prayers, pray for whatever floats into their minds, and afterwards could not tell you what they asked. Would anyone petition a legislature like that? The mind cannot fasten its desires intensely on many things at once. Prevailing prayer fixes on one thing.

It must be according to the revealed will of God — His promises in Scripture, the plain indications of His providence, or the leading of His Spirit, who helps our infirmities when we do not know what to pray for as we ought.

> Likewise the Spirit also helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought: but the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered. ROMANS 8:26

It must be offered with submission — but Finney would not let submission be confused with indifference. He pointed to David praying and fasting for his sick child. While the child lived, David did not know God’s will, and so he pleaded. When the child died, he rose and washed and ate. That, said Finney, is true submission: acquiescence in the will of God once it is revealed, not a shrug offered in advance to excuse a lukewarm prayer.

It must spring from right motives — from a desire for the glory of God, not merely from natural affection or private comfort. It must be offered in the name of Christ, whose name gives us, as it were, the use of His own credit at the bank of heaven. It must be offered by one who has renounced his sins. It must be persevering. And it must be offered in faith, expecting the thing asked.

> And whatsoever ye shall ask in my name, that will I do, that the Father may be glorified in the Son. JOHN 14:13

## Jacob at the brook

Above all, Finney said, prevailing prayer carries a desire that matches the greatness of what is asked. He pointed to Jacob, alone at the brook Jabbok, wrestling through the night and crying, I will not let thee go, except thou bless me. He pointed to Moses standing in the gap for Israel when God threatened to destroy them. He pointed to Christ in Gethsemane. And he told of an old blacksmith with a stammer who, burdened for his dead church, shut his shop one afternoon to pray; that Sunday he asked the minister to call a meeting, and people from every quarter of the town came under conviction, dating it from the hour he had prayed.

> And he said, Let me go, for the day breaketh. And he said, I will not let thee go, except thou bless me. GENESIS 32:26

Finney was honest that such prayer often affected the body. He had known people pray until they were drenched in sweat in winter, or exhausted with the agony of their minds, and he quoted Jonathan Edwards at length to show that this had been common in earlier revivals. But he also said plainly, “These effects of the Spirit of prayer upon the body are themselves no part of religion.” They were simply what happens when a weak body carries a great burden.

## Where care is needed

This is a searching lecture, and much of it is plain Scripture. But two of Finney’s emphases need a gentler hand.

The first is his claim that “Prayer is not effectual unless it is offered up with an agony of desire.” He went so far as to say that he did not believe in any other kind. Scripture does show us agonising prayer, and we have too little of it. But it also shows us prayer that is short, quiet and heard. Nehemiah, standing before the king with a cup in his hand, prayed to the God of heaven and then answered. The publican beat his breast and said a single sentence, and went down to his house justified. And Jesus warned against supposing that we are heard for our much speaking.

> But when ye pray, use not vain repetitions, as the heathen do: for they think that they shall be heard for their much speaking. MATTHEW 6:7

Intensity is not a price we pay to obtain an answer. God hears because of Christ, not because of our sweat. If Finney’s standard makes you despair of ever praying well enough, remember that the Spirit helps our infirmities and intercedes when we cannot.

The second is his severity about motives. He was right that much prayer is selfish, and right that we should care for the glory of God as well as for the welfare of those we love. But he was hard on the mother who prays for her son because she cannot bear to lose him. Scripture is kinder. The Syrophenician woman prayed for her daughter out of a mother’s anguish, and Jesus granted her request and praised her faith.

## The question that remains

Finney closed by turning to his hearers: Have you confessed your sins and got them out of the way? Can you pray now? Most of us know a great deal about prayer. What we lack is the will to take a definite burden to God and hold on.

> Pray without ceasing. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:17

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Choose one definite object for prayer — a person, a church, a need — and write it down. Pray for it daily this month.
2. Before you pray, ask what God has promised that bears on it. Find a verse and pray it back to Him.
3. Examine your motives. Add to your natural concern a desire that God be glorified in the answer.
4. If there is unconfessed sin between you and God, deal with it first, as Finney urged.
5. If you find you cannot pray with intensity, do not stop praying. Tell God so, and ask the Spirit to help your infirmity.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have prayed at random and wondered why nothing changed. Teach me to pray for definite things, according to Your Word.
> Give me desire that matches the greatness of what I ask, and perseverance to hold on as Jacob held on.
> Yet let me never think I am heard for my agony or my many words. I come in the name of Jesus alone.
> Here is the one thing I bring You today. I will not let You go, except You bless. Amen.

# The Spirit of Prayer

> And he that searcheth the hearts knoweth what is the mind of the Spirit, because he maketh intercession for the saints according to the will of God. ROMANS 8:27

Prevailing prayer, Finney knew, cannot be manufactured. A Christian can resolve to pray definitely and perseveringly, but the deep burden that carries such prayer comes from God. His sixth lecture on revivals, which Ochorus also carries as a separate sermon, is about that gift: the Holy Spirit helping our infirmities and making intercession in us. It is one of the most personal things he wrote, because he had known it himself from the first weeks of his Christian life.

## The burden that would not lift

In his Memoirs Finney tells how, not long after his conversion, the husband of a woman he had once boarded with came into the law office and said that his wife could not live through the night. She was not a Christian. The words, Finney wrote, planted an arrow in his heart. A burden came on him that he could not understand, with an intense desire to pray for her. He went to the meeting-house and tried, but could only groan. He walked back to the office and could not sit still. Three times he went back to pray, and the third time, he said, the Lord gave him power to prevail; he was enabled to roll the burden on God, and received an assurance that she would not die, and would never die in her sins. She recovered, and soon after found hope in Christ. When he described the experience to an older Christian, the man told him that it was the travail of his soul, and showed him the Scriptures that speak of it.

> My little children, of whom I travail in birth again until Christ be formed in you. GALATIANS 4:19

He had the opposite experience too. He was praying for a man in the town, a legislator under deep conviction, when he found his mouth shut. It was as if the Lord said, Speak no more to me of that matter. The next day the man told him he had promised his political friends to put religion off until the session was over. He never returned to it. Finney concluded that the Spirit leads prayer, and sometimes withholds it.

## What the Spirit does

In the lecture Finney was careful and, in the main, sober. The Spirit intercedes for the saints, he said, “Not by superseding the use of our faculties.” He does not put words in our mouths or pray instead of us. “He prays for us, by exciting our own faculties.” He enlightens the mind, sets truth before it, and keeps it there until the heart is moved. He makes the Christian feel the value of souls and the danger of sinners. He opens the promises of Scripture and shows how they apply. He gives discernment in reading the providence of God. And sometimes He leads a believer to desire and pray for a particular blessing about which Scripture says nothing specific — a particular person’s salvation, for example.

> Praying always with all prayer and supplication in the Spirit, and watching thereunto with all perseverance and supplication for all saints. EPHESIANS 6:18

The groanings which cannot be uttered, Finney thought, are desires too great for words — the soul too full to speak, able only to groan before God, who understands.

## Testing the spirits

He knew how dangerous this could be. People are sometimes led away, he admitted, by strange fantasies and crazy impulses. His answer was plain: “Try the spirits by the Bible.” Compare the desires you feel with the spirit and temper of religion as Scripture describes it. The Spirit does not contradict His own Word. And we are not to expect to feel some physical contact with God; the Spirit works through our minds, not around them.

> Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God. 1 JOHN 4:1

This is wise. A burden in prayer that leads to humility, love and faithfulness to Scripture bears the marks of the Spirit. An impulse that leads to pride, novelty or contempt for others does not.

## How it is obtained

How, then, does a Christian come to have the spirit of prayer? Finney’s answer was the same as for everything else: by the means God has appointed. Ask for the Spirit, since the Father has promised to give Him to those who ask. Fix the mind on the things that should move it — the state of the church, the condition of the lost — rather than asking and then turning to worldly things. Watch to see whether God answers. Confess and forsake sin, and do not grieve the Spirit. Aim to obey the whole revealed will of God.

> If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children: how much more shall your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him? LUKE 11:13

He added, with his usual sharpness, that the spirit of prayer is a test of character: the Spirit intercedes for the saints, and those who have never known His help in prayer should ask why.

## The hidden intercessors

Finney never believed that the power of his revivals lay in his own preaching. Again and again in his Memoirs he traced it to people praying out of sight. He wrote of Father Nash, who gave himself to prayer for the places where Finney preached, and of Abel Clary, a minister who, during the Rochester revival, never came to the meetings at all but stayed in a house outside the city, praying day and night in such agony that his host did not know what to make of him. At Adams, soon after his conversion, he proposed that the young converts pray in their closets at sunrise, noon and sunset for a week; he wrote that the spirit of prayer was poured out on them so strongly that some could not stand, and the meetings were soon thronged. Whatever we make of the details, his conviction is plain: revival is carried on the knees of people whose names nobody knows.

## Where care is needed

Two of Finney’s claims in this lecture go too far.

He argued that Paul, when he asked three times for the thorn in the flesh to be removed, did not pray in faith, because he had no promise or leading to warrant it. That is special pleading to protect a theory. Paul was not rebuked. He was answered: My grace is sufficient for thee. Faithful prayer is sometimes answered with something better than we asked.

> And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:9

He also dismissed forms of prayer as absurd and destructive of the spirit of prayer. But the Lord Jesus gave His disciples a form, and the church has prayed the Psalms for three thousand years. Written prayers can be dead; so can extempore ones. The Spirit can breathe through both.

Finally, beware of making the felt burden the only sign of true prayer. Finney himself wrote in his Memoirs that after a deep experience of rest in God in 1843 he no longer had the long, agonising struggles in prayer he had once known, but came to God with more calmness because with more perfect confidence. The spirit of prayer has more than one voice.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask the Father, today, for the Holy Spirit to help you pray. Christ has promised He will give Him.
2. When a person or need weighs on you unexpectedly, do not brush it aside. Stop and pray about it then.
3. Test any strong leading in prayer against Scripture and against the fruit of the Spirit before you act on it.
4. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly once a day this week, letting its form carry your own desires.
5. Think of a ministry you value. Commit to pray for it in secret, as Nash and Clary prayed.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Spirit, I do not know what I should pray for as I ought. Help my infirmity, and pray in me according to the will of God.
> Lay on my heart the burdens You want me to carry, and give me grace not to shake them off.
> Keep me from mistaking my own impulses for Your leading. Bring every desire to the test of Your Word.
> And when You answer, whether with what I asked or with Your sufficient grace, let me rest in Your will. Amen.

# The Prayer of Faith

> Therefore I say unto you, What things soever ye desire, when ye pray, believe that ye receive them, and ye shall have them. MARK 11:24

Finney had first stumbled over prayer as an unbeliever. At Adams he heard church members pray week after week for revival and confess that nothing came. He concluded that they did not pray in faith, in the sense of expecting God to give them what they asked. His fifth lecture on revivals, The Prayer of Faith, is the fruit of that early puzzle, and it is at once one of the most bracing and one of the most troubling things he wrote.

## Believe that ye receive

His text was Christ’s promise in Mark 11. Many had said that it referred only to the faith that works miracles. Finney would have none of it. Read the next verse, he said: when ye stand praying, forgive. Is forgiveness required only of those who work miracles? The promise belongs to all who pray.

> But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering. For he that wavereth is like a wave of the sea driven with the wind and tossed. JAMES 1:6

And what are we to believe? Not merely that God exists, or that He will do something good somewhere. “No, we are to believe that we shall receive the very things that we ask for,” Finney wrote. A father praying for his son is not to suppose that God may convert somebody else’s son instead. God does not give a stone to the child who asks for bread.

## Faith must have evidence

Here Finney made a point that is too often forgotten by those who quote him. Faith, he insisted, always needs evidence. “It is the height of fanaticism to believe without evidence.” We have no right to believe that a thing will be done merely because we want it.

He named five kinds of evidence. The first is a particular promise, as when God promises to give the Holy Spirit to those who ask. Here, said Finney, to pray “if it be thy will” is to insult God. “To put an if into God’s promise, where God has put none, is tantamount to charging God with being insincere.” He told of a young convert, newly come from a wicked home, who was grieved that the old members prayed for the Spirit and then added, if it be Thy will. Her minister argued her down, but could not forget it; he came to see she was right, told his church so, and a revival followed.

The second is a general promise that fairly applies to our case — such as, When the enemy shall come in like a flood, the Spirit of the LORD shall lift up a standard against him (Isaiah 59:19). The third is prophecy, as when Daniel read in Jeremiah that the captivity would last seventy years, and set his face to seek the Lord for its end. The fourth is the plain leading of providence. And the fifth is the Spirit of God stirring strong and holy desire for a particular blessing, which Finney took as an intimation that God meant to grant it.

> In the first year of his reign I Daniel understood by books the number of the years, whereof the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah the prophet, that he would accomplish seventy years in the desolations of Jerusalem. DANIEL 9:2

## How to come to such faith

Finney’s counsel on how to pray in faith is simple and sound. You cannot decide to believe, any more than you can decide to feel. Instead, search the Scriptures for the promises, as Daniel did. Fasten on each one you can use. Cherish the least spark of good desire and do not let it be trifled away. Consecrate yourself wholly to God. Persevere, as Daniel persevered for three weeks. Walk with God daily, and He will show you what to pray for.

> And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us. 1 JOHN 5:14

There is great good in this. Most Christians have promises under their eyes that they have never used. Many pray vaguely because they have never asked what God has actually said. And the habit of tacking “if it be Thy will” onto requests God has plainly promised is a real form of unbelief. Finney was right to call it out.

## Where the lecture overreaches

But the lecture pushes its logic too far, in ways that have wounded tender consciences, and honesty requires us to say so.

First, to protect his principle that the prayer of faith always obtains the very thing asked, Finney reinterpreted the hard cases. Christ in Gethsemane, he argued, was not praying to be spared the cross but to be spared dying of anguish in the garden, and that prayer was answered. Paul, praying three times about the thorn in the flesh, did not pray in faith, since he had no promise to rest on. These readings are strained. Most Christians have seen in Gethsemane the model of perfect submission — Nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done — and in Paul’s thorn the lesson that God sometimes answers by giving grace instead of removal.

> Saying, Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done. LUKE 22:42

Second, and more seriously, Finney laid a crushing burden on praying parents. He taught that there is a promise which godly parents may apply to their children, and when asked why so many praying parents have seen their children die unconverted, he answered in effect that God’s promise cannot fail, so the parents must not have done their duty. In another lecture he said that pious parents can render the salvation of their children certain. He even wrote that millions are in hell because professors have not offered the prayer of faith.

This goes beyond Scripture. God’s promises to the families of His people are real and precious, and parents should plead them. But Scripture never makes a child’s salvation the mechanical result of a parent’s faith. Every soul must turn for itself; the son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, nor the father the iniquity of the son (Ezekiel 18:20), and the same is true of faith. A parent who has prayed long and seen no fruit should not be told that the fault is theirs. They should be told to keep praying, and to trust a God whose ways are higher than ours.

> For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts. ISAIAH 55:9

Third, the fifth kind of evidence — strong desire as a sign of God’s will — is real, but fragile. Finney himself admitted that he once drew a false inference about timing from such an assurance. Treat inward leadings humbly and test them long.

## Take God at His word

When all the qualifications are made, the heart of the lecture stands. Finney was converted by taking a promise at its word in the woods. He spent his life urging the church to do the same. God is not reluctant. He means what He says. Search out His promises, and pray them back to Him expecting to be heard.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Find three promises in Scripture that bear on something you are praying for. Write them out and pray them daily.
2. Notice where you add “if it be Thy will” to requests God has plainly promised, such as wisdom or the Holy Spirit, and stop.
3. Where God has not promised the specific thing, pray boldly but leave the outcome with Him, as Jesus did in Gethsemane.
4. If you are a parent grieving over a child, refuse the false guilt of believing you failed to pray hard enough. Keep praying in hope.
5. Test any inward assurance about an answer by Scripture and by time, holding it loosely.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You have given me exceeding great and precious promises, and I have left most of them unused. Forgive my unbelief.
> Teach me to search Your Word, to fasten on what You have said, and to pray it back to You expecting to receive.
> Where You have not told me Your will, give me bold desire and a submissive heart, like my Lord’s in the garden.
> I bring You those I love who do not know You. I plead Your promises for them, and I trust them to Your mercy. Amen.

# Be Filled with the Spirit

> And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit. EPHESIANS 5:18

After three lectures on prayer, Finney turned to the source of all true prayer and all fruitful work: the Holy Spirit. His seventh lecture on revivals, On Being Filled with the Spirit, which Ochorus also carries as a sermon, takes Paul’s four words and presses them home with characteristic force. Its argument is simple. God commands Christians to be filled with the Spirit. Therefore they can be. Therefore, if they are not, it is their own fault.

## A command and a promise

Finney began by answering an objection he knew well. When preachers stress that nothing good happens without the Spirit, hearers tend to conclude that they can do nothing until the Spirit comes, and so are under no obligation meanwhile. Finney wanted to close that door before he opened any other. The Spirit, he said, is promised to those who ask. And the Spirit is commanded: Be filled. “For God to command, is equivalent to an oath that we can do it.”

> If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him? MATTHEW 7:11

So it is a duty. It is necessary for growth in grace, for holiness, for usefulness, for honouring God. Finney would not let his hearers treat the fulness of the Spirit as a luxury for a spiritual elite. It is the normal Christian life, and its absence is a sin.

## Why so many are empty

The most searching part of the lecture is Finney’s list of reasons why Christians are not filled. Some live hypocritical lives, and he told of a woman who begged her visitors to stay and then complained of them the moment they left. Some are given to levity. Some are proud of dress and position. Some are so worldly that their thoughts are all of getting money; they screw down the poor workman to the last penny and wonder why they have no spiritual life. Some have never fully confessed and forsaken their sins, but confess in general terms, guardedly, as if afraid of saying too much. Some are neglecting a known duty: a man who will not pray with his family, a young man who feels called to the ministry and will not go. Some resist the Spirit whenever a sermon touches them. And some, if they are honest, do not on the whole want the Spirit, because they do not want what He would require.

> And grieve not the holy Spirit of God, whereby ye are sealed unto the day of redemption. EPHESIANS 4:30

That last point is the sharpest. We may desire a thing on some accounts, Finney said, and still not choose it on the whole, like a man who admires an article in a shop but does not like the price.

## What to expect

Finney then told his hearers what would happen if they were filled, and he did not make it sound comfortable. “I never knew a person who was filled with the Spirit, that was not called eccentric,” he wrote. You may be thought deranged, as Festus thought Paul was mad. You will feel great distress over the state of the church and the world, because you will see it as Christ sees it. You will meet opposition, sometimes from church leaders. You will have fierce conflicts with Satan, and even fiercer ones with your own corruptions.

> I am not mad, most noble Festus; but speak forth the words of truth and soberness. ACTS 26:25

But you will have peace with God, flowing like a river, and peace of conscience. You will be useful whether you can go out or not; he told of a poor man, sick for years, whose diary after his death showed that he had set apart hours to pray for about thirty ministers and churches, and recorded, date by date, the churches for which he had been enabled to pray in faith for revival. You will be calm under affliction and unafraid of death. You will not be galled when people speak against you. And you will be wise in the use of means, because the Spirit will teach you how to time and apply the truth.

## Yielding as air

His practical counsel was to be childlike and yielding. If you want the Spirit to remain, Finney said, you must yield to His gentlest motions, just as yielding as air. If He draws you to prayer, leave what you are doing and pray. And do not tempt God by passivity: “Do not tempt God, by waiting for his Spirit, while using no means to procure his presence.”

> Quench not the Spirit. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:19

## Where care is needed

This lecture is a tonic, but several of its emphases need balance.

First, Finney made the fulness of the Spirit sound almost entirely like an achievement of obedience. There is truth in this: we can grieve and quench the Spirit, and we can yield to Him. But Paul’s command is in the passive voice. Be filled. It is something done to us, as wine fills a vessel. The Spirit is a gift before He is a duty. The New Testament speaks of believers being filled again and again, often in answer to prayer and in moments of need, not as the reward for a sufficiently thorough self-reformation.

> And when they had prayed, the place was shaken where they were assembled together; and they were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and they spake the word of God with boldness. ACTS 4:31

Second, his logic that command implies full ability — that God would be a tyrant to command what we cannot do — runs through all his theology, and we will weigh it in chapter ten. Here it is enough to say that Scripture often commands what only grace can supply, and then supplies it. Augustine’s prayer is the right response: give what Thou commandest, and command what Thou wilt.

Third, Finney was careless in places about the difference between the Spirit’s indwelling, which every Christian has, and His fulness, which may ebb and flow. He told his hearers that without the Spirit they would go to hell, and that they would reasonably doubt their salvation. It is true that if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his (Romans 8:9). But a believer who has grieved the Spirit and lost the sense of His fulness is not thereby lost, and should be called back to Christ, not told he was never His.

Fourth, being called eccentric is not proof of spirituality. Some people are simply difficult.

## What to keep

Keep the urgency. Finney was right that the church has made peace with living without the Spirit’s fulness, and that this is a scandal, not a normal condition. Keep the list of hindrances and work through it honestly. And keep the promise underneath the command: the Father gives the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Go through Finney’s list of hindrances slowly. Which one describes you? Confess it specifically.
2. Is there a known duty you have been avoiding? Do it this week, and see whether your prayer life changes.
3. Ask God daily this week to fill you with His Spirit, trusting His promise rather than your feelings.
4. When you feel a gentle prompting to pray or to act in love, obey it at once.
5. If you have lost the sense of God’s presence, do not conclude you were never His. Return to Christ and ask again.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Spirit, You dwell in me, and I have grieved You by pride, worldliness and half-hearted confession. Forgive me.
> I want You on the whole, not only in part. Show me what I have been unwilling to give, and make me willing.
> Fill me, as You filled the first disciples, that I may speak Your word with boldness and live in peace with God.
> Make me yielding as air to Your gentlest leading, and useful wherever You place me. Amen.

# Endued with Power from on High

> And, behold, I send the promise of my Father upon you: but tarry ye in the city of Jerusalem, until ye be endued with power from on high. LUKE 24:49

Finney’s whole ministry rested on an experience. On the evening of his conversion, alone in the law office at Adams, he received what he always called a baptism of the Holy Ghost. He did not expect it. He had never heard of such a thing. And for the rest of his life he was convinced that what happened to him that night was not a private luxury but a necessity for every minister of the gospel. This chapter sets out what he taught about power from on high, mainly from his Memoirs, and weighs it.

## The night in the law office

We have already seen the outline of the story. After meeting Christ, as it seemed to him, face to face in the back room, Finney turned towards the fire. “Without any expectation of it,” he wrote, “without ever having the thought in my mind that there was any such thing for me,” the Spirit came upon him, body and soul, “like a wave of electricity,” in “waves and waves of liquid love.” It seemed like the very breath of God. He wept aloud and cried out that he would die if the waves continued. The next morning the baptism returned with the sunrise, and with it a gentle reproof: Will you doubt? He found that he could not.

> Because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us. ROMANS 5:5

Two things came out of that night that shaped everything after. The first was assurance. In that state, he wrote, he was taught justification by faith “as a present experience”; all sense of condemnation had gone. The second was power. That day every person he spoke to seemed pierced. A few words to his employer went into him like a sword. A young man at a tea table fled to his room and came out the next morning converted. Finney came to believe that it was not his reasoning but the Spirit’s anointing that did it.

## What the baptism is

When Finney came to reflect on the experience, he tied it firmly to Pentecost. Christ told His apostles to tarry at Jerusalem until they were endued with power from on high, and that power came when the Spirit was poured out. Finney was clear that this was not chiefly the power to work miracles. The signs and tongues, he said, were given to attest the apostles’ commission. The baptism itself, in his words, “was a divine purifying, an anointing bestowing on them a divine illumination, filling them with faith, and love, with peace and power.” It made their words sharp in the hearts of God’s enemies.

> But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth. ACTS 1:8

Finney believed that this anointing was the missing thing in the ministry of his day. His own teacher, George Gale, was sincere and able, but had never, as Finney saw it, received “the baptism of the Holy Ghost, which is indispensable to ministerial success.” Gale himself later concluded that he had not even been converted at the time. Finney added a sentence that every preacher should weigh: unless a man “can preach the Gospel as an experience, present religion to mankind as a matter of consciousness, his speculations and theories will come far short of preaching the Gospel.”

## Again and again

Finney did not describe the anointing as a single event, never repeated. In his Memoirs he told of many seasons in which it was renewed. One dawn, standing at the door of the meeting-house at Adams, he saw what seemed a light of the glory of God so intense that it nearly prostrated him, and wept that all nature praised God except man. In the winter of 1843, in Boston, after months of prayer, he received what he called a very thorough overhauling and a fresh baptism of God’s Spirit. He came to speak of a filling that could be renewed, and of a Christian’s need to keep the channel clear between his soul and God.

> And they were all filled with the Holy Ghost, and began to speak with other tongues, as the Spirit gave them utterance. ACTS 2:4

He also learned discretion. As a young Christian, he wrote, his seasons of communion with God often ended with an impression like the words, Go, see that thou tell no man. When he tried to describe them to his brethren they looked surprised and sometimes incredulous, and he learned to say little about them.

## Where care is needed

Finney’s teaching here fed into the holiness movement and, later, Pentecostalism. Much good has come of it, and some confusion.

First, Scripture does not require every Christian’s experience of the Spirit to look like Finney’s. His was vivid, emotional and overwhelming. Others have known the Spirit’s power quietly, as a steady conviction and courage in the work. The test is not the intensity of the experience but the fruit: love for Christ, holiness of life, boldness in witness and the conversion of sinners.

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith. GALATIANS 5:22

Second, the phrase baptism of the Holy Ghost is used in the New Testament chiefly of the Spirit’s coming to the church at Pentecost and to each believer at conversion (1 Corinthians 12:13). Finney used it for a subsequent anointing for service. Many sound Christians use the language of filling instead, and reserve baptism for the Spirit’s initial gift. The difference matters less than the reality both are pointing to, but readers should know that Finney’s vocabulary is contested.

Third, it is easy to turn this teaching into a two-tier Christianity, with anointed believers above and ordinary ones below. Finney was not free of this; he was sometimes sharp about ministers he thought lacked unction. But the Spirit is given to all who belong to Christ, and every believer is to seek to be filled, not to envy or despise others.

Fourth, experiences, however real, are not revelations. Finney’s sense that he met Christ face to face was, he admitted later, a mental state, though a most remarkable one. We should thank God for such seasons without building doctrine on them.

## Tarry until

With all that said, Finney’s central claim is right. The work of God cannot be done without God. Learning, eloquence, method and energy are useless unless the Spirit takes the Word and drives it home. The apostles had spent three years with Jesus, and still they were told to wait. We should not suppose ourselves better equipped than they were.

> Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the LORD of hosts. ZECHARIAH 4:6

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask honestly whether your service for God depends more on your ability than on His Spirit.
2. Pray this week, specifically and persistently, to be endued with power for the witness God has given you.
3. Judge any spiritual experience, your own or others’, by its fruit rather than its intensity.
4. If you have known seasons of great nearness to God, thank Him for them — and be slow to make them a standard for others.
5. If you preach or teach, ask whether you present the gospel only as a theory, or also as something you know.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You told Your disciples to wait for power from on high, and I have tried to work without it. Forgive me.
> Pour out Your Spirit on me, and on all who preach and teach Your Word, that our words may be sharp and living in the hearts of those who hear.
> Give me whatever measure of Your presence You see fit, and keep me from despising those whose experience differs from mine.
> Not by might, nor by power, but by Your Spirit, let Your work be done. Amen.

# Make You a New Heart

> Cast away from you all your transgressions, whereby ye have transgressed; and make you a new heart and a new spirit: for why will ye die, O house of Israel? EZEKIEL 18:31

Of all Finney’s sermons, the one that best explains his theology is Sinners Bound to Change Their Own Hearts, which is in the Ochorus library. It was one of his earliest printed sermons, and it set out plainly the ideas that made him both a great evangelist and a controversial theologian. Its text is the command of Ezekiel: make you a new heart. Its question is simple. If God commands the sinner to make a new heart, can the sinner do it? Finney’s answer was yes. The sermon’s last word on the matter is unforgettable: “God commands you to do it, expects you to do it, and if it ever is done, you must do it.”

## What a new heart is not

Finney began by clearing away what he thought were false ideas. A new heart is not a new physical organ, and not a new soul. It is not the creation of new faculties, or a change in the substance of the mind. We already have all the powers of moral agency, he said; we are just as God made us. Some people, he complained, speak of a change of heart as something miraculous, for which the sinner must wait passively in the use of means, as he would wait for a surgical operation or an electric shock.

If regeneration were the implanting of a new nature, he argued, holiness would be a mere appetite with no moral value, backsliding would be impossible, and the sinner would have an excuse: he could not help his heart, and must simply wait.

## What a new heart is

Positively, Finney defined the heart as the deep governing choice of the soul — that preference which lies behind all its other choices and gives them their character. The sinner’s heart is a settled choice of his own interest above God’s glory. The new heart is the reverse: “a change from selfishness to benevolence,” from making oneself the supreme end to making God and His kingdom the supreme end. It is a change in the choice of a ruler, like a rebel coming back to his king, or a man changing sides in politics.

> Wash you, make you clean; put away the evil of your doings from before mine eyes; cease to do evil; learn to do well. ISAIAH 1:16–17

Such a change is something a person can do, because it is a choice. And it is something he must do, because God commands it. Finney drove the point home with an illustration. Suppose God commanded a man to fly without wings, and then threatened to punish him for not flying. The man could never blame himself; he would throw the command back in his Maker’s face. So if God commands sinners to make a new heart, that is the strongest possible evidence that they are able to do it. A man’s obligation, Finney held, is only commensurate with his ability.

## God, the preacher, the truth and the sinner

How then does this fit with the many passages that say God gives the new heart? Finney answered that Scripture ascribes conversion to four agencies — God, the truth, the preacher and the sinner — and all are true at once. He told of a man walking in a daze towards the brink of Niagara Falls. Someone shouts, Stop! He turns just in time. Afterwards he says, That man saved my life. Then, That word saved my life. Then, If I had not turned I would be dead. Then, O the mercy of God! Each is true. So the Spirit, through the preacher, uses the truth to persuade the sinner, and the sinner turns. The Spirit is the efficient cause; the sinner is the one who acts.

> Turn ye unto me, saith the LORD of hosts, and I will turn unto you. ZECHARIAH 1:3

On this view, the sinner depends on the Spirit — but not for power. “The Spirit’s agency is not needed to give him power, but to overcome his voluntary obstinacy,” Finney wrote. Sinners are so unwilling that without the Spirit none would ever turn. But they are not unable.

## What is right in this

It is important to see what Finney got right. He had sat under preaching that told sinners, in effect, that they could not repent until God changed their nature. That turned the gospel invitation into a riddle and gave the unconverted a comfortable excuse. Scripture never speaks that way. It commands, pleads and warns, and treats the refusal to come as guilt, not misfortune.

> And ye will not come to me, that ye might have life. JOHN 5:40

Jesus did not say ye cannot come, but ye will not. The sinner’s problem is a perverse will, not a lack of equipment. Finney was right that sinners must be told to repent now, that they are responsible for refusing, and that no one should sit under the gospel waiting to be zapped.

## Where it goes wrong

But Finney went a long step further than Scripture, and the church’s best teachers have said so.

He treated a command as proof of ability. Yet Scripture often commands what fallen people cannot, of themselves, perform, in order to show them their need. The same prophet who said make you a new heart also recorded God’s promise to do it: A new heart also will I give you (Ezekiel 36:26). The command shows what is required; the promise shows who supplies it. Augustine answered Pelagius on exactly this point fifteen centuries earlier.

> No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him. JOHN 6:44

He also made the Spirit’s work in conversion essentially persuasion — moral influence by the truth, however powerful. But Scripture describes the natural man as dead in trespasses and sins (Ephesians 2:1), unable to receive the things of the Spirit (1 Corinthians 2:14), and with a mind that is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be (Romans 8:7). The new birth is not only a change of mind persuaded from outside; it is a work of God within, as much a creation as the first.

> Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:17

Jonathan Edwards had already given the church a better way through this problem. He distinguished natural ability, which sinners have — they possess understanding, will and every faculty needed to obey — from moral ability, which they lack, because their hearts are set against God. That distinction lets the preacher press the command with full force, as Finney did, while still honouring God as the One who gives the new heart. Finney borrowed the first half and discarded the second.

The practical danger is subtle. If conversion is simply a change of choice that the sinner can make at will, preaching becomes the art of securing that choice, and a decision is easily mistaken for a new birth.

## Hold both

The right response is not to preach less urgently than Finney did, but to preach with fuller hope. Call sinners to repent now, as he did. Tell them they have no excuse. And pray for them, because only God can give what He commands.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask whether you have ever hidden behind the thought that you could not change, when the truth was that you would not.
2. Read Ezekiel 18:31 and 36:26 side by side. Thank God for both the command and the promise.
3. If you are not yet a Christian, do not wait for a feeling. Turn to Christ today, and ask Him to give you the new heart He commands.
4. If you share the gospel, speak with Finney’s directness — but pray as one who knows that only God gives life.
5. Consider whether your church ever treats a decision as if it were the same as the new birth. How could you guard against that?

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, You command me to make a new heart, and You promise to give me one. I have no excuse for my stubbornness, and no power without Your grace.
> Where I have said I cannot, when the truth was that I would not, forgive me.
> Take away the heart of stone and give me a heart of flesh. Put Your Spirit within me and cause me to walk in Your ways.
> And make me urgent with others, calling them to turn, and praying that You would turn them. Amen.

# Depravity and the Will

> Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me. PSALM 51:5

This is the hardest chapter in the book, and the most necessary. Finney’s preaching drew its force from a particular view of human nature, and that view departed, knowingly and deliberately, from the teaching of the Reformers and of the great confessions of the church. He said so himself. Anyone who reads Finney with profit needs to understand what he rejected, why he rejected it, and why most careful readers since have concluded that he went badly wrong.

## The quarrel at Adams

The story begins in the study of George Gale, the Princeton-trained pastor who supervised Finney’s theological training. In his Memoirs Finney set out Gale’s position as he understood it, and it was the strict Calvinism of the day. The guilt of Adam’s first sin is imputed to all his descendants. All receive from Adam, by natural generation, a nature wholly sinful in every faculty, so that they cannot perform any act acceptable to God. They are condemned for Adam’s sin, for their sinful nature and for their own transgressions. Regeneration must therefore be a change of nature, wrought directly by the Spirit on the substance of the soul, in which the sinner is passive.

Finney would have none of it. “I could not receive that theological fiction of imputation,” he wrote. He thought it unreasonable that anyone should be guilty for another’s act or for a nature he did not choose. And he saw what it did in the pulpit. Gale, he said, could never preach repentance without leaving the impression that his people could not repent, nor call them to believe without telling them that faith was impossible until their nature was changed. His orthodoxy, Finney wrote, was a perfect snare to himself and his hearers.

## What Finney put in its place

It is important to be accurate. Finney did not deny that all people are sinners. Among the doctrines he said he preached everywhere was “The total moral, voluntary depravity of unregenerate man.” Everyone sins, and every unconverted person is wholly given over to selfishness. But the depravity is moral and voluntary: it consists in choices, not in nature.

> For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God. ROMANS 3:23

Where then does this universal selfishness come from? In Sinners Bound to Change Their Own Hearts Finney answered that the sinful preference is not created in the child. It arises from temptation to selfishness in the circumstances into which every child is born, as its appetites clamour for gratification before its reason is formed. “Sinners make their own wicked hearts,” he wrote. In a later lecture, Growth in Grace, he allowed that the feelings and appetites of human beings are in a fallen, disordered state, which he called a physical depravity of the sensibility; but because it is involuntary, he held that it carries no guilt. Sin, strictly, is only the will’s consent.

It follows that Adam at his creation had no moral character until he chose; that infants, before they choose, are not guilty; that the new birth is a change of the will’s supreme choice, not of nature; and that the Spirit’s work is to persuade, not to create. It also follows that the imputation of Christ’s righteousness to the believer, the other side of Gale’s scheme, was likewise a fiction to Finney. And among the doctrines he listed as his own was “persistence in holiness as a condition of salvation.”

## Why this matters

Finney’s critics were not merely defending a system. They believed Scripture teaches what he denied.

It teaches that sin is not only in what we do but in what we are. David confessed not only his acts but that he was shapen in iniquity. Jesus said that out of the heart proceed evil thoughts; the actions spring from a corrupt source. Paul said we were by nature the children of wrath (Ephesians 2:3). The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked (Jeremiah 17:9).

> For from within, out of the heart of men, proceed evil thoughts, adulteries, fornications, murders. MARK 7:21

It teaches, too, that Adam’s sin had consequences for the whole race that are more than bad example. In Romans 5, Paul sets Adam and Christ side by side as two heads. By one man’s disobedience many were made sinners; by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous. If we deny that we were made sinners in the first Adam, it is hard to see how we are made righteous in the last.

> For as by one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous. ROMANS 5:19

This is the heart of the matter. The Reformers did not insist on imputation out of love for difficult ideas. They insisted on it because the gospel depends on it. The believer is justified not because his own will has turned, but because Christ’s righteousness is counted as his. When Finney called imputation a fiction, he weakened the ground on which a sinner stands before God, and made his standing depend, in the end, on his own persistence in holiness. Many who sat under that teaching found it more a burden than a gospel.

## The charge of Pelagianism

Finney’s critics often called his theology Pelagian, after the fifth-century monk who taught that human beings are born without inherited corruption and can obey God if they will. Charles Hodge of Princeton wrote a long review of Finney’s Systematic Theology from that standpoint, and Finney replied. The charge needs care. Finney affirmed that all people in fact sin, that none will turn without the Spirit, and that salvation is through Christ’s atonement, and he would have been indignant at being classed with Pelagius. But on the questions of original sin, human ability and the nature of grace he stood much closer to Pelagius than to Augustine, and many sound judges have concluded that his system leans decidedly in that direction.

## What to do with this

What then should a reader do with Finney here?

First, take his warning. There is a way of teaching human inability that makes sinners comfortable and preachers timid, and Finney saw it clearly. Scripture never lets anyone off the hook because of the corruption of his nature. The sinner is responsible, and must be told so.

Second, refuse his remedy. The answer to a distorted doctrine of original sin is not to deny original sin, but to teach it as Scripture does: that we are born corrupt and guilty, that we freely and wholeheartedly sin, that we are wholly responsible, and that only a sovereign work of grace can make us new.

> Who can bring a clean thing out of an unclean? not one. JOB 14:4

Third, read the rest of Finney with this in mind. His practical counsel on prayer, repentance and holiness is often wiser than his theory. But where his theory drives his practice — in his confidence about decisions, his view of the Spirit’s work, and his teaching on sanctification — weigh it carefully.

> But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us, even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ. EPHESIANS 2:4–5

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read Romans 5:12–21 slowly. Note every place where Paul links what Adam did with what Christ did.
2. Ask whether you think of your sin only as things you do, or also as a corruption you need to be rescued from.
3. Consider where your confidence before God rests: on your own continued faithfulness, or on Christ’s righteousness counted as yours.
4. If you teach, ask whether your doctrine of sin ever becomes an excuse. Press responsibility and grace together.
5. Give thanks that God quickened you when you were dead, and not merely when you were willing.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Father, I was shapen in iniquity, and I have added to it by my own choices. I have no righteousness of my own to plead.
> Thank You that as by one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of One many are made righteous.
> Keep me from excusing myself because of my nature, and from trusting myself because of my will. Teach me to rest wholly on Christ.
> You made me alive when I was dead. Let me never forget it, and let me live to Your praise. Amen.

# Sin, the Most Expensive Thing

> For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. JOHN 3:16

Finney’s sermon God’s Love for a Sinning World, which is in the Ochorus library, opens with a sentence few who read it forget: “Sin is the most expensive thing in the universe.” Pardoned or unpardoned, he went on, its cost is infinitely great. If pardoned, the cost falls on the great atoning Substitute; if unpardoned, on the guilty sinner. The sermon is full of warmth, and it shows Finney at his most tender. It also shows his distinctive view of the atonement, which needs to be understood and weighed.

## A salvation open to every sinner

Finney’s first conviction about the cross was that it was for everyone. His first serious theological argument, as a new convert, was with George Gale, who held that Christ died only for the elect. Finney could not see it in his Bible. If the gospel commands all men to repent and believe, how can they believe in a salvation not provided for them? In God’s Love for a Sinning World he put it with typical directness. If the atonement had not been made for the entire race, no one could know it was made for him. As it is, the fact that a man is human, born of woman, is enough; it brings him within the pale.

> And he is the propitiation for our sins: and not for ours only, but also for the sins of the whole world. 1 JOHN 2:2

He loved to picture it. When the Broadway Tabernacle was built, he told the workmen to hang the doors so that they would swing open and fasten themselves open, so that they could not swing shut on the crowds. So, he said, the door of salvation stands always open, fastened back, and no one — not the pope, nor the devil, nor any angel — can shut it. There is no need, he said, for any perhaps.

Whatever one believes about the precise design of the atonement, every preacher should sound that note. Christ is freely offered to every sinner, and none who comes is turned away.

> Him that cometh to me I will in no wise cast out. JOHN 6:37

## The love that gave

The sermon lingers on the nature of God’s love. It is not complacency in a lovely race, for the race was not lovely. It is not blind emotion. It is the love of a father for a rebellious, prodigal child: disinterested, zealous, self-denying, patient. Finney pictured the Father heading the subscription for the world’s redemption with the gift of His only Son, and all heaven following. He spoke of God’s amazing patience, setting Himself to weep sinners down, and bleed them down, and die them down by the death of His Son in their stead.

> But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. ROMANS 5:8

There is real beauty here, and real truth. The cross is the measure of God’s love and of sin’s cost.

## Upholding the law

But what exactly did Christ’s death accomplish? Here Finney took a particular line. God rules the universe, he taught, by moral law, and the law is essential to the happiness of all His creatures. When sin dishonours the law, God cannot simply overlook it, or every creature would learn that His law does not matter. Either the penalty must be executed, or something else must be done that honours the law as much. Christ’s death is that something else. “The Son of God died not to appease vengeance, as some seem to understand it, but under the demands of law,” Finney wrote. It showed the universe how seriously God takes His law, and so made it safe for Him to pardon.

In his Memoirs he was more explicit. In lectures against a Universalist preacher at Adams, he argued that Christ did not pay the literal debt of sinners or suffer just what they deserved. Instead, he said, “Christ had only satisfied public justice,” by honouring the law in His obedience and death, so that it became possible for God to proclaim a universal amnesty. The atonement, on this view, made salvation possible for all without of itself securing it for anyone.

This is known as the governmental view of the atonement, and it goes back to the seventeenth-century Dutch jurist Hugo Grotius. It was widely held among the New England theologians of Finney’s day.

## What is right and what is missing

Finney was right about several things. God’s justice matters, and the cross vindicates it; Paul says God set forth Christ to declare his righteousness … that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus (Romans 3:26). The atonement is not the appeasing of a vindictive temper in God; it flows from the Father’s love. And Finney was right to reject any account that made the cross a mere commercial transaction.

> Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed. 1 PETER 2:24

But the governmental view leaves out what Scripture puts at the centre: that Christ bore our sins, in our place, and suffered the curse that was ours. Isaiah says the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all. Paul says God made Him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us. These are not pictures of a demonstration staged for the watching universe. They are pictures of a substitute taking the penalty of the guilty.

> But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. ISAIAH 53:5

This is not a quarrel about words. It touches the believer’s assurance. If Christ only made salvation possible, then what makes it actual is the sinner’s continuing response, and Finney did in fact teach that persistence in holiness is a condition of salvation. If Christ bore my sins and His righteousness is counted as mine, then my standing rests on His finished work. Finney, strikingly, experienced exactly this at his conversion. Walking to his office he saw, he wrote, the reality and fulness of the atonement: that Christ’s work was a finished work, and that he had only to submit to the righteousness of God through Christ. His heart, in that moment, was sounder than his later theory.

> For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:21

## What to carry away

Carry away the open door. Finney was right that the gospel is for every sinner and that no one who comes will be refused. Carry away the cost. Sin is the most expensive thing in the universe; it cost the Son of God His life, and it should cost us our love of it. And carry away the substitute. Christ did not only honour the law in general. He loved me, and gave Himself for me.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think about the phrase “the most expensive thing in the universe.” What would change if you saw your favourite sin that way?
2. Read Isaiah 53 slowly, noting every phrase in which the Servant bears what belongs to others.
3. Ask where your assurance rests: on your continued response, or on Christ’s finished work.
4. If you share the gospel, make sure people hear both that the door is open to them and that Christ died in their place.
5. Thank God specifically today that Christ bore your sins in His own body on the tree.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You so loved the world that You gave Your only Son. I cannot measure what my sin has cost You.
> Thank You that the door of salvation stands open, and that no one who comes is cast out.
> Lord Jesus, You were wounded for my transgressions and bruised for my iniquities. You bore what I deserved. I rest on You alone.
> Make me hate the sin that cost You so much, and love You who paid for it. Amen.

# What Must I Do to Be Saved?

> And brought them out, and said, Sirs, what must I do to be saved? And they said, Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved, and thy house. ACTS 16:30–31

Finney spent much of his life in rooms full of anxious people. In inquiry meetings, on the anxious seat, in his lodgings late at night, he talked with thousands of men and women who had been awakened and did not know what to do. Two of his Lectures on Revivals, False Comforts for Sinners and Directions to Sinners, gather what he learned. They are among his most practical and his most searching, and anyone who ever speaks to a seeking soul should read them.

## The most important question

No more important question was ever asked, Finney said, than the jailer’s at Philippi. People may give many answers to what shall I eat or what shall I wear, with little harm done. But when a sinner asks in earnest what he must do to be saved, it is of infinite importance that he receive the right answer. And Finney laid down one test for every answer: no direction should be given which, if the sinner followed it, would leave him unconverted. If he would not go to heaven should he die the next moment after doing it, it is the wrong direction.

> Behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation. 2 CORINTHIANS 6:2

That rules out much well-meant counsel — read your Bible, come to meetings, keep seeking. Good in their place, none of them is conversion, and a sinner may do them for years and remain in his sins.

## False comforts

Finney was merciless about what he called false comforts. He listed more than twenty. Telling an anxious sinner, You are not so bad. Telling him conversion is a gradual work. Advising him to put the subject out of his mind for now. Telling him to wait God’s time. Telling him that God is trying his faith in the furnace, as if God were the one delaying. Telling him, I was under conviction for months myself, so do not be discouraged. Telling him, I will pray for you, so that he leans on your prayers instead of submitting to God. Saying, Do what you can, and God will do the rest. Applying to him promises meant only for believers. Assuring him that the good work begun in him will be carried on.

> They have healed also the hurt of the daughter of my people slightly, saying, Peace, peace; when there is no peace. JEREMIAH 6:14

Each of these, Finney argued, gives the sinner a place to rest short of Christ. Out of false pity we relieve his distress, and he goes away comforted and unsaved. It is, he said, like a surgeon who cannot bear to cut.

## True directions

What then should the sinner be told? Finney gave several answers, and said any of them would do, provided the sinner understood and obeyed it. Repent — and he explained that repentance is a change of mind towards God and sin, involving hatred of sin and forsaking it. Believe the gospel — and he explained that faith is not mere assent, which the devil has, but trust. “It is a voluntary trust in his person, his veracity, his word,” he wrote of our trust in Christ. Give God your heart. Submit to God, like a rebel laying down his arms. Confess and forsake your sins, making restitution where you can. Choose this day whom you will serve.

> Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you. JAMES 4:7–8

Whichever of these the Spirit is pressing, Finney said, when the sinner yields at that point he is converted. There is no fixed order of experiences. Sometimes faith comes first, sometimes repentance, sometimes submission. The one thing needful is that the heart yields to God.

## Errors of the anxious

Finney also named the errors anxious sinners fall into. They think they must make themselves better before they come. They think they must suffer long under conviction as a kind of penance. They wait for different feelings. To that he answered: “It is not a question of feeling, but of willing and acting.” Obey, submit, trust, and the feelings will follow. They think they must wait until their hearts are changed, when the change of heart is the very thing required. They say they are willing but God is not — which is to say that God does not want to be loved. And they confuse desiring with willing. Many desire to be Christians who have never chosen to be.

> Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. MATTHEW 11:28

He was not afraid of sudden conversions. Some of the best Christians he knew, he said, had been convicted and converted in a few minutes. “Afraid of sudden conversions!” he exclaimed. The Bible gives no warrant for the idea that long, drawn-out distress is a better sign. In his experience, protracted conviction usually meant defective instruction.

## Where care is needed

There is gold in these lectures, but Finney’s theology left its mark on them, and they need to be read with Scripture open.

He objected to praying that God would enable sinners to repent, because it suggested that they were trying and could not. But Scripture itself speaks of God granting repentance. Paul told Timothy to instruct opponents in meekness, if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth (2 Timothy 2:25). The church at Jerusalem glorified God, saying, Then hath God also to the Gentiles granted repentance unto life.

> When they heard these things, they held their peace, and glorified God, saying, Then hath God also to the Gentiles granted repentance unto life. ACTS 11:18

We should indeed tell sinners to repent now, and refuse to let them hide behind their inability. But we may and should also pray for God to give them repentance. Finney’s scheme made these seem to be in tension. Scripture holds them together.

His method also put great weight on the counsellor’s skill in hunting out the sinner’s hiding-place and pressing him to yield. That can be done with great wisdom and love. It can also become pressure. A soul that yields because it has been cornered by argument or emotion has not necessarily been born again. Finney knew this, and warned against false hopes, but some who copied his methods forgot it.

And the insistence that feelings do not matter, while true as far as it goes, needs balance. The will does lead; but conversion is also the heart being won by the love of Christ. The jailer did not merely decide; he rejoiced, believing in God with all his house.

## Tell them plainly

Finney’s lasting gift here is clarity. He had no patience with religious fog. When a sinner asks what to do, tell him plainly: turn from your sin and trust Christ, now. Do not give him anything to do instead of that.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. If you are not sure you are a Christian, stop reading and do what the jailer was told: believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, now.
2. Think of the last time someone asked you about faith. Did your answer point them to Christ, or to something short of Him?
3. Go through Finney’s list of false comforts and notice any you have given. Resolve to speak more truly next time.
4. Pray this week, by name, for someone you know who is not a Christian, asking God to grant them repentance — and look for an opportunity to speak plainly.
5. If you are waiting for a feeling before obeying God in some matter, obey first.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You are the answer to the jailer’s question and to mine. I believe on You, and I turn from my sins to You.
> Forgive me for the false comforts I have given to others, and for the ones I have taken for myself.
> Give me clarity and courage to tell those who ask what they must do, and love to tell them gently.
> And grant repentance unto life to those I love who are still far off. Amen.

# Confess and Forsake

> He that covereth his sins shall not prosper: but whoso confesseth and forsaketh them shall have mercy. PROVERBS 28:13

When Finney first sat under George Gale’s preaching at Adams, one of the questions that troubled him most was what the word repentance meant. Was it a feeling of sorrow for sin? Was it something that happened to a person, or something a person did? He could not tell, he wrote in his Memoirs, and he did not think his pastor could either. Much of his later preaching was an attempt to answer that question clearly, for sinners and for backslidden Christians alike. His answer was simple and demanding: repentance is a change of mind towards God and sin that shows itself in confessing and forsaking.

## What repentance is not

Finney began, as he often did, by clearing away false ideas. In his lecture Directions to Sinners he listed several. Remorse is not repentance. “Many suppose that remorse, or a sense of guilt, is repentance. Then hell is full of repentance, for it is full of remorse, unutterable and eternal.” Regret at the consequences of sin is not repentance; a thief may regret being caught. Conviction and fear of hell are not repentance. The protests of conscience are not repentance. Even a feeling of sorrow for sin, taken by itself, is not repentance. The devil, Finney said, might have all these things and remain a devil.

> For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death. 2 CORINTHIANS 7:10

## What repentance is

Repentance, he said, is a change of mind regarding God and sin. Not only a change of opinion, but a change of the soul’s deepest choice, which then carries feeling and conduct with it. Everyone understands this in ordinary life. When we hear that a man has changed his mind about a great public question, we expect to see it in how he talks and votes and spends his time. So, Finney wrote, “Evangelical repentance is a change of willing, of feeling, and of life, in respect to God.”

He added two things. “Repentance always implies abhorrence of sin.” The penitent comes to feel about sin as God does. And repentance always implies forsaking sin. A person who confesses a sin and fully intends to go on doing it has not repented at all.

> Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts: and let him return unto the LORD, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon. ISAIAH 55:7

This, Finney noted, changes how the unconverted imagine the Christian life. They suppose that if they become religious they will spend their lives longing for the pleasures they have given up. But repentance is a change of mind about those very pleasures. The one who has truly repented does not look back on them as treasures lost.

## Instantly and wholly

Finney would not allow repentance to be spread out over years. In his later lecture Growth in Grace he wrote that the command to grow in grace does not mean giving up sin gradually: “we are nowhere in the Bible commanded to give up sin gradually, we are everywhere commanded to give it up instantly and wholly.” The call is always now. Behind this lay his whole view of the will. If repentance is a choice, it can be made at once, and delay is simply continued refusal.

## Making it right

Finney’s most practical contribution on repentance is his insistence on restitution. “A man does not forsake his sins till he has made all the reparation in his power,” he wrote. If he has stolen, he must return what he took. If he has cheated, he must repay. If he has slandered, he must go to the people who heard the slander and set it right. It is not enough to resolve to do better from now on. We saw in chapter four that he told his hearers to write letters, and pay the postage, to those they had wronged far away.

> And Zacchaeus stood, and said unto the Lord; Behold, Lord, the half of my goods I give to the poor; and if I have taken any thing from any man by false accusation, I restore him fourfold. LUKE 19:8

He applied the same principle to God. Every sinner has robbed God of time, talents and service. A man who has made money while ignoring God and then leaves it to his heirs as if it were his own, Finney said, has never confessed his sin or acknowledged himself God’s steward. Real repentance hands everything back.

## Repentance for Christians

Finney did not think repentance was only for the unconverted. Revival, as we have seen, begins with Christians coming under conviction of their own sins. In his lecture on the hindrances to revival he said that a revival will decline and cease unless Christians are frequently re-converted — by which he meant that believers need again and again to be convicted, humbled and broken down before God. The Christian life, for Finney, was a life of continual turning.

> I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes. JOB 42:5–6

## Where care is needed

Finney’s teaching on repentance is bracing and mostly biblical, but two points need balance.

First, he treated repentance almost entirely as a voluntary act, and dismissed sorrow as merely an involuntary feeling. He was right that sorrow alone is not enough. But Scripture does not despise godly sorrow; it says that such sorrow works repentance. The tears of Peter after his denial were not a distraction from repentance. The heart is to be broken, not only the will redirected. And Scripture also speaks of repentance as God’s gift: Him hath God exalted with his right hand to be a Prince and a Saviour, for to give repentance to Israel, and forgiveness of sins (Acts 5:31). We are commanded to repent, and we must; yet when we do, we should thank God for granting it.

Second, Finney sometimes spoke as though a true penitent would have no remaining desire for his old sins, and that anyone who still felt the pull of them had never repented. That is too severe. Paul described a believer’s continuing struggle: the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh (Galatians 5:17). Finney himself, in another lecture, spoke of spiritual Christians groaning in conflict with their own corruptions. The question is not whether sin still tempts us, but which side we are on.

> O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord. ROMANS 7:24–25

## Keep short accounts

The heart of Finney’s teaching is simple: do not cover sin; confess it, forsake it, and make it right. Do not settle for remorse or regret. Turn. And keep turning, as often as you find that you have wandered.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name one sin you have regretted without forsaking. What would forsaking it actually involve this week?
2. Is there anything you have taken, damaged or said that you need to put right? Make restitution, as far as it is in your power.
3. Ask whether you treat what you own as yours or as God’s. Make one decision this week as a steward rather than an owner.
4. If you still feel the pull of an old sin, do not conclude you were never converted. Bring it to Christ and choose His side again.
5. Thank God that repentance is both your duty and His gift.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, I have covered my sins and not prospered. I confess them now, and I choose to forsake them.
> Give me not only regret but a change of mind — to hate what You hate, and love what You love.
> Show me whom I have wronged, and give me courage to make it right.
> And when I find myself wandering again, turn me again, for You abundantly pardon. Amen.

# He That Winneth Souls Is Wise

> The fruit of the righteous is a tree of life; and he that winneth souls is wise. PROVERBS 11:30

Finney believed that every Christian is called to win souls, and that it is a skill to be learned. Three of his Lectures on Revivals — Means to be Used with Sinners, To Win Souls Requires Wisdom and A Wise Minister will be Successful — form a small handbook on the subject. “It is the great business on earth of every Christian, to save souls,” he wrote. And because it is a business, it can be done well or badly, wisely or foolishly. Much of what he said is timeless counsel. Some of it needs a careful second look.

## Ye are my witnesses

Finney started not with technique but with life. His text for the first of these lectures was God’s word through Isaiah: Ye are my witnesses. Christians, he said, are to testify to certain great truths: the immortality of the soul, the emptiness of worldly things, the satisfying fulness of Christ, the guilt and danger of sinners, the love of Christ, the necessity of holiness and self-denial. And they are to testify chiefly by how they live.

> Ye are my witnesses, saith the LORD, and my servant whom I have chosen. ISAIAH 43:10

This was blunt. If you live as if this world were everything, he said, your words about eternity will be disbelieved. If you are irritable, your talk of Christ’s meekness will be laughed at. If you cut corners in business, you testify that religion makes no difference. The carelessness of sinners, Finney concluded, is not surprising when the church’s daily testimony so often contradicts its creed. Every Christian makes an impression, for one side or the other, all day long.

## Speaking to the careless

In the second lecture Finney turned to what we would call personal evangelism, and here he was at his most practical. With careless sinners, he said, choose your time. Speak to a man when he is not absorbed in business or roused by some other excitement. Make sure he is sober. Take him when he is in a good temper. Speak to him alone, not in front of others, because most people are too proud to be spoken to freely in company. Use the openings providence gives. Do not put it off. And if you feel a particular concern for someone, act on it while it lasts.

> Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt, that ye may know how ye ought to answer every man. COLOSSIANS 4:6

As for manner: be kind, so that he sees you speak because you love his soul. Be solemn, not flippant. Be respectful; there is no virtue in being coarse. Be plain, and do not hide his real condition from him. Address his conscience, not just his feelings. Keep to the great truths, and do not be drawn into side arguments about denominations. Be patient with real difficulties, but do not argue with mere cavils. Guard your temper. Bring up his particular sins, not sin in general. Be brief. And pray with him before you leave.

## The awakened and the convicted

Finney distinguished between people who are merely awakened, stirred a little by the subject of religion, and those who are truly convicted, who see their guilt before God’s law. The awakened need to have the truth pressed further; the convicted need to be led straight to Christ. With the convicted, he said, there is usually some particular point on which the person is resisting God. Find it, and press there. Do not make any compromise on it, and do not divert his attention elsewhere, or you may destroy his convictions.

> Brethren, if any of you do err from the truth, and one convert him; let him know, that he which converteth the sinner from the error of his way shall save a soul from death, and shall hide a multitude of sins. JAMES 5:19–20

He was fierce with Christians who let an awakened relative live under their roof for weeks without saying a word, on the pretext that the Spirit had begun the work and would finish it.

## The wise minister

The third lecture turned to ministers. A right discharge of the ministry, Finney said, requires great wisdom: to wake up the church, set it to work and keep it there; to understand the gospel and divide it rightly; to reach different kinds of hearers; to drive sinners out of their refuges of lies without giving them new ones; to know what measures to use, and when to stop using them. He had hard words for theological education that shut young men up with books and away from ordinary people, and he insisted that a minister who wins souls is well educated in the sense that matters, whatever his Latin.

> And he that reapeth receiveth wages, and gathereth fruit unto life eternal: that both he that soweth and he that reapeth may rejoice together. JOHN 4:36

## Where care is needed

It is in this third lecture that Finney’s method shows its weakness most clearly. He argued that the amount of a minister’s success in winning souls, other things being equal, decides the amount of wisdom he has used. He went further: the success of any measure demonstrates its wisdom, with a few exceptions, and when blessing follows a measure the proof is unanswerable.

That is a dangerous rule. Scripture does not measure faithfulness by visible results. Noah preached righteousness for many years and saw his own family saved. Jeremiah was faithful and was thrown into a pit. The Lord Jesus, at the end of His earthly ministry, was abandoned by nearly all. And some who drew great crowds and counted many converts were later shown to have built with wood, hay and stubble.

> Moreover it is required in stewards, that a man be found faithful. 1 CORINTHIANS 4:2

Finney added his own exceptions, and they are telling. A measure may produce excitement and later look like a mere trick. A revival may be credited to a measure when in fact the prayers of Christians and the preaching of the truth were doing the work, and the measure was a hindrance. Those exceptions swallow much of the rule. Visible success is a poor guide to wisdom unless it is tested over years by the lives of the converts.

There is also a danger in the whole idea of evangelism as a skill that can be mastered. Finney was right that we should learn to speak well. But the wisest words fail without the Spirit, and the clumsiest witness is sometimes greatly used. Paul, who planned his ministry carefully, also said that he came not with excellency of speech, lest faith should stand in the wisdom of men.

## Learn, and depend

The balance is to take Finney’s counsel seriously and hold it humbly. Learn how to speak to people about Christ. Choose the time; be kind, plain, patient and brief; pray with them. Live so that your words are believed. And then leave the results to God, who alone gives the increase, and who will judge your faithfulness, not your numbers.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask what your daily life is testifying to about eternity, Christ and sin. What would a watching neighbour conclude?
2. Think of one person you know who does not know Christ. Pray for them, and look for a good time — alone, unhurried — to speak with them.
3. When you speak, try Finney’s rules: kind, solemn, respectful, plain, brief, and ending in prayer.
4. If you lead or preach, ask whether you are measuring your ministry by numbers rather than faithfulness.
5. Read Lecture X, To Win Souls Requires Wisdom, in the Ochorus library, and note three counsels to practise.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You came to seek and to save the lost, and You have made me Your witness. Forgive me for the times my life has spoken against my words.
> Give me wisdom to know when and how to speak, and love that makes my words believable.
> Keep me from measuring Your work by what I can count. Make me faithful, and give the increase as You will.
> And use me, clumsy as I am, to win someone for You. Amen.

# How to Preach the Gospel

> For if the trumpet give an uncertain sound, who shall prepare himself to the battle? 1 CORINTHIANS 14:8

Finney was a lawyer before he was a preacher, and he never stopped preaching like one. He spoke without a manuscript, from a brief outline small enough, according to the man who reported his lectures, to fit on one side of a card. He argued his case, anticipated objections, repeated his main points, used homely illustrations, and pressed for a verdict before his hearers left the room. His twelfth lecture on revivals, How to Preach the Gospel, explains why. It is one of the most useful things ever written for preachers, and one of the most provocative.

## Doctrinal and practical

Finney began with the matter of preaching. “The proper end of all doctrine is practice,” he wrote. Anything preached as doctrine that cannot be put to use is not preaching the gospel. But he would not let people despise doctrine either. If a man preaches no doctrine, he preaches no gospel. “All preaching should be doctrinal, and all preaching should be practical.”

> All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness: that the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works. 2 TIMOTHY 3:16–17

Preaching, he went on, must be direct. “The Gospel should be preached to men, and not about them.” Many preachers seemed afraid of making anyone feel they were meant. Finney wanted each hearer to go home convinced that the sermon had been aimed at him. He wanted the preacher to know the opinions of everyone in his congregation, so that he could hunt them out of their hiding-places. And he wanted the sermon to aim at conversion, not merely at instruction or admiration.

## Present obligation

The heart of Finney’s counsel is what he called present obligation. He had talked with thousands of anxious sinners, he said, and found that most had never felt that they were expected to repent now. Preachers left the impression that repentance was something to wait for. “Religion is something to do, not something to wait for,” he wrote. Sinners must be made to feel that they have something to do, that no one can do it for them, that they can do it, and that they must do it now.

> To day if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts. HEBREWS 3:15

He was equally insistent that sinners be made to feel their guilt, not merely their misfortune. He criticised popular religious books of his day for making the sinner think more of his sorrows than of his sins. Until a person blames and condemns himself, he said, the gospel will not take effect.

## Proportion

Finney’s counsel on the balance of truths is wiser than his reputation suggests. A minister, he said, must dwell on the truths his people most need. When he entered the ministry, he found that the doctrines of election and divine sovereignty had become the universal hiding-place of sinners and churches, so he pressed human ability and responsibility. But he acknowledged that in Edwards’s and Whitefield’s day the churches had been resting in their own strength, and those men were rightly blessed in preaching sovereignty and election. And he warned his fellow revivalists that if they dwelt exclusively on ability they would drive their hearers back into self-reliance. “A right view of both classes of truths, election and free-agency, will do no hurt,” he wrote.

> For I have not shunned to declare unto you all the counsel of God. ACTS 20:27

## The manner of preaching

Then came manner, and here Finney’s advice is as fresh as ever. Preaching should be conversational, he said. The minister should speak as a lawyer speaks to a jury he wants to convince, not in a lofty pulpit tone. It should be in the language of common life, as Christ’s was; anyone who fills a sermon with Latin and Greek phrases to seem learned is, in Paul’s word, a barbarian to his hearers. It should be full of illustrations drawn from ordinary life — hens and chickens, merchants and farmers — as Christ’s parables were. It should be repetitious; an eminent advocate had told him that in addressing a jury he expected to repeat anything important two or three times, sometimes as often as there were jurymen. It should not be monotonous. It should anticipate and answer objections, as any lawyer does. And the preacher should feel what he says, and then his manner will follow.

> And the common people heard him gladly. MARK 12:37

His summary of the preacher’s strategy is worth memorising: “A minister should address the feelings enough to secure attention, and then deal with the conscience, and probe to the quick.” Appeals to feeling alone, he warned, produce waves of excitement and false hopes. Only faithful dealing with the conscience produces sound conversions.

## Where care is needed

Finney’s counsel is excellent, but a few emphases need tempering.

He argued that the gospel could never take general effect without a class of extempore preachers, and was scornful of written sermons. Yet Jonathan Edwards read his sermons, and God used them mightily. The issue is not paper but whether the preacher is speaking to real people about their real condition. Many who preach without notes ramble; many who use them preach with fire.

He sometimes spoke as if effective preaching were chiefly a matter of technique, adapting means to ends. But Paul reminds us that the gospel’s power does not depend on persuasiveness.

> And my speech and my preaching was not with enticing words of man’s wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power: that your faith should not stand in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God. 1 CORINTHIANS 2:4–5

Finney did not deny this; his lectures on prayer and the Spirit came first for a reason. But his heirs often took his methods without his prayer, and the result was preaching that was skilful, urgent and hollow.

And his insistence that preaching must annihilate every excuse, including the plea of inability, rests on the view of human ability we weighed in chapter ten. The preacher should indeed strip away excuses. But he must also tell sinners that they need grace, and point them to the Christ who gives it.

## Understood and felt

In the preface to his lectures, Finney said that he had not aimed at elegance, and that his great and only object was to have them understood and felt. That is a good aim for any preacher. Speak plainly. Speak to people, not about them. Make the truth clear, make it press, and ask for a response. And pray that the Spirit will do what no preacher can.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. If you preach or teach, ask of your last message: whom was it aimed at, and what did it ask them to do?
2. Listen to the next sermon you hear as if it were meant for you alone. Write down one thing it requires of you and do it.
3. Pray for your pastor this week, that he may preach plainly and in the power of the Spirit.
4. Notice whether you favour one class of truths — sovereignty or responsibility, law or grace — and seek the other.
5. If you speak to others about Christ, practise saying the gospel in the plainest words you can, as you would to a child.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You spoke so that the common people heard You gladly. Give Your church preachers who speak plainly and to the heart.
> Deliver us from lofty words that hide the truth, and from clever words that replace the Spirit.
> Make Your Word press on my conscience when I hear it, and let me not wait, but obey.
> And let every sermon preached in Your name today be understood and felt, and bear fruit for Your glory. Amen.

# New Measures and the Anxious Seat

> Let all things be done decently and in order. 1 CORINTHIANS 14:40

Nothing in Finney’s ministry caused more controversy in his lifetime than what his critics called the new measures. Protracted meetings held night after night. Anxious meetings for personal conversation with the awakened. Praying for people by name in public. Women praying aloud in mixed gatherings. Plain, pointed, sometimes harsh language from the pulpit. And, most famously, the anxious seat: a bench at the front where those concerned for their souls were invited to come and sit, to be prayed for and spoken to. The fourteenth of his Lectures on Revivals, Measures to Promote Revivals, is his defence. It is worth reading carefully, because the practices he defended are now so common in evangelical churches that most people have no idea they were ever controversial.

## No prescribed system

Finney’s first argument was simple. Under the old covenant, God prescribed forms of worship in detail, because they were types of Christ. When Christ came, those types were fulfilled, and no new system of measures was laid down. The gospel was appointed as the means of promoting religion, and it was left to the church’s discretion to decide from time to time what measures would give it power. The New Testament tells us almost nothing about how the apostles ordered their meetings.

> For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost. ROMANS 14:17

His second argument was historical. Much of what the church now takes for granted, he said, was once new — hymns in place of metrical psalms, choirs, instruments, extempore prayer, preaching without notes, kneeling to pray, laymen praying and exhorting, prayer meetings for women. Every one of them had been opposed as an innovation. The apostles, the Reformers, Wesley and Edwards had all been innovators in their day. The only thing insisted on in the New Testament regarding measures is that there be decency and order, and by order Paul did not mean whatever a particular church happened to be used to. “I believe new measures are right,” Finney wrote, “that is, that it is no objection to a measure that it is new or old.”

## The anxious seat

He then defended the three measures that were drawing the most fire. Anxious meetings, he said, allow instruction to be fitted to each individual and lead people straight to Christ. Protracted meetings concentrate attention for days together; the feasts of Israel, he said, were nothing else. And the anxious seat, he argued, had two purposes. First, it breaks the hold of pride. When a person is convicted but ashamed to be known as anxious, calling him to come forward and publicly take his place helps him break through the false shame that keeps him from God. Second, it tests sincerity. A person who says he has decided but will not rise and come forward shows that he has not really decided.

> Whosoever therefore shall confess me before men, him will I confess also before my Father which is in heaven. MATTHEW 10:32

“The design of the anxious seat is undoubtedly philosophical, and according to the laws of mind,” Finney wrote. He argued that in the apostles’ days baptism served this same purpose, as a public sign of a person’s decision to be on Christ’s side, and that even those who opposed the anxious seat found substitutes: asking the concerned to stay behind, or to sit still while others left, or to bow their heads on the pew in front.

## His own warnings

Finney saw the dangers clearly. In the same lecture he warned against relying on a protracted meeting as if it would produce a revival by itself. “This is the great reason why the church in successive generations has always had to give up her measures,” he wrote, “because Christians had come to rely on them for success.” In an earlier lecture he said that a minister needs wisdom to know when to put a stop to new measures, and that once a measure has enough novelty to gain attention for the truth, no other should be added. He warned that measures introduced merely for effect would later look like tricks and do more harm than good. He wanted measures to serve the truth, not replace it.

> Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost. TITUS 3:5

## Where care is needed

Even so, the new measures need honest weighing, because their long-term effects have been mixed.

First, the comparison with baptism does not hold. Baptism is an ordinance appointed by Christ, a sign of union with Him. The anxious seat, and its descendant the altar call, is a human device. It may be wise or unwise in a given setting, but it has no promise attached to it, and to present it as the moment of salvation confuses a response with a new birth. Countless people since Finney’s day have been assured that they were saved because they walked forward, and have lived without any sign of new life.

Second, measures designed according to the laws of mind can produce their effects whether or not the Spirit is at work. Public pressure, emotional music, repeated appeals and the sight of others going forward can move people who have not been convicted of sin or drawn to Christ. Finney knew this, and warned against false hopes. But a theology in which conversion is essentially a change of choice, and a method aimed at securing that choice, together made it very hard to tell the difference.

> Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven. MATTHEW 7:21

Third, the history of the region where Finney’s revivals burned brightest is sobering. Western New York became known as the Burned-over District, because it had been swept by so many revivals. In the decades that followed it produced not only strong churches but also a remarkable crop of new movements outside historic Christianity — Mormonism, Millerite adventism, the Oneida community, spiritualism — and a great deal of spiritual exhaustion. Finney was not responsible for these, and he opposed many of them. But a climate of repeated excitement, novelty and a belief that the right means will produce the right results proved to be fertile ground for them.

Fourth, the success of the measures helped fix in the American church the assumption that techniques can be judged by their results. That assumption has served the gospel poorly when numbers have been bought at the cost of depth.

> For other foundation can no man lay than that is laid, which is Jesus Christ. Now if any man build upon this foundation gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, stubble; every man’s work shall be made manifest. 1 CORINTHIANS 3:11–13

## Freedom and humility

Finney was right that Scripture gives the church freedom in how it orders its meetings, and right that being new is no argument against a practice. The church should not be enslaved to its customs. But freedom must be used humbly. Every measure should be judged not only by what it produces in a night, but by the lives of those it produces over years. And no measure should ever be allowed to stand in the place of the Spirit, or of Christ Himself.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think about the practices your church uses to invite a response. Which serve the truth, and which might put pressure in its place?
2. If you made a public decision at some point, ask whether your life since then shows the marks of new birth. If not, go to Christ now.
3. Beware of trusting any method — a course, an event, a programme — to produce spiritual results by itself.
4. Pray for those who lead evangelistic meetings, that they may call people to Christ plainly without manipulating them.
5. Ask whether you are more attached to your church’s customs than Scripture requires, or more attracted to novelty than wisdom allows.

### A PRAYER

> Lord of the church, You have given us freedom in how we gather and how we call people to You. Give us wisdom to use it well.
> Keep us from trusting methods, and from mistaking a response for a new birth.
> Let every invitation we give point clearly to Christ, and every decision made be Your work and not ours.
> Build Your church with gold, silver and precious stones, and let our work abide in the day that tests it. Amen.

# I Am Doing a Great Work

> And I sent messengers unto them, saying, I am doing a great work, so that I cannot come down: why should the work cease, whilst I leave it, and come down to you? NEHEMIAH 6:3

Finney saw many revivals begin. He also saw many of them end, and he thought hard about why. His fifteenth lecture, Hinderances to Revivals, took its text from Nehemiah, who refused to come down from building the wall to talk with his enemies. The lecture that followed it, on the necessity of union, and the lectures to young converts and backsliders at the end of the book, belong together with it. They form Finney’s pastoral wisdom on how the work of God is kept, and how it is lost. Some of it is among the most self-searching writing he ever did.

## Why revivals stop

Finney listed more than twenty things that put a stop to revivals, and the list is striking because so few of them are attacks from outside. A revival will cease, he said, when the church believes it is going to cease, or consents to its ceasing. It will cease when Christians become mechanical, and go on using the same words and methods after the warmth has gone. It will cease when they think the work will go on without them, or prefer their own business to God’s. It will cease when those who have worked hardest grow proud of what they have done. It will cease when the church exhausts itself by breaking up all its habits of eating and sleeping and letting the excitement run away with it.

> Let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not. GALATIANS 6:9

It will cease, he went on, when the church turns to speculating about abstract doctrines with no bearing on practice. When denominations begin to compete for converts. When those who have received great blessing are stingy in return. When brotherly love fails. When self-denial is dropped. When controversy breaks out, including controversy about measures. When the public mind is diverted by other excitements. When the church slanders the revival, or grieves the Spirit in any way.

> Endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. EPHESIANS 4:3

## Repent — including us

What is most impressive about this lecture is that Finney did not only blame his opponents. He did blame them, and sharply; he thought that the continued opposition of the Old School, combined with a bad spirit in the New, could put revivals down. But when he came to say what must be done, he began with ministers, including himself. Those who had opposed revivals must repent. But, he added, “Those who have been engaged in promoting the work must also repent.” If they had been censorious, proud, arrogant or severe, if they had mistaken harsh words for faithfulness, they too must humble themselves. “Let each one repent of his own sins, and not fall out, and quarrel about who is most to blame.”

That is a wise word, and a rare one from a man at the centre of a controversy. Finney also warned that censoriousness on either side hinders revival, and especially among the friends of the work. Opponents, he said, can never stop a revival alone, unless those who promote it get wrong.

> Let nothing be done through strife or vainglory; but in lowliness of mind let each esteem other better than themselves. PHILIPPIANS 2:3

## Agreed in prayer

In the lecture on union, from Christ’s promise that if two agree on earth as touching anything they ask, it shall be done, Finney argued that unity is more than being in the same room. Christians must agree in their desires, their motives, their faith, their sense of timing, their measures, their way of dealing with sinners and their willingness to labour and persevere. Many prayers go unanswered, he said, because those who offer them were never really agreed. And a few people perfectly united may do more than a large church divided.

## Keeping the young converts

Finney was equally concerned for those converted in revivals. He had seen too many fall away, and he blamed their teaching. Do not tell young converts, “You will not always feel as you do now,” he said, as if backsliding were to be expected. Do not teach them to taper off their religion. Tell them they have only begun, and that the path of the just is as the shining light, that shineth more and more unto the perfect day (Proverbs 4:18).

He wanted them taught to distinguish emotion from principle — the passing feelings of the heart from the settled decision of the will to obey God — and to do their duty whether they felt like it or not. He wanted them taught that they had given up the ownership of everything to God, to cultivate a tender conscience, to pray without ceasing, to take Christ and not older Christians as their standard, and to aim at usefulness rather than comfort.

> As newborn babes, desire the sincere milk of the word, that ye may grow thereby. 1 PETER 2:2

And in his lecture on backsliding he made a distinction that has helped many. Backsliding in heart, he said, “does not consist in the subsidence of highly excited religious emotions.” Feelings rise and fall. Backsliding is taking back the consecration to God that true conversion involved — leaving the first love and returning to a self-pleasing spirit — and it may happen while every outward form of religion is kept up.

## Where care is needed

Finney’s diagnosis is searching, but it rests on an assumption that needs examination: that revival is the normal state in which a church ought to live, and anything less is decline.

He was honest about the strain this caused. He said that anyone who was to keep working in a revival must pass through the process of being broken down and re-converted every few days. That is an exhausting rhythm, and many who tried to sustain it burned out. Finney himself admitted in his first lecture that powerful excitements injure health, and that it would be far better if the church went on steadily in obedience without them.

Scripture certainly knows of seasons of refreshing, and the church should pray for them. But it also knows of steady, ordinary growth through the Word, prayer, the sacraments and fellowship, like a tree planted by rivers of water that brings forth its fruit in its season. Not every quiet season is a backslidden one. Sometimes the fire burns low and hot.

> And he shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season; his leaf also shall not wither; and whatsoever he doeth shall prosper. PSALM 1:3

Keep Finney’s list of hindrances, and his call to repent without quarrelling over blame. But do not measure your church or your soul only by its temperature.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read through Finney’s list of hindrances and mark any that describe your church — or you.
2. If you have been part of a good work that has cooled, ask whether pride, exhaustion or mechanical habit has crept in.
3. Where there has been conflict, repent of your own part first, without waiting to settle who was most to blame.
4. If you know a new Christian, encourage them to go on and grow, not to expect decline; and teach them to obey whether they feel like it or not.
5. Distinguish between a season of low feeling and a heart that has taken back its consecration. Ask which describes you.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You have done great things among Your people, and we have often let the work cease through pride, weariness and quarrelling.
> Where I have become mechanical, warm my heart again. Where I have grown proud, humble me. Where I have quarrelled, make me the first to repent.
> Keep the young in faith from falling back, and teach them to walk in the light that shines more and more.
> And make me like a tree planted by the rivers of water, bearing fruit in its season, whether the season is quiet or glorious. Amen.

# Holiness to the Lord

> And the very God of peace sanctify you wholly; and I pray God your whole spirit and soul and body be preserved blameless unto the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:23

When Finney arrived at Oberlin in 1835, the college held its services in a great circular tent, a hundred feet across, that his friends in New York had paid for. From the top of the centre pole flew a streamer bearing, in large letters, the words Holiness to the Lord. It was a fitting banner. At Oberlin Finney’s attention turned increasingly from the conversion of sinners to the sanctification of believers, and the teaching he and his colleagues developed there made Oberlin famous and suspect in equal measure. This chapter sets out what he taught and weighs it.

## Why he went looking

Finney explained in his Memoirs how he came to the question. His revival work had shown him the great weakness of Christians. Older church members made little progress, and fell back from a revival state even sooner than the young converts. He was dissatisfied, too, with his own want of stability in faith and love. So he asked whether there was not something higher and more enduring than the church was aware of — whether the gospel had not provided promises and means for establishing Christians in a higher form of life.

> Not as though I had already attained, either were already perfect: but I follow after, if that I may apprehend that for which also I am apprehended of Christ Jesus. PHILIPPIANS 3:12

He studied the Methodist teaching on sanctification, but could not accept it, because it seemed to him to deal almost entirely with feelings. He examined the antinomian perfectionism then being published in New Haven and elsewhere, and rejected that too. But after searching the Scriptures he became convinced, as he put it, that “an altogether higher and more stable form of Christian life was attainable, and was the privilege of all Christians.” He preached two sermons on Christian perfection at the Broadway Tabernacle, and later set out the doctrine at length in his Systematic Theology.

## What he taught

The core of the Oberlin teaching, in Finney’s words, was that entire sanctification was taught in the Bible “in the sense that it was the privilege of Christians to live without known sin,” and that abundant means were provided for attaining it.

It helps to remember how Finney understood sin. For him, as we saw in chapter eleven, sin is a voluntary act of the will; the holy heart is a supreme choice of God’s glory. Sanctification, then, is the strengthening of that choice until it governs every movement of the mind. In Sinners Bound to Change Their Own Hearts he wrote that when this preference gains perfect control, it brings a person back to where Adam was before the fall, and constitutes perfect holiness. Since the will can choose, and since God commands us to be holy, it must be possible to live in unbroken consecration.

> Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect. MATTHEW 5:48

He urged young converts not to rest satisfied with the standard of older Christians, but to aim at being holy until they were as perfect as God. And he told one earnest woman who asked how to obtain sanctification that she must make a thorough and universal consecration of herself and all she had to Christ, and then believe for the sealing of the Holy Spirit.

## His own experience

Finney’s teaching was bound up with his own experience in Boston in the winter of 1843. For weeks he rose at four to pray and search the Scriptures. He struggled to consecrate himself more deeply than ever before, and found that he could not give up his invalid wife unreservedly to God’s will. When at last he did, he said, he gave up everything — his family, the church, even his own salvation — to God’s disposal. That evening the question came to him: what if God should send him to hell? He saw that it was impossible. “Hell could be no hell to me, if I accepted God’s perfect will.” A vein of joy opened that lasted for years. His prayer ran out into Thy will be done, and he found a rest in God he had never known.

> Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee. ISAIAH 26:3

When Lydia died a few years later, the same rest held him. After a night of anguish, he wrote, it was as if God asked him whether he had loved her for her own sake, and if so, why he grieved that she was with Him. From that moment his sorrow was changed.

## The controversy

The Oberlin teaching was attacked on every side. Ministers warned their churches against Oberlin; one, Finney recalled, called its doctrines worse than Roman Catholicism. The charge was antinomian perfectionism, which Finney indignantly denied. Charles Hodge and others criticised his Systematic Theology. Later, B. B. Warfield subjected the whole Oberlin theology to a searching critique.

Much of the criticism was unfair. But the central objection was serious, and it still stands.

## Where care is needed

The problem is that the Oberlin doctrine achieves its high standard by narrowing the definition of sin. If sin is only the deliberate, voluntary transgression of known law, then a Christian may indeed be free of it for long stretches. But Scripture’s idea of sin is wider. It includes the sins we do not know we commit, the disordered desires beneath our choices, and every falling short of the glory of God.

> Who can understand his errors? cleanse thou me from secret faults. PSALM 19:12

The apostle John, writing to believers, said: If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. Paul, near the end of his life, said he had not already attained. The holiest Christians in history have been the most conscious of their remaining sin. A doctrine that invites believers to claim they are living without known sin risks making them either proud, if they think they have arrived, or despairing, if they know they have not.

> If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. 1 JOHN 1:8

There is also the burden it placed on assurance. Finney taught that persistence in holiness is a condition of salvation. Joined to a teaching that entire sanctification is attainable, this could leave earnest believers watching their own consecration anxiously, rather than resting in Christ.

## What remains

Yet Finney was right about much. The Christian life is meant to be one of real, growing holiness, not a resigned coexistence with sin. God commands us to be holy, and He means it. Consecration — giving ourselves and everything we have to God without reserve — is a real step that many Christians have never taken. And the rest Finney found in the will of God in 1843 is a genuine and beautiful thing, available to every believer. The mistake is not in aiming high. It is in supposing that we can ever measure our arrival.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask whether you have settled for a Christianity with no real expectation of growth in holiness.
2. Is there anything — a person, a plan, a possession — you have never yielded to God’s will without reserve? Bring it to Him today.
3. Pray David’s prayer for cleansing from secret faults, and ask God to show you sins you have not noticed.
4. If you are tempted to think you have arrived, read 1 John 1:8–10 slowly. If you are tempted to despair, read it again.
5. Seek the rest Finney found: to say from the heart, Thy will be done, about the thing you fear most.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Father, You call me to be holy as You are holy. I confess that I have often been content to be less.
> I give myself to You again — my family, my plans, my possessions, my future. Do with me as seems good to You.
> Cleanse me from secret faults, and keep me back from presumptuous sins. Let me never say I have no sin.
> And keep me in perfect peace, my mind stayed on You, until You sanctify me wholly at the coming of my Lord. Amen.

# The Law and the Gospel

> Wherefore the law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ, that we might be justified by faith. GALATIANS 3:24

In the two winters after the Lectures on Revivals, Finney preached a series of lectures to Christians in the Broadway Tabernacle. They were published as Lectures to Professing Christians, and they are relentless. Many who heard them, he admitted in his Memoirs, regarded them as rather an exhibition of the Law than of the Gospel. Finney did not accept the criticism. “For me,” he wrote, “the Law and Gospel have but one rule of life; and every violation of the spirit of the Law, is also a violation of the spirit of the Gospel.” How he held law and gospel together is one of the keys to his ministry, and to its strengths and weaknesses.

## The law of love

For Finney, the law of God was not a set of arbitrary rules. It was the law of love, which Jesus summed up in two commandments: love God with all your heart, and your neighbour as yourself. Finney usually called this love benevolence — a settled choice of the highest good of God and all His creatures. The law, he said in God’s Love for a Sinning World, is designed and adapted to secure the highest good of the universe. Obedience to it is essential to happiness; without it there could be no blessedness even in heaven.

> Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law. ROMANS 13:10

Sin, on this view, is selfishness — the choice of one’s own gratification above the good of God and others. And conversion is the change from selfishness to benevolence. That is why Finney could say that law and gospel have but one rule of life. The gospel does not bring a different standard. It brings sinners back to the standard they abandoned, and the power to keep it.

## The law must search

Finney was convinced that the law must do its work before the gospel can do its. “I have long been satisfied,” he wrote, “that the higher forms of Christian experience are attained only as a result of a terribly searching application of God’s Law to the human conscience and heart.” That is why he preached as he did. He wanted sinners to feel their guilt, not merely their misery, and he thought the popular religious books of his day made sinners pity themselves rather than condemn themselves. He wanted Christians to see their sins one by one. He wanted preachers to deal with the conscience and probe to the quick.

> Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin. ROMANS 3:20

There is much wisdom here. A gospel preached without the law becomes sentimental. People who have never seen their sin have no use for a Saviour. Finney’s refusal to let the law be softened was a service to the church, and his insistence that grace does not lower God’s standard is thoroughly biblical.

## Orthodox in practice

Finney pressed the same point on the church. In his lecture Instructions to Converts he complained that the church was “mighty orthodox in notions, but very heretical in practice.” It would discipline a member for denying the divinity of Christ, but not for covetousness. It would guard its creed, but not its conduct. He wanted a church as vigilant about heresy in practice as about heresy in doctrine, and he said that covetousness fairly proved should exclude a man from communion as certainly as adultery.

> What doth it profit, my brethren, though a man say he hath faith, and have not works? can faith save him? JAMES 2:14

This is a word the church always needs. Right belief that does not issue in right living is not the faith of the New Testament. Finney refused to let anyone hide behind a creed.

## Where care is needed

And yet the charge that Finney’s lectures to Christians sounded more like law than gospel was not entirely unfair, and it points to a real weakness in his theology.

The gospel is not only a restoration of the law’s standard. It is the announcement that Christ has done what the law demands, for sinners who could not. The law says do this and live. The gospel says it is finished. Paul’s great argument in Galatians and Romans is that we are justified not by the works of the law but by faith in Christ, whose righteousness is counted as ours. The law is a schoolmaster to bring us to Christ; it is not the road on which we walk to acceptance with God.

> For what the law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, and for sin, condemned sin in the flesh: that the righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit. ROMANS 8:3–4

Finney, having rejected the imputation of Christ’s righteousness, had difficulty saying this plainly. In his system justification is pardon, granted to those who repent and believe, and continuing acceptance depends on continuing obedience. The gospel becomes chiefly the most powerful motive to keep the law. That leaves the earnest believer, who knows how far short of perfect benevolence he falls, with nowhere to rest but his own consecration. Many who sat under Finney’s preaching found it searching and stirring. Some found it crushing.

Strikingly, Finney’s own conversion shows the better way. On his way to the office that October morning, he wrote, an inward voice asked him whether he was trying to work out a righteousness of his own. He saw that salvation was an offer to be accepted, full and complete, and that he had only to submit himself to the righteousness of God through Christ. That is the gospel, and it is the gospel he preached at his best. His theology did not always keep up with it.

> For they being ignorant of God’s righteousness, and going about to establish their own righteousness, have not submitted themselves unto the righteousness of God. For Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth. ROMANS 10:3–4

## Both, in their places

The right response is to keep both law and gospel, each in its place. Let the law search you, as Finney insisted; do not soften it. Let it drive you to Christ. Then rest in Christ, whose righteousness is yours by faith. And from that rest, walk in love, which is the fulfilling of the law, by the power of the Spirit.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read the Ten Commandments and the two great commandments slowly this week, and let them search you.
2. Ask whether you are more orthodox in notions than in practice. Name one area where your conduct contradicts your creed.
3. When you see your sin clearly, go straight to Christ. Do not try to make up the shortfall with more effort.
4. Ask where you rest when you know you have failed: in your consecration, or in Christ’s righteousness.
5. Choose one practical act of love this week that costs you something, as the fruit, not the root, of your acceptance.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, Your law is holy, just and good, and I have broken it. Let it search me until I have no excuse left.
> Then show me Christ, who fulfilled the law for me and bore its curse in my place. I submit to Your righteousness, not my own.
> Keep me from being orthodox in words and heretical in deeds. Let my faith work by love.
> And by Your Spirit, fulfil the righteousness of the law in me, that I may walk in love to Your glory. Amen.

# The Sin of the Church

> Is not this the fast that I have chosen? to loose the bands of wickedness, to undo the heavy burdens, and to let the oppressed go free, and that ye break every yoke? ISAIAH 58:6

Finney is remembered as a revivalist, but he never thought revival could be separated from righteousness in public life. A church that was truly revived, he believed, would set its face against the sins of its nation, and a church that refused to do so would grieve the Spirit and lose its revival. Nowhere did this conviction show more clearly than in his stand against slavery. When most American churches were silent or compromised, Finney barred slaveholders from the Lord’s Table and spent forty years at a college that took in Black students and sheltered fugitives. This chapter sets out what he taught about the Christian’s obligation to reform.

## No neutral ground

In his lecture on the hindrances to revival, Finney argued that revivals are hindered when ministers and churches take wrong ground on any question involving human rights. He took slavery as his chief example. There was a time, he said, when a Christian might be ignorant of its sinfulness. John Newton had continued in the slave trade after his conversion, because the evil of it had not yet dawned on him. But once the light has come, no one can remain neutral. “Christians can no more take neutral ground on this subject,” he said, than they can on the keeping of the Sabbath. “It is a great national sin. It is a sin of the church.”

> Open thy mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy. PROVERBS 31:9

He went further. The churches, by their silence, and by allowing slaveholders to remain in good standing, had given the institution their blessing. It was the fact that ministers and church members held slaves that sanctified the whole abomination in the eyes of the world. “The fact is that slavery is, pre-eminently, the sin of the church.”

## Closing the table

Finney did not only speak; he acted. His New York church, he told his hearers, had taken the same ground on slavery as on strong drink: it had excluded slaveholders and all concerned in the traffic from its communion. Some had called this uncharitable. Finney answered with a clear distinction. Where he had no authority, if the table of Christ was spread, he would sit down to it in obedience to Christ, whoever else was there. “Where I have authority, I exclude slaveholders from the communion, and I always will as long as I live.”

> I wrote unto you in an epistle not to company with fornicators: yet not altogether with the fornicators of this world, or with the covetous, or extortioners … for then must ye needs go out of the world. 1 CORINTHIANS 5:9–10

He was careful not to condemn every slaveholding professor as a hypocrite. But while they continued in that sin, he said, the cause of Christ and of humanity required that they should not be recognised as Christians in good standing, lest the church become a partaker of other men’s sins.

## The spirit of reform

Finney’s counsel on how to take this ground is as instructive as the ground itself. Avoid a bad spirit, he said. Angry controversy harms religion and harms the slaves themselves. Avoid a censorious spirit on both sides; impeaching one another’s motives grieves the Spirit. Let Christians lay aside prejudice and inform themselves. Let temperate, judicious writings circulate. Then let the church, meekly but firmly, take its stand and express its abhorrence of the sin — and having done so, be at peace and attend to the salvation of souls. He did not want slavery to become the all-absorbing topic of every conversation. He wanted it settled.

> Learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow. ISAIAH 1:17

He applied the same principles elsewhere. Temperance, he thought, was a reform no Christian could stand aloof from. The Sabbath must be kept. Missions must be supported. And he told his hearers that politics were part of religion in a country like theirs. He did not want a Christian political party, he said; he wanted Christians to vote for honest men regardless of party, and to refuse their support to any candidate known to be a knave, an adulterer or a drunkard.

## Oberlin

At Oberlin these convictions took concrete form. Finney made it a condition of his coming that the college admit students without regard to colour. It became one of the first colleges in America to educate Black and white students together, and it admitted women to its collegiate course. The surrounding country was hostile; the state legislature sought pretexts to revoke its charter. In his Memoirs Finney recalled that Oberlin became a station on the underground railroad, where escaping slaves on their way to Canada would shelter for a day or two, and that when slave-catchers came after them they found no sympathy among the people — which, he said, turned the feeling of the surrounding towns in Oberlin’s favour.

> Remember them that are in bonds, as bound with them. HEBREWS 13:3

## Where care is needed

Finney’s witness against slavery is one of the most admirable things about him, and it needs little qualification. But a few observations are fair.

His hopes were too simple. He told his hearers that if the churches of all denominations would pronounce slavery sin and clear their communions, then in three years there would not be a shackled slave in the land. It did not happen. When he revised the lectures in 1868, he added a sober note: the church had been too late in its testimony to avoid the war. His optimism, like his confidence in revival, reflected a belief that the right means rightly used would transform the nation and hasten the millennium. Scripture calls us to work for justice without promising that we will see it complete before Christ returns.

He was also more cautious than the radical abolitionists wished. He warned the editor of the New York Evangelist not to go too fast on the question, lest he destroy his paper, and he insisted that reform must not displace the salvation of souls. Some saw this as wisdom, others as timidity. Christians today will weigh it differently. What is clear is that he held revival and reform together, and refused to let either swallow the other.

Finally, his temperance teaching, which he placed alongside slavery, rested partly on his conviction that any use of alcohol was only injurious. Christians may reach different judgments on that particular question. The principle beneath it — that the church cannot be neutral when its members profit from the ruin of others — stands.

> He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the LORD require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God? MICAH 6:8

## Faith that works

Finney’s great conviction was that faith must be obeyed, and obedience cannot stop at the church door. A revival that left slavery untouched would not, in his eyes, have been a revival at all.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask what injustice in your own society your church is tempted to stay silent about. Pray about it this week.
2. Inform yourself carefully on one such question, laying aside prejudice, as Finney urged.
3. When you speak about it, watch your spirit. Avoid censoriousness and impugning motives, without softening the truth.
4. Consider whether you profit, directly or indirectly, from the harm of others. What would repentance and restitution look like?
5. Pray for the persecuted and the oppressed by name, as if bound with them.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, You loose the bands of wickedness and let the oppressed go free. Forgive Your church for the times it has been silent, and forgive me.
> Give me courage to stand where You stand, and humility to do it without bitterness.
> Keep me from making reform my gospel, and from making the gospel an excuse for indifference.
> Teach me to do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with You, until the day when every yoke is broken. Amen.

# Conclusion

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

Nineteen chapters can make it look as though Charles Finney taught a great many things. At bottom he taught one: that God’s Word is to be obeyed, now, by the one who hears it. Revival is a church beginning again to obey. Prevailing prayer takes God at His word and holds on. The sinner has no excuse for refusing. Repentance confesses, forsakes and makes restitution. And a church that obeys God will not stay silent while its members hold others in chains. One conviction, pressed into every corner of life.

## What to keep

Much of what Finney taught the church still needs to hear, perhaps more than ever.

Keep his refusal to let doctrine become a hiding-place. He saw that a true teaching about God’s sovereignty could be twisted into an excuse for idleness, and he would not allow it. No one reading him can remain comfortable in doing nothing.

Keep his teaching on prayer. The lectures on prevailing prayer, the spirit of prayer and the prayer of faith, for all their overstatements, have sent generations of believers to their knees with definite requests and expectant faith.

Keep his dependence on the Spirit. For all his talk of means, he never believed that preaching or measures could convert a single soul without the Spirit, and he traced his revivals to hidden intercessors.

Keep his plainness. Speak to people, not about them, and ask for a response.

And keep his conviction that the gospel has public consequences. His stand against slavery was costly and right.

> Therefore, my beloved brethren, be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:58

## What to set aside

We have been honest throughout about where Finney went wrong, and it is worth gathering the cautions in one place.

He rejected the doctrine of original sin as the church had received it, denying that Adam’s guilt is reckoned to his descendants or that we are born with a nature that is itself sinful. He taught that people have full ability to obey God, and that the Spirit’s work is to persuade the unwilling rather than to give life to the dead. On these points he stood closer to Pelagius than to Augustine, Luther or Calvin, and Scripture does not support him.

He held a governmental view of the atonement, in which Christ’s death upheld the honour of God’s law rather than bearing the penalty of sinners in their place, and he rejected the imputation of Christ’s righteousness. That left the believer’s standing resting, in the end, on his own persistence in holiness.

His confidence that revival follows the right use of means, and his defence of measures designed to bring people to decide, helped to create a tradition in which technique could do the work of the Spirit and a decision could be mistaken for a new birth. The Burned-over District, with its revivals and its sects, is a warning.

His teaching on entire sanctification narrowed the definition of sin in order to raise the standard of attainment, and it burdened some of the most earnest believers.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21

None of this is said to diminish him. Finney tested everything by his Bible and refused to accept any doctrine on the authority of great names. We owe him the same honesty.

## The man and his Master

It is striking how often, at the decisive moments of his life, Finney’s experience was sounder than his system. On the way to his office in October 1821, he saw that salvation was a finished work to be accepted, not a righteousness to be worked out. In the woods he was broken by his pride and saved by a promise. In the law office he was overwhelmed by the love of God that he had done nothing to deserve. In Boston in 1843 he found rest not in his own consecration but in the perfect will of God. At those moments he knew that everything came from grace.

> For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast. EPHESIANS 2:8–9

That is the gospel, and it does not contradict his urgency. It is the ground of it. The one who knows that Christ has done everything is free to obey with all his heart, not to earn God’s favour but because he has received it.

## The question he always asked

Finney ended almost every lecture with the same question: Will you do it? He had no interest in hearers who came to learn about revivals and went home unchanged. This book ends the same way. You have read about prayer, repentance, the Spirit, preaching, holiness and justice. What will you do?

Do not try to do everything. Choose the chapter that made you most uncomfortable, and act on it this week. Break up one patch of fallow ground. Pray definitely for one person. Make restitution for one wrong. Speak plainly to one friend about Christ. And do it not as a man trying to make himself a new heart, but as one who has been given one.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Return to the chapter that made you most uncomfortable. Choose one application point and act on it this week.
2. Write down one thing God has shown you through this book that you have not yet obeyed. Obey it.
3. Where Finney’s theology has unsettled you, go back to Romans 3–5 and rest again on Christ’s finished work.
4. Choose one of Finney’s own works from the Reader’s Guide that follows and begin reading it, with discernment.
5. Pray for revival — in your own heart first, then in your church and your nation.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, You have spoken, and I have heard again. Do not let me be a hearer only.
> Thank You for Your servant Charles Finney: for his urgency, his prayers and his courage. Forgive where he went beyond Your Word, and forgive me where I have fallen short of it.
> Give me grace to obey what I know, and humility to rest on what Christ has done.
> Revive Your work in the midst of the years, and begin with me. Amen.

# A Reader’s Guide to Charles G. Finney

> Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. 2 TIMOTHY 2:15

Finney is easy to read and hard to read well. His style is plain, direct and argumentative, and his lectures were taken down in pencil as he spoke, so they read like a man talking — which is their charm. The difficulty is not the language but the judgement. Much of his practical counsel is wise, and much of his theology departs from historic teaching, often on the same page. Read him with the Bible open and your wits about you.

## Begin here

Lectures on Revivals of Religion (1835). His most influential book: twenty-two Friday-evening lectures to his New York congregation on what a revival is, how to pray, how to deal with sinners, how to preach and how to keep young converts. The chapters on fallow ground and on prayer are the best entry point. In the Ochorus library.

Memoirs (1876). Written in his mid-seventies and published after his death, this is the story of his conversion and his revivals in his own words, frank and vivid and sometimes self-justifying. Read the first three chapters, on his early life, conversion and first days as a Christian, before anything else. In the Ochorus library. A fuller restored text, edited by Garth M. Rosell and Richard A. G. Dupuis, was published in 1989 and restores passages cut by the first editors.

## Sermons

Several of his sermons are in the Ochorus library. Sinners Bound to Change Their Own Hearts is the clearest short statement of his view of regeneration and human ability, and the one to read alongside chapters ten and eleven of this book. God’s Love for a Sinning World is his warmest sermon on the cross. Breaking Up the Fallow Ground, Prevailing Prayer, The Spirit of Prayer, Be Filled with the Spirit and What a Revival of Religion Is correspond to lectures in the Lectures on Revivals, and are convenient to read one at a time.

## Other works worth knowing

Lectures to Professing Christians (1837). Addresses to church members in the Broadway Tabernacle, searching and severe, on self-deception, true and false religion, and Christian perfection. Useful for self-examination; heavy on law. Not yet in the Ochorus library.

Power from On High. A short collection of late articles on the baptism of the Holy Spirit and the need for spiritual power in ministry. Read it with chapter nine of this book. Not yet in the Ochorus library.

## Approach with care

Lectures on Systematic Theology (1846–1847). Finney’s full theological system, written for his Oberlin students. It sets out his moral government theology, his view of the will, his denial of inherited sin, his governmental atonement and his doctrine of entire sanctification. It is closely argued and repays study, but it is where his departures from Scripture and the historic faith are most explicit. Read it after, not before, his practical works, and alongside a sound account of the doctrines it rejects.

## About Finney

G. Frederick Wright, Charles Grandison Finney (1891), is an early life by an Oberlin professor who had studied under him, warm and sympathetic. Keith J. Hardman, Charles Grandison Finney, 1792–1875: Revivalist and Reformer (1987), and Charles E. Hambrick-Stowe, Charles G. Finney and the Spirit of American Evangelicalism (1996), are fuller modern biographies. Whitney R. Cross, The Burned-over District (1950), is the classic study of the region where his revivals burned, and of what followed them. The Ochorus author page carries a shorter biography.

For critical assessments, Iain H. Murray’s Revival and Revivalism (1994) contrasts the older view of revival with the new measures, and B. B. Warfield’s studies of perfectionism include a searching critique of the Oberlin theology. Both are written from a Reformed standpoint, and both are worth reading after Finney himself.

## Three counsels for reading him

Read him for his practice more than his theory. His counsel on prayer, confession, soul-winning and preaching is often better than the system he built to explain it.

Read him with his critics beside you. Where he dismisses a doctrine as a fiction, look up the texts and see whether he is right. He would have wanted you to test him by Scripture, not by his reputation.

And read him with a pen. Finney’s one question to every hearer was, Will you do it? Write down what you will do, and do it.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21
