It is a companion to him — an independent work of exposition and appreciation, written by Ochorus, that gathers the heart of his teaching into sixteen short chapters for the ordinary reader. It tells his life plainly, as the life of an African bishop, an Egyptian who spent forty-five years as bishop of Alexandria and seventeen of them in exile, and it draws throughout on the two of his books that sit in the Ochorus library, On the Incarnation and The Life of Antony.

  Athanasius had one great theme: God Himself has come the whole way down to us. The Word who made the world took a real body, died a real death and rose again, so that a ruined creation might be remade by the same hand that first made it. Everything he fought for, and everything he suffered, followed from refusing to let that truth be made smaller. This volume follows the thread through creation, the cross, the resurrection, the long battle over the Son’s full divinity, the Holy Spirit, the Scriptures and the desert, and it is honest about where he must be read with care. # Introduction

> And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth. JOHN 1:14

Every Sunday, in churches of almost every tradition, Christians stand and say that Jesus Christ is “of one substance with the Father”, or “of one being with the Father”. Most of them have never heard of the man who more than any other kept those words in the creed. His name was Athanasius. He was bishop of Alexandria for forty-five years, and he spent seventeen of them in exile, driven from his city five times by four different emperors. He was accused of murder, sorcery, treason and sacrilege, and hunted by soldiers through the Egyptian desert. What he would not do was sign a statement of faith he believed to be false. That refusal, held for half a century, is a large part of the reason the church still confesses that the One who died on the cross was God.

He was an African. That needs saying plainly at the outset. Athanasius was born in Alexandria, on the Mediterranean coast of Egypt, around the year 296 or a little after. He grew up in a Greek-speaking city, but he knew the Coptic-speaking Egypt of the villages and the desert as well, and when the empire turned against him it was Egyptians who hid him. He is one of the great fathers of the whole church, East and West; he is also, first of all, a son of the ancient African church of Alexandria, the church that claimed Mark the evangelist as its founder and gave the world some of its first great Christian teachers.

## Why he still matters

Athanasius matters because he saw, earlier and more clearly than almost anyone, what is at stake in the question, Who is Jesus Christ? In his day a popular and persuasive teacher named Arius was saying that the Son of God, though the highest and noblest of all creatures, was nevertheless a creature — that there was a time when He was not. It sounded reverent. It could quote Scripture. Athanasius saw that it emptied the gospel. If the Son is a creature, then God has not come to us at all. He has only sent the best thing He had made, and we are still on our own.

He also matters because he wrote one of the most readable books of the ancient church. On the Incarnation is short, unhurried and plain, and sets out with a kind of glad astonishment why the Word of God became man. And in The Life of Antony he told the story of an Egyptian hermit, a book that did more than any other to spread the monastic life through the Christian world and played its part in the conversion of another African, Augustine.

## The one thread

If you look for the single thread that runs through everything Athanasius taught and suffered, you find it in a sentence from On the Incarnation that the Greek church has never stopped quoting: “For He was made man that we might be made God.” We shall come to that sentence in its own chapter, and read it carefully, because it is easily misread. But its meaning is simple enough. God has come the whole way down. The Word who made us has taken a real body, lived a real life, died a real death and risen again, so that we, who were sliding back into the nothing we came from, might share His life.

> For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich. 2 CORINTHIANS 8:9

Every part of his teaching is that thread followed out. Creation is good because the Word made it. Salvation must be the Word’s own work, because only the Maker can remake. The Son and the Spirit must be fully God, or we are not truly joined to God. And the desert monks, fighting in the strength of Christ, are the proof that the victory is real.

## How this book is arranged

The second chapter tells his life. The sixteen that follow each take one of his key teachings. Six follow the argument of On the Incarnation, from the Word made flesh through the cross and the resurrection to the great saying about being made God. Two take up the doctrines he defended for fifty years, the full divinity of the Son and of the Spirit. Two more give his answer to the pagan world. Four draw on The Life of Antony, and the last two turn to the Scriptures and to the cost of standing for the truth.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. Athanasius would have approved. He closed On the Incarnation by telling his reader that no one can understand the things of God without a pure life, and that the searching of the Scriptures needs a soul made clean. Doctrine, for him, was never an idle matter. So read a chapter, stop at the questions, and pray.

## Reading him with care

This is not a book of hero-worship. Athanasius was a fighter, and fighters leave marks. Several matters must be named plainly.

First, he could be savage towards his opponents. He called the Arians madmen and enemies of Christ, and rarely allowed that they might be sincere. Some of that heat was earned; much of it would not be allowed in a Christian argument today. Second, his enemies accused his supporters in Alexandria of intimidation and violence against rival Christians, and at least one surviving papyrus letter from the time seems to support part of that charge. The evidence is contested, and many of the accusations against him were plainly invented; but a fair reader should know that the charge exists, and should not assume that the side that was right about the Son of God was always right in its methods. Third, On the Incarnation contains a long and harsh argument against the Jews of his day, written in a tone that Christians who know the later history of that tone cannot read without sorrow.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21

Beyond these, The Life of Antony is full of demons, visions and miracles that must be read with discernment, and it prizes the ascetic life in ways many Protestant readers will question. And the language of being “made God” is easily misunderstood if torn from its setting. Each of these is taken up where it belongs.

None of this is said to diminish him. It is said so that you can trust the rest. Where Athanasius stands with Scripture — on the Word made flesh, on the cross and resurrection, on the full divinity of Christ, on the courage to hold the truth when it is unpopular — he stands like a rock, and the whole church has stood on him since.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Say the words of the Nicene Creed slowly this week and ask yourself which phrases you have been saying without meaning.
2. Write down in one sentence who you believe Jesus Christ to be. Then ask whether your prayer and worship treat Him as that.
3. If you have never read an early Christian writer, resolve to read On the Incarnation for yourself, a few sections a day.
4. Think of a teacher you admire who has been harsh or wrong in some matter. Practise naming the fault honestly without throwing away the truth he taught.
5. Set a regular time to read one chapter of this book, and do not move on until you have prayed its prayer.

> Lord Jesus Christ, eternal Word of the Father, You came the whole way down to where we were, and You did not come half-heartedly.
> Thank You for Your servant Athanasius, who would not let Your glory be made smaller, even when it cost him his home and his safety.

# The Bishop Who Kept Coming Home

> Though an host should encamp against me, my heart shall not fear: though war should rise against me, in this will I be confident. PSALM 27:3

A son of Alexandria. Athanasius was born in Alexandria, in Egypt, around 296 or a year or two later. The city was the second of the Roman world, a great harbour and a great library, Greek in its learning and Egyptian in its bones. We know almost nothing of his family. We do know the world of his boyhood: the last and fiercest of the imperial persecutions fell on the Egyptian church in the first years of the fourth century, and Alexandria had its martyrs, among them its bishop, Peter, put to death in 311. Athanasius grew up in a church that knew what it cost to confess Christ. He learned Greek and the Scriptures thoroughly, and he knew the Coptic-speaking countryside too. That would matter later.

In the bishop’s house. As a young man he came into the household of Alexander, bishop of Alexandria, and was made a deacon and the bishop’s secretary. It was a turbulent place to serve. One of Alexander’s own presbyters, Arius, had begun to teach that the Son of God was a creature — the first and greatest of creatures, through whom all else was made, but made nonetheless. Alexander condemned the teaching, Arius appealed to friends in other cities, and the quarrel spread across the eastern church.

Nicaea. In 325 the emperor Constantine, who wanted one church in one empire, summoned the bishops to Nicaea in Asia Minor. Some two or three hundred came. Alexander brought his young deacon. Athanasius had no vote, but he was there when the council declared that the Son is “of one substance” with the Father — homoousios, in the Greek — true God from true God, begotten, not made. Arius was condemned. It looked like the end of the matter. It was the beginning.

Bishop. When Alexander died in 328, Athanasius, then about thirty, was chosen to succeed him. His election was disputed from the start, and he inherited not only the Arian quarrel but a bitter schism in Egypt led by the followers of Meletius, a bishop who had broken with Alexandria years before. The Meletians and the friends of Arius found common cause against him. For the rest of his life he would be surrounded by charges.

The charges. They were extraordinary. He was accused of taxing the people for linen vestments, of breaking a chalice at a presbyter’s altar, of murdering a Meletian bishop named Arsenius and keeping his severed hand for magic — until Arsenius was found alive. At the Council of Tyre in 335 a synod packed with his opponents condemned him anyway. He went straight to Constantinople and confronted the emperor in person. Then came a new charge, that he had threatened to hold back the grain ships from Egypt that fed the capital, and Constantine sent him away to Trier, on the Moselle, at the far edge of the empire. That was the first exile.

Rome. Constantine died in 337, and his sons let the exiles return. Athanasius was home within the year. But the eastern emperor, Constantius, favoured the party of Arius, and in 339 a rival bishop, Gregory, was installed in Alexandria with the help of soldiers. Athanasius fled to Rome. Julius, bishop of Rome, and a western council received him as the rightful bishop. It was the second exile, and it lasted until 346, when political pressure from the West forced Constantius to let him go back. Alexandria welcomed him with rejoicing.

The night in the church. Ten years of uneasy peace followed. Then, when Constantius had become sole emperor, he set out to bring the whole church into line, and one bishop after another in the West was compelled to condemn Athanasius. On a night in February 356, soldiers surrounded the church of Theonas in Alexandria, where he was leading a vigil. He sat in his bishop’s chair and told the deacon to begin Psalm 136, and the people answered each verse with its refrain, for his mercy endureth for ever. He would not leave until the congregation had gone out safely. Then his clergy half carried him away through the crowd, and he vanished.

The desert. For six years he was hidden. He moved among the monks of the Egyptian desert, and among friends in the city, and no one betrayed him, though the emperor’s agents searched hard. Antony, the old hermit he had known and loved, had died in 356, and in these years Athanasius wrote his Life for monks in other lands who had asked to hear of him. He wrote defences of his conduct, a history of the Arian persecution, and letters to his friend Serapion on the Holy Spirit. The years of hiding were among the most fruitful of his life.

Julian and Valens. Constantius died in 361, and his cousin Julian, who had abandoned the faith and wanted to restore the old gods, allowed the exiled bishops home, perhaps hoping they would tear the church apart. Athanasius returned in 362 and at once began to heal divisions among those who confessed the Nicene faith. Julian, finding him too effective, banished him again that same year — the fourth exile — calling him an enemy of the gods. Julian died on campaign in 363, and Athanasius came back. A fifth exile followed under the emperor Valens in 365, a short one; tradition says that he hid for some months in his father’s tomb outside the city. In 366 he was restored for the last time.

The last years. He had seven quiet years. He went on writing, teaching, ordaining and corresponding with bishops across the empire, and in 367 his annual Easter letter to the churches of Egypt set out the books of the Old and New Testaments, the earliest surviving list of exactly the twenty-seven books of our New Testament. He died in Alexandria on 2 May 373, in his own house and his own city, having outlived every emperor who had banished him. Eight years later, at the Council of Constantinople in 381, the church confirmed the faith of Nicaea and confessed the Holy Spirit as Lord and giver of life. He did not live to see it. He had held the line long enough for others to see it.

The man. He was small, and his enemies mocked him for it. He was quick, shrewd and sometimes ruthless in controversy, and his opponents were not wrong that he could be hard to deal with. But the people of Alexandria loved him, the monks of the desert trusted him, and the old hermit Antony left him, as a last gift, the worn garment Athanasius had once given him new. A bishop who could win the love of both the city crowd and the desert fathers was more than a politician. He was a pastor who believed that the truth about Christ was worth everything, and who spent everything on it.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Athanasius grew up in a church that remembered its martyrs. Find out about one Christian in your own country’s history who suffered for the faith, and thank God for them.
2. He was falsely accused again and again. If you are carrying an unjust accusation, bring it to God in prayer and leave its vindication with Him.
3. He would not leave the church until his people were safe. Ask what it would mean for you to put the safety of others ahead of your own comfort this week.
4. Psalm 136 was the psalm of his worst night. Read it aloud this week, and say its refrain after every verse.
5. Consider what you would be unwilling to sign, say or deny, whatever it cost. Write it down, and ask God for the courage to keep it.

> Lord God, You kept Your servant Athanasius through five exiles, through false charges and the hatred of the powerful, and You brought him home in peace.
> Thank You for his faithfulness to Your Son when faithfulness was costly, and for the friends You gave him in the desert and the city.
> When I am falsely accused, keep me from bitterness; when the truth is unpopular, keep me from silence.
> Teach me, in the dark night as in the day, to say with Your people that Your mercy endureth for ever. Amen.
# The Word Made Flesh

> In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. JOHN 1:1

On the Incarnation begins with a young man writing to a friend. Athanasius addresses him as Macarius, a name that means blessed, and calls him a true lover of Christ. He has already written one book for him, answering the idol-worship of the pagan world. Now he wants to go further, and to set out what he calls the Word’s becoming man, and His divine appearing among us — the thing, he says, “which Jews traduce and Greeks laugh to scorn, but we worship.” The whole treatise is an answer to one question. Why did God become man?

It is a question many Christians have never thought to ask. We know that Christ was born at Bethlehem, and we sing about it every December. But why did it have to be that way? Could God not have forgiven us from heaven? Could He not have sent a prophet, or an angel, or simply spoken a word? Athanasius takes the question seriously, and his answer has shaped the church’s thinking ever since.

## The Maker comes to remake

The first thing he does is surprising. He does not begin with Bethlehem at all. He begins with creation. You cannot understand why the Word came, he says, unless you understand who made the world in the first place. And the reason is this: the One who saves the world is the One who made it. He puts it in a single sentence at the start of the book, that “the renewal of creation has been the work of the self-same Word that made it at the beginning.”

> All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made. JOHN 1:3

That is the key to everything that follows. Salvation is not a second project, an afterthought bolted on to a world that went wrong. It is the Maker coming back to His own work. The artist returns to the damaged painting. The builder returns to the ruined house. The One who spoke the world into being steps into it Himself to set it right. There is something deeply fitting about that, and Athanasius wants his reader to feel it.

> For by him were all things created, that are in heaven, and that are in earth, visible and invisible … all things were created by him, and for him: and he is before all things, and by him all things consist. COLOSSIANS 1:16–17

This is why he insists so fiercely, later in his life, that the Son is God and not a creature. If the Word were only a creature, however exalted, then the one who came to rescue us would not be our Maker, and the rescue would be a lesser thing done by a lesser being. The whole logic of On the Incarnation depends on the Son being the Creator Himself.

## He was not far from us before

One of the loveliest touches in the book is a small phrase in the eighth section. The Word of God, Athanasius says, comes to our realm, “howbeit he was not far from us before.” He was never absent. He filled all things and held all things together; no part of creation was ever empty of Him. And yet He came — in a new way, to a particular place, in a particular body — to show His loving-kindness and to visit us.

> That they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: for in him we live, and move, and have our being. ACTS 17:27–28

This guards against two mistakes. The first is to imagine that God was far away until Christmas, watching from a distance. Athanasius will not have it. The Word was always present, upholding the world. The second mistake is to imagine that the Incarnation was merely a kind of appearance, a costume God wore for a while. Athanasius will not have that either. He insists that the Word “takes unto Himself a body, and that of no different sort from ours.” He did not simply will to appear. He could have done that in any number of dazzling ways. He took our actual nature, born of a virgin, and made it His own.

## Not to make a display

Near the end of the book Athanasius faces an objection from the educated pagans of his day. If God wanted to show Himself, why use something as humble as a human body? Why not appear in the sun, or the stars, or some nobler part of creation? His answer is one of the finest things he wrote: “the Lord came not to make a display, but to heal and teach those who were suffering.”

The point is simple and searching. A person who wants to impress will dazzle. A person who wants to heal will come close, and come in a way that the sick can bear. It was not the sun or the stars that had gone wrong, Athanasius says; they had kept their order. It was man alone who had turned away. So it was as a man that God came, to meet human beings where they were.

> Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil. HEBREWS 2:14

There is a whole theology of ministry in that answer. The Lord did not stand on a height and shout down to us. He came down and walked among us, at a pace we could follow and in a form we could touch.

## Walking as man, upholding all things

Athanasius is careful here, and his care is worth learning. He does not think that the Word was shrunk or trapped by taking a body. “For He was not, as might be imagined, circumscribed in the body,” he writes. While He walked the roads of Galilee, He was still upholding the universe. Athanasius puts it in a marvellous line: the Word was “at once walking as man, and as the Word was quickening all things, and as the Son was dwelling with His Father.”

> Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high. HEBREWS 1:3

We cannot picture this, and we are not meant to. But we can worship it. The baby in the manger was the One who held the stars in place. The man asleep in the boat was the One who made the sea. That is not a contradiction; it is the mystery of the Incarnation, and Athanasius wants us to marvel at it rather than explain it away.

> And without controversy great is the mystery of godliness: God was manifest in the flesh, justified in the Spirit, seen of angels, preached unto the Gentiles, believed on in the world, received up into glory. 1 TIMOTHY 3:16

## Why it matters now

We live in an age that is often happy to admire Jesus as a teacher, a prophet or an example, and uneasy to go further. Athanasius shows why that is not enough. A good teacher can tell us how to live; he cannot remake us. A prophet can tell us about God; he cannot bring God to us. Only the Maker can remake what He has made. If Christ is less than the Word who was with God and was God, then we are still waiting for our rescue. If He is the Word made flesh, it has come.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read John 1:1–14 slowly each day this week, and notice how John moves from creation to Incarnation in a single breath.
2. Ask yourself honestly whether you think of Jesus more as a good teacher than as your Maker come to remake you. What difference would the second make to how you pray?
3. The Lord came to heal, not to make a display. Look at one place where you serve others and ask whether you are aiming to impress or to help.
4. Spend a few minutes each day thanking God that He was never far from you, even before you knew Him.
5. Begin reading On the Incarnation for yourself this week. Its first sections are short and repay slow reading.

# Out of Nothing

> Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear. HEBREWS 11:3

Before Athanasius says a word about Bethlehem or Calvary, he spends three sections of On the Incarnation on the creation of the world. He knows his reader may be puzzled. You are wondering, perhaps, he says in effect, why a book about the Incarnation is talking about the origin of mankind. But it belongs to the aim of the treatise. You cannot understand the rescue until you understand what was made, who made it, and how it came to be in danger.

So he begins with three wrong answers to the question of where the world came from, and one right one.

## Three wrong answers

The first wrong answer belonged to the Epicureans, the philosophers who taught that everything came about by chance, that the universe simply assembled itself without purpose or providence. Athanasius replies with an argument any child can follow. If the world had made itself by accident, everything would be the same, a shapeless mass. Instead we see sun, moon and earth, each distinct; we see bodies with hands and feet and heads, each fitted to its task. Such order, he says, tells us not that things came to be of themselves, but that a cause went before them.

> The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. PSALM 19:1

The second wrong answer was Plato’s. The great philosopher, whom Athanasius notes is “in such repute among the Greeks”, taught that God shaped the world out of matter that had always existed, as a carpenter works with wood that is already to hand. It sounds respectable. Athanasius sees at once what it does to God. If God can only work with material He did not make, then He is weak; He depends on something outside Himself. As he puts it, “God will be on their theory a Mechanic only, and not a Creator out of nothing.” A craftsman, however skilled, is not a Creator.

The third wrong answer came from the sects we now call Gnostic, who imagined that the world was made by some lesser, alien maker, not by the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. Athanasius answers them from the Gospels. The Lord Himself spoke of the One who “from the beginning” made them male and female, and John says without exception that all things were made by Him. There is no second god behind the world, no rival maker. The Father of Jesus is the Maker of all.

## The true doctrine

Against all three, Athanasius sets out what he calls the godly teaching: God made the universe out of nothing, through His Word. Not by chance, because He is wise. Not from existing matter, because He is not weak. Out of nothing, because He is God.

> In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth. GENESIS 1:1

And then he says why. This is the heart of the chapter, and it is one of the most beautiful things in the book. “For God is good, or rather is essentially the source of goodness: nor could one that is good be niggardly of anything.” Grudging existence to none, he goes on, God made all things out of nothing by His own Word, Jesus Christ our Lord.

Stop and consider that. The reason there is a world at all is that God is generous. He did not need to create. He was not lonely or incomplete. He made the world because goodness overflows, because it is the nature of the Good to give. Every star, every sparrow, every human being exists because God did not grudge existence. The world is a gift before it is anything else.

> Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power: for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created. REVELATION 4:11

## Made for more than nature could give

But Athanasius has more to say, and here he lays the groundwork for everything that follows. Of all the creatures on earth, God took special pity on the human race. He saw that, because we came from nothing, we could not by our own nature continue in one stay; left to ourselves we would drift back towards the nothing from which we came. So He gave us a further gift. He did not simply create man as He created the animals. He made us in His own image, giving us a share in the power of His own Word, so that we might know Him and abide in blessedness.

> So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them. GENESIS 1:27

This is a subtle and important idea. For Athanasius, human beings are not naturally immortal in the sense that we could keep ourselves in being. We are creatures, made out of nothing, and by nature we are fragile. What held us in life and goodness was our relation to God — the grace of His image, the knowledge of His Word. As long as we kept our eyes on Him, we would remain as He made us. If we turned away, we would begin to unravel.

He goes on to say something that sounds strange to modern ears but is worth pondering. Evil, he says, in a phrase that echoes the philosophers but is turned to Christian use, “is not, but what is good is”. He does not mean that evil is unreal or that it does no harm. He means that evil is not a thing God made. It is a falling away, a turning from the One who IS towards nothing. That is why sin is so destructive: it is a turning from the source of our very being.

## The goodness of creation

It follows that Athanasius will never despise the created world. The body is not a prison; matter is not evil; the world is not a mistake. All of it was made by the Word, and all of it was made good. This matters for the rest of his teaching. It is why the Word could take a real human body without being defiled. It is why the resurrection of the body is good news and not an embarrassment. It is why, much later in the book, he can argue with the pagans that if the Word is present in the whole universe, there is nothing absurd in His being present in one human body.

> And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good. GENESIS 1:31

There is a caution to set beside this. The Life of Antony, as we shall see, sometimes speaks of the body with a severity that sits uneasily with this doctrine: Antony eats with shame, will not wash, and treats his body as something to be subdued. Athanasius admired him for it. Readers should notice the tension, and hold fast to the better principle, which is Athanasius’s own: the world and the body are the good work of a good God, spoiled by sin but never hateful in themselves.

## Why it matters now

We live among people who are tempted by versions of all three wrong answers. Some think the world is an accident. Some imagine God as a kind of engineer tinkering with material He did not make. Some, disillusioned with the world, treat it as worthless. Athanasius offers a better way. The world is the free gift of a good God, made out of nothing by His Word, and held in being moment by moment by His power. That should make us humble, because we did not make ourselves. It should make us thankful, because every breath is given. And it should make us hopeful, because a Maker who made us out of nothing can remake us.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Take a walk this week with no purpose but to notice created things, and at each one say, This was made by the Word, out of nothing, because God is good.
2. Consider where you treat your life as your own achievement. Thank God specifically for three things you did not make or earn.
3. If you have ever thought of your body or the physical world as unspiritual, read Genesis 1 and ask God to correct your view.
4. Athanasius says we were made to keep our eyes on God. Choose one daily habit that will turn your attention to Him at the start of each day.
5. Read the first five sections of On the Incarnation and mark every place Athanasius speaks of God’s goodness.

# What Was God to Do?

> Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned. ROMANS 5:12

At the centre of On the Incarnation there is a question that Athanasius asks again and again, almost like a refrain. Having described the ruin of the human race, he stops and says: “what was God in His goodness to do?” It is a daring question. It sounds as if God were in a difficulty. In a sense Athanasius means exactly that — not that God was helpless, but that the fall of man had created a situation in which two things that are both true about God seemed to pull in opposite directions. The way he works through the dilemma is one of the clearest pieces of reasoning in the early church, and it is worth following step by step.

## The ruin of the image

First, the problem. God had made man in His own image and given him a law: keep the grace you have been given, and you will live; turn away, and you will die. Man turned away. And the death that followed, Athanasius says, was not simply the end of the body. It was corruption, a slow unravelling of everything God had made us to be. Human beings began to sink back towards the nothing from which they had come.

> For the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord. ROMANS 6:23

Nor did they stop at one sin. Athanasius paints a grim picture of a world that had become insatiable in sinning: adulteries and thefts everywhere, cities at war with cities, nations rising against nations, every new evil followed by another. “the race of man was perishing,” he says; the rational creature made in God’s image “was disappearing, and the handiwork of God was in process of dissolution.” It is a picture of a masterpiece being eaten away.

## Two things God could not do

Now the dilemma. On one side stood God’s truthfulness. God had said that if man sinned he would die. If God simply cancelled the sentence, He would be breaking His own word. Athanasius will not allow that for a moment. “For it were monstrous for God, the Father of truth, to appear a liar for our profit and preservation.” God cannot save us by ceasing to be true.

> God is not a man, that he should lie; neither the son of man, that he should repent: hath he said, and shall he not do it? or hath he spoken, and shall he not make it good? NUMBERS 23:19

On the other side stood God’s goodness. It was unthinkable that creatures made in His image, made to share His Word, should be allowed to perish through their own carelessness or the deceit of the devil. That would be unworthy of Him. Athanasius makes a sharp point here: if God had simply let His work be ruined, it would have shown weakness, not goodness. “For neglect reveals weakness, and not goodness on God’s part,” he writes. Better never to have made man at all than to make him and then abandon him to ruin before the eyes of the Maker.

> Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. ISAIAH 49:15

So God could not simply forgive, because He is true. And He could not simply abandon us, because He is good. What was God to do?

## Why repentance was not enough

Athanasius then considers the obvious answer. Why not simply call men to repent? Surely that would be worthy of God? He answers carefully, and his answer goes deeper than many modern sermons. Repentance, he says, fails on two counts. First, it does not satisfy God’s truthfulness; the sentence of death would still stand unfulfilled. Second, and more profoundly, repentance cannot heal what has gone wrong in our nature. In his words, repentance does not “call men back from what is their nature—it merely stays them from acts of sin.”

This is a searching insight. If our problem were only a list of wrong actions, repentance would be enough; we could stop doing them. But our problem is deeper. We have been infected by corruption; we are dying. A man with a fatal disease is not cured by resolving to live more healthily. He needs a physician who can reach the disease itself.

> Can the Ethiopian change his skin, or the leopard his spots? then may ye also do good, that are accustomed to do evil. JEREMIAH 13:23

Athanasius does not despise repentance; the whole Christian life is full of it. But he sees that repentance is our response to salvation, not its cause. Something had to happen that we could not do.

## Only the Word

Here the argument reaches its resolution. What was needed, he says, was the Word of God, who had made everything out of nothing at the beginning. Only He could do what the situation required. He alone was able to recreate everything; He alone was worthy to suffer on behalf of all; He alone could stand as ambassador for all with the Father.

The Word, then, took a body of our kind. In that body He could die, and so the sentence of death would be truly carried out, and God’s word would stand. But because the body was His own, the body of the very Life, death could not hold it. And so, as Athanasius puts it, two marvels came to pass at once: the death of all was accomplished in the Lord’s body, and death and corruption were done away by reason of the Word united with it.

> But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. ROMANS 5:8

In the cross, God’s truth and God’s goodness meet. Neither is sacrificed. The sentence is carried out, and the sinner goes free. This is what Paul meant when he said that God is both just and the justifier of him who believes in Jesus.

> To declare, I say, at this time his righteousness: that he might be just, and the justifier of him which believeth in Jesus. ROMANS 3:26

## Why it matters now

Much popular religion offers a God who is good but not true — a kindly grandfather who overlooks everything and requires nothing. Much harsh religion offers a God who is true but not good — a judge who keeps the rules and does not care. Athanasius will have neither. The God of the gospel is both, perfectly, and the cross is where we see it. If you have ever wondered whether God can really forgive you without pretending your sin does not matter, here is the answer. He has not pretended. He has paid.

# The Portrait Renewed on the Same Wood

> For whom he did foreknow, he also did predestinate to be conformed to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brethren. ROMANS 8:29

Some teachers are remembered for a sentence, others for a picture. Athanasius left the church one of its best pictures of salvation, and he tucked it into a single short paragraph in the fourteenth section of On the Incarnation. Imagine, he says, a portrait painted on a wooden panel. Over time it has been spoiled by stains from outside, until the likeness can hardly be seen. What does the painter do? He does not throw the panel away. The person whose likeness it is must come again and sit for the artist, so that the portrait can be renewed on the same wood.

Here are his words: “for, for the sake of his picture, even the mere wood on which it is painted is not thrown away, but the outline is renewed upon it.” In the same way, he goes on, the most holy Son of the Father, being the Image of the Father, came to our region to renew man once made in His likeness.

## The image spoiled

To feel the force of the picture we need to remember what Athanasius has already said. God made human beings in His own image, giving them a share in His Word so that they could know Him and live in blessedness. That image was the most precious thing about us. It was what made us more than animals; it was the likeness of our Maker written into our nature.

> And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness. GENESIS 1:26

But the image was spoiled. Men turned their eyes away from God and downwards, towards things of sense. They made idols of wood and stone, worshipped the creature rather than the Creator, fell into magic and the worship of demons. The likeness of God in them was smeared and darkened, until, as Athanasius says, men became so brutish that they seemed hardly rational at all.

> Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools, and changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man. ROMANS 1:22–23

## Who could restore it

Athanasius asks the obvious question. Who could repair such damage? Not human beings, he says, for they are only made after the image; a copy cannot restore a copy. Not angels, for they are not the image of God either. Not creation, for creation was there all along, and men had looked at it and still gone astray. If creation had been enough, these great evils would never have come to pass.

Only one could do it: the original Himself. The Son is not merely made in the image of God; He is the Image of God. So He came, in His own person, to renew the likeness in us. Just as the painter needs the sitter to come back before the portrait can be restored, so the human race needed the very Image of the Father to come among us before the image in us could be made new.

> Who is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature. COLOSSIANS 1:15

This is one of the reasons Athanasius could never accept that the Son is a creature. A creature, however exalted, would be at best another copy. Only the Original can restore the likeness.

## The same wood

Now notice the detail that makes the picture so tender. The painter does not throw away the panel. He works on the same wood. God did not scrap the human race and begin again with a new species. He did not discard our bodies, our history, our nature. He came into that very nature and restored it from within.

There is great comfort here. The stains of sin can make us feel that we are fit only to be thrown away, that God must have given up on us. Athanasius says the opposite. For the sake of His picture, the wood is kept. You are the panel on which God has painted His likeness, and He has not thrown you away. He has come to renew the outline on the same wood.

> Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:17

The new creation is not a replacement of the old but its renewal. That is why Christians hope not for escape from the body but for its resurrection; not for the destruction of the world but for its making new. The same wood, restored.

## Born again

Athanasius links the picture directly to the words of Jesus. The Son came, he says, to find the lost and to save them. And He told Nicodemus that a man must be born again, not meaning, as the Jews supposed, a second birth from a woman, but speaking of the soul born and created anew in the likeness of God’s image.

> Jesus answered and said unto him, Verily, verily, I say unto thee, Except a man be born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God. JOHN 3:3

This is what salvation means for Athanasius. It is forgiveness, certainly; he speaks of the remission of sins. But it is more than forgiveness. It is restoration. The purpose of the gospel is not only that we should be let off, but that we should become again what we were made to be: men and women who bear the likeness of God and know Him.

> And have put on the new man, which is renewed in knowledge after the image of him that created him. COLOSSIANS 3:10

## Why He came as man

The picture also explains, for Athanasius, why the Word had to come in a body. Men had turned their eyes downwards, towards things of sense. So the Word met them there. He took a body like ours, so that those who would not know Him from His rule over the universe might know Him from the works done in His body. Like a good teacher who comes down to the level of pupils who cannot yet follow the higher lesson, He met our senses halfway, so that by seeing Him as man we might come to know Him as God.

> For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:6

## Why it matters now

We live in an age that talks a great deal about identity, and very often we are told to find ourselves by looking inward. Athanasius offers a better way. Your true likeness is not hidden inside you, waiting to be discovered; it was given by God, spoiled by sin, and is restored by Christ. You find yourself by looking at Him, the Image of the Father, and by sitting, as it were, for the Painter, day after day, until His likeness is renewed in you.

# Death Trampled Underfoot

> But we see Jesus, who was made a little lower than the angels for the suffering of death, crowned with glory and honour; that he by the grace of God should taste death for every man. HEBREWS 2:9

When Athanasius comes to the cross in On the Incarnation, he says that it is “the sum of our faith”. Everything he has written so far leads here. The Word took a body for one great purpose: that in it He might die, and by dying undo the death that had taken hold of the human race. He does not treat the cross as a tragic accident or a noble example. He treats it as the centre of God’s saving work, and he asks, with the frankness of a man who expects hard questions, why it had to happen the way it did.

He wrote in a church that still carried the wounds of the fiercest persecution in its history. Men and women he knew, or whose names he had heard from childhood, had gone to their deaths rather than deny Christ; among them was Peter, bishop of Alexandria. So when Athanasius argues that Christ truly died and truly defeated death, he holds two things together that belong together: the cross, where the death of all was accomplished, and the martyrs, who showed that death had lost its sting.

## In the stead of all

The heart of his teaching is simple. The human race lay under the sentence of death. The sentence could not simply be cancelled, for God is true. So the Word, who could not die in His own nature, took to Himself a body that could die, “that it, by partaking of the Word Who is above all, might be worthy to die in the stead of all.” He offered that body to death as a sacrifice free from any stain, and so, Athanasius says, He put away death from all His peers.

> Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. ISAIAH 53:4–5

Notice the words: in the stead of all. Athanasius uses them again and again. The debt owing from all was paid; the death of all was accomplished in the Lord’s body. He does not use the technical vocabulary later centuries would develop, and he is more interested in death and corruption than in guilt and wrath. But the substance is plain. Christ died in our place, and because He died, the law of death has spent its power.

He gives a homely illustration. When a great king enters a large city and takes up residence in one of its houses, the whole city is honoured and protected; no bandit dares attack it, because the king lives there. So it is, he says, with the Monarch of all. Now that He has come to our realm and dwelt in one body among His peers, the whole conspiracy of the enemy against mankind is checked.

## Why not a quiet death

Then come the questions. Why did He not die privately, on a bed, as other men do, instead of being crucified in public shame? Athanasius answers that the Lord was not weak. He could not fall sick, for He healed the sickness of others. And His death had to be public, before witnesses, so that His resurrection could be believed. A man who died in secret and then appeared saying he had risen would be dismissed as telling idle tales.

Why, then, did He not choose some glorious death for Himself? Here Athanasius uses one of his best images. A noble wrestler does not pick his own opponents, lest he be suspected of fearing some of them. He lets the onlookers choose, especially his enemies, so that whoever they set against him, he may throw, and be known as superior to them all. So the Lord accepted the death His enemies chose — the cruellest and most shameful they could devise — so that by overcoming it He might be seen to have overcome death in every form. And so, Athanasius says, something startling happened: “the death, which they thought to inflict as a disgrace, was actually a monument of victory against death itself.”

> And having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly, triumphing over them in it. COLOSSIANS 2:15

## Why the cross

For believers who ask, not to argue but to learn, why He suffered on a cross rather than any other way, Athanasius offers three reasons. First, the curse. If Christ came to bear the curse that lay on us, how else could He become a curse than by the death that the law called accursed?

> Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree. GALATIANS 3:13

Second, the outstretched hands. This is one of the most moving passages in the book. “For it is only on the cross that a man dies with his hands spread out.” It was fitting, Athanasius says, that the Lord should spread out His hands, so that with one He might draw the ancient people and with the other the Gentiles, and unite both in Himself.

> And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me. JOHN 12:32

Third, the air. The devil, Athanasius believed, ruled in the lower air as the prince of the power of the air, and by dying lifted up the Lord cleared the way and opened a road into heaven for us. Modern readers may find this third reason the least persuasive; it depends on ideas of the heavens that belong to his age. But the first two are deeply scriptural, and the picture of the Saviour’s arms spread wide to gather Jew and Gentile together is one the church has never forgotten.

The body that died there did not see corruption. He rose on the third day — not sooner, lest anyone say He had not really died; not later, lest His body be thought another, or the witnesses of His death be scattered. Everything about the timing was fitted to make His death undeniable and His resurrection believable.

> For thou wilt not leave my soul in hell; neither wilt thou suffer thine Holy One to see corruption. PSALM 16:10

## The tyrant bound

What did that death and rising achieve? Athanasius answers by pointing to the martyrs. Before the Saviour came, he says, death was terrible even to the saints; everyone wept for the dead as though they had perished. But now that the Saviour has raised His body, those who believe in Christ tread death underfoot as nothing, and choose rather to die than to deny their faith. Man is by nature afraid of death. Yet he had seen young people, men and women, even children, face torture with a courage that astonished their persecutors. Something had happened to death itself.

> Forasmuch then as the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself likewise took part of the same; that through death he might destroy him that had the power of death, that is, the devil; and deliver them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage. HEBREWS 2:14–15

He gives one of his most vivid pictures. When a tyrant has been defeated by a true king and bound hand and foot, the people who once trembled at him pass by and mock him, no longer afraid of his fury, because of the king who conquered him. So it is with death. Death has been conquered and exposed by the Saviour on the cross, bound hand and foot, and now “all they who are in Christ, as they pass by, trample on him”, scoffing at him and saying what was written of old: O death, where is thy victory? O grave, where is thy sting?

> Death is swallowed up in victory. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:54

He piles up images. If you see a serpent trodden underfoot, you know it has lost its strength. If you see a lion made sport of by children, you know it is either dead or powerless. So, he says, when you see death despised by believers in Christ, do not doubt that Christ has brought it to nothing.

## Come and see

Then he makes an invitation that is both an argument and an appeal. Some, he admits, will still doubt. Very well; let them test it. A man who doubts that fire has no power over asbestos, a substance said not to burn, should put it to the flame and know. In the same way, “let him who is incredulous about the victory over death receive the faith of Christ, and pass over to His teaching, and he shall see the weakness of death, and the triumph over it.”

> O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him. PSALM 34:8

And he goes further. A dead man can do nothing; his influence ends at the grave. But Christ is working every day, persuading multitudes from every nation to turn from idols, making the adulterer chaste and the murderer peaceful, the coward brave. How could a dead man do that? “But this work is not that of one dead, but of one that lives.”

> I am he that liveth, and was dead; and, behold, I am alive for evermore, Amen; and have the keys of hell and of death. REVELATION 1:18

The argument still holds. The best evidence that Jesus is alive is not a museum exhibit. It is a changed life. When a person enslaved to a sin is set free, when a frightened person is made brave, when a dying believer faces the end with peace, the risen Christ is showing Himself to be alive.

## Sown, not lost

For those who believe, death has changed its nature. We still die, since our bodies are mortal. But we no longer die as condemned. We are like seeds sown in the earth; we do not perish in the ground but will rise again.

> It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: it is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:42–43

This is the hope that carried the martyrs, and it carried Athanasius through five exiles. The worst the emperors could do was to kill him, and death had already been trampled underfoot.

A word of care is needed. Athanasius, like many in his age, admired the eagerness with which some Christians rushed towards martyrdom, and his language can suggest that seeking death is itself a mark of faith. The wiser tradition of the church, and indeed the Life of Antony itself, where Antony ministers to the martyrs but will not hand himself over, teaches that we should not throw our lives away. Courage in the face of death is one thing; courting it is another.

But the comfort stands, and so does the warning against a softer cross. Every age is tempted to make the cross only an example of self-giving or a protest against injustice. It is those things, but it is more. Something was done there that could not be done any other way. A sentence was carried out; a debt was paid; death met the Life and was destroyed. If you are afraid of dying — and most of us are — Athanasius does not scold you. He points you to the place where your death died, and to the One who has bound the tyrant.

# That We Might Be Made God

> Whereby are given unto us exceeding great and precious promises: that by these ye might be partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust. 2 PETER 1:4

Near the end of On the Incarnation, having laid out his whole argument, Athanasius gathers it into three short clauses. It is the most famous sentence he ever wrote, and it has been quoted in the Eastern church for sixteen centuries: “For He was made man that we might be made God; and He manifested Himself by a body that we might receive the idea of the unseen Father; and He endured the insolence of men that we might inherit immortality.”

Many Western Christians, meeting the first clause for the first time, are startled or even alarmed. Made God? It sounds like the serpent’s promise in the garden, ye shall be as gods. It sounds like the claims of cults and mystics who say that human beings can become divine. It must therefore be read with great care. But read carefully, in its setting, it is not a boast about human nature at all. It is a hymn to the humility of God.

## What he does not mean

Let us first be clear about what Athanasius does not mean. He does not mean that human beings become additional gods, or that the line between Creator and creature is erased. His whole life was spent defending that line. He fought the Arians precisely because they blurred it, making the Son a creature and yet worshipping Him. For Athanasius there is one God, eternal and uncreated, and everything else is made out of nothing. No creature ever becomes the Creator.

> I am the LORD, and there is none else, there is no God beside me. ISAIAH 45:5

Nor does he mean that we lose ourselves in God like a drop in the ocean. We remain creatures, persons, ourselves. What he means must be learned from the rest of the book.

## What he does mean

Recall the argument. God made human beings out of nothing and gave them the grace of His image, a share in His Word, so that they might live in incorruption. By nature we were mortal; by grace we were meant to share the life of God. Earlier in the treatise Athanasius says that if man had kept that likeness, “being incorrupt, he would live henceforth as God”, and he cites the psalm that Jesus Himself quoted.

> I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High. PSALM 82:6

When he says that we are made God, then, he means that we are brought back into that shared life — freed from corruption, clothed with immortality, brought to know the Father, made partakers, as Peter says, of the divine nature. It is the gift of life, not a change of rank. The sentence’s other two clauses explain the first: He manifested Himself so that we might know the unseen Father; He endured insult so that we might inherit immortality. To be made God, in Athanasius’s language, is to know God and to live with the undying life that is His.

> Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is. 1 JOHN 3:2

The Life of Antony puts the same truth in plainer words. Speaking to the philosophers, Antony says that the Word, unchanged, took a human body for our salvation, “that having shared in human birth He might make man partake in the divine and spiritual nature.” That is the heart of it. He shared what is ours so that we might share what is His.

## The great exchange

The pattern of the sentence is an exchange, and the exchange is all on God’s side. He was made man; we are made to share His life. He came into the body; we come to know the Father. He took insult; we receive immortality. Every movement downward is His, and every gift upward is ours.

> For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich. 2 CORINTHIANS 8:9

This is not a new idea in Athanasius. It is Paul’s idea, and John’s, and Peter’s. The Reformers expressed it in their own way when they spoke of the wonderful exchange in which Christ takes our sin and gives us His righteousness. The Eastern church has tended to stress our share in God’s life; the Western church has tended to stress our share in Christ’s righteousness. Both are true, and both come from the same Scriptures.

> But as many as received him, to them gave he power to become the sons of God, even to them that believe on his name. JOHN 1:12

## Why he held it so fiercely

This sentence explains why Athanasius would not bend on the full divinity of the Son. If Christ were only a creature, then what we receive in Him would be only a creature’s life. A creature cannot make us sharers in God, because a creature does not have God’s life to give. Only if the One who took our flesh is truly God can our union with Him be union with God. The great doctrine of Nicaea, that the Son is of one substance with the Father, is not a piece of hair-splitting. It is the guarantee that the gift is real.

> That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us. JOHN 17:21

## Reading it with care

Because the language is easily misread, a Christian reader should use it thoughtfully. It is safest, in most settings, to say what Athanasius meant in the words of Scripture: that we are made partakers of the divine nature, that we are made sons of God, that we shall be like Him. Where the older phrase is used, it should always be explained, and never be allowed to suggest that human beings can become what God is by nature. The Creator remains the Creator; the creature remains the creature, and is raised to share His life only by grace.

But we should not be so afraid of the phrase that we lose the glory behind it. The gospel does not only forgive us and leave us as we were. It brings us into fellowship with the living God, now in part and one day fully. That is what Athanasius wanted his reader to marvel at. And that is why, having written the sentence, he says that to count all the achievements of the Word made man would be like gazing at the sea and trying to count its waves.

1. Read 2 Peter 1:3–4 slowly and ask what it would mean for you to live today as a partaker of the divine nature.
2. If Athanasius’s sentence troubled you, write down exactly what troubled you, and then read this chapter again and see whether the trouble is with him or with a misreading.
3. Trace the three exchanges in his sentence and thank God for each: His becoming man, His making the Father known, His enduring insult.
4. Consider whether your view of salvation stops at being forgiven. Ask God to show you the fuller gift of sharing His life.
5. Read section 54 of On the Incarnation in full, and pray it back to God as a hymn.

> You showed us the Father, You took our insults, and You clothed us with Your undying life.
> Keep me from pride that would make myself great, and from fear that would make Your gift small.
> Draw me into Your life, until I see You as You are and am made like You. Amen.
# Of One Substance with the Father

> I and my Father are one. JOHN 10:30

On the Incarnation never mentions Arius. The controversy that would fill the rest of Athanasius’s life leaves no mark on its pages. But everything in the book points towards it. A treatise that says only the Maker can remake, only the Image can restore the image, only the Life can conquer death, has already decided what it thinks about the Son of God. When Arius began to teach that the Son was a creature, Athanasius did not need to invent a new position. He only needed to defend what he had always believed.

## What Arius taught

Arius was a presbyter in Alexandria, admired for his learning and his austere life. He taught that God the Father alone is without beginning. The Son, he said, was brought into being by the Father before all ages, as the first and greatest of creatures, and through Him all other things were made. He was divine in a secondary sense, exalted above every other creature, but He was not God as the Father is God. His followers put it in a phrase that became a slogan: there was a time when He was not.

The Life of Antony records the old hermit’s response to this teaching when he came down to Alexandria to denounce it. He taught the people, Athanasius writes, “that the Son of God was not a created being, neither had He come into being from non-existence, but that He was the Eternal Word and Wisdom of the Essence of the Father.” And therefore, he said, it was impious to say “there was a time when He was not”.

Arius’s view had real attractions. It seemed to protect the oneness of God. It fitted the philosophy of the day, which pictured a supreme God far above the world, reaching down through a chain of lesser beings. And it could quote Scripture. Its supporters pointed to texts where the Son is called the firstborn, or where He says that the Father is greater than He, or to the words of Wisdom in Proverbs, which in the Greek version they read spoke of Wisdom being created at the beginning of God’s ways.

## Nicaea

In 325 the bishops gathered at Nicaea and answered Arius with a creed. The Son, they said, is begotten of the Father, only-begotten, that is, from the substance of the Father; God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, of one substance with the Father. The key word was homoousios, of the same substance or being. It was not a word found in Scripture, and many bishops were uneasy with it for that reason. But the council chose it because every scriptural phrase they tried, the Arians could accept and then quietly reinterpret. Homoousios could not be slipped around. It said plainly that whatever the Father is, as God, the Son is also.

> Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God. PHILIPPIANS 2:6

> For in him dwelleth all the fulness of the Godhead bodily. COLOSSIANS 2:9

For the next fifty years that word was fought over. Emperors pressed for vaguer formulas. Councils met and produced creeds that said the Son was like the Father, or like Him in all things, or simply like Him — anything but of one substance. Many bishops signed, some from conviction, some from weariness, some from fear. Athanasius would not.

## Why it mattered

In his great Orations against the Arians, written years after Nicaea, Athanasius argued the case at length from Scripture. But his deepest reasons are already visible in On the Incarnation, and they can be put simply.

First, salvation. Only God can save. If the Son is a creature, then we have been rescued by a creature, and our union with Him is union with a creature, not with God. The whole gift of sharing God’s life falls to the ground.

> For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus. 1 TIMOTHY 2:5

Second, worship. Christians had always worshipped Christ. They prayed to Him, sang hymns to Him, were baptised into His name alongside the Father’s. If He is a creature, then all this is idolatry — the very sin of worshipping the creature rather than the Creator that Paul condemns in Romans. The Life of Antony makes the point sharply: the Arians, it says, “number the Creator, the Lord of all, by whom all things came into being, with those things which were originated.”

Third, revelation. Jesus said that he who had seen Him had seen the Father. That is only true if the Son is not a lesser copy but the very Word of the Father, His own and not another.

> He that hath seen me hath seen the Father. JOHN 14:9

> No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him. JOHN 1:18

## How he read the hard texts

Athanasius did not ignore the texts the Arians cited. He answered them with a principle that remains one of the most useful keys to reading the Gospels. Scripture speaks of Christ in two ways, he said: as the eternal Word, and as the Word made flesh. When He hungers, weeps, grows in wisdom or says the Father is greater, He speaks as man, in the nature He took for our sake. When He says that He and the Father are one, He speaks as God. We have already seen the principle in On the Incarnation, where Athanasius says that things are said of Him as man “to shew Him to have a body in truth, and not in seeming.” Hold the two together, and the difficult texts fall into place.

## A caution

It must be said honestly that Athanasius fought this battle with a hard tongue. He called his opponents madmen and enemies of Christ, and the Life of Antony speaks of Arian madmen whose words were worse than serpents’ poison. Some of them were cruel men who persecuted his people; others were sincere believers, confused or cautious, who deserved patience. He did not always distinguish. We can hold his doctrine with gratitude and still wish that he had defended it with more gentleness.

> In meekness instructing those that oppose themselves; if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth. 2 TIMOTHY 2:25

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read the Nicene Creed slowly and underline every phrase about the Son. Ask what each one guards against.
2. When you pray to Christ or sing to Him, pause to remember that you are worshipping God Himself, not a creature.
3. Take one hard text about Christ’s humanity, such as Mark 13:32, and practise Athanasius’s principle: is this said of Him as God or as man?
4. Consider how you speak about those who disagree with you on important matters. Can you hold firm without contempt?
5. If you have friends who admire Jesus but deny He is God, pray for them by name, and look for a gentle way to ask them who they think He is.

> Lord Jesus Christ, You are God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, of one substance with the Father.
> I worship You, and I am not ashamed to worship You, for You are not a creature but my Creator and my Saviour.
> Give me the courage of Your servant Athanasius to hold the truth when it is costly, and a gentler tongue than he sometimes had.
> Keep Your church faithful to the confession of Your glory until You come. Amen.
# The Spirit Is God

> Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. MATTHEW 28:19

On the Incarnation ends with a doxology. Having set out the whole work of the Word made flesh, Athanasius gives glory to the Father through the Son, “with the Son Himself, in the Holy Spirit”. It is a line that believers had been praying for generations. For most of the fourth century nobody thought to argue about the third name in it. The battle was over the Son. But once the Son’s full divinity had been fought for, a question about the Spirit could not long be avoided. And when it came, Athanasius was among the first to answer it.

## The letters from the desert

During his third exile, while he was hiding among the monks of Egypt, Athanasius received letters from his friend Serapion, bishop of Thmuis, a town in the Nile delta. Serapion was troubled. Some Christians in his region, he wrote, were saying that while the Son was indeed God, the Holy Spirit was a creature — a ministering spirit, higher than the angels perhaps, but made nevertheless. Athanasius replied in a series of letters, known as the Letters to Serapion, which are among the earliest full defences of the Spirit’s divinity. Serapion, a monk before he was a bishop, is very probably the same “Serapion the bishop” to whom the dying Antony left one of his sheepskins.

Athanasius gave his opponents a nickname, the Tropici, because they explained away inconvenient Scriptures as mere figures of speech. And he answered them, as he had answered the Arians, from Scripture and from the logic of salvation.

## One Trinity

His first argument was simple. The church does not believe in a Father and a Son who are God, plus a third being who is something else. It believes in one Trinity, one God, into whose single name believers are baptised. If the Spirit were a creature, then the Trinity would be a mixture of Creator and creature, and Christians would be baptised into the name of God and of something made. That, he said, is not the faith handed down from the apostles.

> Now there are diversities of gifts, but the same Spirit. And there are differences of administrations, but the same Lord. And there are diversities of operations, but it is the same God which worketh all in all. 1 CORINTHIANS 12:4–6

Paul speaks of Spirit, Lord and God in one breath, as the single source of every gift in the church. Athanasius’s case rested on this whole shape of Scripture, above all on the baptismal command of Christ and the blessing with which Paul closed his second letter to Corinth.

> The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost, be with you all. Amen. 2 CORINTHIANS 13:14

## What the Spirit does

His second line of argument follows the same path as On the Incarnation. What the Spirit does shows who He is. Creatures are sanctified; the Spirit sanctifies. Creatures receive life; the Spirit gives it. Creatures are in one place; the Spirit fills all things. Creatures change; the Spirit is unchanging. Creatures partake of God; the Spirit is the One through whom we partake of God.

> Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? PSALM 139:7

> Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwelleth in you? 1 CORINTHIANS 3:16

This last point is the heart of it, and it mirrors exactly his argument for the Son. If the Spirit joins us to God, He must Himself be God. A creature cannot make us sharers in the divine nature, because a creature does not possess it. Paul says that because of the Spirit dwelling in us we are the temple of God. If the Spirit were a creature, we would be the temple of a creature. But we are God’s temple, because the Spirit who lives in us is God.

## Of the Father and the Son

Athanasius also insisted that the Spirit belongs to the Son as the Son belongs to the Father. Scripture calls Him both the Spirit of God and the Spirit of Christ. He is sent by the Father and by the Son; He takes what is Christ’s and makes it known. There is one activity of the Trinity: the Father does all things through the Word in the Spirit. We never meet one Person of the Godhead without the others.

> But when the Comforter is come, whom I will send unto you from the Father, even the Spirit of truth, which proceedeth from the Father, he shall testify of me. JOHN 15:26

> Now if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his. ROMANS 8:9

He was careful, too, not to say more than Scripture allowed. The Spirit is not a second Son, nor a grandson of the Father; Scripture does not speak that way, and we should not try to picture the relations within God as if they were human family ties. Here, as always, Athanasius combined boldness about what God has revealed with reverent reticence about what He has not.

## The fruit of his letters

Athanasius did not live to see the question settled. But his letters shaped the thinking of the next generation, above all the great Cappadocian fathers, Basil of Caesarea and the two Gregorys. In 381, eight years after his death, the Council of Constantinople expanded the creed of Nicaea to confess the Holy Spirit as the Lord and giver of life, who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified. That line is said in churches every Sunday. Much of it was first argued in letters written by a hunted bishop in the Egyptian desert.

## Why it matters now

Many Christians today have a firm belief in the Father and a warm devotion to the Son, but think of the Spirit vaguely, as a force or an influence or a feeling. Athanasius would have us think better. The Spirit is not an it but He, not a power God sends but God Himself, present with us and within us. When we pray, it is the Spirit who helps us. When we are made holy, it is the Spirit who does it. When we are joined to Christ, it is by the Spirit. To neglect Him is to neglect God.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Notice this week how you speak of the Holy Spirit. If you have been calling Him it, practise saying He, and ask what difference it makes.
2. Read Romans 8:1–27 and list everything the Spirit does. Beside each, ask whether a creature could do it.
3. Begin one prayer each day by asking the Holy Spirit to help you pray, as Romans 8:26 says He does.
4. Think of one area where you need to be made holier. Ask the Spirit, who sanctifies, to do in you what you cannot do yourself.
5. Learn the Nicene Creed’s words about the Spirit by heart, and say them slowly as an act of worship.

> Holy Spirit, Lord and giver of life, You are not a creature but God, worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son.
> Forgive me for neglecting You, for speaking of You as a force, and for trying to be holy without You.
> Dwell in me as in Your temple; sanctify what is unholy, give life to what is dead, and join me to Christ.
> To the Father, through the Son, in You, be glory for ever and ever. Amen.
# Known by His Works

> But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts: and be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you with meekness and fear. 1 PETER 3:15

Athanasius wrote for a world that found the Christian message ridiculous. The educated pagans of Alexandria had been raised on Plato and the poets. To them the idea that the supreme God should take a human body, be born of a woman, and die on a cross like a slave was not only false but absurd — a joke in poor taste. The first lines of On the Incarnation admit it: the Word’s appearing among us is the thing “which Jews traduce and Greeks laugh to scorn, but we worship.” Athanasius did not pretend the objections were not there. He answered them.

## Faith is not afraid of reason

The first thing to notice is his confidence. He does not treat the pagan objections as dangerous questions that must be hushed. He takes them one by one and asks whether they hold. Is it absurd for the Word to be in a body? Why a human body rather than something nobler? Why not save us by a mere command? He reasons with his opponents on their own ground, using their own philosophers against them.

> Come now, and let us reason together, saith the LORD: though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow. ISAIAH 1:18

Take the charge that it is unworthy of God to be in a body. “The philosophers of the Greeks say that the universe is a great body; and rightly so,” he replies. And the same philosophers agree that the Word, or Reason, of God pervades the universe, ordering and giving life to it. Very well, then. If the Word can be present in the whole great body of the universe without absurdity, why is it absurd for Him to be present in one part of it, a human body? A man’s power is in his whole body, and no one thinks it foolish that it reaches his toe as well.

## Why not by a word

Another objection went like this: if God made the world by a word, why did He not simply restore it by a word, without all this business of a body and a cross? Athanasius’s answer is profound. When nothing existed, a word of command was all that was needed to make everything. But once man existed and had fallen, the need was different. It was not things without being that needed saving, but a real man, already in existence, going to corruption. The cure had to be applied where the disease was.

And the disease was inside. Death was not something outside our bodies that could be kept at arm’s length by a command; it had become wound into our nature. So life had to be wound into our nature too. He uses the picture of straw and fire. Keep fire away from straw by a command, and the straw is still straw, always liable to burn. But wrap it in asbestos, and it no longer fears the fire. So the Word put on a body, and now the body “has life as a garment, and corruption is done away in it.”

> For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:53

## Known by His works

The heart of Athanasius’s reasoning is that God, who is invisible, is known by what He does. We do not see God; we see His works in creation and infer the Maker. In the same way, he says, we may not see the risen Christ, but we can see His works. Where His name is named, idols are abandoned, fear of death is conquered, and lives are changed. He offers a simple image: “For even a blind man, if he see not the sun, yet if he but take hold of the warmth the sun gives out, knows that there is a sun above the earth.”

> For the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made, even his eternal power and Godhead. ROMANS 1:20

> Believe me that I am in the Father, and the Father in me: or else believe me for the very works’ sake. JOHN 14:11

This is reasoning from evidence, and it is reasoning of a very practical kind. Athanasius does not ask his readers to accept a proof on paper. He asks them to look at the world around them and ask what explains it.

## The hermit and the philosophers

The Life of Antony adds a different note. When Greek philosophers came to the desert to test the unlettered hermit, Antony asked them which came first, mind or letters. When they said mind, he answered that one with a sound mind has no need of letters. Later, when some tried to mock the cross, he answered them first with arguments and then with a question: which is better, faith that comes through the working of God, or demonstration by argument? They admitted that faith was better. “Sight itself carries the conviction of these things,” Antony told them.

> For the preaching of the cross is to them that perish foolishness; but unto us which are saved it is the power of God. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:18

The two approaches belong together. Athanasius shows that the faith is reasonable; Antony shows that it is more than reasoning. Arguments can clear away objections, but they cannot give faith. Faith, as Antony told the philosophers, is “faith through love which is wrought in us towards Christ.” We should give reasons for our hope, gladly and carefully. But we should not imagine that a clever argument will bring anyone to Christ without the work of God.

## A caution

Honesty requires a word about one part of On the Incarnation. Several of its later sections are an argument against the Jews of Athanasius’s day, drawn from the prophecies of the Old Testament. The argument from prophecy is sound and scriptural; the apostles used it constantly. But the tone is harsh and contemptuous. He calls the Jews perverse and irreligious and says that they “are trifling”. Christians who know the long and terrible history that such language helped to feed cannot read these pages without sorrow. We should use Athanasius’s arguments with his confidence, and never with his contempt.

## A pure life to understand

The treatise ends on a note that every Christian thinker needs. To understand the things of God, Athanasius says, it is not enough to be clever. A pure soul and an honourable life are needed. Just as a man who wants to see the sun must clean his eye, so one who would understand the saints must cleanse his life and imitate their works. Reason matters. But the heart matters more.

1. Write down the objection to Christianity that you find hardest to answer. Look for how Athanasius or Scripture addresses something like it.
2. Ask whether you have been afraid of hard questions about the faith. Resolve to face one of them honestly this month.
3. Think of someone who scoffs at the gospel. Pray for them by name, and ask God for an opportunity to give a reason for your hope with meekness.
4. Where have you seen the works of Christ in changed lives? Write one example down, and be ready to tell it.
5. Consider whether any contempt has crept into how you speak of those of other faiths or none. Confess it, and ask for a gentler spirit.

> Lord God, You are invisible, and yet You have made Yourself known by Your works, above all by the works of Your Son.
> Thank You that the faith is not foolish, though the world laughs at it, and that it stands up to honest questions.
> Give me a clear mind to give reasons for my hope, a gentle tongue to give them kindly, and a pure heart to understand Your ways.
> Work faith in those I love who do not yet believe, by the love that only You can give. Amen.
# Swords into Ploughshares

> And he shall judge among the nations, and shall rebuke many people: and they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks: nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more. ISAIAH 2:4

Near the end of On the Incarnation, Athanasius turns from arguments to facts. He has reasoned with the philosophers; now he points to what anyone can see. Look at what has happened in the world since Christ came, he says. Look at what He is doing now. No mere man could have done this. And the evidence he chooses is not miracles of healing or wonders in the sky. It is changed lives.

## What the philosophers could not do

He begins with a comparison that must have stung his educated readers. The wise men of Greece, he says, wrote a great deal about immortality and virtue, and yet they could hardly persuade even a few of their own neighbours. They argued among themselves, envied one another, and their influence ended with their deaths. But Christ, he says, “by ordinary language, and by men not clever with the tongue, has throughout all the world persuaded whole churches full of men to despise death, and to mind the things of immortality.”

> For ye see your calling, brethren, how that not many wise men after the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble, are called: but God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:26–27

That is still one of the great arguments for the faith. The gospel did not spread through the eloquence of its preachers or the power of its patrons. It spread through fishermen and tentmakers, slaves and widows, whose lives had been changed. Athanasius asks his readers to explain that.

## The fierce made gentle

Then he describes what he regarded as one of the plainest signs of all. Before Christ, he says, the nations beyond the empire’s borders lived by the sword. It was impossible to cross land or sea without weapons in hand; the sword was their staff and their support. And yet, when such people heard the teaching of Christ, they laid down their savagery. He has a memorable line for it: “instead of arming their hands with weapons they raise them in prayer.”

> Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God. MATTHEW 5:9

And they did not stop fighting altogether, he says; they changed their enemy. Instead of warring against each other, they now stood against the devil and evil spirits, subduing them by self-control and virtue. They became self-restrained in youth, patient under insult, generous when robbed, and brave in the face of death.

> For we wrestle not against flesh and blood, but against principalities, against powers, against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places. EPHESIANS 6:12

He saw the same change in the new virtue of chastity. In a world where self-indulgence was taken for granted, young men and women were choosing lives of purity for Christ’s sake. Adulterers became faithful, murderers laid down the sword, cowards played the man. Who, he asks, has done this, if not the Saviour?

## A physician for Egypt

The Life of Antony shows the same thing on a smaller scale. Athanasius describes the old hermit in his later years as a kind of physician given by God to all Egypt. He writes that no one came to him in grief who did not go home rejoicing; no one came mourning his dead who did not put off his sorrow; no one came in anger who was not turned to friendship. Poor men went away content, careless monks went away strengthened, and young men went away resolved to live soberly.

> Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. MATTHEW 11:28

There is some idealising in the picture, as there always is when a disciple writes about a master he loved. But the point stands. Where Christ is present, through His people, lives are changed — not only in dramatic conversions but in the steady healing of anger, grief, greed and fear.

## Evidence we can offer

What Athanasius offers here is a kind of evidence every Christian can give. Not every believer can argue with philosophers. But every believer can say, with the man born blind, One thing I know, that, whereas I was blind, now I see (John 9:25). The changed life is the most persuasive argument there is, because it cannot easily be dismissed.

> Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature: old things are passed away; behold, all things are become new. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:17

It is also a searching test. If the proof of Christ’s power is changed lives, then the question comes back to us. Are we living proof or living counter-evidence? Does anyone who knows us see in us the gentleness, purity and courage that Athanasius saw in the Christians of his day?

## An honest word

A careful reader will notice that Athanasius was sometimes too confident. He wrote as though the triumph of the gospel over idolatry and violence was nearly complete, and as though Christian peoples would never again take up the sword against each other. History has not been kind to that hope. Within a few generations of his death, Christians were killing Christians over doctrine and power, and the church has too often blessed wars it should have condemned. Athanasius’s own party in Alexandria, as we have noted, was accused of using force.

> Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven. MATTHEW 7:21

But the failures of Christians do not cancel the power of Christ. They only show that the change He works is not automatic, and that it is always at war with what remains of the old nature. The swords beaten into ploughshares are real, in countless lives. The call is for us to be among them.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Write a short, honest account of one way Christ has changed your life. Be ready to tell it to someone this month.
2. Name one area of your life where the old sword is still in your hand — anger, a grudge, a harsh tongue. Ask Christ to beat it into a ploughshare.
3. Consider someone you know who is weighed down by grief, anger or fear. How could you be, in a small way, a physician for them this week?
4. Where Christians have used force or violence in Christ’s name, pray that the church will repent and learn the way of peace.
5. Read sections 50 to 52 of On the Incarnation and list every change Athanasius says Christ has made in people.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You have changed the fierce into the gentle and the fearful into the brave, and You are still doing it.
> Thank You for every life You have remade, and for what You have begun in mine.
> Take out of my hands whatever sword I still hold, and teach me to raise them in prayer.
> Make me living evidence that You are alive, for the sake of those who do not yet believe. Amen.
# Go and Sell That Thou Hast

> Jesus said unto him, If thou wilt be perfect, go and sell that thou hast, and give to the poor, and thou shalt have treasure in heaven: and come and follow me. MATTHEW 19:21

Athanasius wrote only one biography, and it became one of the most influential books in Christian history. The Life of Antony was written soon after the old hermit’s death in 356, at the request of monks in other lands who wanted to know how he had lived. Athanasius had known him for years. He tells us in the preface that he had seen Antony many times and had been “his attendant for a long time, and poured water on his hands.” The book spread across the empire within a generation. Years later, in a garden in Milan, Augustine heard the story of two imperial officials who had read it and given up everything on the spot, and he could not get it out of his mind. The Life of Antony is the story of a man who took one sentence of Jesus at its word.

## A plain man at home

Antony was an Egyptian, born to Christian parents of good family and considerable wealth in a village near the Nile. As a boy he would not go to school; he wanted, Athanasius says, to live a plain man at home, like Jacob. He went to church with his parents, listened to the readings, and kept in his heart what was profitable. When he was about eighteen or twenty, his parents died, leaving him with a young sister and a large estate.

Not six months later, walking to church, he was thinking about the apostles who left everything to follow Christ, and the believers in Acts who sold their possessions and laid the money at the apostles’ feet. He went in, and the Gospel being read was the story of the rich young ruler. Antony felt as though the words had been read on his account. He went straight out, gave his family’s land — three hundred fertile acres — to the villagers, sold everything else, and gave the money to the poor, keeping a little for his sister. Soon after, hearing in church the Lord’s command not to be anxious for the morrow, he gave that away too, entrusted his sister to a community of faithful virgins, and began a life of prayer and discipline.

> Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. MATTHEW 6:34

## Like the prudent bee

What followed was not a sudden leap into the desert. Antony began humbly, near his own village, learning from an old man who had lived a solitary life from his youth. Whenever he heard of a good man anywhere, Athanasius says, he went and sought him out “like the prudent bee”, gathering from each some provision for the way. He studied the graciousness of one, the prayerfulness of another, the gentleness, patience and love of others, and tried to join them all in himself.

> Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend. PROVERBS 27:17

He worked with his hands, because Scripture says that he who will not work should not eat, and gave part of what he earned to the needy. He prayed constantly. And he listened so carefully to the Scriptures read in church that, as Athanasius puts it, “his memory served him for books.” Antony never learned to read, yet he knew the Bible deeply, because he had heard it with his whole attention.

## Daily beginning

One of the most striking things about Antony, as Athanasius describes him, is that he never thought he had arrived. He believed that progress in the Christian life “ought not to be measured by time, but by desire and fixity of purpose.” He gave no thought to the years behind him, but every day, as if he were just beginning, pressed on with fresh effort, repeating to himself Paul’s words about forgetting the things behind.

> Brethren, I count not myself to have apprehended: but this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus. PHILIPPIANS 3:13–14

After years near his village and in the tombs, Antony crossed the Nile to a deserted fort in the mountains and shut himself in for nearly twenty years. When at last his friends broke down the door, he came out neither wasted nor fat, calm and full of grace, and many were drawn to follow him. Cells sprang up in the mountains, and the desert, Athanasius says, was filled with monks who had left their own people and “enrolled themselves for the citizenship in the heavens.”

> For our conversation is in heaven; from whence also we look for the Saviour, the Lord Jesus Christ. PHILIPPIANS 3:20

## Reading Antony with care

Here the Protestant reader, and indeed any reader, must think carefully. Antony heard a command that Jesus gave to one particular rich young man and took it as a command to himself. Was he right? For him, surely yes; God plainly called him. But the Life of Antony, and the monastic movement it inspired, came in time to suggest that the life of renunciation was a higher kind of Christianity, and that those who married, worked in the world and raised families were settling for something less. The Reformers rightly protested against that two-tier picture. Scripture honours marriage, daily work and family life as callings from God, and the New Testament does not ask every believer to sell all his property.

> Marriage is honourable in all, and the bed undefiled. HEBREWS 13:4

We should also notice, without judging a man in a distant age, that Antony placed his young sister in the care of others to pursue his calling. Athanasius tells us, with evident joy, that she grew old in the same devotion and led other women in it. But families today would rightly ask more questions.

## What remains for us

And yet the challenge of Antony cannot be dismissed so easily. Most of us are not tempted to give away too much. We are tempted to hold on to everything, and to explain away every hard saying of Jesus about wealth. Antony heard the Gospel read and did what it said, immediately. Whatever our calling, that is the spirit we need. The question is not whether we must all live in the desert. It is whether there is anything we would refuse to give up if Christ asked for it.

> For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? MARK 8:36

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read Matthew 19:16–26 and ask honestly what Jesus might be asking you to let go of. Write it down.
2. Antony listened so closely to Scripture that his memory served him for books. Choose a passage to learn by heart this month.
3. Like the prudent bee, seek out one mature Christian this week and ask them what they have learned about following Christ.
4. Measure your progress not by years but by desire. Begin tomorrow as if you were starting the Christian life afresh.
5. Give something away this week that costs you — money, time or a possession — quietly and to someone in need.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You called Antony through one sentence of the Gospel, and he rose and followed You.
> Speak to me through Your Word, and give me the same readiness to obey without delay.
> Loosen my grip on what I own; show me what to give, and to whom, and give me joy in giving it.
> Whatever my calling, in the city or the desert, let me live as a citizen of heaven. Amen.
# Not Against Flesh and Blood

> Put on the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to stand against the wiles of the devil. EPHESIANS 6:11

No one can read The Life of Antony without noticing the demons. They are everywhere. They whisper thoughts, take the shapes of women and wild beasts, beat Antony until he lies senseless, fill his cell with noise, pretend to be angels, and knock at his door. For many modern readers this is the hardest part of the book. And yet Athanasius believed that it described something real, and so did the New Testament he loved. The question is not whether there is a spiritual battle, but how to fight it well — and, just as important, how to tell the voice of God from the voices that only imitate it. Of all the gifts Athanasius praises in Antony, the one he returns to most often is discernment, and in Antony’s long address to the monks the two lessons run together.

## The battle begins in the mind

It is worth noticing where Antony’s battle began. Not with apparitions, but with thoughts. When he first gave himself to God, Athanasius says, the devil tried to turn him back by whispering to him the memory of his wealth, his care for his sister, the claims of family, the love of money and glory, the pleasures of the table, and at last the difficulty and length of the road ahead. He raised in his mind, Athanasius says, a great dust of debate. Then came the temptations of the flesh, night and day, and Antony answered them with prayer, fasting and faith.

> Casting down imaginations, and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God, and bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ. 2 CORINTHIANS 10:5

That is where most of our battles are fought too. Few of us will ever see a demon. All of us know the voice that says, Think of what you are giving up; think how hard this is; think how long it will take. Antony’s first victory was simply that he kept going. And Athanasius is clear whose victory it was. This, he says, “was the Saviour’s work in Antony”. The Lord who took flesh for our sake gave the body victory over the devil, so that everyone who truly fights can say, with Paul, not I, but the grace of God which was with me.

## In the tombs

The most famous scene comes when Antony shuts himself in a tomb some distance from his village. One night, Athanasius tells us, a multitude of demons beat him so severely that he lay on the ground speechless, and a friend who came with bread found him apparently dead and carried him to the village church. At midnight Antony revived and asked to be carried back. Lying on the floor of the tomb, too weak to stand, he called out: “Here am I, Antony; I flee not from your stripes.” Nothing, he said, would separate him from the love of Christ.

> Nay, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him that loved us. ROMANS 8:37

Then, Athanasius says, a ray of light came down, the demons vanished and the pain ceased. Antony asked the Lord where He had been. The answer came: “Antony, I was here, but I waited to see thy fight.” Whatever we make of the details, the truth in it is one every tried believer needs to hear. The Lord is present in the struggle even when He seems absent, and He is watching over the fight.

## The weakness of the enemy

Antony’s address has one great theme: the enemy is weak. Since the Lord came, he says, “the enemy is fallen and his powers weakened.” The demons make noise, take terrifying shapes and threaten, but their very displays prove their weakness. If they had real power, one would be enough; they would not need crowds and costumes. They are, he says, like actors on a stage frightening children. The devil could not touch Job’s cattle without God’s permission; he could not even enter the swine without asking the Lord. Their apparitions, Antony tells the monks, “are nothing and quickly disappear”, especially when met with faith and the sign of the cross.

> Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. JAMES 4:7

What, then, are the weapons? Antony’s answer is not exotic. Prayer. Fasting. The Psalms, which he sang against every attack. The name of Christ. Watchfulness over the heart. And above all a holy life: “a good life and faith in God is a great weapon.” The demons, he says, fear the fasting, the prayers, the meekness, the quietness, the contempt of money and vainglory, the humility, the love of the poor, and chief of all, piety towards Christ.

> Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour: whom resist stedfast in the faith. 1 PETER 5:8–9

This is deeply practical. The best defence against the enemy is not an obsession with him but a life full of Christ. The demons, Antony warns, adapt their attacks to the state of mind in which they find us. If they find us fearful and faint-hearted, they increase our fear. If they find us rejoicing in the Lord and mindful of Him, they are turned back.

## Not every voice is God’s

But the enemy does not always come looking like an enemy. Sometimes, Antony says, the demons take the appearance of monks and speak like holy men. They quote Scripture, repeat the words of the readings like an echo, wake the brethren for prayer, and urge them to fast. They even praise a monk’s discipline and call him blessed.

> Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God: because many false prophets are gone out into the world. 1 JOHN 4:1

Why would the enemy urge a man to pray and fast? Antony’s answer is shrewd. Not for the sake of piety or truth, but to drive the simple to excess and then to despair — to make the life of discipline seem an intolerable burden, so that they give it up altogether. He tells of a time when he was fasting and a figure dressed as a monk came to him with loaves and said, Eat, and cease from your labours; you are a man and may fall sick. Antony saw through it and rose to pray.

> And no marvel; for Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light. 2 CORINTHIANS 11:14

We tend to assume that anything which sounds religious must come from God. Antony knew better. A voice that pushes us towards spiritual pride, or towards excess that ends in collapse, or towards trusting our own experiences more than the Word of God, is not to be trusted merely because it uses holy words. Even when the demons spoke truly, he points out, the Lord silenced them, so that we might learn never to take our teaching from them.

## The mark of the holy

How then can the difference be told? When a holy presence comes, Antony says, it comes quietly and gently. “The vision of the holy ones is not fraught with distraction.” Joy, gladness and courage rise in the soul; the thoughts remain calm. And if there is fear at first, it is soon taken away, as the angel said to Zacharias, to Mary, to the shepherds and to the women at the tomb: Fear not. The coming of evil, by contrast, is full of confusion, noise and disturbance, and from it come fear, dejection, sloth, grief and at last the desire of evil things. So Antony gives the monks a rule: “joy and a settled state of soul show the holiness of him who is present.” And the reverse: “whenever the soul remains fearful there is a presence of the enemies.”

> For God is not the author of confusion, but of peace, as in all churches of the saints. 1 CORINTHIANS 14:33

There is real wisdom here. The voice that accuses without hope, that fills us with panic, that drives us into frantic activity or paralysed despair, is not the voice of the Good Shepherd. His voice may convict us deeply, but it always leads towards peace.

Antony adds a piece of plain advice. When anything appears or presses upon you, do not collapse in fear, but “first boldly ask, Who art thou? And from whence comest thou?” For us the application is seldom about apparitions. It is about the thoughts, impulses and teachings that come to us. Before we act on a strong feeling, or follow a persuasive voice, or accept a new teaching, we should stop and ask: Where does this come from? Where is it leading? Does it agree with the Word of God?

> To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them. ISAIAH 8:20

## Curiosity is not holiness

Antony also warns against a fascination with the future. The demons, he says, sometimes seem to predict things, such as the arrival of travellers or the rising of the Nile. But they are only running ahead with what they have already seen, like a fast runner outpacing a slow one. And even if the knowledge were true, what use would it be? “For none of us is judged for what he knows not,” he says. What we shall be judged on is “whether he have kept the faith and truly observed the commandments.”

> The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law. DEUTERONOMY 29:29

Many believers today are drawn to the promise of hidden knowledge — predictions, special revelations, secret keys to Scripture. Antony’s counsel still stands. Do not pray to know the future. Pray to be faithful in the present.

## Reading the demons with discernment

A careful reader must hold several things together. The Life of Antony is full of stories that should be read with discernment rather than simple credulity: visions of beasts, voices, souls hindered in the air, and a demon with the legs of an ass. Some of these may be the honest reports of a man worn by solitude and fasting; some may have grown in the telling before Athanasius wrote them down. One image, in which the spirit of lust appears as a black boy, reflects a symbolism of darkness that later ages turned to ugly and cruel uses, and it should be left behind. We are not bound to believe every detail, and we should not model our spiritual lives on a hunt for apparitions.

Nor can Antony’s test of peace and joy stand alone. Feelings can mislead; a person may feel great calm about a sinful decision and great distress about a right one. The New Testament’s first test of spirits is doctrinal. Antony would have agreed — he fought the Arians because their teaching denied the Son — but his emotional tests belong under the rule of Scripture, not above it.

> Hereby know ye the Spirit of God: Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God. 1 JOHN 4:2

Yet we should not be so modern that we dismiss what Scripture plainly teaches. There is an enemy. He does tempt, accuse and deceive. And Christ has defeated him. Antony’s great message is not that demons are everywhere, but that Christ is stronger, and that a believer who clings to Him and stays under His Word need not be afraid.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Identify the great dust of debate the enemy raises in your mind when you try to follow Christ. Answer it with a Scripture you can say aloud.
2. When you feel God is absent in a struggle, remember the words, I was here, and I waited to see thy fight. Keep going.
3. Choose one psalm to pray whenever you are tempted this week, as Antony sang the Psalms against his attackers.
4. Think of a religious impulse you have felt recently. Ask of it: Who art thou? Where does it lead — towards humility and peace, or towards pride, excess or despair?
5. If you have been drawn to predictions or secret knowledge, lay that curiosity down and pray instead for faithfulness today.
6. Read sections 5 to 10 and 16 to 43 of The Life of Antony and note every weapon, and every test of spirits, that Antony gives.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You came into the world and broke the power of the enemy; he is fallen, and his strength is gone.
> When I am tempted, bring Your Word to my mind; when I am afraid, show me how weak he is and how strong You are.
> Many voices speak to me, and not all of them are Yours, even when they use Your words; give me the gift of discernment, and keep me under Your Word, so that I do not trust my feelings above Your truth.
> Whatever the fight, let me say with Your servant, nothing shall separate me from Your love. Amen.
# Live as Though Dying Daily

> So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom. PSALM 90:12

When Antony was about a hundred and five years old, Athanasius tells us, he went down one last time to visit the monks of the outer mountain, and told them that this would be his final visit. They wept and embraced him. But he spoke joyfully, “as though sailing from a foreign city to his own”, and he left them a few plain counsels. Among them was a phrase he had used for years, and which he repeated again on his deathbed: live as though dying daily. It gathers up much of his practical wisdom, and it is a good door into the ordinary, daily shape of the life he taught.

## The shortness of life

Antony’s great address to the monks begins, not with demons, but with time. The whole life of man, he says, is very short compared with the ages to come. In the world everything is sold at its price, one equivalent for another; “but the promise of eternal life is bought for a trifle.” If we give up a few acres, or even the whole earth, it is nothing compared with the kingdom of heaven. And whatever we do not give up for God’s sake we shall leave behind when we die anyway, often to people we would not have chosen.

> For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:17

This is why he urged the monks never to say, We have lived in the discipline a long time. Each day was to be a fresh beginning. A servant does not say, I worked yesterday, so I need not work today. Neither should a Christian live on past devotion.

## I die daily

Then he explains the phrase. Paul said, I die daily (1 Corinthians 15:31). If we too live as though dying daily, Antony says, we shall not sin. When we rise in the morning, let us think that we may not live till evening; when we lie down, let us think that we may not rise. Life is uncertain, and Providence gives it to us one day at a time.

> Go to now, ye that say, To day or to morrow we will go into such a city … Whereas ye know not what shall be on the morrow. For what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away. JAMES 4:13–14

This is not morbid. Antony was, by every account, a cheerful man. Athanasius says that his face shone with joy because his soul was at peace. Living as though dying daily frees the heart. If this may be my last day, I will not waste it nursing a grudge, chasing a possession or indulging a secret sin. I will forgive, give thanks, and do the good that is in front of me.

## The sun and your anger

One of Antony’s favourite counsels came from Ephesians. He urged the monks to meditate constantly on the apostle’s word.

> Be ye angry, and sin not: let not the sun go down upon your wrath. EPHESIANS 4:26

He applied it not only to anger but to every sin. Let not the sun set on any sin of ours, he said. Each day, let every one take account of his actions by day and by night; if he has sinned, let him stop, and if he has not, let him not boast. He even gave a practical method: “Let us each one note and write down our actions and the impulses of our soul as though we were going to relate them to each other.” We would be ashamed to sin before our brethren, he reasoned; so if we write our thoughts as though they would be read, we shall be more careful of them.

> Examine yourselves, whether ye be in the faith; prove your own selves. 2 CORINTHIANS 13:5

The practice is worth adopting, in some form, by any Christian. A few minutes at the end of each day, looking back honestly, confessing what was wrong and giving thanks for what was right, does more to keep a soul clean than many resolutions.

## Prayer and the Psalms

Antony’s rule of prayer was simple. “Pray continually,” he told the monks; “sing psalms before sleep and on awaking.” Hold the commandments of Scripture in your heart. Remember the lives of the saints, so that your soul may be stirred to imitate them. Again and again in the Life we see him praying with the Psalms: against temptation, in the tombs, in the fort, on his long journeys. The Psalms were his armour and his food.

> Evening, and morning, and at noon, will I pray, and cry aloud: and he shall hear my voice. PSALM 55:17

## The dignity of work

It would be easy to imagine that Antony did nothing but pray. Not so. From the beginning, Athanasius says, he worked with his hands, remembering that the idle should not eat, and gave part of his earnings to the poor. In the inner mountain he dug a plot of ground and grew his own grain, so as not to be a burden to the brethren who brought him bread, and he grew vegetables to refresh visitors after their hard journey. He wove baskets and gave them to those who brought him supplies. Athanasius notes, almost in passing, that “he was careful to work hard.”

> And that ye study to be quiet, and to do your own business, and to work with your own hands, as we commanded you. 1 THESSALONIANS 4:11

Prayer and work belonged together. The desert was not an escape from labour but a place where labour and prayer were woven into one life.

## A caution

In the same address Antony says that virtue is within us and easy if only we are willing; “virtue hath need at our hands of willingness alone.” Read by itself, this could suggest that holiness is simply a matter of trying harder. That would be a serious mistake, and it is not the whole of Antony’s teaching; the Life insists again and again that his victories were the Saviour’s work in him. We should take his counsel to be willing and diligent, and hold it always inside the greater truth that God works in us both to will and to do.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Tomorrow morning, before anything else, say to God: This may be my last day. Help me live it for You. Notice what changes.
2. Before you sleep each night this week, look back over the day, confess what was wrong, and let the sun not go down on it.
3. Try Antony’s method for a week: write down briefly the thoughts and actions you would not want others to read, and bring them to God.
4. Choose one psalm to pray on waking and one before sleep, every day this week.
5. Look at your work, paid or unpaid. How could you offer it to God as part of your prayer, and use it to serve others?

### A PRAYER

> Lord, my life is a vapour, and my days are in Your hand; teach me to number them.
> Let me live today as though it were my last, forgiving quickly, giving thanks gladly, and letting no sin outlast the sun.
> Teach me to pray at my rising and my lying down, and to work with my hands as an offering to You.
> And when my last day comes, let me go joyfully, as one sailing home. Amen.
# Not Mine, but the Saviour’s

> Not unto us, O LORD, not unto us, but unto thy name give glory, for thy mercy, and for thy truth’s sake. PSALM 115:1

The Life of Antony is full of wonders. People are healed, spirits are cast out, water springs up in the desert. A modern reader may find some of these stories hard to credit, and should read them with discernment. But whatever one makes of the miracles, there is something in the way they are told that is unmistakably true and deeply Christian. Every time Antony is used to help someone, he turns the credit away from himself. Athanasius seems to have loved this about him more than any of the wonders, and it is the part of the book most worth imitating.

## I also am a man

Once, when Antony had shut himself away to pray, an army officer named Martinian came to his door. His daughter was afflicted by an evil spirit, and he knocked and knocked, begging the hermit to come out and pray for her. Antony would not open the door. Instead he looked out from above and said: “Man, why dost thou call on me? I also am a man even as you.” If you believe in Christ whom I serve, he told him, go, and pray to God according to your faith, and it shall come to pass. The man went away believing, Athanasius says, and his daughter was delivered.

> And as Peter was coming in, Cornelius met him, and fell down at his feet, and worshipped him. But Peter took him up, saying, Stand up; I myself also am a man. ACTS 10:25–26

It is striking how exactly Antony echoes the apostles. When people at Lystra tried to worship Paul and Barnabas, they tore their clothes and cried out that they were only men.

> Sirs, why do ye these things? We also are men of like passions with you, and preach unto you that ye should turn from these vanities unto the living God. ACTS 14:15

Antony knew the temptation of the holy man: to let people look to him instead of to God. He refused it.

## Wretched man that I am

The same pattern appears again and again. When the parents of a sick girl brought her across the desert and begged that she be admitted to see him, Antony would not allow it. Go, he said, and if she is not dead, you will find her healed; “for the accomplishment of this is not mine, that she should come to me, wretched man that I am, but her healing is the work of the Saviour.” They went and found her well.

Athanasius draws the lesson himself. Antony, he says, healed not by commanding but by prayer and the name of Christ, so that it was clear to all that it was not he himself who worked, but the Lord. When his prayers were answered, he did not boast; when they were not, he did not complain. He taught sufferers that “healing belonged neither to him nor to man at all, but only to the Lord”, and those who were healed were taught to give thanks not to Antony but to God alone.

> And his name through faith in his name hath made this man strong, whom ye see and know. ACTS 3:16

That last point is the real test of humility in ministry. It is one thing not to claim credit. It is another to make sure that the people we help end up thanking God rather than us.

## Rejoice not in this

In his address to the monks, Antony warned against the pride that spiritual gifts can breed. It is not fitting, he said, to boast in casting out demons or to be lifted up by healing diseases. Nor should the monk who works wonders be esteemed above the one who does not. “For the working of signs is not ours but the Saviour’s work.” And he quoted the words of Jesus.

> Notwithstanding in this rejoice not, that the spirits are subject unto you; but rather rejoice, because your names are written in heaven. LUKE 10:20

The same humility made him flee from fame. When crowds pressed on him and the signs God worked through him began to draw attention, Athanasius says, Antony feared that he might be puffed up, or that others might think of him above what they ought. So he went deeper into the desert, to the inner mountain, among people who did not know him.

## Bowing the head

Antony’s humility showed also in how he treated others. Though he was the most famous Christian in Egypt, Athanasius tells us, he kept the order of the church strictly and wished all the clergy to be honoured above himself; “he was not ashamed to bow his head to bishops and presbyters.” If a young deacon came to him, he gave him the first place in prayer. He asked questions, listened to others, and if anyone said something useful, he admitted that he had learned from it.

> Likewise, ye younger, submit yourselves unto the elder. Yea, all of you be subject one to another, and be clothed with humility: for God resisteth the proud, and giveth grace to the humble. 1 PETER 5:5

When the emperor Constantine and his sons wrote to him as to a father, the monks were amazed. Antony was not. “Do not be astonished if an emperor writes to us, for he is a man,” he told them; be astonished rather that God wrote the law for men and has spoken to us through His own Son. He answered the letters only when urged, and then counselled the emperors to think little of the present, remember the judgment, and care for justice and the poor.

## Why it matters now

Our age is in love with celebrity, and the church has not escaped it. We build ministries around personalities, measure success by attention, and treat gifted leaders as though the gifts were theirs. Antony’s example is a rebuke and a relief. It is a rebuke because it shows how far we have drifted. It is a relief because it shows that we do not have to carry the weight of being impressive. The power is the Saviour’s. Our part is to pray, to obey, and to get out of the way.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Recall the last time you helped someone. Did you point them to God, or did you enjoy their gratitude a little too much?
2. When you are praised this week, practise turning it quietly to God, in words or at least in your heart.
3. If you lead or serve in any way, ask whether people are becoming more dependent on you or more dependent on Christ.
4. Find someone younger or less experienced than you, and ask them what they have learned about God. Listen to learn.
5. Pray for a Christian leader you admire, that God will keep them humble and keep the credit for Himself.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, every good thing that has ever come through me has come from You.
> Forgive me for the times I have taken the credit, enjoyed the praise, and let others look to me instead of You.
> Teach me to say with Your servant, I also am a man; the work is not mine, but the Saviour’s.
> Let my name be forgotten if Yours is honoured, and let my joy be that my name is written in heaven. Amen.
# Fountains of Salvation

> Therefore with joy shall ye draw water out of the wells of salvation. ISAIAH 12:3

Athanasius was, above everything, a man of the Bible. His arguments against the Arians were fought text by text; his treatise on the Incarnation is threaded with Scripture from beginning to end; and the old hermit he admired most could not read, but knew the Scriptures so well that his memory served him for books. At the close of On the Incarnation, having set out his case, Athanasius tells his reader that what he has written is only a sketch. Go to the Scriptures, he says, and you will learn the whole more completely and clearly, for “they were spoken and written by God, through men who spoke of God.”

## The list of 367

Every year, the bishop of Alexandria sent a letter to the churches of Egypt announcing the date of Easter. These festal letters usually carried a pastoral message as well. The thirty-ninth, written in 367, did something that has made it one of the most important documents in the history of the Bible. In it Athanasius listed the books that the churches received as Scripture.

His list of the New Testament names the four Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, the seven letters called catholic — James, two of Peter, three of John and Jude — the fourteen letters he attributed to Paul, including Hebrews, and the Revelation of John. That is the twenty-seven books of our New Testament, exactly those and no others. His is the earliest surviving list to name precisely that collection. He called these books fountains of salvation, from which the thirsty may drink, and he warned that no one should add to them or take away from them.

> Every word of God is pure: he is a shield unto them that put their trust in him. Add thou not unto his words, lest he reprove thee, and thou be found a liar. PROVERBS 30:5–6

It is important to understand what he was doing. Athanasius was not inventing the canon or imposing it by decree. The churches had been reading these books for generations; there had been discussion at the edges about a few of them. What he did was write down, clearly, the books the churches had come to receive, so that believers would not be led astray by the many other writings, some of them forged in apostles’ names, that heretical groups were circulating. He also named a few other books, such as the Wisdom of Solomon and the Shepherd, which he said could be read with profit by new converts, but which were not to be counted among the Scriptures. Readers from different traditions will notice that his Old Testament list does not exactly match their own; the edges of the Old Testament were still debated then, as they are now. But on the New Testament he set down what the whole church has held ever since.

> All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness. 2 TIMOTHY 3:16

## The Scriptures are enough

The Life of Antony shows what this trust in Scripture looked like in a single life. When the monks gathered to hear Antony, he began: “The Scriptures are enough for instruction, but it is a good thing to encourage one another in the faith, and to stir up with words.” That sentence holds the right balance. Scripture is sufficient. It needs no supplement from visions, secret traditions or clever philosophy. And yet believers need one another; the word of a wise elder, the encouragement of a friend, the teaching of the church, all help us to hear what Scripture says.

> Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path. PSALM 119:105

Antony’s own knowledge of the Bible came almost entirely from hearing it read in church and holding it in memory. It was the Gospel read aloud that called him to leave everything; it was the Psalms he sang against every temptation; it was Paul’s words he repeated to himself day after day. And when he lay dying, he charged the monks to keep “the holy faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, which you have learned from the Scripture.”

## A mirror for the soul

Athanasius wrote one short work on a single book of the Bible that has been loved by readers ever since: a letter to a man named Marcellinus on how to use the Psalms. In it he says, in effect, that the other books of Scripture we read as the words of others, but the Psalms we take on our lips as our own. They are like a mirror in which a person sees the movements of his own soul — joy, grief, fear, repentance, gratitude — and they give him words to bring every one of them to God. The one who sings them, he says, learns to make those words his own. He goes on to suggest particular psalms for particular needs: psalms for when you are persecuted, for when you have sinned, for when you want to give thanks.

> Is any among you afflicted? let him pray. Is any merry? let him sing psalms. JAMES 5:13

This is wise counsel, and it fits everything we see in the Life of Antony. Christians who pray the Psalms learn that there is no condition of the heart that cannot be brought honestly to God.

## A pure life to read aright

Finally, Athanasius insists that understanding Scripture is not only a matter of the mind. At the very end of On the Incarnation he writes: “For without a pure mind and a modelling of the life after the saints, a man could not possibly comprehend the words of the saints.” A man who wants to see the sun must clean his eye; a man who wants to understand those who spoke of God must cleanse his life.

> Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God. MATTHEW 5:8

Some readers approach the Bible as a puzzle to be solved or a text to be mastered. Athanasius would say that we cannot master it; it must master us. We understand it as we obey it.

2. If you have been drawn to writings or voices you treat as equal to Scripture, set them in their proper place below it.
3. Take one psalm each day this week that matches the state of your soul, and pray it as your own words.
4. Find one fellow believer to read Scripture with regularly, so that you may encourage one another, as Antony said.
5. Ask God to show you one command of Scripture you understand but do not yet obey, and obey it this week.

> Lord God, You have spoken through men who spoke of You, and given us Your Word as fountains of salvation.
> Thank You for the Scriptures, and for all who kept and handed them on through the centuries.
> Give me a pure heart to understand them, a humble will to obey them, and a thirst to drink from them every day.

# Athanasius Against the World

> Watch ye, stand fast in the faith, quit you like men, be strong. 1 CORINTHIANS 16:13

There is a Latin phrase that has attached itself to Athanasius for centuries: Athanasius contra mundum, Athanasius against the world. He never said it of himself, and it was coined long after his death. It is only half true. But it captures something that every Christian generation has needed to remember. There are times when the truth is held by a few, when the powerful are against it, when councils sign what they should not sign and most people go along. In such times, faithfulness may mean standing nearly alone.

## When the world signed

For much of Athanasius’s life, the faith of Nicaea was out of favour. Emperors wanted peace in the church, and peace, to them, meant a formula everyone could sign. Council after council produced creeds that avoided saying the Son was of one substance with the Father. At Arles in 353 and at Milan in 355, western bishops were pressed to condemn Athanasius personally, and most of them did. Those who refused were exiled. In 359, at the twin councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, the bishops of both halves of the empire were brought, under heavy pressure, to accept a vague creed that said only that the Son was like the Father. Jerome, writing a generation later, said that the whole world groaned and was astonished to find itself Arian.

> Thou shalt not follow a multitude to do evil. EXODUS 23:2

Through all of it, Athanasius would not sign. He wrote, he argued, he hid, and he waited. He did not have armies or the emperor’s favour. He had the Scriptures, a clear mind, and a conscience that would not bend.

## Never really alone

And yet the phrase is only half true, because he was never actually alone. The people of Alexandria loved him and defended him. The monks of the desert hid him for years and never betrayed him. Bishops in Rome and the West received him when the East drove him out. He wrote endlessly to friends and allies across the empire, and they wrote back. When he fled on that night in 356, it was his clergy who carried him to safety.

> Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour. For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth. ECCLESIASTES 4:9–10

There is a lesson here for anyone who must stand for the truth. We may be outnumbered, but we are rarely as alone as we feel. Elijah thought he was the only faithful one left, and God told him there were seven thousand others. Part of standing firm is finding and strengthening those who stand with us.

## Endurance

Five times Athanasius was driven from his city, and five times he came home. Seventeen years of his forty-five as bishop were spent in exile — in Trier, in Rome, in the desert, in hiding. The worst night of all, in February 356, found him in his chair in the church of Theonas while soldiers broke in, calling on his people to sing the psalm whose refrain is that God’s mercy endures for ever.

> O give thanks unto the LORD; for he is good: for his mercy endureth for ever. PSALM 136:1

That choice of psalm says much about how he endured. He did not survive by bitterness or by counting his wrongs. He survived by fixing his mind on the mercy of God, which outlasts every emperor. The Life of Antony records the old hermit’s vision, two years before the Arian party seized the churches of Alexandria, of mules kicking over the Lord’s table. Antony wept at it. But then he comforted his companions: as the Lord has been angry, he said, “so again will He heal us, and the Church shall soon again receive her own order, and shall shine forth as she is wont.” Athanasius clearly held on to that hope.

> And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not. GALATIANS 6:9

## Unity in truth

It would be a mistake to picture Athanasius as a man who loved a fight for its own sake. His goal was not division but a church united in the truth. When he returned in 362, he at once called a council in Alexandria to heal the divisions among those who confessed the faith. He saw that some who had hesitated over the word homoousios, and preferred to say the Son was like the Father in substance, meant substantially the same thing as he did. He was willing to receive them as brothers. He also saw that some quarrels among orthodox believers were about words rather than meanings, and he urged them to ask what each other meant before condemning each other. This generosity helped prepare the way for the settlement at Constantinople in 381.

> Endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. EPHESIANS 4:3

So Athanasius teaches both halves of a hard lesson. Do not buy peace by giving up the truth; a unity built on a vague formula is no unity. But do not make every difference of words a battle; seek out those who share the substance of the faith, and be glad of them.

## An honest caution

We must also say again what was said at the beginning. Athanasius’s enemies accused his party of using intimidation and even violence against rivals, especially the Meletians, and not every such charge can be dismissed as invention. Some of his opponents were genuinely cruel; some were simply cautious bishops he treated as enemies. A man who stands against the world can come to see enemies everywhere, and to excuse in his friends what he condemns in his foes. Standing for the truth does not sanctify every means used to defend it.

> Speaking the truth in love, may grow up into him in all things, which is the head, even Christ. EPHESIANS 4:15

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is there a truth of the faith that is unpopular where you live or work? Ask God for the courage to hold it without apology and without harshness.
2. Name the people who stand with you in the faith. Write to or call one of them this week to strengthen them.
3. If you are in a season of loss or exile, pray Psalm 136 aloud, saying the refrain after each verse.
4. Think of a Christian you disagree with. Ask whether your difference is about the substance of the faith or only about words, and treat them accordingly.
5. Examine whether, in standing for something right, you have excused wrong methods in yourself or your side. Confess it.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, Your mercy endures for ever, longer than every emperor and every exile.
> When the truth is unpopular, make me steadfast; when I feel alone, show me those who stand with me.
> Keep me from buying peace with falsehood, and from making enemies of those who are Your friends.
> Heal Your church, bring her again to her own order, and let her shine as she was meant to shine. Amen.
# Conclusion

> Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised; and his greatness is unsearchable. PSALM 145:3

Sixteen chapters may give the impression that Athanasius taught many different things. In one sense he did; he wrote on creation and salvation, on the Son and the Spirit, on the Scriptures and the desert. But at the centre of it all is one conviction, and it is the conviction of his first book. God has come the whole way down. The Word who made the world has taken a real body, died a real death and risen again, so that what He made might be remade.

Follow that thread, and the whole shape of his teaching appears. The world was made out of nothing by a good God who grudges existence to none. Man was made in God’s image, sharing His Word, and when he turned away he began to slide back towards nothing. God could neither break His word nor abandon His work, and repentance alone could not heal a corrupted nature. So the Word Himself came, the original to restore the portrait on the same wood. In His body the death of all was accomplished, and death was trampled underfoot; the martyrs and the changed lives of ordinary believers were the proof. He was made man so that we might share His life. And because only God can give the life of God, the Son must be of one substance with the Father, and the Spirit who joins us to Him must be God as well. In the desert, Antony showed what that victory looks like in one human life: renunciation, spiritual warfare, discernment, daily prayer and work, humility. And through it all, the Scriptures were the fountains from which every believer must drink, and the truth was worth standing for, even against the world.

## What must be read with care

This book has not hidden the harder parts of Athanasius’s legacy, and it would be a poor tribute to pretend they were not there. He was harsh with opponents, and did not always tell the sincere from the cruel. His party in Alexandria was accused of violence, and not every charge can be waved away. On the Incarnation speaks of the Jews in a tone Christians should not repeat. The Life of Antony is full of demons and wonders that must be read with discernment, and it prizes an ascetic ideal that can make ordinary Christian life seem second-rate. And his great sentence about being made God is easily misread unless it is read in its setting.

None of this is raised to diminish him. It is raised because he himself would have us test everything by Scripture. At the end of On the Incarnation he sent his reader away from his own sketch to the Bible, to learn the whole more fully there. We honour him best by doing just that: reading him gratefully, critically, with the Word of God open.

> To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them. ISAIAH 8:20

## What to carry away

If you carry nothing else from this book, carry two things.

First, carry the wonder of the Incarnation. Do not let familiarity make it small. The One who holds the universe in being lay in a manger and hung on a cross. He did not send a messenger; He came Himself. He did not save us from a distance; He took our nature and healed it from within. Whenever you say the creed, whenever you sing at Christmas, whenever you take the bread and cup, let that astonishment come back.

> And without controversy great is the mystery of godliness: God was manifest in the flesh. 1 TIMOTHY 3:16

Second, carry his courage. Most of us will never be exiled by an emperor. But all of us will face moments when the truth about Christ is unwelcome, when it would be easier to say something vaguer, when the crowd has signed and we are asked to sign too. In those moments, remember the small bishop in his chair in the church of Theonas, with soldiers at the door, calling his people to sing that God’s mercy endures for ever.

> Therefore, my beloved brethren, be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:58

## A last word about the man

Athanasius died in Alexandria in May 373, in his own city, having outlived every emperor who had banished him. He did not live to see the council of 381 confirm the faith he had held. He had simply held it long enough for others to receive it. Every Sunday since, when Christians confess that the Son is of one being with the Father, they are standing where he stood.

And among his possessions was an old sheepskin and a worn garment, left to him by the dying Antony — the garment, Antony said, that Athanasius had once given him new. The Life tells us that those who received such gifts guarded them as a precious treasure. It is a fitting picture of the man: a bishop who gave, and who was loved in return by the humblest and holiest of his people, because in all his fighting he never stopped pointing to the Word made flesh.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Return to the chapter of this book that unsettled you most, not the one you enjoyed most, and work through its application points again.
2. Read On the Incarnation from beginning to end this month, a few sections a day, with a pencil and a prayerful heart.
3. Write down one truth about Christ that you are resolved never to deny, and one habit of harshness you are resolved to put away.
4. Say the Nicene Creed each day this week as an act of worship, not a recitation.
5. Tell one other person what you have learned from Athanasius, and invite them to read him with you.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus Christ, eternal Word, You came the whole way down to us, and You did not come in part.
> Thank You for Your servant Athanasius: for his clear mind, his steadfast heart and his long faithfulness. Forgive us where we follow him into harshness, and keep us where he followed You.
> Fill us again with wonder at Your coming, and give us courage to confess You when it costs.
> Remake in us what You first made, until we see You face to face and share Your life for ever. Amen.
# A Reader’s Guide to Athanasius of Alexandria

> Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path. PSALM 119:105

Athanasius wrote a great deal, most of it in the heat of controversy: treatises, defences, histories, letters. All of it is in the public domain in its original Greek, and much is freely available in older English, above all in the fourth volume of the second series of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers (1892), edited by Archibald Robertson. What follows is a short map.

## Begin here

On the Incarnation. Begin here, and come back here. Fifty-seven short sections on why the Word became man, written in a clear, unhurried style. Robertson’s translation is in the Ochorus library. C. S. Lewis wrote an introduction to a 1944 translation, the essay that urges readers to read old books; it is worth reading for its own sake. John Behr’s modern translation (2011) is careful and readable.

The Life of Antony. The story of the Egyptian hermit, told by a man who knew him. It is in the Ochorus library in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation. Read it for Antony’s address to the monks and his counsels on prayer, discernment and humility; read its wonders with discernment. Robert C. Gregg’s translation (1980) sets it beside the Letter to Marcellinus.

## The defence of the faith

Against the Heathen. The first half of the two-part work of which On the Incarnation is the second: an answer to idolatry and an argument for the one God. Read it after On the Incarnation.

Orations against the Arians. His longest and most important theological work, several discourses answering the Arian case text by text. Demanding, but the place to see how he read the hard sayings about Christ.

On the Decrees of the Nicene Synod. His defence of the council’s language, including the word homoousios. Shorter and more direct than the Orations.

Letters to Serapion. His defence of the divinity of the Holy Spirit, written from exile. Not in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers; C. R. B. Shapland’s translation (1951) is the classic English version.

## Letters and histories

The Festal Letters. Annual Easter letters to the churches of Egypt. The thirty-ninth, of 367, contains his list of the books of Scripture.

The Letter to Marcellinus. A short, warm guide to praying the Psalms. The best thing he wrote for ordinary devotion.

History of the Arians, Apology against the Arians and Defence of his Flight. His own accounts of his struggles and exiles, written to justify himself. Vivid and valuable, but read them as the pleadings of one side.

## About Athanasius

Gregory of Nazianzus preached an oration in his praise within a few years of his death; it is the portrait of a hero by a younger admirer. Khaled Anatolios’s Athanasius (2004) is a clear introduction with translated selections. Timothy D. Barnes’s Athanasius and Constantius (1993) is a searching modern history of his career, not afraid of the hard questions about his conduct. David Brakke’s Athanasius and the Politics of Asceticism (1995) studies his relations with the monks.

## Approach with care

His polemical writings against the Arians, and the sections of On the Incarnation that argue against the Jews, should be read with the cautions raised in this book in mind: gratefully for their truth, sorrowfully for their tone. The Life of Antony’s demonology and its ascetic ideal should be weighed by Scripture, not simply copied.

## Three counsels for reading him

Read him slowly. His arguments are simple, but they build, and the force of On the Incarnation lies in following them step by step.

Read him with the Bible open. He would insist on it. He called his own book a sketch and sent his reader to the Scriptures to fill it in.

Read him as worship. Behind every argument is a man astonished that the Word became flesh. Let his astonishment become yours, and let it lead you to the One he spent his life defending.

> For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. JOHN 3:16
