# Introduction

> If we believe not, yet he abideth faithful: he cannot deny himself. 2 TIMOTHY 2:13

Hudson Taylor was a slightly built Yorkshireman, the son of a Barnsley chemist, who never held an important pulpit and never wrote a book of theology. He went to China at twenty-one with a half-finished medical training and no money of his own. When he died at Changsha in 1905, the mission he had founded forty years earlier had placed hundreds of men and women across the empire, and the churches it helped to plant would outlast the empire itself.

But the reason his name still means something to Christians who will never go near China is not his achievement. It is the way he lived. More than almost anyone of his century, he took the promises of God at their plain meaning and acted on them, in money, in danger and in grief, and left a record of what happened.

## One conviction beneath all the others

Read Taylor for any length of time and one conviction comes up underneath everything he says: God is faithful, and can be trusted absolutely. He learned it as a boy, when a tract about the finished work of Christ taught him that there was nothing left for him to do but receive. He tested it as a medical student, when he gave away his last coin and found it returned fourfold by the next morning’s post. He staked a mission on it on the sands at Brighton in 1865. And he came to rest in it in 1869, when he saw that he was not to lean on the strength of his own faith but on the faithfulness of Christ.

> Faithful is he that calleth you, who also will do it. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:24

Everything else he taught flows from that spring. Prayer, for Taylor, was business transacted with a God who keeps His word. Finance without debt or appeal was that trust written into a ledger. Abiding in Christ was resting on the Faithful One instead of straining after feelings. His meditations on the Song of Solomon are about a Bridegroom who never withdraws and a bride who drifts and is received back. And his burden for inland China was what that faithfulness looks like when it faces a million people a month dying without having heard.

That is the thread of this book. Do you trust your faith, or do you trust the Faithful One?

## Why he still matters

Taylor is worth reading, first, because he is honest. A Retrospect, his best-known book, is full of his own failures: his clutching at a half-crown, his reluctance to leave a friend, his fear of taking responsibility for other lives. He tells these stories against himself so that the credit goes to God.

He is worth reading, second, because he joined the inner life and the outer life without a seam. The man who wrote Union and Communion, a tender meditation on the love of Christ for His people, also ran a large mission, balanced its accounts and travelled tens of thousands of miles for it.

> Not slothful in business; fervent in spirit; serving the Lord. ROMANS 12:11

He is worth reading, third, because he brings the Great Commission down out of theory. For Taylor, Go ye into all the world was a command given by a Master who meant it, and the only question was how to obey.

## How this book is arranged

After a chapter on his life, eighteen short chapters set out his teaching. The first five follow the lessons he learned before he sailed: the finished work, consecration, prayer, the faithfulness of God, and money. The next six come from his great inner discovery and from Union and Communion: the exchanged life, the living water, the unsatisfied life, communion broken and restored, what we are before what we do, and the morning watch. Three come from Separation and Service and A Ribband of Blue. The last four take up his outward calling: self-denial, the burden for China’s millions, guidance, and suffering.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. Taylor’s own addresses often end with a searching question put to the hearer; he did not think a truth understood until it was obeyed. Read a chapter a day, or one a week. Stop at the application points. Pray the prayer, or better, pray your own.

## Where he must be read with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and it is better to say plainly where Taylor must be weighed rather than copied.

First, his early intensity was costly. As a student he lived for long stretches on brown bread and apples, and in China he drove himself until his health broke more than once. Not every young reader should copy the diet along with the dedication.

Second, his calling laid heavy costs on others. His wife Maria and several of their children died in China; other children grew up in England far from their parents; colleagues bore hardships they had not always foreseen. Taylor felt these losses deeply, and they should not be tidied away.

Third, he worked in an age of unequal treaties forced on China by Western guns, and of an opium trade that Britain protected by force. Taylor opposed that trade and grieved over it. But the treaties opened the interior to missionaries as well as merchants, and the mission he founded could not wholly escape the shadow they cast.

Fourth, his reading of the Song of Solomon is allegorical throughout, and in places he builds more on details of the poem than they will bear. His warm doctrine of union with Christ does not depend on those details, and chapters ten to twelve try to keep the one while holding the other loosely.

Fifth, his principle of never appealing for money has blessed many and burdened some. It can harden into a test of spirituality that condemns faithful people who work differently, or into a quiet boast. Chapter seven takes this up.

The counsel of this book is the counsel of Scripture: Prove all things; hold fast that which is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Where Taylor is faithful — on the sufficiency of Christ, on prayer, on obedience, on the lostness of the unreached — receive it gladly and act on it. Where he presses further than Scripture goes, set it aside and lose nothing.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself honestly whether you rely more on the strength of your own faith or on the faithfulness of God.
2. Choose a pace for this book — a chapter a day or a chapter a week — and decide now when you will read it.
3. Write down one promise of God you believe in theory but have never yet acted on.
4. Resolve to test everything in these pages against Scripture, keeping what is true and setting aside what is not.
5. Ask God to use this book to send you not to Hudson Taylor but to Hudson Taylor’s Master.

### A PRAYER

> Faithful God, You have never failed one of Your children, though we have failed You more often than we can count.
> Teach me what Your servant Hudson Taylor learned: that the work is Yours, the supply is Yours, and the keeping of my soul is Yours.
> Keep me from admiring a missionary and ignoring his Master. Where he is faithful to Your Word, let me receive it; where he is not, give me discernment.
> And if You have anything for me to do for the millions who have never heard of You, make me willing to hear it and to obey. Amen.
# A Son for China

> Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee. JEREMIAH 1:5

A praying father. James Hudson Taylor was born on the twenty-first of May, 1832, in Barnsley, a mining and linen town in the West Riding of Yorkshire. His father, James Taylor, was a chemist and a Methodist lay preacher; his mother, Amelia, was a gentle and deeply devout woman. Some years before the boy was born his father had been stirred by reading about China, and knowing he could never go there himself, he prayed that if God gave him a son, that son might be called to labour in the vast empire that seemed then so closed to the gospel. Hudson did not learn of that prayer until he came home from China more than seven years after he first sailed. He was a delicate child.

The finished work. He grew up in the faith of his home without possessing it, and in his teens was glad of sceptical friends who seemed to offer a way out of a religion he could not make work. In June 1849, when he was seventeen, with his mother away from home, he picked up a gospel tract from a basket in his father’s library, meaning to read the story and skip the sermon. The phrase the finished work of Christ stopped him. If the whole work was finished and the whole debt paid, what was there left for him to do? Nothing, he saw, but to fall on his knees and accept the Saviour. That same afternoon his mother, many miles away, had locked herself in her room to pray for him until she was assured that it was done. Within months, alone in his room, he gave himself to God for any service, and soon afterwards came the settled conviction that the service was to be in China.

Hull and London. He prepared with an almost fierce thoroughness. He studied Chinese from what books he could find, took up medicine, and in 1851 went to Hull as assistant to a doctor. There he tithed his whole income, moved into a single cheap room so that he could give more, lived largely on oatmeal and rice, and began what he called learning to move man, through God, by prayer alone. In 1852 he went to London to study at the London Hospital, living for a time on brown bread and apples and walking miles each day. He nearly died of a fever caught from a pricked finger in the dissecting room, and came through it convinced that he had work to do in China.

Shanghai in wartime. On the nineteenth of September, 1853, he sailed from Liverpool in the Dumfries under the Chinese Evangelisation Society, a small and poorly managed body, and reached Shanghai on the first of March, 1854. He landed in the middle of a civil war. Rebels held the Chinese city; an imperial army besieged it; the price of living was ruinous and his allowance inadequate. The first years were lonely, dangerous and hard. He travelled up the rivers and canals preaching and giving away Scriptures, and in 1855, on the advice of the veteran missionary Walter Medhurst, he put on Chinese dress, shaved his forehead and wore the queue. Many Europeans thought it absurd. He found that people listened.

William Burns. For some months in 1855 and 1856 he travelled in company with William Burns, a Scottish evangelist who had seen revival at home and had come to China to preach. Burns adopted Chinese dress after watching the difference it made to Taylor. Taylor learned from Burns a love of Scripture and a conviction that evangelism by ordinary men and women was the great work of the Church. Together they went south to Swatow, but a fire, a robbery and the outbreak of war kept Taylor from returning.

Ningbo and Maria. In the autumn of 1856 he settled in Ningbo. The following year, troubled that his society was repeatedly in debt, he resigned from it and depended on God alone. In January 1858 he married Maria Dyer, the orphaned daughter of a pioneer missionary to the Chinese, a woman of strong faith and sound judgement who had been living in Ningbo for some years. When the doctor who ran the mission hospital had to leave in 1859, Taylor took it over with no guaranteed funds, and it did not close. But by 1860 his health had broken, and the Taylors sailed for England.

The map on the wall. He was away for more than five years. He completed his medical qualifications, worked with F. F. Gough on a revision of the New Testament in the Ningbo dialect, and sat in his study facing a large map of China on which eleven inland provinces had no Protestant missionary at all. The burden became unbearable. On Sunday the twenty-fifth of June, 1865, at Brighton, unable to sit among a thousand comfortable Christians while millions perished, he walked out alone on the sands, surrendered himself to God for the work, and asked for twenty-four willing workers, two for each of the eleven provinces and two for Mongolia. That year, with the help of friends, above all William Berger, the China Inland Mission was formed. Its principles were plain: workers from any denomination, no guaranteed salaries, no debt, and no personal solicitation of funds.

The Lammermuir and after. On the twenty-sixth of May, 1866, Taylor, Maria, their four children and a party of sixteen missionaries sailed from London in the tea clipper Lammermuir, and after a voyage that twice nearly ended in shipwreck reached Shanghai that autumn. The years that followed were the hardest of his life. The party quarrelled, partly over Chinese dress. In 1867 their eldest daughter, Grace, died at the age of eight. In August 1868 a mob attacked the mission house at Yangzhou, and several of the party, Maria among them, were injured; British officials took the affair up in a way Taylor had not sought, and he was attacked in Parliament and the press. In 1869, worn out by his own spiritual failures, he read a letter from his colleague John McCarthy and came into the rest in Christ that changed the rest of his life. It came none too soon. In 1870 his son Samuel died, the older children were sent home to England, and in July, after the birth and death of a baby son, Maria died at Zhenjiang, aged thirty-three.

Jennie and the widening work. In 1871, in England, he married Jennie Faulding, who had sailed in the Lammermuir party, and who shared his work for more than thirty years. The mission grew beyond anything he had asked at Brighton. He prayed for eighteen workers in 1875, for seventy in 1881 and for a hundred in 1886, and they came. In 1885 seven well-known young men from Cambridge sailed to join it, and their going stirred the universities of Britain and beyond. Councils were formed in North America and Australia. He travelled constantly, and spoke against the opium trade that Britain had forced upon China.

The Boxer year and the end. In 1900 the Boxer uprising swept north China, and the mission lost fifty-eight missionaries and twenty-one children, along with many Chinese believers. Taylor, old and ill in Switzerland, was nearly broken by the news. The mission refused to claim compensation for its losses. He handed its leadership to Dixon Hoste, and in 1904 Jennie died. In the spring of 1905 he went back to China once more, and on the third of June, at Changsha, the capital of Hunan, a province that had once been fiercely closed to foreigners, he died quietly in the evening. He was buried at Zhenjiang, beside Maria and the children who had gone before him.

2. His conversion came through a single phrase in a tract. Consider what small thing you could leave in someone’s hands this week.
3. He prepared for China years before he went. Ask what God may be preparing you for, and what preparation you are neglecting.
4. The hardest years of his life came after his greatest act of faith. If you are in such a season, do not conclude that God has left you.
5. Choose one of Taylor’s own writings in the Ochorus library and read its first chapter this week.

# The Finished Work

> When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost. JOHN 19:30

Everything Hudson Taylor taught began in an old warehouse in Barnsley on a summer afternoon in 1849. A bored teenager with a free day found a basket of pamphlets and picked out a gospel tract, expecting a story at the beginning and a sermon at the end, which he meant to leave for those who liked it.

He never got as far as leaving it. A single phrase caught him: “The finished work of Christ.” At once the words of Jesus on the cross came to mind: It is finished. What was finished? A full and perfect atonement for sin. The debt had been paid by the Substitute. Then came the question that changed his life. If the whole work was finished and the whole debt paid, what was left for him to do?

## Nothing left to do

There was, he writes, “nothing in the world to be done but to fall down on one’s knees, and accepting this Saviour and His salvation, to praise Him for evermore.”

Taylor had grown up in a devout Methodist home, and he had often tried, he says, to make himself a Christian. He had failed, and concluded that for some reason he could not be saved. He had been treating salvation as a task to achieve, and he could not achieve it.

> For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast. EPHESIANS 2:8–9

The tract turned the whole thing round. Salvation was not a work for him to do but a work already done by Another. His part was to receive it. The logic of that discovery runs through everything he wrote. The man who would later be exhausted by his own efforts at holiness, and who found rest only when he saw that Christ was his holiness as well as his pardon, was learning the lesson of the Barnsley warehouse a second time.

## The Substitute

Taylor’s gospel had hard edges. In his New Year’s address Under the Shepherd’s Care he insists on the substitutionary character of Christ’s death as the plain teaching of Isaiah 53 and of the whole Bible. The God of the Bible, he says, is a God who punishes sin and cannot pardon without atonement.

> But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed. ISAIAH 53:5

A finished work is only good news if there was a debt to finish. Taylor’s joy in the tract was the joy of a man who had understood that his sins were real, that they deserved judgement, and that the judgement had fallen on Christ instead of on him. In the address Self-Denial versus Self-Assertion he sums up the believer’s standing with a sentence that leaves no room for pride: “As unbelievers, we were condemned criminals; as believers, we are pardoned criminals.”

> Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed. 1 PETER 2:24

It shaped his preaching in China. He could have preached a moral system, and his hearers had several of their own. Instead he preached the finished work. In Ningbo in 1857 one of the first to profess faith under his ministry was an officer of a reformed Buddhist sect who stood up after such a sermon and said he had sought the truth for years in Confucianism, Buddhism and Taoism and had found rest in what he heard that night. The message did not ask him to climb. It told him the climbing had been done.

## A prayer already answered

Taylor always told his conversion together with his mother’s prayer. She was away from home that day, and rose from the dinner table with such a yearning for her son that she went to her room, turned the key, and resolved not to come out until her prayer was answered. After some hours she could only praise. When she came home a fortnight later and he ran to tell her his news, she said she already knew. His sister, too, had resolved in her diary to pray daily until he was converted.

> The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much. JAMES 5:16

From the beginning, he says, he was led to feel that the promises were very real, and that “prayer was in sober matter of fact transacting business with God.” He never separated the two: salvation is wholly God’s work, and God is pleased to do His work in answer to the prayers of His people.

## Joy, not gloom

One remark in the same chapter tells us much. In his boyhood, he says, conversion was commonly called becoming serious, and judging by the faces of some who professed it, it was a very serious matter indeed. Would it not be well, he asks, if the people of God had tell-tale faces, showing the gladness of salvation so plainly that the unconverted would have to call conversion becoming joyful instead?

> Rejoice in the Lord alway: and again I say, Rejoice. PHILIPPIANS 4:4

A man who has understood that the work is finished has nothing left to prove. He can give himself away without anxiety, because nothing he gives is buying anything. Taylor’s later life had more sorrow in it than most, but underneath lay the settled gladness of a pardoned criminal whose debt was paid.

> There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit. ROMANS 8:1

## Where this leaves us

There are two ways of misusing this chapter. One is to treat the finished work as a doctrine for the beginning of the Christian life, and then to move on to the real business of effort and achievement. Taylor tried to live like that for years, and nearly gave up the mission field. The finished work is not the doorway to the Christian life; it is the ground it stands on all the way through.

The other is to make it an excuse for idleness. The very first thing he did after his conversion was to ask God for something to do for Him, as an outlet for love and gratitude. The work that saves is finished. The work that love does in response has only just begun.

> For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God’s. 1 CORINTHIANS 6:20

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself whether, in practice, you are still trying to make yourself a Christian. Name the effort you are relying on, and lay it down.
2. Read John 19:28–30 slowly, and write in a sentence what it means for you that the work is finished.
3. Take one sin that still troubles your conscience and thank God aloud that its debt was paid by the Substitute.
4. If someone is praying for your salvation, or you for theirs, write down that name and pray today with the confidence that prayer is real business with God.
5. Consider whether your face tells the people around you that your faith is a joy or a burden.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You cried It is finished, and it was. I have nothing to add to Your work and no right to try.
> Forgive me for the years in which I have lived as if my standing with God depended on my efforts. I am a pardoned criminal, and You paid what I owed.
> Let the gladness of that finished work show in my face and in my giving, so that others may see that conversion is becoming joyful.
> And make me as eager to serve You out of gratitude as I once was to earn what You had already given. Amen.
# Not My Own

> I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. ROMANS 12:1

A few months after his conversion, Hudson Taylor spent another free afternoon alone in his room with God. In the gladness of his heart, he says in A Retrospect, he poured out his gratitude to the One who had saved him when he had given up all hope, and asked for some work to do for Him as an outlet for love. It did not matter what, however trying or however trivial.

What followed was, by his own account, a moment of unreserved consecration. He put himself, his life, his friends and his all upon the altar, and a deep solemnity came over him with the assurance that the offering was accepted. The presence of God became, he says, unutterably real. Though he was not yet sixteen by his own reckoning, he lay stretched on the ground, silent before God, in unspeakable awe and unspeakable joy.

## A very practical consciousness

He did not know for what service he had been accepted. But in his own words, “a deep consciousness that I was no longer my own took possession of me, which has never since been effaced.”

> What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own? 1 CORINTHIANS 6:19

It has been, he adds, “a very practical consciousness.” He gives an example. Two or three years later a doctor who was his friend and teacher offered him favourable terms for medical study, on condition that he bind himself as an apprentice. Taylor declined. He did not know when or how the One whose alone he was might call for service, and he felt he dared not give away what was not his to give.

This is consecration with its sleeves rolled up. Many Christians have had moments of solemn dedication; far fewer have let one decide a contract. Taylor understood that if he was not his own, then his time, his career and his future were not his to sign away, and he acted on it at some cost.

## The altar and the cords

In Union and Communion Taylor comes back to this theme with the wisdom of fifty years. Commenting on the bride’s plea, Set me as a seal upon thine heart, he quotes the Psalmist’s prayer about binding the sacrifice to the horns of the altar, and then observes that it is comparatively easy to lay the sacrifice on the altar; the hard thing is keeping it there. That requires, in his phrase, divine compulsion, the cords of love.

> God is the LORD, which hath shewed us light: bind the sacrifice with cords, even unto the horns of the altar. PSALM 118:27

The young man in his bedroom was sincere; the old man knew how often sincere offerings are quietly taken back. The answer is not a fiercer resolution but a deeper grip of love. Taylor did not trust his own constancy. He asked to be bound, and trusted the love of Christ to do the binding.

## Lord of all, or not at all

In Separation and Service Taylor presses the logic of consecration to its end. Writing on the blessing of Numbers 6, he says that many Christians fail to see there can be but one Lord, and that “those who do not make God Lord of all do not make Him Lord at all.” The slightest reservation, he argues, shows that we still hold ourselves as our own, free to give God as much or as little as we think fit.

> Know ye not, that to whom ye yield yourselves servants to obey, his servants ye are to whom ye obey? ROMANS 6:16

Then he adds the other side. If we have nothing of our own, because we and all we have are His, then in return all He has and all He is become ours. He calls it a blessed portion. Who, he asks, would not wish to have no private property in himself, in his members, his possessions, his family and his affections? But he meant it as an exchange, and he was sure the exchange was in our favour.

> For all things are yours; … And ye are Christ’s; and Christ is God’s. 1 CORINTHIANS 3:21–23

Taylor would have been happy with the link Paul makes. Everything is ours because we are Christ’s. The one who gives himself away receives everything.

## The wardrobe and the library

Taylor’s consecration also reached his cupboards. In Hull a friend pointed him to the Scriptures about the return of Christ. He studied them and became convinced that the Lord might come at any hour, and that the right response was to live as a man waiting for his master. The practical result, he says, was that he went through his little library and his small wardrobe to be sure they held nothing he would be sorry to give account of if the Master came at once. The library was considerably diminished, to the benefit of some poor neighbours.

> Let your loins be girded about, and your lights burning; And ye yourselves like unto men that wait for their lord. LUKE 12:35–36

He repeated the exercise throughout his life, going through his house from basement to attic, and says he never did so without a great accession of spiritual joy. He believed that most of us accumulate, through thoughtlessness or pressure of work, things that would be useful to others and are useless to us, and that we lose blessing by keeping them. It is one of the most searching pages in A Retrospect because it is so ordinary.

## What this does not mean

Two cautions belong here. First, consecration is a response to grace, not a way of earning it. Taylor’s self-giving came after the finished work, not before it, and he never confused the two. Separation and Service is emphatic that even the best offerings of a consecrated life are accepted only through Christ.

Second, a single moment of consecration, however real, is not the whole of the Christian life. Taylor’s account shows a boy giving everything he knew about, and then an old man discovering how much more there was to give and how hard it is to keep giving it. Those who have never had a dramatic moment should not despair. Those who have should not rest on it. The call is the same for both.

> And he said to them all, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me. LUKE 9:23

Taylor’s afternoon on the floor of his bedroom was the beginning of a life, not a substitute for one.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Write down, honestly, what you still treat as your own: time, money, reputation, plans, a relationship, a child.
2. Consider whether any commitment you are about to make would bind you in a way that leaves God no room to call you.
3. If you have made a consecration in the past, ask whether you have quietly taken any part of it back, and give it again.
4. Go through one room of your house this month with Taylor’s question: what here would I be sorry to give account of if the Master came today? Give it away.
5. Ask God not for a stronger resolve but for the cords of His love to hold you to what you have given.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You bought me with a price, and I am not my own. Forgive me for living as though I were.
> I give You again all I am and all I have, and I confess that I have often taken back what I once laid on the altar.
> Bind me there with the cords of Your love, for my own resolve will not hold me.
> Make my consecration practical: in my diary, my wallet, my cupboards and my plans. And let me find, as Your servant did, that all You are is mine. Amen.
# Moving Man through God

> Is any thing too hard for the LORD? GENESIS 18:14

When Hudson Taylor looked ahead to China as a young man in Hull, one prospect weighed on him more than danger or disease. He would be far from human help, dependent on God alone. He did not doubt that God would be faithful; he feared his own faith might fail. So he set himself a course of training, summed up in a sentence that became the motto of his life: “When I get out to China, I shall have no claim on any one for anything; my only claim will be on God. How important, therefore, to learn before leaving England to move man, through God, by prayer alone.”

## A forgetful employer

The first exercise was modest. His employer, a busy and kindly doctor, had asked Taylor to remind him when his salary fell due. Taylor resolved not to remind him directly, but to ask God to bring it to his mind. The quarter-day passed; Taylor went on praying, and his money ran down until, one Saturday night, he had a single half-crown left.

What happened next is told in chapter six of this book. Here we follow the salary. A fortnight later he was in the same plight, with the added burden that his landlady would need her rent on Saturday. Ought he to speak? To do so would be, to his own mind at least, an admission that he was not fit for China. He gave most of Thursday and Friday to prayer, and on Saturday morning felt assured that he should wait.

> My soul, wait thou only upon God; for my expectation is from him. PSALM 62:5

At five that afternoon the doctor leaned back in his chair and asked, out of nowhere, whether Taylor’s salary was not due again. Taylor, with his eyes fixed on a boiling pan so that his face would not give him away, said it was overdue. The doctor was sorry; he had just sent all his money to the bank. Taylor fled the room with his pan and poured out his heart to God. That night, near ten o’clock, as he was about to leave, the doctor came back laughing: one of his richest patients had just called, unable to rest, to pay his bill in bank notes. The doctor handed some of them to Taylor. He went home, he says, to praise the Lord that after all he might go to China.

## Why it mattered so much

It would be easy to smile at this; the money was owed to him. But the question uppermost in his mind was not the money but whether he could trust God in China. He was testing himself in small things before the stakes became large.

> He that is faithful in that which is least is faithful also in much. LUKE 16:10

He drew the moral himself: “If we are faithful to God in little things, we shall gain experience and strength that will be helpful to us in the more serious trials of life.” Years later in Separation and Service he gave the same counsel to young people whose parents opposed their going abroad. Thank God for the obstacle, he says, and pray for its removal: “When they have learnt to move man through God at home, they will be the better prepared to do the same thing in the mission-field.”

This is the heart of his teaching on prayer. It is the way the child of God gets things done, because God is the One who moves hearts, opens doors and supplies needs. Taylor believed that the most effective thing he could do about a stubborn employer, a closed province or an empty bank account was to speak to God about it.

> The king’s heart is in the hand of the LORD, as the rivers of water: he turneth it whithersoever he will. PROVERBS 21:1

## Prayer as business

Taylor’s phrase from the story of his conversion sums up his view: prayer is “in sober matter of fact transacting business with God.” Business is definite. It deals in particular needs and particular answers, and it expects results. Taylor prayed for specific sums, specific workers, specific places. When he founded the mission he asked for twenty-four workers, not for some workers. Later he and his colleagues asked for seventy in three years, and then for a hundred in one.

> Hitherto have ye asked nothing in my name: ask, and ye shall receive, that your joy may be full. JOHN 16:24

Taylor did not think prayer a technique for extracting things from God. He thought it the natural dealing of a child with a Father who delights to give, and whose answer might differ from the request. In Union and Communion he rebukes the habit of bringing our questions to God and then leaving the closet without waiting for His reply. Quiet waiting before God, he says, would save us from many a mistake. Business with God includes listening as well as asking.

## The prayer that waits

The second part of the salary story is easily forgotten. On Saturday morning Taylor’s earnest cry was for guidance: should he speak or wait? He received, as far as he could judge, the assurance that he should wait, and his heart was at rest. Then came the doctor’s question, and the crushing answer that the money was in the bank. Taylor had prayed, received guidance and obeyed it, and the door had apparently slammed.

> Rest in the LORD, and wait patiently for him. PSALM 37:7

He did not decide that he had misheard. He went to his room, poured out his heart, and came to the conclusion that God had His own way and was going to work in some other manner. That evening God did. Prayer that moves man through God must be willing to let God choose the man, the method and the hour.

## Honest limits

Two cautions are worth adding. First, Taylor’s silence about his salary was a private discipline for a particular purpose, not a law for everyone. There is nothing unspiritual about reminding an employer that wages are due, and Scripture says the labourer is worthy of his hire (Luke 10:7). What Taylor models is not silence as such, but a readiness to take every need first to God.

Second, stories of answered prayer can make those whose prayers seem unanswered feel second-class. Taylor knew many long waits and some refusals; A Retrospect records a burnt store of medicines, a robbery, and a door to Swatow that closed for good. He told his stories not to prove that prayer always produces what we ask, but to show that God is real, and attentive, and able.

> And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us. 1 JOHN 5:14

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name one person whose mind you have been trying to change by argument or pressure, and bring that person to God before you speak again.
2. Practise definiteness: write down three specific requests, with the date, and note how and when they are answered.
3. After you pray about a decision this week, stay on your knees a few minutes longer and wait for God’s leading before you move.
4. If you have an obstacle in your path, try thanking God for it and asking Him to remove it, instead of forcing it.
5. Take one small matter this week in which you would normally rely on your own arrangements, and deliberately trust God in it first.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You hold the hearts of kings and employers, landlords and officials, in Your hand. Teach me to come to You before I go to them.
> Forgive me for praying vaguely, as if I did not expect an answer, and for rising from my knees before You had spoken.
> Train me in little things, so that I may trust You in great ones. Let me learn at home what I shall need wherever You send me.
> And when You close a door I thought You had opened, keep me at rest, knowing that You have Your own way and will not fail. Amen.
# The Faithful One

> It is of the LORD’S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness. LAMENTATIONS 3:22–23

Late one Sunday night in Hull, Hudson Taylor was walking home after preaching in the lodging-houses when a poor Irishman asked him to come and pray with his wife, who was dying. The priest would not come without eighteenpence, and the family had nothing. Taylor had one coin in the world, a half-crown, and his supper and breakfast were waiting at home, but nothing for the next day’s dinner. He went. In a wretched upstairs room he found four or five children with the sunken cheeks of slow starvation and a mother lying exhausted beside a baby a day and a half old.

## Trusting God plus one-and-sixpence

A Retrospect tells what followed with painful candour. Taylor wished he had two shillings and a sixpence instead of a single half-crown; then he would gladly have given them a shilling. He began to tell the family that there was a kind and loving Father in heaven, and something within him said, You hypocrite. He knelt to pray, and got as far as Our Father before his conscience asked whether he dared call God Father with that half-crown in his pocket. He rose in great distress.

Then the father of the family said, If you can help us, for God’s sake do. The words Give to him that asketh of thee flashed into Taylor’s mind. He handed over the half-crown, and told the man that in giving it he was giving his all, and that God really was a Father and might be trusted. The joy came back in a flood. He sang hymns all the way home through the empty streets.

> Give to him that asketh thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away. MATTHEW 5:42

The next morning the post brought a packet in an unknown hand, containing nothing but a pair of kid gloves, from which half a sovereign fell to the floor. Four hundred per cent, he said, for twelve hours’ investment.

What gives the story its power is not the return but Taylor’s diagnosis of himself. The truth, he writes, was that “I could trust in God plus one-and-sixpence, but was not yet prepared to trust Him only.” Most of us trust God. What we find hard is trusting God only, without the reserve in our pocket that we privately rely on.

## Failing faith, unfailing God

It would be easy to turn this into an exhortation to have more faith. But Taylor himself kept moving the emphasis away from faith and onto God. In the same book, describing his first years in Ningbo after he had resigned from his society and was depending on God alone, he confesses: “My faith was not untried; it often, often failed.” He was ashamed of the failures. But he adds at once what he was learning through them. God became, he says, so near, so real, so intimate.

Then he sets down the truth that was to become the hinge of his whole inner life. It is good to know that those who trust God wholly find Him wholly true. But it is better still to know that “when we fail to trust fully He still remains unchangingly faithful. He is wholly true whether we trust or not.”

> If we believe not, yet he abideth faithful: he cannot deny himself. 2 TIMOTHY 2:13

He tells us that he had not learned this when he first went to China. As a young man, the serious question in his mind was not whether God was faithful but whether he had strong enough faith to warrant the enterprise. That is a crushing question, because no one can ever be sure the answer is yes. The discovery that set him free, first in principle in these Ningbo years and then fully in 1869, was that the weight does not rest on the strength of our faith. It rests on the One we trust. Faith is only the hand that takes hold. It is the Faithful One who holds.

## Small faith in a great God

Taylor is sometimes remembered as a giant of faith, but his own picture of faith was much humbler. In A Full Reward, an address on the book of Ruth, he says that every true Christian worker, at home or abroad, is a faith-worker, and that it is precisely in weakness that God comes near. Those who have gone out without a human guarantee of support, not knowing when the next help will come, find what he calls an additional link with the loving heart of the Father.

> And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:9

For Taylor weakness was not an embarrassment to be hidden from God but the very place where God’s faithfulness is displayed. When we are weak in ourselves, our friends and our circumstances, then we are strongest in Him. Our faith may be feeble; His arm is not.

In his short address All Sufficiency, on the text The LORD God is a sun and shield, he adds a further point. Sweet as the promises are, he says, “the Promiser is greater and better.” No promise, however richly claimed, can exhaust what God is able to do. Faith looks past the promise to the One who made it.

> For all the promises of God in him are yea, and in him Amen, unto the glory of God by us. 2 CORINTHIANS 1:20

## A bank that could not break

After the gloves and the half-sovereign, the young Taylor made a resolution that his later life bore out. A bank that could not break, he decided, should have his savings or earnings. He never regretted it.

This was not recklessness; Taylor kept careful accounts. But he was convinced that the safest place for anything we value is God’s hands, and that we lose nothing by giving it to Him, though we may have to wait for the return.

> He that hath pity upon the poor lendeth unto the LORD; and that which he hath given will he pay him again. PROVERBS 19:17

A caution belongs here. The half-crown story is not a promise that every gift will be repaid fourfold by the next post. Taylor never said so. What the story teaches is what Taylor learned in that room: that he had been relying on a coin, that God was asking him to rely on God, and that when he did, the joy he had lost came back. God’s faithfulness may be shown in a returned half-sovereign or in grace to go without. Either way He is faithful.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself what your one-and-sixpence is: the reserve you privately rely on while saying that you trust God.
2. If there is a need in front of you that you could meet at some cost to yourself, pray about it honestly today, and do what you are given to do.
3. When your faith feels weak this week, stop examining it and turn your attention to the faithfulness of God. Read Lamentations 3:21–26 aloud.
4. Write down one occasion when God was faithful to you although you had not trusted Him fully.
5. Consider whether you have been treating any story of answered prayer as a formula. Replace it with trust in the Person.

### A PRAYER

> Faithful Father, I have trusted You plus a reserve of my own, and called it faith. Forgive me.
> Teach me to trust You only. When my faith fails, as it often does, let me rest on the truth that You remain faithful, for You cannot deny Yourself.
> Make me generous to those in need, not because I expect a return, but because You are my Father and may be trusted.
> And when I am weak, be my strength, that the glory may be Yours and not mine. Amen.
# Owe No Man Any Thing

> Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law. ROMANS 13:8

No part of Hudson Taylor’s teaching has been more admired, imitated or argued about than his principles of money. The China Inland Mission guaranteed its workers no salary. It would not go into debt. And it would not solicit funds: no personal appeals, no collections, no collecting-books. A saying long attached to Taylor’s name puts it in a sentence: God’s work done in God’s way will never lack God’s supply. Whether or not those were ever his exact words, they are a fair summary of what he believed.

## A society in debt

The principle was born in Ningbo in 1856 and 1857. Taylor was then an agent of the Chinese Evangelisation Society, and he noticed that the society was frequently in debt; the bills he was instructed to draw were often met with borrowed money. He himself had always avoided debt. But could that which was wrong for one Christian be right for an association of Christians?

His reasoning in A Retrospect is worth reading slowly. To borrow money seemed to him “a confession that God had withheld some good thing, and a determination to get for ourselves what He had not given.” He could not believe that God was poor, or short of resources, or unwilling to supply what His work really needed. So he drew a startling conclusion: “if there were lack of funds to carry on work, then to that degree, in that special development, or at that time, it could not be the work of God.”

> The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. PSALM 23:1

In 1857 he resigned from the society, and his colleague John Jones did the same. They parted without ill feeling. Taylor had no friends from whom he expected support, and was ready to work for his own living if God did not supply. But God did. The story of the one remaining cash, the broken bridge of boats and the remittance that arrived while they were on their knees is told in the same chapter, and he adds a line that sums up the whole: “I would not, if I could, be otherwise than I am — entirely dependent myself upon the Lord, and used as a channel of help to others.”

## Not asking, but praying

When the mission was formed in 1865 the principle was built into its foundations. Taylor determined never to use personal solicitation, make collections or issue collecting-books. Why? Partly because he did not want to divert money from existing societies. But chiefly because he was convinced from Scripture that the way to obtain workers and means was not, in his words, “elaborate appeals for help, but, first, earnest prayer to God to thrust forth labourers, and, second, the deepening of the spiritual life of the church.”

> Pray ye therefore the Lord of the harvest, that he will send forth labourers into his harvest. MATTHEW 9:38

A Retrospect tells of a lecture he gave on China in 1866, on the condition that there be no collection. The chairman proposed one anyway, and Taylor insisted on the condition. He wanted people to go home burdened with China’s need and to ask God what He would have them do. He added that the tendency of a collection was “to leave the impression that the all-important thing was money, whereas no amount of money could convert a single soul.” The next morning the chairman, who had spent much of the night in prayer, handed him a cheque for five hundred pounds. That morning’s post had brought an offer of the whole passenger space of the Lammermuir.

Taylor compared that season to Exodus 36, where Moses had to restrain the people from bringing more gifts for the tabernacle. He thought that if there were less solicitation and more dependence on the Holy Spirit, that would be the common experience of the Church.

> For the stuff they had was sufficient for all the work to make it, and too much. EXODUS 36:7

## Giving to a rich Christ

Behind the method lay a view of God. In his address Coming to the King, Taylor asks a question that cuts deep: “Do we not often give to a poor Christ rather than to a rich one?” We give, he says, when His work is in straits, moved by the recital of need. But the Queen of Sheba brought her gifts to Solomon not because he was poor but because he was glorious. Taylor wanted Christians to give to Christ out of worship, not pity, and he wanted Christian workers to depend on the King, not on the sympathy of His subjects.

> The silver is mine, and the gold is mine, saith the LORD of hosts. HAGGAI 2:8

God is not poor. His work does not hang on our cleverness in raising funds. Much Christian fundraising, then and now, has been manipulative, anxious or unscrupulous, and Taylor’s protest against it still stands.

## Where care is needed

But here he must be read with care. First, Scripture does not forbid asking. Paul asked the Corinthians plainly for the collection for Jerusalem and wrote two chapters urging them to give generously (2 Corinthians 8–9). Many faithful missions and churches make their needs known openly, and it would be wrong to treat them as less spiritual. Taylor’s method was a calling for his mission; it is not a law for the Church. His conclusion that a lack of funds proves a work is not of God is especially too strong. The apostles themselves were at times hungry and in want (1 Corinthians 4:11).

Second, the line between praying and appealing is finer than it looks. The mission published a magazine, China’s Millions, which reported its work and needs to thousands of readers. But it shows that information and appeal are not always easy to separate, and critics were quick to say so.

Third, a no-appeal policy can place a heavy burden on workers. Missionaries who were promised nothing sometimes received very little, and some lived close to want. Taylor shared their hardship and never asked of others what he did not bear himself, but the burden was real.

> But godliness with contentment is great gain. 1 TIMOTHY 6:6

Fourth, and most subtly, such a principle can become a boast. It is easy to take quiet pride in not asking, and to look down on those who do. Taylor’s own emphasis was on the faithfulness of God, not on the virtue of those who trusted Him.

What remains, when all these cautions are made, is a challenge that every Christian and every church can hear. Do we trust God to supply what He wants done? Do we keep out of debt that presumes on a provision He has not given? And do we pray first, and then speak, rather than speak and forget to pray?

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Look honestly at any debt you carry and ask whether it presumes on a provision God has not given. Make a plan to be free of it.
2. Before you next ask anyone for help with a need, spend a set time bringing it to God first.
3. Examine your giving: do you give mainly when moved by crisis, or as worship to a rich Christ?
4. If you belong to a church or ministry, pray for its finances by name, and ask whether its fundraising honours God.
5. Guard against pride: if you have adopted a principle of faith in money matters, refuse to judge those who work differently.

### A PRAYER

> Lord of the harvest, the silver and the gold are Yours, and You are not poor. Forgive me for the anxiety that forgets it.
> Keep me from debt that presumes on You, and from fundraising that trusts in pressure rather than in prayer.
> Teach me to give to You as to a glorious King, not as to a beggar, and to depend on You rather than on the sympathy of others.
> And keep me from pride in any way of trusting You that I have chosen. Let the glory be Yours alone. Amen.
# The Exchanged Life

> I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me. GALATIANS 2:20

By the summer of 1869 it was fifteen years since Hudson Taylor had first landed in China. He had founded a mission, led a party across the world, survived a riot, buried a daughter and been denounced in Parliament. Outwardly he was a man of faith. Inwardly he was close to despair. In his address Unfailing Springs he describes that time without disguise: “I was painfully conscious that I was not living all that I was trying to teach the Chinese.” He struggled for victory and was too often defeated. He fasted, prayed, meditated on the Word, and nothing seemed to help. He asked himself whether he ought to give up preaching altogether and leave missionary work.

What happened next became, in the hands of his biographers, the most famous episode of his inner life. They called it the exchanged life, and the phrase has stuck.

## Resting on the Faithful One

The turning point came, as far as the story is securely known, in September 1869 at Zhenjiang, through a letter from a younger colleague, John McCarthy. McCarthy had himself been seeking holiness and had found rest. The burden of his letter, as Taylor’s biographers record it, was that the way to growth in faith was not striving after faith but resting on the Faithful One. We have no need to strain for a union with Christ that already exists. We are to abide in Him, as branches in a vine, and let His life do what our efforts cannot.

> Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me. JOHN 15:4

Taylor read it and saw it, and later said that God had made him a new man. The details of that afternoon come from later accounts, not from his own books in the Ochorus library, and they should be held with that in mind. But the substance of the discovery is written all over his own pages, and it is not hard to state.

He had been trying to make himself a branch, to draw strength out of Christ by effort, as if the vine and the branch were two separate things that had to be joined afresh each day. He came to see that he was already joined. The branch does not make itself a branch. It is one, by the act of the vinedresser. Its whole business is to remain where it has been placed, and to receive.

## Fruit, not work

This conviction shapes nearly everything Taylor wrote afterwards. In Blessed Prosperity, his meditations on the first psalm, he draws a distinction that goes to the root of it. God, he says, does not work through us in a mere mechanical way, as a man uses a tool. He makes us branches of the true Vine, “the very organs by which Its fruit is produced.” And there is “a fundamental difference between fruit and work.” Then comes one of the best sentences he ever wrote: “Work is the outcome of effort; fruit, of life.”

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, Meekness, temperance: against such there is no law. GALATIANS 5:22–23

He goes on to notice that Paul speaks of the fruit of the Spirit, singular, not fruits, as though we might pick the graces we prefer. It is one rich cluster. And if he were asked to put that fruit in a single word, he says, it would be Christliness.

A workman has to find his materials and tools, and set himself to his task with painful perseverance. Fruit is the glad, free outcome of the life within, and it ripens in its season. The Christian life, Taylor had discovered, is not a job to be done for Christ but a life to be lived from Him.

## The tender shoot

In his New Year’s address Under the Shepherd’s Care, Taylor puts the same truth in a homely picture. A heavy bunch of grapes on a tender shoot would break it. But if the shoot abides in the vine, it grows stronger as the fruit develops, and the life left to its own healthy growth comes in time to perfection.

> Being confident of this very thing, that he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ. PHILIPPIANS 1:6

Many earnest Christians load themselves with the fruit they think they ought to bear, and the load breaks them. Taylor had nearly broken under it. The remedy is not less fruit but a different source. We are not to fret and fume, he says in the same address, about how the divine life shall show itself, but to look to our Saviour and let the life work through us.

He quotes with approval a saying he once heard from Professor Charteris in Edinburgh: that the true Christian life begins where the life of Christ ended, at the cross, and grows back towards the cradle, until the child of God can rest like a child in the arms of infinite wisdom and love. That is the exchanged life: our striving laid down at the cross, and Christ’s life received in its place.

## Resting and working

Taylor’s discovery did not make him passive. The years after 1869 were the busiest of his life. He travelled, organised, wrote, prayed for seventy and then a hundred new workers, and bore burdens that would have crushed most men. What changed was not the amount of work but its source. He had stopped trying to supply the life himself.

> For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure. PHILIPPIANS 2:13

The exchanged life is not a technique for feeling better, or a second blessing that removes all struggle. Taylor struggled again, grieved again, and knew seasons of darkness to the end. Nor was the discovery itself new truth. It is plain New Testament teaching, taught by the Puritans and by many others before him. What was new was that Taylor, having taught it, came to live on it.

A reader who hears of a sudden transformation may be tempted to seek the same kind of moment, and miss the point. The discovery was not an experience to be chased but a fact to be believed: that the believer is already in Christ, and Christ in him. Some come to see it in a single afternoon, as Taylor did; others grow into it over years. The branch is joined either way.

> As ye have therefore received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk ye in him. COLOSSIANS 2:6

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself whether you are living as a branch or as a workman: are you drawing life from Christ, or trying to supply it yourself?
2. Name the fruit you have been straining to produce by effort. Bring it to Christ and ask Him to produce it in you.
3. Read John 15:1–11 each day this week, paying attention to what the branch does and does not do.
4. If you are exhausted in Christian service, consider whether the problem is the amount of work or its source.
5. Resist the temptation to chase an experience. Thank God instead for the plain fact that you are in Christ.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You are the Vine and I am a branch. I have tried to make myself fruitful, and the effort has worn me out.
> I stop striving after faith and rest on You, the Faithful One. I am in You, not because I have joined myself to You, but because You have joined me.
> Let Your life flow through me, and let the fruit be Yours: love, joy, peace, and all Your likeness.
> And when I am busy for You, let it be from You, so that my labour may be fruit and not merely work. Amen.
# Shall Never Thirst

> Whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life. JOHN 4:14

Hudson Taylor told the story of his spiritual crisis more than once, and not always in the same way. In the account most people know, the key was a letter about abiding in the vine. In his own address Unfailing Springs, which is in the Ochorus library, he tells it through a different text. Alone in inland China, painfully aware of his failures and wondering whether to give up missionary work, he came one afternoon in his ordinary reading to the fourth chapter of John. It had always been, he says, ancient history to him. That afternoon, for the first time, it became a present message to his soul.

The two accounts do not compete. They describe the same discovery from different sides: that Christ Himself is the life of the believer, received freely and continually. The vine speaks of union; the well speaks of supply. Taylor lived on both.

## Shall means shall

What struck him in John 4 was the plainness of the promise. In Separation and Service he recalls the same moment, and describes how he came to see that Christ literally meant what He said: that shall meant shall, and never meant never, and thirst meant thirst. He had sat down thirsty, he says, and sprang up praising the Lord that the thirsting days were past for ever.

> In the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink. JOHN 7:37

In Unfailing Springs he explains what thirst means: any unsatisfied need, any longing left faint and unrefreshed. And he tells how he took the promise: “believing from His own Word that my thirsty days were all passed, not from any present feeling, but because of His promise.”

Taylor did not feel any different at first. That evening he took his usual Bible-reading with the Chinese and spoke freely, but without any special sense of power. The next morning he learned that one of his hearers had been so convicted of sin that he could not sleep. From that time, he says, his ministry was owned of God as it had not been for some while. The promise was taken on the word of Christ, and the evidence followed, not the other way round.

## Drinketh, not drank

In both places where he writes about it, he stops over the tense of the verb. Jesus does not say whosoever has drunk, as if a single draught settled everything. He says whosoever drinketh, and the word describes a habit, a continuing action. The living water is not a deposit made once, but a spring that keeps flowing to the one who keeps drinking.

> He that believeth on me, as the scripture hath said, out of his belly shall flow rivers of living water. JOHN 7:38

It would be easy to hear the story of his crisis and conclude that a single decisive experience lifts the believer above all further need. Taylor did not teach that. What he found was not a one-time filling but a well: a source always available, to be drawn on moment by moment.

## Whatever the size of the vessel

In one of his loveliest illustrations, Taylor imagines a walker on a hot mountain day, parched with thirst, finding a stream falling from an overhanging rock. His cup may be small, but he can fill it again and again. A bucket would take longer to fill, a barrel longer still, but each would overflow in the end, for the overflow “depends not on the size of the vessel but on the unfailing supply of the stream.”

> My cup runneth over. PSALM 23:5

The Samaritan woman had no training, no standing and a bad reputation, yet she at once brought a whole town to Jesus, while many an eloquent preacher sends his hearers home unsatisfied. It ceases to be a question, Taylor says, of what we are or what we can do. The question is whether we have brought our vessel to Christ to be filled. We cannot all be great or wise or strong, but Christ is made unto us all that we need.

> But of him are ye in Christ Jesus, who of God is made unto us wisdom, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:30

## The test of 1870

Taylor’s testimony does not end there, and what follows is hard to read. Some months later, he says, came a time of great trial: the death of a beloved child, the sending home of three others, and the most trying time the mission had ever known. In Tianjin the Sisters of Mercy, French priests and the consul were massacred, and every inland station was in excitement and peril. Letters came almost daily from workers asking whether to stay or leave. Then his wife Maria fell ill; a baby was born and lived only a fortnight; and the morning after the baby’s funeral, Maria herself died.

> For the Lamb which is in the midst of the throne shall feed them, and shall lead them unto living fountains of waters: and God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes. REVELATION 7:17

Then, he writes, he understood why the Lord had made John 4 so real to him. In the weeks of illness that followed, lonely, missing his wife and the pattering footsteps of children far away in England, he would feel the heart-thirst returning. “Perhaps twenty times in a day,” he writes, he cried to the Lord that He had promised he should never thirst, and each time the Lord came and more than satisfied his sorrowing heart.

The promise did not mean that Taylor stopped grieving. He grieved deeply, and he says so. It meant that in his grief he had somewhere to go, twenty times a day if necessary, and that he was never turned away. The living water did not prevent the heart-thirst from coming back. It answered it every time it came.

## Coming to the spring

Taylor ends Unfailing Springs with a question. Are we among the thirsty, or among those who have come to the one great Source and are drinking, believing and receiving, for their own need and for the blessing of others?

The invitation is not to the spiritually advanced. It is to whosoever. The only qualification for drinking is thirst.

> And the Spirit and the bride say, Come. And let him that heareth say, Come. And let him that is athirst come. And whosoever will, let him take the water of life freely. REVELATION 22:17

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name the thirst that nothing else has satisfied, and bring it to Christ in plain words today.
2. Take one promise of Christ this week and believe it on His word, before you feel anything.
3. When the old thirst returns, as it will, go back to Him at once. Keep count, if it helps, and do not be ashamed of the number.
4. If you feel too small or too unqualified to be of use, remember the Samaritan woman, and bring your vessel to be filled.
5. If you are grieving, read Taylor’s testimony in Unfailing Springs, and ask God to meet you in your loss as He met him.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You stood and cried, If any man thirst, let him come unto Me, and drink. I am thirsty, and I come.
> Let Your word be enough for me, before I feel anything. Let shall mean shall, and never mean never.
> When the heart-thirst comes back in the lonely hours, let me come back to You as often as I need, and find You more than enough.
> Fill my small vessel until it overflows, for the blessing of others who are thirsty too. Amen.
# The Unsatisfied Life

> Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth: for thy love is better than wine. SONG OF SOLOMON 1:2

Union and Communion is Hudson Taylor’s most personal book. A short series of meditations on the Song of Solomon, written out of his experience of Christ after 1869, it reads less like a commentary than a letter from a man who has found what he was looking for. Its opening section is called The Unsatisfied Life and its Remedy.

## A key to the book

Taylor knew that many Christians find the Song baffling. “Read without the key,” he says, “this book is specially unintelligible.” The key, he believed, lies in the rest of Scripture. The prophets taught Israel that her Maker was her Husband. John the Baptist called Christ the Bridegroom. Paul, in Ephesians 5, taught that the union of Christ and His Church underlies marriage itself. “The Incarnate Word,” Taylor writes, “is the true key to the written Word.”

> For thy Maker is thine husband; the LORD of hosts is his name. ISAIAH 54:5

This way of reading the Song, as a picture of the love between Christ and His people, has a long history in the Church, from the early fathers through Bernard of Clairvaux to the Puritans. Many careful readers today take the Song first as what it appears to be, a poem celebrating the love of man and woman, and apply it to Christ more cautiously. Taylor reads it as allegory throughout, and at times presses details of the poem further than they will bear. But his central conviction does not depend on those details. The New Testament itself calls Christ the Bridegroom and the Church His bride, and Taylor’s book is a meditation on what that means.

## Longing without rest

The section opens with the bride longing for the Bridegroom. She has seen His beauty; the world can never again be to her what it was. But her experience ebbs and flows like the tide. His visits are precious but occasional. Now her joy in Him is heaven below; now she is longing, and longing in vain.

Taylor turns at once to his readers. Are there not many of the Lord’s people, he asks, whose experience is just like hers? They know Christ, and love Him, but they do not know the rest of abiding in Him, nor why it is not theirs. Some look back to the delight of their first love and feel they have lost it. Others find the interruptions to their communion more and more unbearable.

> O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is. PSALM 63:1

## The unsurrendered will

The Bridegroom is not reluctant. He is waiting all the time, and the conditions that keep Him away are of the bride’s own making. Taylor pictures a betrothed woman who longs for her lover but will not trust him fully, who wants to claim him without giving herself to him, keeping her own name, her own possessions and her own will. It can never be, he says. While she keeps her own name she cannot take his. She cannot promise to love and honour if she will not promise to obey.

Then comes the sentence at the heart of the section: “The real secret of an unsatisfied life lies too often in an unsurrendered will.”

> Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven. MATTHEW 7:21

What holds us back? Taylor names it: a deep-seated distrust of our loving Lord, which fears that if we give ourselves entirely to Him He might ask something beyond our powers, or something we should find it hard to give. He asks what an earthly bridegroom would feel if he discovered that his bride was dreading the wedding for fear he would make her life unbearable once he had the power. Yet that is how many redeemed people treat Christ, and it is no wonder they are neither happy nor satisfied.

## Not a cross, but a King

At last the bride can bear His absence no longer, and resolves to surrender everything, whatever it costs. If He leads her to Moriah, or even to Calvary, she will follow. Draw me, she cries; we will run after thee. And what follows, Taylor says, is “a wondrously glad surprise.” No Moriah, no Calvary; instead, a King. “When the heart submits, then Jesus reigns. And when Jesus reigns, there is rest.”

> Draw me, we will run after thee: the king hath brought me into his chambers. SONG OF SOLOMON 1:4

This is not a promise that surrender brings no suffering. Taylor’s own life after 1869 held some of the heaviest sorrows he ever knew. What he means is that the thing we fear in surrender, the loss of everything good, is a lie. The One to whom we yield is not a tyrant but a Bridegroom-King whose will for us is love.

And where does He lead the surrendered bride? Not first to the banqueting house, Taylor notes, though that will come, but into His chambers, to be alone with Him. Before public service comes private communion.

## Seeking Him for His own sake

The section then turns the searchlight further in. Are we not all too apt, Taylor asks, “to seek Him rather because of our need than for His joy and pleasure?” We do not admire children who think only of what they can get from their parents. Many of us once thought that to please God meant simply not to sin against Him. But pleasing God, he says, “means giving Him pleasure.” The Bridegroom longs for our fellowship more than we long for His.

> Let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice; for sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely. SONG OF SOLOMON 2:14

The section ends with the bride at rest in His arms, and the Bridegroom charging the daughters of Jerusalem not to disturb her. “It is never by His will,” Taylor writes, “that our rest in Him is disturbed.”

> Jesus Christ the same yesterday, and to day, and for ever. HEBREWS 13:8

## What to take from it

A caution and an encouragement belong together. The caution is that surrender can become one more thing to measure anxiously. Some tender consciences read a chapter like this and spend years wondering whether they have yielded enough. Taylor’s point is not that we must achieve a perfect surrender before Christ will draw near. It is that He is already near, already willing, and that our distrust of Him is what keeps us restless. The remedy is not more effort but more trust.

The encouragement is that the unsatisfied life is not the normal Christian life, however common it may be. Christ does not create longings in order to tantalise them. He means to satisfy them, and He will.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask whether your experience of Christ ebbs and flows like the bride’s, and be honest about why.
2. Name the thing you most fear God might ask of you. Consider whether that fear rests on a lie about His character.
3. Tell Christ, in your own words, that you give Him your name, your possessions and your will, and ask Him to reign.
4. Spend some time in prayer this week asking nothing for yourself, simply seeking to give Him pleasure.
5. If you are anxious about whether your surrender is complete enough, turn your eyes from your surrender to His love.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, Bridegroom of my soul, I have loved You and still kept my own will, and I have wondered why I was not at rest.
> Forgive my distrust. I have feared that You would ask too much, when You have only ever asked to love me.
> Draw me, and I will run after You. Reign in me, and let me find, as Your servant did, not a cross I dreaded but a King I can trust.
> And teach me to seek You not only for what You give, but for Your own joy. Amen.
# Communion Broken and Restored

> Therefore we ought to give the more earnest heed to the things which we have heard, lest at any time we should let them slip. HEBREWS 2:1

If the first section of Union and Communion describes the soul finding rest in Christ, the second and fourth describe it losing that rest. Taylor was too honest to write a book in which the bride, once satisfied, never falters. Most of us, he says, know how easily communion with Christ may be broken. The failure is never on His side.

He traces two different falls. The first is a drift back into the world. The second, more subtle and more dangerous, is a slide into spiritual self-satisfaction. Between them they explain how Christians who love their Lord find themselves, without quite knowing how, far from Him.

## Drifting

In the second section the bride has slipped back into worldliness. Taylor does not suggest she has stopped loving her Lord. She loves Him truly, though not wholly. Perhaps, he suggests, the very restfulness of her new joy made her feel too secure. Perhaps she thought she might safely go a little way back into her old life and win her friends to follow Christ. Perhaps she did not think at all, and simply forgot that the current of this world was against her.

It is not necessary, he says, “when the current is against us, to turn the boat’s head down the stream in order to drift.” Nobody decides to backslide. They simply stop rowing.

> Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world. If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him. 1 JOHN 2:15

“We have to take our choice: we cannot enjoy both the world and Christ.” The Bridegroom comes leaping over the hills, looks in through the lattice, and calls to her: Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away. But He cannot come where she is. And she, instead of running out to Him, comforts herself with the thought that she is His and He is hers, and that she can find Him later. Until the day be cool, she says, turn, my beloved. And He goes.

## The little foxes

In the middle of that section Taylor dwells on the little foxes. The enemies may be small, he says, but the damage great. A tiny spray of blossom is easily spoiled, and with it the fruitfulness of a whole branch.

> Take us the foxes, the little foxes, that spoil the vines: for our vines have tender grapes. SONG OF SOLOMON 2:15

Then he names them: “Little compromises with the world; disobedience to the still small voice in little things; little indulgences of the flesh to the neglect of duty; little strokes of policy; doing evil in little things that good may come.” There is nothing dramatic on the list. That is the point.

The bride learns the hard way. The day cools and the shadows flee, but He does not return. At night she seeks Him and cannot find Him. Only when she rises, goes out into the dark, and begins to confess her Lord openly does she find Him again. The section closes with her restored, and the Bridegroom once more charging the daughters of Jerusalem not to disturb her.

## Sleeping with the door barred

The fourth section is different, and in some ways more searching. This time the bride has not gone back to the world. She is still, in Taylor’s phrase, a garden shut up so far as the world is concerned. Her failure is slothful self-indulgence. Perhaps, he suggests, it began with spiritual pride in what grace had enabled her to do, or with satisfaction in the blessing rather than in the Blesser.

> I sleep, but my heart waketh: it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh, saying, Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled: for my head is filled with dew, and my locks with the drops of the night. SONG OF SOLOMON 5:2

Why is His head wet with dew? Because He has been out all night on the mountains seeking the lost sheep. And will she, who so recently went with Him through lions’ dens, now leave Him to seek the wandering alone? Her answer is pitiful: she has taken off her coat, she has washed her feet; how can she get up?

How sadly possible it is, he says, “to take delight in conferences and conventions, to feast on all the good things that are brought before us, and yet to be unprepared to go out from them to self-denying efforts to rescue the perishing.” It is possible to delight in the rest of faith and forget to fight the good fight of faith. That, he thought, was the peculiar danger of comfortable, well-taught Christians.

> Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me. REVELATION 3:20

When at last she rises, her hands drip with myrrh upon the handles of the bolt, and He is gone. This time the search is harder. The watchmen strike her and take away her veil. Restoration, Taylor says, is more difficult after a second fall, and tardy obedience is no small matter.

## The road back

Yet this is not a gloomy book. In both sections the bride is restored, and in both Taylor lingers over the manner of it. When, wounded and searching, she is asked what her beloved is more than any other, she forgets herself and pours out a description of His beauty: He is the chiefest among ten thousand; yea, he is altogether lovely. And no sooner has she confessed that she is His than He appears, and, in Taylor’s words, “upbraideth not.” There is no scolding, only tenderness.

> I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him. HOSEA 14:4

Taylor also notices a change in the bride’s words over the course of the book. Early on she says, My beloved is mine, and I am his: her claim comes first. After her second restoration she says, I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine: His claim comes first. Near the end she says only, I am my beloved’s, and his desire is toward me. Her claim has disappeared into His. That, for Taylor, is the path of growth: from what Christ is to me, to what I am to Christ.

Some of the details Taylor draws from the Song are more his own than the poet’s, and a reader need not follow them all. But the pattern he describes is true to Scripture and to experience. Communion is broken by drift and by self-satisfaction. It is restored by rising, seeking and confessing. And the One we have grieved does not reproach us when we come back.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask where in your life you have simply stopped rowing, and the current has been carrying you.
2. Write down your own little foxes, the small compromises and delays you have excused. Deal with one today.
3. Consider whether you enjoy good teaching more than you obey it. Choose one self-denying act of service this week.
4. If you have lost your sense of Christ’s nearness, do what the bride did: rise, seek Him, and confess Him openly.
5. Trace your own growth: is it moving from what Christ is to you towards what you are to Him?

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You have stood at my door and knocked, and I have been too comfortable to rise. Forgive me.
> Show me the little foxes that are spoiling the vine, the small compromises I have called harmless, and give me grace to deal with them.
> Keep me from drifting with the current of this world, and from resting in blessings while You are out on the mountains seeking the lost.
> When I wander, bring me back; and when I return, let me find You as You have always been, without reproach and full of love. Amen.
# Mine Own Vineyard

> They made me the keeper of the vineyards; but mine own vineyard have I not kept. SONG OF SOLOMON 1:6

It may seem strange that the founder of a great mission should write so insistently about the danger of too much activity. But Hudson Taylor knew it from the inside. He had worked himself into collapse in Ningbo, and in 1869 he had come close to giving up the mission field because his work had outrun his walk with God. So when he came, in Union and Communion, to the bride’s confession that she had kept other people’s vineyards and neglected her own, he stopped and spoke to his own generation.

## A danger of this day

Our attention, he says, is drawn here to a danger “pre-eminently one of this day”: that intense activity may lead to zeal in service at the expense of personal communion with Christ. Such neglect does not only lessen the value of the service. It tends to unfit us for the highest service altogether. If we watch over others’ souls and neglect our own, we shall be disappointed with our powerlessness to help, and our Master will be disappointed in us.

Then he writes the sentence that could stand as a summary of his whole inner teaching: “Let us never forget that what we are is more important than what we do; and that all fruit borne when not abiding in Christ must be fruit of the flesh, and not of the Spirit.”

> Take heed unto thyself, and unto the doctrine; continue in them: for in doing this thou shalt both save thyself, and them that hear thee. 1 TIMOTHY 4:16

Taylor adds a harder word. The sin of neglected communion may be forgiven, he says, and yet its effect remain, as a healed wound often leaves a scar.

## Service with Jesus

None of this made Taylor suspicious of service. The very next verses of the Song show the bride’s heart going out instinctively to the feeding of the flock. She asks the Shepherd where He feeds His sheep, because she does not want to labour alone, or in any company but His. Taylor loves this. Let her show her love, he says, by feeding His sheep and caring for His lambs, and she need not fear to miss His presence. It will be, in his phrase, “service with Jesus as well as for Jesus.”

> Feed my lambs. JOHN 21:15

The distinction matters. Much Christian service is done for Christ in His absence, as it were: we work for Him, report to Him, and hope He is pleased. Taylor wanted service done in His company. The Shepherd is out on the hills already; the bride simply goes where He is.

## Come with me

In the third section of the Song, the section of unbroken communion, the Bridegroom calls the bride to service in words Taylor found deeply significant: Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse. It is always so, he says. When the Saviour sends His people to disciple the nations, He first says, All power is given unto me, and then promises, Lo, I am with you alway. It is never go without Me; it is always come with Me.

> Come with me from Lebanon, my spouse, with me from Lebanon: look from the top of Amana, from the top of Shenir and Hermon, from the lions’ dens, from the mountains of the leopards. SONG OF SOLOMON 4:8

What are lions’ dens, Taylor asks, when the Lion of the tribe of Judah is with us? He wrote it after decades of work in places where missionaries were robbed, threatened and sometimes killed.

He also noticed that in the same passage the Bridegroom says, Thou hast ravished my heart, and that the margin of the Revised Version gives an alternative: Thou hast given me courage. If the Lord Himself may be heartened by the faithful company of His bride, Taylor says, it is no wonder that we can encourage one another in our shared service.

## A garden for Him

The bride, in the same section, is described as a garden enclosed, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed. Yet she is also a fountain of gardens, a well of living waters. Taylor sees no contradiction. She carries refreshment wherever she goes, and yet her garden is for Him alone. The fruit she bears may bless many; the garden is kept for the Master.

> Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south; blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out. Let my beloved come into his garden, and eat his pleasant fruits. SONG OF SOLOMON 4:16

He finds in her prayer a readiness for any experience. Let the north wind blow, and the south, cold and heat alike, if only the fragrance may flow out for Him. Consecration, he adds, far from lessening our power to give to others, increases both the power and the joy, as the five loaves given to Christ fed a multitude.

## What she was, not what she did

The last section of the Song brings Taylor back to his theme. The bride tends her vineyard and says the Lord shall have the thousand pieces of silver from it. Taylor draws from the order of these verses a conviction he held all his life: that “what she was (by grace) was more important than what she did; and that she did not work in order to earn favour, but being assured of favour, gave her love free scope to show itself in service.”

> We love him, because he first loved us. 1 JOHN 4:19

This is the right order, and it is the key to the whole chapter. We do not keep our own vineyard in order to earn His love; we keep it because we are loved, and because fruitfulness for others grows only from a life that is first His. Communion comes before service, not instead of it, and service flows out of communion rather than replacing it.

A modern reader may find some of Taylor’s interpretations in these sections fanciful, and they are not always secure. But the lesson he draws is plain New Testament teaching. Martha served, and was rebuked not for serving but for being distracted and troubled; Mary sat at His feet, and had chosen the good part (Luke 10:38–42). Taylor was a Martha by temperament and a Mary by conviction, and he spent his life trying to be both in the right order.

> Abide in me, and I in you. JOHN 15:4

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself honestly which you have been tending more carefully in recent months: other people’s vineyards or your own.
2. Look at your diary for the coming week. Where is the time for communion with Christ? Put it in before anything else.
3. Before your next piece of Christian service, pray that it may be service with Jesus and not only for Him.
4. Encourage one fellow-worker this week, remembering that even the Lord is said to be heartened by faithful company.
5. If you are working in order to feel loved by God, stop and receive His love first. Then go back to work.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, I have kept other people’s vineyards and neglected my own. I have been busy for You and far from You.
> Forgive me, and heal the wounds of neglected communion, even if they leave a scar.
> Let what I am in You matter more to me than what I do for You. Call me to come with You, and let me never go without You.
> Make my life a garden kept for You, from which others may be refreshed, and let all my service flow from Your love for me. Amen.
# The Morning Watch

> He wakeneth morning by morning, he wakeneth mine ear to hear as the learned. ISAIAH 50:4

Hudson Taylor’s life was full of dramatic answers to prayer. But the steady ground under all of them was an undramatic habit: he met God early each day over an open Bible. His biographers describe him, in his later years of constant travel, lighting a candle in the small hours to read and pray before the day’s journey began. In his own writings it appears as a conviction, stated briefly, as if it hardly needed saying.

## Nothing can make up for it

In Union and Communion, in the middle of his meditation on the bride brought into the King’s chambers, he pauses to add a word about what he calls the morning watch. “There is no time so profitably spent as the early hour given to Jesus only.” Do we give it sufficient attention? If possible it should be redeemed; nothing can make up for it. And then a short sentence that has been quoted by Christians ever since: “We must take time to be holy!”

> My voice shalt thou hear in the morning, O LORD; in the morning will I direct my prayer unto thee, and will look up. PSALM 5:3

He says the same in Blessed Prosperity, his meditations on the first psalm, and with the same realism: “To some it is not at all easy to secure time for the morning watch, but nothing can make up for the loss of it.” He does not pretend that every Christian has leisure. He knew mothers, labourers and missionaries whose days were not their own. He simply insists that the loss is real, and that it cannot be made good by anything else.

## Delight, not duty

Taylor’s ground for the habit is not discipline but delight. Blessed Prosperity is built on the words, His delight is in the law of the LORD; and in his law doth he meditate day and night. The unregenerate, Taylor says, may read the Bible as a religious duty and admire much of it. But when they are converted they find that among the “all things” that become new, they have a new Bible. The child of God feeds on it as a newborn baby on milk.

> But his delight is in the law of the LORD; and in his law doth he meditate day and night. PSALM 1:2

It is not hard, he adds, to discover what a man delights in: out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks. The mother speaks of her baby, the politician of politics, the athlete of his sport. The happy Christian will speak naturally and often of what he finds in the Word.

He also warns against a way of reading that looks only for promises. The phrase the law of the LORD tells us, he says, that the Bible is meant to show us what God would have us be and do, not merely what we can get from Him. Ezra prepared his heart to seek the law of the LORD, and to do it, and to teach it (Ezra 7:10), and the hand of his God was upon him for good.

## Reading in regular course

One of the most charming pages in Taylor’s books is the introduction to Separation and Service. He tells how, travelling in Zhejiang, he spent a night in a wicked town where every inn was dreadful and the people seemed hardened against the truth. He woke cast down and spiritually hungry, opened his Bible at his regular place, and found it was Numbers 7, one of the longest and most repetitious chapters in Scripture. He felt he could not face it. But he was not happy to leave his regular portion, so after a little conflict he read it, praying for a blessing he hardly expected.

God, he says, gave him a feast. The chapter that looked like a list of offerings opened to him as a revelation of God’s delight in the gifts of His people, and became the seed of the book. And he begins the story with a sentence worth taking to heart: “I have never ceased to be thankful that I was early led to read the Word of God in regular course.”

> All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness. 2 TIMOTHY 3:16

If we only read the passages we already love, we will only ever hear what we already know. Reading straight through, including the long chapters, lets God speak on His own agenda rather than ours.

## Feeding and feasting

When ill health drove Taylor home in 1860, he spent years helping to revise the New Testament in the Ningbo dialect. At the time he thought of the work only as a help to Chinese Christians. Later, he says in A Retrospect, he saw that without “those months of feeding and feasting on the Word of God,” he would have been quite unprepared to form the China Inland Mission on the basis on which it stood. The principles of the mission, its dependence on prayer, its refusal of debt, its conviction that God would supply, came out of those hours with the text.

> Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path. PSALM 119:105

In the first psalm Taylor finds a picture of what such a life becomes: a tree planted by the rivers of water. It is stable, not blown about by circumstances; its hidden supplies do not fail in drought. Taylor notes that the evergreen shows its life most clearly in winter, when the trees around it are bare.

> And he shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season; his leaf also shall not wither; and whatsoever he doeth shall prosper. PSALM 1:3

## A word of balance

Taylor held a very high view of Scripture, which he called plenary and verbal inspiration, and he had little patience with the critical scholarship of his day. In Unfailing Springs he asks why so much time is worse than wasted over criticism of the Bible’s books. Readers today may wish he had engaged those questions more patiently. But his central point stands: the best evidence for the Word of God is found in the Word itself, when it is studied, loved, obeyed and trusted.

Two cautions are worth adding. The morning is not magic; for some, because of work, health or small children, the quiet hour must come at another time, and Taylor would not have made the clock a law. And a habit can harden into a performance to be ticked off. The point of the watch is not the watch. It is the One we meet there.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Decide on a regular time, if possible early, when you will meet God over an open Bible, and guard it this week.
2. Begin a plan of reading Scripture in regular course, including the parts you would normally skip.
3. When you read, ask not only what God promises but what He would have you be and do.
4. After you pray about a question, wait a few minutes in silence before you leave.
5. If your circumstances make the morning impossible, choose another time without guilt, and keep it faithfully.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path, and I have too often gone out into the day without it.
> Give me a delight in Your law that makes the early hour a pleasure rather than a burden. Teach me to take time to be holy.
> Feed me from the whole of Your Word, the long chapters as well as the loved ones, and speak to me on Your agenda rather than mine.
> Plant me by the rivers of water, so that my leaf does not wither in the drought, and let my fruit come in its season. Amen.
# Separated unto the Lord

> All the days of his separation he is holy unto the LORD. NUMBERS 6:8

Separation and Service is Hudson Taylor’s meditation on Numbers 6 and 7: the law of the Nazarite, the priestly blessing, and the long record of the princes’ offerings. He saw one movement running through them. Separation to God is followed by blessing from God, and those who receive large blessing from Him render Him service in which He delights. This chapter follows the first step, separation.

## An open invitation

Taylor begins with grace. The Nazarite vow, he points out, was a gift from God, given unasked. Israel had forfeited the privilege of being a kingdom of priests, but God still opened a way for any individual, man or woman, who wished to draw near to Him, and for as long as their own hearts desired. Whosoever will might come.

> Speak unto the children of Israel, and say unto them, When either man or woman shall separate themselves to vow a vow of a Nazarite, to separate themselves unto the LORD. NUMBERS 6:2

For the Christian, Taylor says, what was temporary and optional under the old covenant is permanent: the believer is always separated to God. Yet devotion is still in some sense voluntary, and that, he thinks, explains why Christians differ so much in what they attain. Salvation is a free gift. Knowing Christ deeply is possible only through unreserved consecration and unquestioning obedience. And this, he insists, is not a hardship but the highest privilege.

## Obedience in indifferent things

The first mark of the Nazarite was abstinence from the fruit of the vine: not only wine and strong drink, but fresh grapes, raisins, even the seeds and skins. Grapes are good. As Adam’s obedience was tested by one tree, pleasant and good for food, so the Nazarite’s was tested by a thing harmless in itself. “It was not that the thing was harmful in itself, but that the doing the will of God, in a matter of seeming indifference, was essential to his acceptance.”

> Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God. 1 CORINTHIANS 10:31

He applies it to a question every generation of Christians asks about amusements and pleasures: where is the harm in this? The question itself, he suggests, may be the wrong one. Even if a thing is harmless, it does not follow that it pleases God or helps us spiritually. Paul gave up not bad things but good things, things that were gain to him, to win Christ.

Then comes a sentence that summarises his life: “The highest service demands the greatest sacrifice, but it secures the fullest blessing and the greatest fruitfulness.” Christ could not stay in His Father’s bosom and redeem the world. Missionaries, he adds, cannot win the unreached and enjoy the comforts of home.

## Unmistakably peculiar

The second mark was the uncut hair. God claimed the right to determine the appearance of His servant, so that separated ones would be manifestly such. Taylor admits that many shrink greatly from appearing peculiar. His counsel: “While we are not to seek to be peculiar for its own sake, we are not to hesitate to be so when duty to God renders it necessary, or when the privilege of self-denial for the benefit of others calls for it.”

> And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind. ROMANS 12:2

It is impossible to read those words without thinking of Taylor’s own Chinese dress. He knew what it cost to be peculiar, but he would not let fear of looking odd decide the path of obedience.

## Death and defilement

The third mark was that the Nazarite must not touch a dead body, not even for father or mother. Taylor does not use it to cheapen the command to honour parents; indeed, he counsels young Christians whose parents oppose their missionary call to thank God for the obstacle and pray patiently for its removal. But he draws from it the solemn truth that death is the wages of sin, and that the consecrated life must keep apart from sin and its fruits.

Then there is unwitting defilement. If someone died suddenly beside the Nazarite, his vow was broken, even though he had done nothing deliberately. The days already served were lost, and he had to begin again after sacrifice. Taylor finds here a truth he thought his generation badly needed: that sin is guilt and not merely misfortune, and that God’s holiness is not measured by our awareness. But if we become conscious of the slightest failure, even by inadvertence, we are to confess it at once and receive cleansing through Christ’s sacrifice, and walk on in the light.

> If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. 1 JOHN 1:9

## Blameless but not sinless

The last part of the law is the most evangelical. When the days of his vow were fulfilled, the Nazarite had to bring a burnt-offering, a sin-offering and a peace-offering. Why a sin-offering, if he had kept his vow? Because, Taylor says, there is an important difference “between being blameless and being sinless.” Even the best service is stained, and “not our worst, but our best, is only acceptable to God through the atonement of our Lord Jesus Christ.”

> But we are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags. ISAIAH 64:6

This keeps separation from turning into pride. The Nazarite is not a spiritual aristocrat. He is a sinner who has been allowed to draw near, and who remains accepted only in the Beloved. Taylor quotes at length an anonymous poem about a mother mending a garment who finds her little daughter’s clumsy attempt to help, and loves her the more for it; and he applies it to the Father who receives our marred and bungled service for the love that was in it.

Taylor also notices that the text says the Nazarite shall be brought to the door of the tabernacle, not that he shall come. Even at the end of a faithful vow, he needed a priest to present him. So do we. Our High Priest will present us faultless before the presence of His glory.

> Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy. JUDE 1:24

## Reading it wisely

Taylor’s typology is sometimes more confident than the text, and his reasoning about the offerings goes further than some readers will follow. The call to be peculiar can also be misused as licence for mere oddity, or for a separation that despises others. But his main lesson is sound, and it is needed. Separation is not a list of taboos; it is a heart given wholly to God, obedient even in small things, and resting always on Christ’s sacrifice rather than its own achievement.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Replace the question Where is the harm in this? with the question Does this please God and help me spiritually? Apply it to one habit this week.
2. Name one good thing you might give up for the sake of Christ or of others, and pray about whether He is asking it.
3. Consider where fear of seeming peculiar is keeping you from obedience.
4. When you become aware of a failure, even an unintended one, confess it at once and receive cleansing, rather than excusing it.
5. Thank God that your best service is accepted in Christ, and let that free you from both pride and despair.

### A PRAYER

> Holy God, You have opened a way for whosoever will to draw near to You. I want to come, and to be wholly Yours.
> Teach me to obey You in small things and in things that seem indifferent, and to care more for Your pleasure than for my own.
> Give me courage to be different when love and duty require it, and keep me from pride when I am.
> And when my best service is marred, receive it for Christ’s sake, and present me faultless at the last by His grace. Amen.
# Blessing and Princely Service

> The LORD bless thee, and keep thee: The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee: The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace. NUMBERS 6:24–26

Directly after the law of the Nazarite, Numbers 6 gives the great priestly blessing, and directly after that, Numbers 7 records at wearying length the gifts of the twelve princes of Israel. Hudson Taylor believed the order was deliberate. Separation to God opens the way to blessing from God, and blessing from God overflows in service to Him. In Separation and Service he follows that movement, and shows us his heart.

## What blessing means

We use the word blessing loosely, Taylor says. Sometimes we mean praise, as when we bless God; sometimes a gift, as when we speak of the blessing of peace or plenty. But neither is the heart of it. His definition is exact: “Blessing is the moving of the heart towards an object of affection and complacency.” The gift or the song follows naturally, but the blessing is the feeling that prompts it. When the Lord blesses His people, it is His open heart that moves His loving hand.

> Bless the LORD, O my soul: and all that is within me, bless his holy name. PSALM 103:1

He illustrates it with a child at play who hears his mother’s step, drops his toys and runs to her, while she hurries to meet him with longer strides. Both hearts are full; but hers is larger, and her joy deeper. So it is with our Father. His joy in His people exceeds all their joy in Him as the infinite exceeds the finite.

Reading the threefold blessing in the light of the New Testament, Taylor hears in it the Father who blesses and keeps, the Son whose face shines upon us in grace, and the Spirit who lifts up His countenance and gives peace. On the Father, he says: “He is poorly kept who tries to keep himself.” On the Spirit, he asks searching questions of anyone who lacks peace. Is there sin unconfessed, or wrong not put right? Is anything withheld from God: ourselves, our property, our children, our testimony?

## The consecrated always blessed

The soul that is fully consecrated, he says, always receives the blessing of God. Where the blessing is not enjoyed, something in the consecration is unreal or defective. Perhaps we have separated ourselves to carry out our own plan of service rather than to learn and do His will.

> And they shall put my name upon the children of Israel; and I will bless them. NUMBERS 6:27

This needs care. Taylor does not mean that consecrated people never suffer; his own life disproves it, and chapter twenty of this book takes up his teaching on adversity. He means that God’s heart is always moving towards those who are wholly His, and that a lack of peace often has a cause we can find and deal with. But it would be cruel to tell every troubled believer that his consecration must be defective. Sometimes the cloud has another source, and Taylor himself knew seasons of darkness he did not attribute to sin.

## The longest chapter

Then comes Numbers 7, eighty-nine verses long, in which each of the twelve princes brings exactly the same offering, and each offering is listed in full, twelve times over. Why, Taylor asks, is so much space given to it in a book so marvellous in its range? His answer is the key to the whole book: to reveal the heart of God, and His delight in the loving gifts of His servants.

> And he that offered his offering the first day was Nahshon the son of Amminadab, of the tribe of Judah. NUMBERS 7:12

The record, he says, is not tedious to God. He compares it to a bride and bridegroom setting out their wedding presents for friends to see, each with the giver’s name attached, even when several are alike. He compares it to letters from his wife describing, with a mother’s tenderness, the birthday gifts the children had brought her. Twelve times the detail is repeated; it is, he says, “a most emphatic evidence that God never wearies in noting the service of each one of His people.”

## A ship to take you to China

His best illustration comes from his own family. On one of his birthdays his little daughter, then about five, came to him with a present she had made herself: a piece of wood with a hole bored in it by her scissors, a peg stuck in the hole, and half a cockle-shell hung on the top. He had no idea what it was, and at last had to ask. “Why, don’t you know, papa? I thought you would like best a ship to take you to China!”

He treasured it, he says, more than almost any gift he ever received. Years later, when the same daughter was herself a missionary in China and introduced him to the first two Chinese women she had led to Christ, he reminded her of the little ship. His conclusion is simple and very beautiful: “It is not hard to please those who love us.”

> For if there be first a willing mind, it is accepted according to that a man hath, and not according to that he hath not. 2 CORINTHIANS 8:12

The same truth underlies his comment on the rough ox-wagons the princes first brought to carry the tabernacle. Moses may have wondered whether such rude offerings could please the glorious God. God answered at once: Take it of them. “God is not hard to please,” Taylor says. He values love-gifts by the love they express, not by their intrinsic worth.

## According to his service

One more detail from Numbers 7 comforted Taylor. The wagons were not shared equally among the Levites. The sons of Merari, who carried the heavy boards and pillars, got four; the sons of Gershon, who carried the curtains, got two; and the sons of Kohath got none, because they bore the holy things of the sanctuary on their shoulders.

> But unto the sons of Kohath he gave none: because the service of the sanctuary belonging unto them was that they should bear upon their shoulders. NUMBERS 7:9

Sometimes, Taylor says, the children of God are tempted to murmur when their service is heavy and help is scarce, and to compare their lot with others. But God makes no mistakes. “Those who are called to the holiest service are those who can have least assistance.” They carry on their own shoulders burdens that cannot be shared.

He ends the book by bringing it all to the cross. The princes’ gifts were for the dedication of the altar, and the altar points to Christ. And our true self-denial and giving, he says, practically show our real estimate of the value of the cross of Christ.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think of God’s blessing this week not first as a gift, but as His heart moving towards you. Thank Him for that.
2. If you lack peace, work honestly through Taylor’s questions: is there sin unconfessed, a wrong not righted, anything withheld?
3. Give God one gift this week that costs you something, however small, and give it for love.
4. If your service feels heavy and unshared, remember the sons of Kohath, and ask for grace to carry it without comparing.
5. Ask what your giving says about your estimate of the cross.

### A PRAYER

> Father, Son and Holy Spirit, Your heart moves towards Your people in blessing, and Your joy in us is greater than ours in You.
> Keep me, for I am poorly kept when I try to keep myself. Make Your face shine on me, and give me Your peace.
> Accept my small and clumsy gifts for the love that is in them, as a father treasures a child’s homemade ship.
> And when my burden is heavy and no one can share it, let me carry it gladly, for the sake of the One who bore the cross for me. Amen.
# A Ribband of Blue

> Speak unto the children of Israel, and bid them that they make them fringes in the borders of their garments throughout their generations, and that they put upon the fringe of the borders a ribband of blue. NUMBERS 15:38

A Ribband of Blue is perhaps the most practical of Hudson Taylor’s addresses. It is an exposition of the end of Numbers 15, where God tells Israel to put fringes on the borders of their garments with a thread of blue, so that they would look upon it and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them. Taylor found in that small detail a whole theology of everyday holiness.

## Sins of ignorance and presumption

He begins with a sobering context. The chapter distinguishes between sins committed in ignorance, for which God appointed a sacrifice, and presumptuous sin, done deliberately in the face of God’s known will, for which there was no sacrifice. Taylor draws two lessons.

First, sins of ignorance and forgetfulness are real sins. Our knowledge and consciousness do not make wrong right. Even when we walk in the light, we are not beyond the need of atonement; our fellowship with God continues unbroken only because the blood of Jesus Christ keeps cleansing us.

> But if we walk in the light, as he is in the light, we have fellowship one with another, and the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin. 1 JOHN 1:7

Second, deliberate sin is deadly. The one who presumes on God’s forgiveness and does knowingly what he knows to be contrary to God’s will will find, Taylor warns, spiritual dearth and spiritual death inevitably follow. It is possible not merely to grieve and resist the Spirit, but to quench Him.

> Keep back thy servant also from presumptuous sins; let them not have dominion over me: then shall I be upright, and I shall be innocent from the great transgression. PSALM 19:13

It was on the heels of a judgement for presumptuous sin that God gave the ribband of blue. The badge was meant to keep His people from forgetting.

## The colour of heaven

Blue, Taylor says, is the colour of heaven. The sea reflects it and is as blue as the cloudless sky. Only when clouds come between is the deep blue lost. It is God’s will that there should never be a cloud between His people and Himself, and that, as the Israelite carried his thread of blue wherever he went, so Christians should carry a heavenly spirit and temper everywhere.

He paints the picture with obvious enjoyment: the farmer carrying his ribband into the field, the merchant to his place of business, the maid-servant into the innermost rooms as she did her daily work. Is it less important, he asks, that the Christian today should be manifestly marked by the Spirit of Christ, not only on special occasions but in the smallest details of life?

> Be ye therefore followers of God, as dear children; And walk in love, as Christ also hath loved us, and hath given himself for us. EPHESIANS 5:1–2

## A perfect needle

Taylor then takes up the command, Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect. He knows the difficulty it raises for tender consciences, and he answers with an illustration that is both homely and wise.

God’s perfection is absolute; ours at best is relative. A needle, he says, may be a perfect needle, exactly fitted for its work. Under a microscope it becomes a rough, honeycombed poker with a ragged hole for an eye. But it was not made to be looked at under a microscope. Being fitted for the purpose for which it was made, it may properly be called perfect. “So we are not called to be perfect angels, or in any respect Divine, but we are called to be perfect Christians, performing the privileged duties that as such devolve upon us.”

> Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect. MATTHEW 5:48

This is a gentle and freeing reading. It does not lower the standard; Taylor lists the demands of the Sermon on the Mount in full, from the refusal of anger and impurity to love for enemies. But it removes the despair of those who think Christ requires of them a flawlessness that belongs to God alone. He asks us to be what we were made for, fully.

## Glorify God in your handwriting

Then Taylor becomes wonderfully specific. God makes the least thing He makes according to His perfection: the tiniest fly, the dust on a butterfly’s wing, however highly magnified, is perfect. Should not the little things of the Christian’s daily life be relatively perfect in the same way? “Ought we not to glorify God in the formation of each letter that we write,” he asks, and as Christians write a more legible hand than the unconverted can be expected to?

> And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men. COLOSSIANS 3:23

He goes on. We should be thorough in our work, not simply doing well what will be noticed, but doing all we can, as under His eye, as God makes many a flower bloom unseen in the desert. We may take our rest and recreation to please Him. We may lay our clothes aside neatly at night, for He is in the room. We may wash and dress and brush our hair with His eye in view.

Some readers smile at this, but not for long. Taylor had spent years among people who judged the gospel by the lives of those who preached it, and he knew that a slovenly Christian is a poor advertisement for a holy God. More deeply, he believed that there is no part of life too small to be done for Christ. Holiness is not reserved for Sundays and crises. It is carried, like a thread of blue, into every room of the house.

## Not a new legalism

There is a danger here, and it should be named. A chapter like this can produce a fussy, anxious perfectionism that polices handwriting and folded clothes and loses the joy of the gospel. Taylor did not mean that. The ribband of blue was given to help Israel remember, not to give them one more rule to fail. His own image for the result is not strain but gladness: he hopes for “tell-tale faces, and glad hearts, and loving service,” reflecting the very hue of heaven.

> Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven. MATTHEW 5:16

The point is not that God is watching to catch us out. It is that He is present, everywhere and always, and that love wants to please Him in small things as well as great.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask whether you treat sins of forgetfulness and neglect as real sins, and confess any you have excused.
2. Examine yourself for any deliberate sin you have been presuming God will overlook, and turn from it today.
3. Choose one ordinary task this week and do it thoroughly, as for Christ, whether or not anyone will notice.
4. Replace any despairing view of perfection with Taylor’s needle: ask what God made you for, and aim to be that fully.
5. Find a small, private reminder, your own ribband of blue, to recall you to God’s presence during the working day.

### A PRAYER

> Heavenly Father, You make the smallest things perfectly, and You are present in every room of my life.
> Forgive my sins of ignorance and neglect, and keep me back from presumptuous sins. Let no cloud come between us.
> Teach me to do little things well for Your sake, in my work, my home and my rest, not anxiously, but gladly.
> Make me a perfect Christian in the sense You intend, and let my face and my service reflect the colour of heaven. Amen.
# Self-Denial versus Self-Assertion

> To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some. 1 CORINTHIANS 9:22

In 1855, a year after he landed in Shanghai, Hudson Taylor did something most of his fellow Europeans found faintly ridiculous. He put on Chinese clothes, shaved the front of his head and wore the long braid of hair then required of Chinese men. A Retrospect describes it in a single matter-of-fact paragraph. The suggestion, he says, came from the veteran missionary Walter Medhurst, as a way to make travel and residence inland easier, and “much benefit was found to result from this change of costume.” He and most of those who worked with him continued to wear it.

The benefit was plain to see. When William Burns travelled with him the following winter, Burns noticed that the younger man had the quiet listeners while he himself was followed by rude boys; that Taylor was invited into homes while he was not, because of the crowd his foreign clothes drew. After some weeks Burns changed too. The China Inland Mission later made native dress its common practice.

## More than clothes

It was a clever piece of missionary strategy. But it grew out of something deeper, which Taylor set out in a short address called Self-Denial versus Self-Assertion. Its text is the call of Christ to deny ourselves and take up the cross daily, and its argument goes somewhere most sermons on self-denial never reach.

We usually think of self-denial, Taylor says, as a slight lessening of our self-indulgences. But when Peter denied Christ he utterly disowned Him. In the same way we are to disown self daily. The question can no longer be whether I like this or that: “the only question must daily be, What would Jesus like?”

> Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus: Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: But made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant. PHILIPPIANS 2:5–7

## Giving up our rights

Then Taylor turns to what he calls another aspect of self-denial, often overlooked: the surrender of our rights. What does Scripture teach about our rights and claims? That our deserts are condemnation. Believers are pardoned criminals, and whatever good is in us is imparted. Can such people consistently be self-asserting?

He points to the parable of the unmerciful servant, forgiven a vast debt, who seized his fellow-servant by the throat over a small one. He points to Christ before Pilate, who did not assert His rights but waited for the Father’s vindication. And then he asks the question that must have made some of his hearers uncomfortable. Shall we, as ambassadors of a King whose kingdom is not of this world, be jealous of our own honour and rights “as men and as citizens of Western countries,” and seek to assert them, when what our Master wants is a reflection of His own forbearing character?

> Jesus answered, My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight. JOHN 18:36

This was not an abstract question in Taylor’s China. Foreigners there lived under treaties imposed by force, which gave them legal privileges and the protection of their consuls and gunboats. A missionary who was robbed or attacked could often call on that power. Taylor’s address closes with a prayer that God would work in His people “not ease and honour-seeking and right-maintaining, but right-abandoning and cross-taking.”

## A thief forgiven

A Retrospect gives an early example. In 1856, on the road to Ningbo, Taylor’s servant disappeared with everything he owned in China. It would not have been hard to have the man punished. But Taylor had prayed for him and taught him the Sermon on the Mount, with its command not to resist evil. Concluding, as he says, that the man’s soul was of more value than the forty pounds’ worth of things he had lost, he wrote to him urging repentance and faith instead. A friend in England, hearing of it, later sent him a cheque for the same amount.

> But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also. MATTHEW 5:39

At the other end of his life, after the Boxer massacres of 1900 had killed fifty-eight of the mission’s workers and twenty-one of their children, the mission declined to claim compensation for its losses, even where it was offered. It was the same principle on a much larger and more painful scale.

## Honest complications

Taylor did not always live this perfectly, and it does not answer every question.

After the riot at Yangzhou in 1868, in which his wife and others were injured, the British consul intervened with warships, and the affair became a diplomatic incident. Taylor had not asked for gunboats, but he did seek redress through the authorities, and the mission was widely blamed at home for provoking trouble. The episode shows how hard it was to be a foreigner in China in those years without being entangled in the power of one’s own government. In later years the mission counselled its workers to avoid appealing to consular power wherever they could.

Nor was identification ever complete. Chinese dress removed a barrier, but it did not make a Yorkshireman Chinese, and Taylor never pretended it did. His phrase in Separation and Service is careful: we are not to hesitate to be peculiar when “the privilege of self-denial for the benefit of others calls for it.” Identification was a form of love, not a disguise.

Finally, surrendering our own rights is not the same as ignoring the rights of others. Scripture calls us to defend the poor and the oppressed. Taylor himself spoke against the opium trade that was ruining Chinese lives. What he asked Christians to lay down was their own claim to honour and redress, not their concern for justice for others.

> Open thy mouth for the dumb in the cause of all such as are appointed to destruction. PROVERBS 31:8

## The mind of Christ

What remains is a searching challenge for any Christian, missionary or not. We are quick to defend our dignity, insist on our due, and resent slights. Taylor asks whether our Master did the same. Christ laid aside His glory, took the form of a servant, and did not open His mouth before His accusers. The one who follows Him will find that the cross touches not only his comforts but his rights.

> For even hereunto were ye called: because Christ also suffered for us, leaving us an example, that ye should follow his steps. 1 PETER 2:21

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Replace the question What do I like? with Taylor’s question, What would Jesus like? Ask it before each decision one day this week.
2. Name a right, honour or claim you are clinging to. Pray about whether Christ is asking you to lay it down.
3. Think of someone who has wronged you. Ask whether their soul matters more to you than your loss, and act accordingly.
4. Consider what barriers of custom, language or manner stand between you and the people you are trying to reach, and which you could remove.
5. At the same time, identify one person whose rights you could help defend.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You made Yourself of no reputation and took the form of a servant. I have been quick to defend my own reputation and slow to serve.
> Teach me to deny myself daily, and to ask not what I like but what You like.
> Give me grace to lay down my rights and claims for the sake of others, and to care more for the souls of those who wrong me than for what they have taken.
> Yet make me bold to speak for those who cannot speak for themselves, as You did. Amen.
# A Million a Month

> But when he saw the multitudes, he was moved with compassion on them, because they fainted, and were scattered abroad, as sheep having no shepherd. MATTHEW 9:36

In the middle of a quiet meditation on the Levites who carried the tabernacle, Hudson Taylor breaks off in Separation and Service with a sentence that sounds like a cry: “A million a month in China are dying without God.” The figure was his own estimate, and it need not be exact to do its work. Behind every plan, every prayer for workers and every mile he travelled lay the thought of a multitude passing into eternity without having heard the name of Christ.

## It is not convenient

The most vivid expression of that burden in A Retrospect is a story from 1856. Taylor was travelling by boat to Ningbo, and among his fellow passengers was a Chinese man who had been to England and was called Peter there. They had talked earnestly about his soul the night before, and he had been moved to tears. The next day Taylor heard a splash. Peter had fallen overboard. Taylor leapt into the water, could not find him, and saw a fishing-boat nearby with a drag-net that would surely bring him up. He begged the fishermen to come. Their answer was, “It is not convenient.” They were busy fishing. They haggled over the price. By the time they dragged the spot, Peter was dead.

Taylor told the story not to condemn the fishermen but to search himself and his readers. Were they not guilty of the man’s death, having the means to save him and refusing to use it? Assuredly. But what then of Christians who leave souls to perish, and say, like Cain, Am I my brother’s keeper? The Lord Jesus commands us to go. Shall we tell Him that it is not convenient, that we are busy fishing?

> If thou forbear to deliver them that are drawn unto death, and those that are ready to be slain; If thou sayest, Behold, we knew it not; doth not he that pondereth the heart consider it? PROVERBS 24:11–12

## Too late for his father

Another story pierces more deeply still. One of the first converts in Ningbo was a middle-aged man who had sought the truth all his life. A few nights after he believed, he asked Taylor how long the gospel had been known in England. Some hundreds of years, he was told. He was amazed. His own father had sought the truth for more than twenty years and died without finding it. “Oh, why did you not come sooner?”

> How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard? and how shall they hear without a preacher? ROMANS 10:14

Taylor adds that a whole generation had passed since then, and that in the meantime more than two hundred million had died in China without an offer of salvation. How long, he asks, shall this continue, and the Master’s words, To every creature, remain unheeded?

## On the sands at Brighton

This burden drove Taylor to the crisis of 1865. Back in England, with a map of China on his study wall, he had come to believe that a special agency was needed for the unreached inland provinces, and that God would have him ask for workers and go with them. But he would not ask. He was afraid. He had not yet learned, he says, to trust God for keeping power and grace for himself, and so he could not trust Him to keep others; he feared that young workers might break down in the dangers of the interior and reproach him bitterly for leading them there.

Meanwhile, he writes, “the feeling of blood-guiltiness became more and more intense.” Because he refused to ask, the labourers did not go, and every day tens of thousands were passing into Christless graves. He could not rest by day or sleep by night, and his health broke down.

> Then said I, Here am I; send me. ISAIAH 6:8

On Sunday the twenty-fifth of June, 1865, at Brighton, unable to bear the sight of a thousand Christians rejoicing in their own security while millions perished, he walked out on the sands alone. “There,” he says, “the Lord conquered my unbelief, and I surrendered myself to God for this service.” He told God that all the responsibility for issues and consequences must rest with Him; that it was Taylor’s part to obey and follow, and His to direct, care for and guide. Peace flowed in at once. He asked for twenty-four workers and wrote the request in the margin of his Bible.

The heart of that surrender was not a new burden but the laying down of one. Taylor did not become less concerned for China. He became unwilling to carry what only God could carry. Years later, in Union and Communion, he wrote that if we let Christ be our instructor in service and leave the responsibility with Him, our strength will not be exhausted with worry and anxiety.

## Go, or help others to go

Taylor never thought every Christian should go to China, but he thought every Christian should ask the question honestly. In his address A Full Reward, on the story of Ruth, he urges his hearers at least to become strangers and pilgrims at home, to toil somewhere in the harvest, and, if they may lawfully do so, not to be slow to obey the command to go. When He says Go, he asks, shall we reply No?

> Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. MATTHEW 28:19

He had a particular word for parents. In Coming to the King he asks how many parents who refuse to let a son or daughter go to the mission field would refuse the Queen of England if she offered their child an honourable post abroad. And in the introduction to A Retrospect he reminds his readers how much missions owe to people who never see the field, whose love, sympathy and prayers will surprise them on the great day.

## A word of care

Taylor’s language of blood-guiltiness should be handled carefully. It expressed a real and biblical sense of responsibility (Ezekiel 33:6). But in some consciences it can breed a crushing, unending guilt that drives people into service out of fear rather than love, and burns them out. Taylor’s own relief at Brighton points to the cure: the burden is real, but it belongs to God, and our part is obedience, not omnipotence. We are not the saviours of the world. We are sent by the One who is.

> All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth. MATTHEW 28:18

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Find out which peoples in the world today have least access to the gospel, and begin to pray for one of them by name.
2. Ask yourself honestly whether you have ever told the Lord, in effect, that it is not convenient.
3. If God is laying a burden on you, ask whether you are carrying the responsibility that belongs to Him. Hand it back.
4. If you are a parent, pray about whether you would gladly give your children to God’s service anywhere.
5. Whether or not you go, decide on one concrete way to help others go: by prayer, giving, encouragement or hospitality.

### A PRAYER

> Lord of the harvest, You were moved with compassion for the multitudes, and they are still without a shepherd.
> Forgive me for the times I have told You it was not convenient, and for my comfort while others perish without hearing Your name.
> Lay on me as much of Your burden as You mean me to carry, and take from me the part that is Yours alone.
> Here am I. Send me, or send others through me, and let none of us be too busy fishing to obey. Amen.
# Man Proposes, God Disposes

> A man’s heart deviseth his way: but the LORD directeth his steps. PROVERBS 16:9

One chapter of A Retrospect bears the old proverb Man Proposes, God Disposes as its title, and it could stand over much of Hudson Taylor’s life. His guidance rarely came as a clear inner voice. More often it came through closed doors, spoiled plans and apparent disasters that only made sense afterwards. He learned most about guidance by being disappointed.

## A chain of calamities

In the summer of 1856 Taylor was settled with William Burns in Swatow, with a promising opening for medical work. He went back to Shanghai to fetch his medicines and instruments, meaning to return within weeks. He found the building where they were stored burnt down and the medicines destroyed. He set out for Ningbo to replace them, and on the way his servant ran off with everything he owned. He reached Shanghai again, exhausted and penniless, and was about to leave for Swatow when news came that war had broken out, and missionary work in the south was largely suspended. Burns had meanwhile been arrested and sent to Canton. The door was shut.

Taylor admits that at the time he was more disposed to say with Jacob, All these things are against me, than to believe that all things work together for good. He had not yet learned, he says, to think of God as “the One Great Circumstance,” in whom we live and move and have our being, and of all lesser circumstances as necessarily the kindest, wisest and best, because either ordered or permitted by Him.

> And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose. ROMANS 8:28

The result of that chain of calamities was that he settled in Ningbo. There he met Maria Dyer, there the first church he gathered was formed, and there the pattern of the future mission took shape. The detour was the road.

## A long illness

The same thing happened on a larger scale in 1860. When his health collapsed and he had to return to England, it seemed, he says, a great calamity: the Ningbo work was more fruitful than ever, and the young church needed him. Doctors told him he could not go back for years. Only later did he see that the separation was a necessary step towards something greater. In the field, the pressure of needs around him had left no room to think about the vast interior. In England, facing the map on his study wall, he was as near to the unreached provinces as to the district where he had worked, and prayer was often the only relief for his burdened heart.

> For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. ISAIAH 55:8

Taylor introduces that section of A Retrospect with the lines of Cowper’s hymn: Blind unbelief is sure to err, and scan His work in vain; God is His own interpreter, and He will make it plain. That was his settled view. We do not see enough to judge God’s providences as they happen. We see enough to trust Him.

## Spreading the letters before the Lord

For day-to-day guidance Taylor’s method was simple. He brought the question to God, waited, and then acted on the best light he had. In Unfailing Springs he describes the terrible summer of 1870, when after the massacre at Tianjin letters came almost daily from workers asking whether to stay at their stations or leave. He did not know what to advise. But in each case, he says, “like Hezekiah, I spread the letters before the Lord, and trusted Him to teach me how to reply to them.”

> And Hezekiah received the letter of the hand of the messengers, and read it: and Hezekiah went up into the house of the LORD, and spread it before the LORD. 2 KINGS 19:14

“There was no conscious revelation,” he writes. He received no voice, no vision, no special feeling. But in every instance he was guided to reply in the way that led to the best results, and he sent each letter off in peace, knowing he had asked and that God had given the wisdom that directs.

> If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him. JAMES 1:5

This is an unglamorous account of guidance, and a helpful one. Many Christians wait for a sense of certainty before they act, and are paralysed when it does not come. Taylor asked, trusted, and used his judgement, believing that God would guide a mind yielded to Him even when he could not feel it.

## Faith and means

One of the most valuable lessons in A Retrospect concerns a swimming-belt. On his first voyage in 1853, the ship was caught in a gale in the Irish Sea and seemed certain to be wrecked. To please his mother Taylor had bought a swimming-belt, but as a young believer he felt that he could not simply trust God while he had it, and he gave it away. Then, he admits, without any sense of inconsistency, he gathered some light things together that might float.

Later he saw his mistake, and says so plainly. “The use of means ought not to lessen our faith in God; and our faith in God ought not to hinder our using whatever means He has given us for the accomplishment of His own purposes.” He adds that the same mistake was common in his day, when erroneous teaching on faith-healing was doing much harm. As a doctor, he always asked God’s blessing on appropriate treatment and gave Him thanks for recovery. To neglect the means God puts within reach, he thought, would be as presumptuous as refusing to eat and expecting to live by prayer alone.

> Is there no balm in Gilead; is there no physician there? JEREMIAH 8:22

This is real wisdom from a man often thought a pure enthusiast. Trust in God is not the opposite of prudence. Guidance does not mean abandoning reason, and faith does not mean refusing the doctor, the lifebelt or the careful plan. It means using them all as gifts from the hand of the One on whom alone we rely.

## He knoweth the way

In his New Year’s address Under the Shepherd’s Care Taylor gathers a series of texts about what God knows. One of them is Job’s: He knoweth the way that I take. Job did not understand the way he was being led, Taylor says; he was bewildered. But he had this comfort. And when we cannot understand God’s dealings with us, we may rest on the same truth. “He knows all; this suffices for us.”

> But he knoweth the way that I take: when he hath tried me, I shall come forth as gold. JOB 23:10

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think of a closed door or spoiled plan in your life that later turned out to be God’s guidance. Thank Him for it.
2. If a door has closed recently, write down what you know of God’s character, and trust Him with what you do not know of His plan.
3. Take a decision you are facing and literally spread it before God, in writing if it helps. Then act on the best light you have.
4. Examine whether you have been waiting for a feeling of certainty that God has not promised.
5. Ask whether you have been neglecting any sensible means, medical, practical or financial, in the name of faith.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You are the One Great Circumstance of my life, and every lesser circumstance is in Your hand.
> Forgive me for saying All these things are against me, when You were working all things together for good.
> Teach me to spread my questions before You, to trust You when there is no conscious revelation, and to use wisely the means You give.
> You know the way that I take. When I cannot understand it, let that be enough for me. Amen.
# Blessed Adversity

> Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, and naked shall I return thither: the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD. JOB 1:21

Few Christian teachers have earned the right to speak about suffering as fully as Hudson Taylor. He buried his daughter Grace in 1867, his son Samuel early in 1870, a newborn son that summer, and his wife Maria a few days later. He saw his mission attacked by a mob at Yangzhou and slandered in Parliament. He lived to hear that in the Boxer rising of 1900 fifty-eight of his missionaries and twenty-one of their children had been killed. His second wife, Jennie, died of cancer in 1904. He knew illness, exhaustion and seasons of deep depression. When he speaks of blessed adversity, he is not speaking from a comfortable study.

His fullest teaching on the subject is an address called Blessed Adversity, a companion to his meditation on the first psalm, Blessed Prosperity. Its text is Job.

## A trustworthy child

Taylor begins with a point many miss. It was not Satan who first challenged God about Job. It was God who drew Satan’s attention to His servant: Hast thou considered my servant Job? God bore witness that there was none like him in the earth, and allowed him to be tested to vindicate the power of His grace.

This leads Taylor to a thought that transforms trial. Satan, he says, often harasses believers in sorrow by suggesting that God is angry with them, that this is punishment for some unknown offence. But Scripture shows God more like a glad father who encourages a strong son to attempt some hard athletic feat or difficult examination, knowing he will gain lasting honour by it. So “our Heavenly Father delights to trust a trustworthy child with a trial” through which he may bring great glory to God and receive enlargement of heart.

> My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. JAMES 1:2–3

Taylor does not mean that every suffering is a compliment, and it would be cruel to say so to a grieving person. But for many believers weighed down by the fear that their trials prove God’s displeasure, his words are a release. Job’s sufferings were declared by God Himself to be without cause in his life. They were not punishment. They were a trust.

## The postman and the writer

The heart of the address is Taylor’s answer to a question: when Job said, The LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away, was he mistaken? Should he not have said that Satan had taken away? No, Taylor answers. Satan knew that none but God could touch Job; he asked God to put forth His hand. When Satan was allowed to afflict Job, Job was right to recognise the Lord as the One who permitted it. “Satan is servant, and not master.”

> The cup which my Father hath given me, shall I not drink it? JOHN 18:11

Then comes one of his most memorable images. Judas betrayed Christ with a kiss, yet our Lord did not stop at Judas, nor at the enemy who filled Judas’s heart. He spoke of the cup His Father had given Him. How much resentment, Taylor says, would be removed if we could take an injury from the hand of a loving Father instead of looking chiefly at the agent through whom it came. “It matters not who is the postman — it is with the writer of the letter that we are concerned.”

This is strong medicine. Taylor does not say that evil is good, or that wicked men are not responsible for their wickedness. Judas was guilty; the Boxers who murdered children were guilty. What he says is that nothing reaches a child of God except by the permission of a Father who will bring good out of it. “Come joy, or come sorrow, we may always take it from the hand of God.”

## The hedge and the leak

Taylor also has practical counsel for those in trouble. Satan found a hedge around Job that he could not break. Is there a spiritual equivalent today? Yes, Taylor says, and he finds its conditions in Philippians 4: rejoicing in the Lord, a yielding gentleness, and bringing everything to God by prayer with thanksgiving. Then the peace of God will keep our hearts and minds like a garrison.

> Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. PHILIPPIANS 4:6

Why do so many believers miss that peace? His answer is searching: “We may bring nine difficulties out of ten to Him, and try to manage the tenth ourselves.” That one reserved difficulty, like a small leak that runs a ship dry, or a small breach in a city wall, lets the enemy in.

## Light out of darkness

Job’s story ends in restoration: his possessions doubled, and ten children given again. Taylor notes a tender detail. The flocks were doubled, but the number of children was not, because those who had died were not lost; they had only gone before. Job had learned, through his trial, to know God by sight and not by hearsay.

> I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. JOB 42:5

“Light out of darkness is God’s order,” Taylor writes, and he ends with a promise: that if our heavenly Father can trust us with a trial, “it is a sure presage that, if by grace the trial is accepted, He will ere long trust us with a blessing.” In Blessed Prosperity he says the same from the other side: “the path of real prosperity will often lie through deepest suffering.” And in Union and Communion, meditating on the words Many waters cannot quench love, he writes that suffering, pain, bereavement and loss may test the constancy of divine love in the believer’s heart, but they will not quench it.

> Many waters cannot quench love, neither can the floods drown it. SONG OF SOLOMON 8:7

## What Taylor did not say

It would be dishonest to make Taylor sound serene in all his sorrows. In the weeks after Maria’s death he cried to the Lord twenty times a day. He is remembered as saying, in the dark summer of 1900, that he could not read, or think, or even pray, but that he could trust. That is not the language of a man above suffering. It is the language of a man holding on.

His teaching is therefore not that the Christian should feel no pain, but that pain is not the last word. The shadow, as he says in A Full Reward, is but the shadow of His wing.

> He shall cover thee with his feathers, and under his wings shalt thou trust: his truth shall be thy shield and buckler. PSALM 91:4

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. If you are suffering, reject the suggestion that it must mean God is angry with you. Read Job 1 and 2 and notice who began the conversation.
2. Think of an injury someone has done you. Try to look past the postman to the Writer, without excusing the wrong.
3. Name the tenth difficulty, the one you have kept to manage yourself, and bring it to God with thanksgiving.
4. If someone near you is grieving, do not offer them a formula. Sit with them, pray for them, and trust God with the rest.
5. Write down one way in which a past trial led you to know God by sight rather than by hearsay.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You gave, and You have taken away. Help me, as You helped Job and Your servant Hudson Taylor, to bless Your name in both.
> When I am tempted to think You are angry with me, remind me that You may be trusting me with a trial.
> Teach me to look past the messenger to the One who permits all things and will bring good out of them.
> And when I can no longer read, or think, or even pray, give me grace still to trust. Amen.
# Conclusion

> Faithful is he that calleth you, who also will do it. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:24

Eighteen chapters can make it seem that Hudson Taylor taught a great many things. He taught one thing, and lived it in a great many places. The one thing is that God is faithful and can be trusted absolutely. Follow it through and the whole shape of his life appears. The finished work: God has done what we could not. Consecration: we are not our own, and are safe in His hands. Prayer: business with a God who keeps His word. Money: He is not poor, and will supply what He wants done. The exchanged life: we rest on the Faithful One, not on our faith. The living water: He satisfies every time we come. Union and communion: the Bridegroom never withdraws, and receives the wanderer back without reproach. Separation and service: His blessing is sure, and His delight in our gifts is real. Self-denial: we can lay down our rights because He will vindicate us. The unreached millions: He has commanded us to go, and He goes with us. Guidance and suffering: He is the One Great Circumstance, and nothing reaches us except through His hands.

## The danger of this book

If you have read this far, you face a danger. It is possible to admire a man of faith and never exercise any. His stories are so vivid that they can become a kind of spiritual entertainment. Taylor told them for the opposite reason. He wanted his readers to put God to the test, as he had, in the small things of their own lives.

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

There is a second danger, the reverse of the first. It is to try to copy Taylor’s outward life instead of trusting Taylor’s God. Not every Christian is called to live on brown bread, to wear the dress of another nation, to refuse all appeals for money, or to leave children in another continent. Those were his callings. What every Christian is called to is the thing underneath them all: to take God at His word and act on it.

So the plainest counsel of this book is the same as that of its companion volumes. Go back to the chapter that made you least comfortable, not the one you enjoyed most. Take its application points and work at them slowly over months. Then take the next.

## Two things to carry

First, trust the Faithful One, not your faith. This was Taylor’s great discovery, and it is the most liberating thing he has to give. Many earnest believers exhaust themselves examining their faith and measuring their surrender. Taylor did that for years and nearly gave up. His release came when he stopped looking at his own grip and looked at the hand that held him. Your faith will often fail, as his did. God will not.

> Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you. 1 PETER 5:7

Second, do not separate the inner life from the outer task. Taylor wrote Union and Communion and ran the China Inland Mission, and he saw no contradiction between them. Communion with Christ was the spring of his service, and the needs of the world were what communion with Christ sent him out to meet. A Christianity all inward grows selfish; one all outward grows dry.

## A last word about the man

This book has tried to be honest about Taylor’s weaknesses. He drove himself too hard and sometimes asked too much of others. His family paid a high price for his calling. He worked in China under the protection of treaties imposed by force, and his mission could not wholly escape the shadow of that power, even as he opposed the opium trade and taught his workers to lay down their rights. His reading of the Song of Solomon sometimes outruns the text, and his principles about money, wise for his mission, were not a law for the whole Church. Some of his eschatology, in the appendix to Union and Communion and elsewhere, is speculative, and few readers now follow him in it.

None of this is said to diminish him. His whole message requires it. A man whose one theme was the faithfulness of God cannot be commended by pretending that he himself never failed. His failures are part of the evidence. God used a nervous, over-earnest, often ill and frequently grieving Yorkshireman to open the interior of the largest nation on earth to the gospel, and He did it because the man learned to rely on God rather than on himself.

> But God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise; and God hath chosen the weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:27

He died at Changsha in June 1905 and was buried at Zhenjiang beside Maria. The churches in China that trace part of their story to the work he began have since grown beyond anything he asked on the sands at Brighton, through suffering he could not have imagined, and mostly without foreign missionaries at all. He would have wanted it so. The work was never his. It was God’s own work through man, and God has been faithful to it.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name one promise of God you will act on this month, in a practical matter, as Taylor did.
2. Stop examining your faith for a week. Whenever you catch yourself doing so, turn instead to a statement of God’s faithfulness in Scripture.
3. Look at your life and ask whether the inner and the outer are both present, and in the right order.
4. Decide which of Taylor’s callings are not yours, and let go of any guilt about them. Then ask what yours is.
5. Choose one of Taylor’s own writings and begin reading it this week.

### A PRAYER

> Faithful God, You have never failed one of Your children. I have read of Your faithfulness to Your servant, and I want to trust You as he did.
> Keep me from admiring faith without exercising it, and from copying another’s calling instead of obeying my own.
> When my faith fails, let me rest on Your faithfulness. When my strength fails, be my strength. When I grieve, be near.
> And let my life, like his, be a small piece of evidence that You can be trusted absolutely. Amen.
# A Reader’s Guide to Hudson Taylor

> Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. 2 TIMOTHY 2:15

Taylor is easy to read and easy to finish. He wrote little, and nearly all of it is short: a memoir, two small books of Bible meditations, and a number of addresses first printed in the mission’s magazine, China’s Millions. His style is plain and warm, and many pages end in a question to the reader. The difficulty with Taylor is not quantity or obscurity. It is that his books are so readable they can be admired in an evening and forgotten by the next.

## Begin here

A Retrospect. Taylor’s own account of his conversion, his training in Hull and London, his early years in China and the founding of the China Inland Mission, first gathered as a book in 1894. It is the best single entry point. In the Ochorus library, where it is also retold for teens and for children.

Union and Communion. His meditations on the Song of Solomon, the fruit of the inner life he found after 1869. Short, tender and searching; the basis of chapters ten to twelve of this volume. Read it slowly. In the Ochorus library.

## On consecration and service

Separation and Service. Meditations on Numbers 6 and 7: the Nazarite, the priestly blessing and the princes’ offerings. Less known than it deserves, and full of homely illustrations. The basis of chapters fourteen and fifteen. In the Ochorus library.

China’s Spiritual Need and Claims. The little book he wrote with Maria’s help in 1865 to lay the needs of inland China before British Christians. It is a window into the burden behind the mission. Not yet in the Ochorus library.

## Addresses

Nine of Taylor’s shorter addresses are in the Ochorus library. Begin with Unfailing Springs, which contains his own testimony to the crisis of 1869 and the grief of 1870, and A Ribband of Blue, on holiness in small things. Blessed Prosperity and Blessed Adversity belong together, on the first psalm and on Job. Self-Denial versus Self-Assertion is only a few pages long and one of the most searching things he wrote. All Sufficiency, Under the Shepherd’s Care, Coming to the King and A Full Reward complete the set.

## Approach with care

The appendix to Union and Communion and the closing pages of the second part of Separation and Service set out Taylor’s view that not all Christians will share in the first resurrection or be caught up before the great tribulation, but only those whose lives show them ready. It is not the heart of his teaching and can be set aside.

In Blessed Prosperity he treats the promise that whatsoever the righteous man doeth shall prosper as absolute, while adding that its prosperity may be visible only to faith. Read it together with Blessed Adversity, which corrects any impression that faithfulness guarantees outward success.

## About Taylor

Dr and Mrs Howard Taylor, his son and daughter-in-law, wrote the standard two-volume life: Hudson Taylor in Early Years: The Growth of a Soul (1911) and Hudson Taylor and the China Inland Mission: The Growth of a Work of God (1918). Rich in letters and warmly devotional, they are the source of most later accounts. Their shorter Hudson Taylor’s Spiritual Secret (1932) concentrates on the exchanged life and has been read by millions. A. J. Broomhall’s Hudson Taylor and China’s Open Century, in seven volumes (1981–1989), is the most detailed history, fuller on the mission’s setting and its controversies. Roger Steer’s J. Hudson Taylor: A Man in Christ (1990) is a good single-volume life. Alvyn Austin’s China’s Millions (2007) is a scholarly history of the mission and more critical than the family biographies. The older lives were written by people who loved him, and it shows in what they leave out; read more than one. The Ochorus author page carries a shorter biography.

## Three counsels for reading him

Read him with the Bible open. Taylor claimed no authority of his own and built everything on texts. Check them, and notice when his allegories go beyond them.

Read him for the God he trusted, not for the man. His stories are memorable, and the temptation is to be impressed. He told them so that his readers would trust God in their own circumstances. Ask, after each one, what it tells you about God.

And read him with something to obey. Taylor tested the Bible by living it. A chapter of his acted on, in money, in prayer or in service, will do more than all his books admired.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21
