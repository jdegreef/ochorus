# Introduction

> As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God. PSALM 42:1–2

Some teachers are remembered for a system. A. W. Tozer is remembered for an ache. He was a Chicago pastor with no schooling beyond the elementary years, a man who taught himself from second-hand books and public libraries, who preached for more than forty years in one small denomination and wrote only a handful of books with his own hand. Yet more than sixty years after his death his sentences are still copied into notebooks, pinned above desks and read aloud from pulpits in every part of the world. The reason is not that he told Christians anything new. It is that he told them something old which they had quietly stopped believing: that God Himself is to be sought, found, known and enjoyed, and that anything less is a religion of words.

He was not a theologian in the academic sense, and he never pretended to be. He had little patience with men who could describe God in fine detail and had never been silent before Him. What he had was a single conviction pressed home from every angle, and it is the thread that runs through this whole book. The Christian’s great need is not more activity, more method or more information. It is God. Not the blessings of God, not the gifts of God, not even the doctrines about God, true as they are — but God Himself, known personally, loved above everything, worshipped for His own sake.

## Why he still matters

Tozer saw, earlier and more clearly than most, what happens to a church that keeps its orthodoxy and loses its awe. The creed stays correct. The meetings grow bright and busy. The talk is all of programmes, techniques, numbers and results. And somewhere along the way the sense of the living God simply goes out of the building, and nobody notices, because everything else is still running. He called the church of his day to account for exactly this, and the call has not grown less urgent.

> Thus saith the Lord, Stand ye in the ways, and see, and ask for the old paths, where is the good way, and walk therein, and ye shall find rest for your souls. JEREMIAH 6:16

He matters, too, because he held together things that are often torn apart. He was an evangelical who believed in the new birth, the blood of Christ and the authority of Scripture without reservation. He was also a man who read the old devotional writers of every century, Catholic and Protestant alike, and who insisted that the Christian faith was meant to be experienced and not merely assented to. He was a fierce critic of the church and a tender lover of it. He could write about the attributes of God with a grandeur that silences the reader, and then turn in the next paragraph to the washing of dishes and the mending of fences as work to be offered to God.

## How this book is arranged

After a chapter on his life, fifteen short chapters set out his teaching. The first group follows the soul’s search for God: the pursuit itself, the importance of how we think of God, and a sequence on His holiness, His infinitude and self-existence, and His sovereignty and love. The second group turns to the response God asks of us: worship, the surrender of our possessions, faith as the gaze of the soul, the voice of God in Scripture and the awareness of His presence. The third group is the most searching: the cross that ends the old life, the fulness of the Holy Spirit, the sacred character of ordinary work, the worldliness of the church, and the deeper life that Tozer believed was open to every believer who would pay its price. A conclusion and a reader’s guide close the book.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. That is deliberate. Tozer held that truth which does not move the heart and change the life has not really been received. He warned constantly against the Christian who reads about God with pleasure and never goes near Him. Read one chapter at a time. Stop at the application points. Pray the prayer, or better, pray your own — and then stay a while in silence before God, which is what he would have most wanted.

## A word about his words

Tozer’s books remain in copyright and in print, and this companion does not reproduce them. Every description of his teaching here is a summary in our own words. His books are named throughout so that the reader may follow any thread back to its source, and a short guide to them is given at the end. Readers are warmly encouraged to buy and read them. Nothing here can stand in for the compressed, burning sentences that made him loved.

## Where he must be read with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and there are places where Tozer must be read with care. It is kinder to say so at the start.

First, he had a strong mystical streak. He loved the medieval and later contemplative writers — men and women who wrote of union with God, of the dark cloud of unknowing, of the vision of God — and he commended them freely, sometimes with too little warning about where they departed from Scripture. He was also inclined to speak slightingly of reason and theology, as if careful thought about God were a rival to knowing Him rather than a servant of it. Chapters six and twelve weigh this.

Second, he could be severe. His prophetic temper made him a necessary voice, but he sometimes painted his fellow believers in broad and bitter strokes, and his censures of the church’s music, entertainment and methods were not always fair to the sincere people behind them.

Third, the man who wrote so movingly about the presence of God was, by the frank account of his biographers, a distant husband and father. That cost was real, it was borne by others, and it should not be hidden.

Fourth, he sometimes spoke as though the deeper life were the possession of a spiritual few, set apart from the ordinary run of believers. The New Testament knows no such aristocracy.

So the counsel of this book is the counsel of Scripture:

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21

Where Tozer is faithful — on the majesty of God, the emptiness of religion without Him, the necessity of worship, the cross and the Spirit — receive it gladly and act upon it. Where he presses beyond what is written, mark it and set it aside. You will lose nothing. He would not have wanted you to follow him. He would have wanted you to follow hard after God.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Write down, honestly, what you most want from God at present. Then ask yourself whether it is God Himself, or something from Him.
2. Recall the last time you were silent before God with no request to make. If you cannot remember, note that, without excusing it.
3. Decide now how you will read this book: one chapter a day or a week, with a Bible open beside it and a few minutes of quiet after each prayer.
4. Obtain one of Tozer’s own books — The Pursuit of God is the place to begin — and read it alongside these chapters.
5. Ask God to show you where your faith has become correct but cold.

> O God, You have made us for Yourself, and we have filled our lives with lesser things and called it religion.
> Forgive me for knowing so much about You and seeking You so little. Forgive me for loving Your gifts more than I love You.
> As I read these pages, do not let me admire a hungry man and remain full of myself. Make me hungry too.

# A Man of God

> O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is. PSALM 63:1

A farm in the hills. Aiden Wilson Tozer was born on the twenty-first of April, 1897, in the farm country of Clearfield County, in western Pennsylvania, near a hamlet called La Jose. The family was poor, the land was hard and the schooling was thin. There was no high school in his future and no college, and certainly no seminary. What there was, from very early, was a boy who read whatever he could reach. The habit never left him. For the rest of his life the most unlettered of American pastors would also be one of the best read, and every scrap of it was gathered by his own effort, at his own expense, from libraries, second-hand bookshops and borrowed volumes.

Akron. When he was in his middle teens the family moved west to Akron, Ohio, a city growing fast on the back of the rubber industry, and the young Tozer went to work in a tyre factory. It was ordinary labour in an ordinary place, and nothing about it suggested what was coming. Then, one day in 1915, when he was about seventeen, he was walking home from work and heard a street preacher. The man was not remarkable, and the words were not new. He was urging his hearers, in substance, that if they did not know how to be saved they could simply call on God and ask Him for mercy as a sinner. Tozer went home, climbed into the attic of the family house, and did exactly that. There was no church in it, no counsellor, no ceremony. There was a sentence heard in the street and a boy alone with God under a roof. It set the pattern for the whole life that followed. The decisive things would happen in private, between him and God, with the door shut.

> God be merciful to me a sinner. LUKE 18:13

A preacher without papers. The change was complete, and it went to work at once. He began to pray, to read the Bible and to preach, first on the street corners of Akron and wherever else he could find hearers. In 1918 he married Ada Cecelia Pfautz, a young woman of devout family, and the two were to be married for some forty-five years and to have seven children. He had no training and no degree, and he never acquired either. Yet the Christian and Missionary Alliance, the movement A. B. Simpson had founded a generation before, recognised what it was hearing. After a start among small congregations in West Virginia he was ordained in the Alliance in 1920, and there followed pastorates in Toledo, Ohio, and in Indianapolis. He was learning his craft in public and his theology in private, reading the Puritans, the poets, the church fathers and the old devotional writers with a hunger that astonished those who knew how little formal education lay behind it.

Southside. In 1928 he was called to Southside Alliance Church in Chicago, a modest congregation on the south side of the city. He stayed thirty-one years. It was there that he became the Tozer people remember. The church never grew into a large one; he was not that kind of preacher and did not want to be. But the preaching drew hearers from well beyond the congregation, including students from the Chicago colleges who came to hear a man speak of God as though he had just come from His presence. He broadcast on the radio station of the Moody Bible Institute. In 1943 he published Wingspread, a life of A. B. Simpson, the founder of his own movement. In 1950 he became editor of the Alliance’s magazine, The Alliance Weekly, later renamed The Alliance Witness, and he kept that post until his death. His editorials, short, sharp and often severe, were read far beyond his own denomination, and many of them were later gathered into books.

The book written on a train. His most famous work came, by the account of his biographers, almost in a single rush. He was travelling by train to Texas, and through the long hours of the night he wrote, with the pages coming faster than he could have planned them. The result was The Pursuit of God, published in 1948. It is a short book, and in some ways a simple one. It is also, by general consent, one of the devotional classics of the twentieth century. It gathered up everything he had been preaching for twenty years — that God is to be sought for Himself, that He may be known and not merely known about, that the heart must be emptied of its possessions and its pride before it can be filled — and set it in a form that ordinary believers could carry about with them. It has never been out of print.

> Blessed are they that keep his testimonies, and that seek him with the whole heart. PSALM 119:2

The man on the floor. Behind the books was a life of prayer that those who knew him found hard to account for. He would go early to his study, and it was known that he would change out of his good suit into old clothes so as not to spoil it, because much of his praying was done not in a chair but stretched full length upon the floor. He was not, most of the time, bringing a list of requests. He was worshipping. His habit was to set before God not what he wanted but what God is, and to stay there. He loved the old hymns as some men love a garden, and he commended a good hymnbook as a companion to the Bible in private devotion. Those who heard him preach sensed that they were hearing the overflow of those hours, and they were right.

Honours and plainness. He had no earned degree of any kind, but two colleges gave him honorary doctorates, Wheaton in 1950 and Houghton in 1952. He accepted them, though he is said to have disliked being called Doctor. In other ways he kept himself plain almost to the point of austerity. He is said never to have owned a car, travelling by bus and train. He gave away much of what his books earned and kept his household on very little. He distrusted money as he distrusted celebrity, and he was sharply aware of the power of both to dull the edge of a servant of God.

The cost at home. It would be dishonest to leave the portrait there, and his biographers have not done so. The same absorption in God and in his books that made him a prophet in the pulpit made him, by their frank account, a distant husband and father. He was often withdrawn at home, sparing with affection, hard to reach. His frugality, admirable in a single man, fell heavily on a wife raising seven children. Lyle Dorsett, whose biography is sympathetic, records that after Tozer’s death Ada married again, and that when she was asked about her happiness she is reported to have said, in effect, that her first husband had loved Jesus Christ, while her second husband loved her. It is not a bitter remark. It is a sad and honest one, and it belongs in any true account of him. A man may pursue God with his whole heart and still fail those nearest to him. Both things were true of Tozer, and neither cancels the other.

> But if any provide not for his own, and specially for those of his own house, he hath denied the faith, and is worse than an infidel. 1 TIMOTHY 5:8

That verse speaks of material provision, and Tozer did provide. But the wider principle beneath it — that a man’s faith is proved first under his own roof — is one his readers are right to hold before themselves as they read him.

Toronto and the last years. In 1959 he left Chicago for Avenue Road Church in Toronto, where he spent his final years. His great book on the attributes of God, The Knowledge of the Holy, appeared in 1961. It is his most sustained piece of writing and, alongside The Pursuit of God, the book by which he is best known. He continued to preach and to write his editorials, and to lament, as he had lamented for forty years, the shallowness of a church that had kept its creed and lost its God.

The stone. On the twelfth of May, 1963, he died of a heart attack in Toronto, still in the pastorate. He was sixty-six. He was buried near Chicago, and his remains were later moved to Ellet Cemetery in Akron, not far from the streets where he had heard the preacher and the attic where he had prayed. The stone is a plain one. It bears his name and three short words describing him as a man of God. Much of the large shelf of books that now carries his name was compiled after his death by editors working from his sermons, editorials and recorded addresses, and it is still growing. He himself wrote comparatively little. But what he wrote came, as he always insisted everything true must come, out of the place of prayer.

> Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God. MATTHEW 5:8

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Tozer’s conversion happened in an attic with no one present. Where is your place of meeting with God, and when did you last use it with the door shut?
2. He educated himself by steady reading. Choose one serious Christian book and set aside a fixed time each week to read it.
3. Consider honestly whether your devotion to God has become an excuse for neglecting anyone near you. Ask someone in your home, and listen.
4. Tozer kept himself free of money and celebrity. Name one thing you own or want that is quietly dulling your appetite for God.
5. Give thanks for one ordinary believer whose word, like the street preacher’s, turned your life toward God.

> Lord, You found a boy on a street corner and met him in an attic. You still meet those who call on You for mercy.
> Thank You for the servants You have given Your church, for their hunger and their faithfulness. Keep me from admiring them and forgetting You.
> Where I have loved You in words and failed the people You have given me, forgive me, and teach me to love them for Your sake.
> Let my life, when it is over, be summed up as simply as his stone. Make me a man, a woman, of God. Amen.

# The Pursuit of God

> My soul followeth hard after thee: thy right hand upholdeth me. PSALM 63:8

Every one of Tozer’s books is, in the end, about one thing, and the title of his most famous book names it. The Christian life is a pursuit of God. Not a pursuit of happiness, or of usefulness, or of blessing, or even of holiness considered as a thing in itself, but of God — the living, personal God, sought for His own sake and loved for what He is. Tozer believed that this pursuit had been almost entirely forgotten in the church of his day, and that its absence explained nearly everything else that was wrong.

## God seeks first

He began where Scripture begins, with God. It would be easy to read a book called The Pursuit of God as a manual of human effort, and Tozer was at pains to prevent it. Before any man can follow hard after God, God must first have come after him. The desire itself is a gift. The very hunger that sends a believer to his knees was put there by the One he is hungering for.

> No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him: and I will raise him up at the last day. JOHN 6:44

This is the ground of all hope in the matter. If the pursuit depended upon our native appetite for God, there would be no pursuit at all, for the natural heart has none. But God has taken the initiative. He called to Adam in the garden when Adam was hiding. He sought out Jacob at Bethel and wrestled him at Jabbok. He came to Moses in a bush in the desert. And in the fulness of time He came to us in His Son, seeking and saving the lost. The believer’s pursuit of God is only ever a response. We follow, Tozer liked to point out, because His right hand upholds us, and we seek because we were first sought.

## The great deception

Yet having said that, Tozer turned sharply on what he saw as the characteristic error of the evangelicalism around him. It had reduced the Christian life to a single transaction. A man accepts Christ, is told that he now has everything, and is encouraged to go on his way. What was meant as the door into a lifelong fellowship with God was presented as the end of the matter. The effect, Tozer believed, was disastrous. It produced a generation of Christians who were sincerely converted and yet had no expectation of ever knowing God more deeply than they did on the day they first believed. They had been taught to find Him and then stop looking.

Against this he set the testimony of the whole Bible and of the saints of every age. Those who have known God best have always wanted Him most. Far from ending the search, finding God begins it. Moses had spoken with God face to face, as a man speaks with his friend, and it was precisely then that he asked for more.

> And he said, I beseech thee, shew me thy glory. EXODUS 33:18

Paul had seen the risen Christ, founded churches, suffered for His name and been caught up into the third heaven. Yet near the end of his life he wrote as a man who had only begun.

> That I may know him, and the power of his resurrection, and the fellowship of his sufferings, being made conformable unto his death. PHILIPPIANS 3:10

That, Tozer said, is the true shape of spiritual life. Finding God and going on seeking Him is no contradiction; it is simply the way love behaves. The heart that has met Him is content and hungry together. The more it has, the more it wants. Complacency, not desire, is the mark of spiritual decline.

## One thing

The pursuit requires singleness. Tozer loved the verse in which David gathers all his longings into one.

> One thing have I desired of the Lord, that will I seek after; that I may dwell in the house of the Lord all the days of my life, to behold the beauty of the Lord, and to enquire in his temple. PSALM 27:4

Most Christians want many things from God, and God in addition. Tozer argued that the blessed life begins at the moment a man wants God alone, and wants everything else only in Him. He did not mean that other desires are evil. He meant that they must take their place beneath the one great desire, or they will quietly replace it. The man who seeks God and something else has already begun to seek the something else. The man who seeks God only finds, to his surprise, that everything else is given back to him in its proper place.

> But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you. MATTHEW 6:33

## God is a person

Beneath all this lay a conviction that Tozer returned to constantly. God is a Person, and a Person may be known. Not known exhaustively — the creature can never exhaust the Creator — but truly, increasingly and with all the intimacy of friendship. He insisted that the knowledge of God is not the same as the knowledge of doctrine. A man may master every article of the creed and never once have met the God it describes. He said as much, repeatedly, in his preaching, and his sermon The Infinite God, which is in the Ochorus library, presses the point that theology tells us about God while eternal life is to know Him.

> And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent. JOHN 17:3

It follows that the pursuit of God is personal, not technical. There is no method that guarantees it and no technique that shortens it. It is conducted the way any friendship is conducted: by time spent together, by attention, by honesty, by love. The saints whom Tozer most admired had no secrets in the modern sense. They simply wanted God badly enough to give Him their time, their attention and their hearts.

## Weighing it

Tozer’s call to pursue God is one of the most bracing things in modern Christian writing, and it is overwhelmingly faithful to Scripture. But it needs one safeguard, which he himself supplied in places and not in others. The pursuit of God must never be confused with a restless anxiety about whether we have yet found Him. The believer’s acceptance with God is settled by the finished work of Christ. We do not pursue God in order to be accepted; we pursue Him because we have been accepted and have tasted His goodness. Where this order is reversed, the call to seek God more deeply can become a new law, and the sensitive conscience can be driven into endless self-examination, always wondering whether it has sought hard enough.

> Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us … let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith. HEBREWS 10:19–22

The pursuit, in other words, starts from assurance and moves into intimacy. It is the joyful exploration of a relationship already secured, not a desperate climb towards one still in doubt. Read in that light, Tozer’s summons is pure gospel: the God who has given Himself to you in Christ is inviting you to know Him more.

## Beginning the pursuit

How does a man begin? Tozer’s counsel was plain. Stop being content. Confess the coldness. Ask God to create the hunger that you lack. Then make room for Him — in time, in attention, in silence — and keep making room. The pursuit is not a crisis to be passed through but a direction to be kept.

> Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you. JAMES 4:8

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself whether you have treated conversion as the end of your search for God rather than the beginning. Write down what has changed in your knowledge of Him since you first believed.
2. Read Psalm 27 and Psalm 63 slowly, aloud, once a day this week. Notice how often the writer wants God rather than something from Him.
3. Set aside fifteen minutes this week in which you ask God for nothing. Spend it thanking Him for what He is.
4. Name one good thing you have been seeking alongside God that has begun to crowd Him out. Put it back beneath Him.
5. If you have been anxious about your standing with God, read Hebrews 10:19–22 and rest in it before you seek anything more.

# What Comes into Our Minds

> These things hast thou done, and I kept silence; thou thoughtest that I was altogether such an one as thyself: but I will reprove thee, and set them in order before thine eyes. PSALM 50:21

The Knowledge of the Holy opens with a claim so simple that it is easy to read past it, and so large that it governs everything Tozer ever taught. The most important thing about any of us, he said in effect, is not our circumstances or our achievements or even our conduct, but what we actually think God is like when we think of Him. Not what we would say if asked to recite a creed, but the picture that rises in the mind, unbidden, when the name of God is spoken. That picture, more than anything else, determines the whole shape of a life. And the same is true of a church.

## The idea beneath the idea

Tozer was careful to distinguish between two things. There is the doctrine of God a man professes, and there is the idea of God he actually holds. They are often not the same. A man may confess with complete sincerity that God is holy, almighty, all-knowing and good, and yet live day by day as if God were distracted, indulgent, easily managed and a little out of date. The creed is in the head; the real conception is buried deeper, in the heart, and it is found out not by what a man says in church but by what he does when he is alone, afraid, tempted or offended.

> The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God. PSALM 14:1

Notice where the fool says it. Not aloud, necessarily, and not in argument. In his heart. Practical atheism is compatible with a great deal of religious observance. So is practical idolatry. And this was the heart of Tozer’s diagnosis. He believed that the church of his day had not abandoned its doctrine of God. It had simply lost its sense of Him. It had kept the words and let the reality shrink, until the God of its imagination was a smaller, softer, more manageable figure than the God of the Bible.

## Idolatry of the mind

That shrinking, Tozer argued, is the essence of idolatry. We think of idolatry as the carving of images, and so it is. But the image in wood or stone is only the outward form of something that happens first within. Before a man bows down to an idol, he has already imagined a god unlike the true God, and then made something to match his imagination. Paul traces exactly that sequence.

> Because that, when they knew God, they glorified him not as God, neither were thankful; but became vain in their imaginations, and their foolish heart was darkened. ROMANS 1:21

> And changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man. ROMANS 1:23

The Christian who never goes near a pagan shrine may still be an idolater in this deeper sense. If the God he worships is less holy, less just, less loving, less great than the God who has revealed Himself, then he is worshipping a figment, however orthodox his vocabulary. Tozer did not soften this. He believed that low thoughts of God were not a minor defect of modern piety but the root of most of its other failings.

## Why it matters so much

He traced the consequences with some care. A low view of God produces low worship, because no one is moved to awe by a being he thinks he understands. It produces low morals, because a God who does not much mind sin will not much restrain it. It produces shallow evangelism, because a sinner who has never trembled before God will not know what he has been saved from. It produces a church that relies on technique and entertainment, because it no longer expects anything from God Himself. It produces prayer that is thin and occasional, because a small God is hardly worth talking to. Put right the thought of God, Tozer believed, and a great many of these other troubles would begin to put themselves right.

> To whom then will ye liken God? or what likeness will ye compare unto him? ISAIAH 40:18

> Lift up your eyes on high, and behold who hath created these things, that bringeth out their host by number: he calleth them all by names by the greatness of his might, for that he is strong in power; not one faileth. ISAIAH 40:26

That was why he wrote The Knowledge of the Holy at all. He did not intend a textbook of theology. He intended a series of meditations on the attributes of God — His self-existence, eternity, infinitude, holiness, wisdom, faithfulness, justice, mercy, love and the rest — that would lift the reader’s thoughts to something nearer the truth, and so move him to worship. Each chapter in the book begins with a prayer. The purpose was devotion, not debate.

## The only true image

Here a word of balance is needed. Tozer’s method in The Knowledge of the Holy is largely to reason from the attributes of God as the church has classically confessed them. That is a sound and ancient method, and Christians have benefited greatly from it. But the New Testament places one image of God above every other. When Philip asked to be shown the Father, the Lord answered him plainly.

> Jesus saith unto him, Have I been so long time with you, and yet hast thou not known me, Philip? he that hath seen me hath seen the Father. JOHN 14:9

The fullest and surest thought of God that any human mind can have is the face of Jesus Christ.

> For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:6

Tozer knew and believed this; he was a thoroughly Christ-centred preacher. But a reader who takes up his book on the attributes should keep the Gospels open beside it. The holiness, justice, mercy and love of God are not abstractions to be defined; they have walked among us, wept at a grave, been nailed to a cross and risen from the dead. The cure for a small idea of God is not only to think harder about His perfections but to look longer at His Son.

## Correcting the picture

How is a distorted idea of God corrected? Tozer’s answer was that it cannot be done by the mind alone. The thought of God must be conformed to God’s own revelation of Himself in Scripture, and that is the work of the Holy Spirit, who takes the things of God and shows them to us. It requires reading the Bible with the question constantly in view: what does this tell me God is like? It requires repentance, because many of our wrong thoughts of Him are not innocent mistakes but convenient ones. And it requires worship, because the heart learns God by adoring Him as surely as the mind learns Him by study.

> Be still, and know that I am God. PSALM 46:10

3. As you read the Bible this week, ask of every passage the single question: what does this show me God is like?
4. Read one of the Gospels through at a sitting, looking only at how Jesus reveals the Father.
5. Consider whether any of your wrong thoughts about God are convenient ones — ideas you keep because they make Him easier to live with. Confess them.

# Holy, Holy, Holy

> And one cried unto another, and said, Holy, holy, holy, is the Lord of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory. ISAIAH 6:3

If Tozer had to choose one attribute of God which the modern church had most thoroughly forgotten, it would have been His holiness. He believed that evangelicals had come to speak of God with a familiarity that bordered on impudence, and that the reverent fear which fills the Bible from end to end had simply drained out of their worship. The recovery of the holiness of God was, for him, not one subject among many. It was the precondition of any true revival.

## Beyond comparison

The first thing he wanted his readers to grasp about holiness was that it cannot be understood by comparison. We naturally approach the attributes of God by starting from something we know in ourselves and imagining it made infinite. We know a little of power, and so we try to imagine all power. We know a little of wisdom, and so we try to imagine perfect wisdom. Tozer argued that this method breaks down entirely when we come to holiness. God’s holiness is not simply the best human goodness multiplied without limit. It is something of another order altogether — not a higher point on our scale but a different thing, of which our goodness is at best a faint and broken reflection. We cannot build up to it from below. We can only bow before it when it is revealed.

> Who is like unto thee, O Lord, among the gods? who is like thee, glorious in holiness, fearful in praises, doing wonders? EXODUS 15:11

That is why the seraphim in Isaiah’s vision do not describe the holiness of God. They simply repeat the word, three times over, and cover their faces. It is the language of creatures who have come to the end of language.

## Woe is me

The second thing he stressed was the effect of that holiness on those who see it. In Scripture no one ever meets the holy God and comes away pleased with himself. Isaiah was, by any human measure, a good man and a prophet of God. His response to the vision was not rapture but ruin.

> Then said I, Woe is me! for I am undone; because I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips: for mine eyes have seen the King, the Lord of hosts. ISAIAH 6:5

Job, after all his protests, came to the same place. So did Peter in the boat, and John on Patmos. Tozer drew the obvious conclusion. A religion that leaves people comfortable in the presence of God has not yet brought them into His presence at all. A sense of one’s own uncleanness is not a sign of spiritual failure. It is one of the first signs that God has actually been seen.

> I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes. JOB 42:5–6

## Holiness and wrath

Tozer did not flinch from the hard corollary. The holiness of God is not a placid quality. It is active, and it is set against all that opposes it. What Scripture calls the wrath of God is not a fit of temper, nor something foreign to His love; it is His holiness in its settled opposition to sin. A holy God cannot be neutral about evil any more than light can be neutral about darkness. Tozer believed that a church which had quietly dropped the wrath of God from its preaching had not made God more loving. It had made Him less holy, and so less God.

> For our God is a consuming fire. HEBREWS 12:29

He was equally clear that this is precisely why the gospel is good news. The holy God has not lowered His standard in order to receive sinners. He has met it Himself. In Isaiah’s vision the seraph flies with a live coal from the altar, touches the prophet’s lips, and pronounces his iniquity taken away. The altar is the place of sacrifice. The cleansing comes from there, and nowhere else.

> Lo, this hath touched thy lips; and thine iniquity is taken away, and thy sin purged. ISAIAH 6:7

The cross is where the holiness of God and the love of God meet without either being compromised. There the consuming fire fell, and fell on Christ, so that the unclean might come near and live.

## Be ye holy

Holiness is not only something God is; it is something He commands. Tozer took that command with complete seriousness.

> But as he which hath called you is holy, so be ye holy in all manner of conversation; because it is written, Be ye holy; for I am holy. 1 PETER 1:15–16

He did not mean by this a fussy external separatism, though he could sometimes sound as though he did. He meant that a man who has truly seen God will begin to hate what God hates and love what God loves. Holiness, in the believer, is the family likeness produced by long exposure to the Father. It is not reached by rule-keeping, but it will certainly show itself in the rules a man keeps. The more clearly we see the holiness of God, the less tolerance we will have for the sins we once excused, and the more patience we will have with the sinners God is still dealing with, knowing that He is still dealing with us.

## Weighing it

Tozer’s recovery of the holiness of God is a precious gift to the church, and there is nothing in it to set aside. But a reader should watch for one danger in the way he sometimes pressed it. Reverence can curdle into dread, and the believer who has been rightly humbled by the holiness of God can be wrongly kept at a distance by it. The New Testament does not ask the Christian to stand trembling outside the veil. It invites him in.

> Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need. HEBREWS 4:16

The balance is the one the writer to the Hebrews strikes in a single breath: we are to serve God acceptably with reverence and godly fear, and we are to do so as those who have received a kingdom which cannot be moved. Boldness and awe are not rivals. The child who knows his father is great is not less free for knowing it; he is more secure. Read Tozer, then, to recover your awe. Read Hebrews to be sure that your awe leads you in, not away.

> Wherefore we receiving a kingdom which cannot be moved, let us have grace, whereby we may serve God acceptably with reverence and godly fear. HEBREWS 12:28

# The Infinite God

> Great is our Lord, and of great power: his understanding is infinite. PSALM 147:5

Tozer once told his congregation that the sermon he was about to preach would make a greater demand on their minds than anything he had brought them before. The subject was the infinitude of God, and the sermon, preached on Psalm 147:5, is among his addresses in the Ochorus library under the title The Infinite God. It is worth reading, and it shows him at his most characteristic: plain-spoken, homely in illustration, and reaching all the while after something that cannot be put into words.

## A different mode of being

His starting point was that God does not exist as we exist. We are creatures of time and space and matter; we have beginnings, limits and dependencies. God has none of these. He is not simply a very large, very old, very powerful version of ourselves. He is of a wholly different order of being, and every attempt to picture Him in our own terms falls short before it begins.

> To whom then will ye liken me, or shall I be equal? saith the Holy One. ISAIAH 40:25

For this reason Tozer commended an old way of thinking about God which the classical theologians had used for centuries. Since our minds cannot reach up to God directly, we reason towards Him by denying our own limitations of Him. We say that He is self-existent, meaning that He has no origin. We say that He is self-sufficient, meaning that He depends on nothing and no one. We say that He is eternal, meaning that He has no beginning and no end. We say that He is immutable, meaning that He cannot change. We say that He is omniscient, meaning — and Tozer liked to startle his hearers with this — that God has never learned anything and never can, because there is nothing He does not already know. And we say that He is infinite, meaning that whatever He is, He is without limit.

## I am that I am

At the root of all this lies the name God gave Himself at the burning bush.

> And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM: and he said, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you. EXODUS 3:14

Everything else that exists was made, and so owes its existence to something outside itself. God alone simply is. He does not receive His life; He has it in Himself. Tozer drew from this a truth that is both humbling and freeing. God does not need us. He was perfectly complete and perfectly blessed before anything was made, and nothing we do can add to Him or take from Him.

> Neither is worshipped with men’s hands, as though he needed any thing, seeing he giveth to all life, and breath, and all things. ACTS 17:25

This is humbling, because it removes at a stroke the flattering thought that God is somehow dependent on our service or diminished by our neglect. It is freeing for the same reason. If God needed us, His love for us might be self-interest. Because He needs nothing, His love is pure gift. In another of his sermons in the Ochorus library, God Made Man to Worship, Tozer presses exactly this point: God has no need of us, and yet for reasons of His own He desires our love and our adoration. That He wants us, needing nothing, is one of the wonders of the gospel.

## No limit anywhere

Infinitude, Tozer said, applies to everything God is. His power has no ceiling. His wisdom has no edge. His love has no bottom. His mercy is not a great deal of mercy but mercy without measure. This, he believed, is the believer’s great comfort. We are always running up against our own limits and the limits of others: limited patience, limited strength, limited time, limited understanding. With God there is never a point at which the supply runs out.

> O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! ROMANS 11:33

> For I am the Lord, I change not; therefore ye sons of Jacob are not consumed. MALACHI 3:6

It follows, too, that God is never tired of us, never surprised by us, and never at a loss what to do with us. What He has promised He is able to perform, and what He has begun He is able to finish.

## Knowing and knowing about

In the same sermon Tozer drew a sharp line between knowing about God and knowing God. Theology, he said in effect, is what the human mind can grasp about God, and an intelligent unbeliever may grasp a great deal of it and still be lost. Eternal life is not a set of correct propositions; it is a living acquaintance with God Himself, given by the Holy Spirit. He compared this knowledge to the way a man knows he is alive — not by reasoning towards it but by a direct and inward awareness which no argument can create and no argument can remove.

> But God hath revealed them unto us by his Spirit: for the Spirit searcheth all things, yea, the deep things of God. 1 CORINTHIANS 2:10

There is deep truth in this, and every Christian who has known the difference between reciting a doctrine and being overwhelmed by it will recognise it.

## Weighing it

Yet here the reader meets one of the places where Tozer must be read with care. He sometimes set the heart’s knowledge of God against the mind’s so sharply that careful thought seemed almost a hindrance. He could speak with disdain of theologians and scholars, and at times he described the gulf in the church as lying between evangelical rationalists and evangelical mystics, and left no doubt which side he was on.

Scripture does not permit that division. The first and great commandment requires us to love God with all our mind as well as all our heart.

> Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. MATTHEW 22:37

The Spirit who gives the inward knowledge of God is the same Spirit who inspired the reasoned arguments of Romans and Hebrews. Doctrine does not save, but it guards, and the inward awareness of God that Tozer prized is only safe when it is tested by the written Word. Tozer himself, it should be said, was a careful reader and a precise thinker, and The Knowledge of the Holy is full of sound theology. The corrective he offered was needed: a faith of propositions only is a dead faith. But the cure for dead orthodoxy is living orthodoxy, not less of it.

# Our Refuge and Strength

> God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble. PSALM 46:1

Tozer rarely preached about current events. He said so himself, in one of the sermons now in the Ochorus library, before making an exception. He had watched, he told his congregation, a mounting fear take hold of the country around him — fear of war, fear of the bomb, fear of the future — and he had watched politicians and even evangelists use that fear to move people where they wanted them. He had nothing to say to the world but that it must repent and believe the gospel. But he had something to say to God’s people, and he took as his text the forty-sixth Psalm. The sermon, God Is Our Refuge and Strength, is a fine example of what happened whenever Tozer brought his doctrine of God to bear on ordinary human anxiety. He began with the first word of the verse, and stayed on it: God.

## The sovereign God

Everything in Tozer’s theology of comfort rested on the sovereignty of God. He meant by it that God is the one absolute ruler of all things, answerable to no one, dependent on no one, and able to accomplish whatever He purposes. Nothing takes Him by surprise. Nothing escapes His notice. Nothing can finally frustrate His will.

> Our God is in the heavens: he hath done whatsoever he hath pleased. PSALM 115:3

> And all the inhabitants of the earth are reputed as nothing: and he doeth according to his will in the army of heaven, and among the inhabitants of the earth: and none can stay his hand, or say unto him, What doest thou? DANIEL 4:35

In The Knowledge of the Holy Tozer treated this attribute with care. He was not a Calvinist; he belonged to a movement whose theology leaned the other way, and he believed that men make real choices for which they are truly responsible. He held the two truths together by insisting that God in His sovereignty has freely chosen to give men a real measure of freedom, and that even their rebellion takes place within limits He has set and cannot overturn His purposes in the end. Christians have argued about how best to state this relationship for many centuries, and will go on doing so. Tozer’s pastoral point did not depend on settling the argument. It was that the world is not out of control, because God has never let go of it.

## Therefore will not we fear

That is where the forty-sixth Psalm goes, and where Tozer followed it. The Psalm pictures the worst that can happen — the earth removed, the mountains carried into the sea, the waters roaring, the nations raging, kingdoms tottering — and then declares that God’s people will not be afraid. Not because the catastrophe will not come, but because God is in the midst of them.

> Therefore will not we fear, though the earth be removed, and though the mountains be carried into the midst of the sea. PSALM 46:2

> The Lord of hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our refuge. Selah. PSALM 46:7

Tozer believed that a great deal of Christian fear arises from a low view of God. The believer who thinks of God as well-meaning but somewhat overstretched will naturally be anxious, because there is always the chance that something will slip past Him. The believer who has seen God as the Scriptures show Him — sovereign, infinite, holy and good — can face the news with the calm of a child whose father holds the reins. He does not pretend that the danger is unreal. He simply knows who is greater.

## The love of God

Sovereignty alone would not comfort. An almighty God who did not love us would be more terrible than a weak one. The reason the sovereignty of God is good news is that the Sovereign is love. Tozer insisted that love is not merely something God does from time to time, or a mood into which He can be persuaded. It belongs to His nature. He loves because He is love, and He loves without any cause in the one loved.

> He that loveth not knoweth not God; for God is love. 1 JOHN 4:8

> Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins. 1 JOHN 4:10

Because God is infinite, His love is infinite. Because He is immutable, His love does not change. Because He is self-existent, His love owes nothing to our deserving and cannot be withdrawn because of our failures. And because He is holy, His love is not indulgence. It will not leave us as we are. It wills our highest good, which is to be made like Christ, and it will spare no pain that is necessary to accomplish it.

> And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose. ROMANS 8:28

Put together, the sovereignty and the love of God are the believer’s whole security. The God who loves me rules everything, and the God who rules everything loves me. There is no power in heaven or on earth that can come between.

> Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord. ROMANS 8:39

## Weighing it

There is little here to set aside. Tozer’s handling of the sovereignty of God was balanced and pastoral, and his handling of the love of God was sober and deep. One caution is worth adding for the reader who goes on to the sermon. Tozer’s contempt for what he regarded as fear-mongering in the politics and evangelism of his day was sharp, and in places he speaks of his fellow preachers with more scorn than charity. The truth he was defending — that the child of God need not be driven by fear — is right. The temper in which he sometimes defended it need not be copied.

## Being still

The Psalm ends with a command that sums it all up.

> Be still, and know that I am God: I will be exalted among the heathen, I will be exalted in the earth. PSALM 46:10

Stillness, for Tozer, was not passivity. It was the posture of trust. It meant ceasing to strive, ceasing to calculate, ceasing to lie awake rehearsing disasters, and resting the whole weight of the heart on the character of God.

# Worship, the Missing Jewel

> So shall the king greatly desire thy beauty: for he is thy Lord; and worship thou him. PSALM 45:11

In the last years of his life Tozer preached a series of sermons on worship, and one of them, God Made Man to Worship, is among his addresses in the Ochorus library. It opens with a thesis he had been pressing for decades. God made man for a purpose, and the purpose was worship. Man fell, and in falling lost both the ability to worship rightly and the knowledge of whom to worship. Redemption restores what the fall destroyed. We are saved, in the first place, not to be busy for God but to adore Him. Tozer summed up the condition of the church around him in a phrase that became the title of one of his best-known booklets: worship, he said, was the missing jewel in evangelical Christianity.

## Made for this

He built the case from the whole drift of Scripture rather than from a single proof text, and he told the preachers in his audience that this was the right way to handle any doctrine. From Eden to the new Jerusalem, the Bible presents God as desiring the adoration of His creatures. It was God who came looking for Adam in the garden, not Adam who came looking for God. And the vision of heaven that closes the Bible is a vision of worship.

> Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power: for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created. REVELATION 4:11

Tozer found it one of the most moving truths in Scripture that the God who needs nothing should nevertheless want to be loved and admired by the creatures He has made. He noticed, too, the strange contradiction in fallen human nature: the longing to worship survives the fall, even when the capacity to worship the true God has been lost. Men will worship something. The whole history of religion bears witness to it. The question is never whether a man will worship, but what and how.

## Worship God refuses

Here Tozer turned, as he often did, to hard words. He believed it was necessary to clear away false worship before true worship could be recognised, and in the same sermon he named several kinds that God rejects.

There is the worship of Cain, which comes to God without atonement. It rests on a false idea of God, as though He were too easy-going to require a sacrifice for sin; on a false idea of man, as though he were already on good terms with God by nature; and on a false idea of sin, as though it were a small matter.

> By faith Abel offered unto God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain. HEBREWS 11:4

There is the worship of the Samaritans, which picks and chooses among the truths of God, keeping those it likes and quietly neglecting the rest. Tozer observed that a truth need not be denied to be killed; it is enough to leave it alone.

> Ye worship ye know not what: we know what we worship: for salvation is of the Jews. JOHN 4:22

And there is the worship that is really an aesthetic rapture — the feeling of sublimity a man may have before a sunset or a mountain, or the pleasant religious sensation stirred by soft music, candlelight and a sonorous voice. Tozer did not despise beauty. He loved poetry and music. But he insisted that such feelings are not yet the worship of God, and that a man may enjoy them richly on Sunday and live as a scoundrel the rest of the week.

## In spirit and in truth

Against all of these he set the words of Christ to the woman at the well.

> God is a Spirit: and they that worship him must worship him in spirit and in truth. JOHN 4:24

True worship, as Tozer described it across many sermons, has several marks. It is directed to the true God as He has revealed Himself, and so it is bound to the truth of Scripture. It comes through Christ and His blood, and not by any other way. It is inspired by the Holy Spirit, and not manufactured by atmosphere. And it is a response of the whole person — mind, will and affections — to the sight of God’s glory. At its heart there is a kind of astonished love, a reverent wonder that fills the soul when it sees God as He is, mingled with trust, delight and self-forgetfulness.

> O come, let us worship and bow down: let us kneel before the Lord our maker. PSALM 95:6

## Worship before work

One of Tozer’s most persistent themes was the order of priority between worship and service. He believed that the church of his day had reversed it. It prized activity, measured success by numbers and organisation, and recruited workers eagerly while neglecting to make worshippers. Tozer held that God wants worshippers first, and that work which does not spring from worship is of little value to Him, however impressive it looks. He liked to point to Mary of Bethany, who sat at the Lord’s feet while her sister was distracted with much serving.

> But one thing is needful: and Mary hath chosen that good part, which shall not be taken away from her. LUKE 10:42

The point is not that service is unimportant. It is that service is meant to overflow from adoration. A man who has been with God will work for God, and his work will carry something of God in it. A man who works for God without being with Him will sooner or later work for himself.

## Weighing it

Tozer’s recovery of worship is among the most valuable things he left the church, and much of it is simply the Bible read aloud. Two cautions are worth adding. First, his critique of the music and methods of his day was sometimes sweeping, and a reader should not assume that every form of worship Tozer disliked is displeasing to God. The test he himself gave — spirit and truth, through Christ, by the Spirit — is the right one, and it cuts across styles.

Second, worship in the New Testament is wider than the act of adoration, precious as that is. Paul describes the whole of the Christian’s life, offered to God, as his reasonable service.

> I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. ROMANS 12:1

Tozer knew this well, and he would develop it in his teaching on the sacrament of living. But it is worth holding the two together from the start: the hour on the knees and the day in the world are one offering.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read Tozer’s sermon God Made Man to Worship in the Ochorus library, with John 4 open beside it.
2. Examine your own worship against the three kinds God rejects. Is any of it without atonement, selective, or merely aesthetic?
3. Begin your prayer each day this week with five minutes of adoration only — no confession yet, no requests, only praise.
4. Ask whether your service for God has outrun your worship of Him. Cut back one activity if necessary, and give the time to God.
5. Read Revelation 4 and 5 aloud. Let the worship of heaven set the tone for your own.

> Lord, You made me to worship You, and I have given my adoration to a thousand lesser things.
> Forgive me for coming to You without the blood of Christ, for choosing which of Your truths to hear, and for mistaking pleasant feelings for worship.
> Teach me to worship You in spirit and in truth. Show me Your glory, and let my heart answer with wonder and love.
> Make me a worshipper first, and then a worker, and let all my work flow out of my worship. Amen.

# The Blessedness of Possessing Nothing

> Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. MATTHEW 5:3

Among the short chapters of The Pursuit of God, one has troubled and helped its readers more than almost any other. Its title sounds like a paradox, and it is meant to. Tozer argued that the chief obstacle to knowing God is not our sins, taken one by one, but our possessiveness — the deep habit of the fallen heart that clutches at things, people, reputation and even spiritual gifts, and calls them mine. The blessed life, he said, belongs to the one who has let go.

## Things in the wrong place

He traced the trouble back to the fall. In the beginning, he suggested, God made a world full of good things for man’s use and enjoyment, and man received them with thanks, while God Himself occupied the centre of his heart. When sin came in, the order was overturned. God was pushed out of the centre, and things rushed in to fill the space. Now the things which were meant to be held lightly in the hand have taken root in the heart, and the heart cannot let them go without pain.

> For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also. MATTHEW 6:21

Tozer did not mean that possessions are evil, or that the Christian must be literally poor. He meant that our relationship to possessions has become disordered. We do not simply use things; we depend on them. We do not simply enjoy people; we cling to them. We do not simply receive gifts from God; we guard them as if our life depended on keeping them. And the little words my and mine, spoken over all of it, betray the true condition of the heart. We have made ourselves owners of what we were meant only to hold in trust.

## The root must come out

He was insistent that this disorder cannot be cured by a little gentle adjustment. The roots go too deep. The Lord Himself spoke of the matter in the most radical terms.

> For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: but whosoever will lose his life for my sake, the same shall save it. LUKE 9:24

The self that clings must be denied, and the things it clings to must be surrendered. Tozer knew that this is painful, and he did not pretend otherwise. The tearing out of a root always hurts. But he held that the pain is the pain of healing, and that the one who submits to it finds an unexpected freedom on the other side. Things which were once masters become servants again. People who were once idols become beloved companions. And God, restored to the centre, becomes the heart’s true treasure.

## Abraham on the mountain

To illustrate this, Tozer turned to the most searching test in the Old Testament. Abraham had waited decades for the son of promise. Isaac was not only his child but the channel through which every promise of God was to be fulfilled. And God asked for him.

> And he said, Take now thy son, thine only son Isaac, whom thou lovest, and get thee into the land of Moriah; and offer him there for a burnt offering upon one of the mountains which I will tell thee of. GENESIS 22:2

Abraham obeyed, and at the last moment God stayed his hand and provided a ram in Isaac’s place. Tozer drew attention to what Abraham received back. He received Isaac, of course. But he received him differently. Before Moriah, Isaac had been in danger of becoming the centre of Abraham’s life. After Moriah, he was still dearly loved, but he was held in open hands, as God’s gift and God’s possession. Abraham, having surrendered everything, now possessed everything — because he possessed it in God.

> For now I know that thou fearest God, seeing thou hast not withheld thy son, thine only son from me. GENESIS 22:12

That is the paradox of the chapter. The man who possesses nothing, in this inward sense, is the richest man alive, because nothing can be taken from him that he has not already given to God.

## Counting all things loss

Paul describes the same transaction in his own life.

> Yea doubtless, and I count all things but loss for the excellency of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord: for whom I have suffered the loss of all things, and do count them but dung, that I may win Christ. PHILIPPIANS 3:8

It is worth noticing why Paul let go. It was not because the things he gave up were worthless in themselves — his heritage, his learning, his reputation — but because they were worthless compared with Christ. Surrender, in the New Testament, is never simply loss. It is exchange. We let go of what we cannot keep in order to lay hold of what we cannot lose.

## Weighing it

This teaching is thoroughly biblical, and many who have taken it to heart have found it the door into a new freedom. But it can be misread in two ways, and Tozer’s own life shows the risk of one of them.

The first misreading is to think that God is against the good things He has made, and that the spiritual man must despise them. Scripture says otherwise.

> Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not highminded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy. 1 TIMOTHY 6:17

The aim is not to stop enjoying God’s gifts but to stop trusting in them. A believer who has surrendered his possessions to God can enjoy them more, not less, because he is no longer afraid of losing them.

The second misreading is to practise detachment at other people’s expense. Tozer’s biographers record that his own austerity — his refusal of comfort and his giving away of what his books earned — weighed heavily on a wife and seven children who had not chosen it. Surrender of what is ours is costly and good. Surrender of what belongs to others is not ours to make. The man who would possess nothing must be careful that his family does not end up possessing less than love requires.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Make a list of the things, people and gifts you most often call mine. Beside each, write whether you hold it in the hand or in the heart.
2. Choose the one you would find hardest to lose. Bring it deliberately to God in prayer, and give it to Him.
3. Read Genesis 22 slowly. Ask what your Isaac is, and whether God has ever asked you for it.
4. Give away something this week that you would normally keep. Do it quietly.
5. If you are inclined to austerity, ask whether anyone else is paying for your self-denial. Talk honestly with them.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I have called so many things mine, and they have taken root in my heart where You should be.
> I give them back to You now — my possessions, my plans, my reputation, the people I love, and even the gifts You have given me for Your service.
> I do not ask You to take them all away. I ask You to take them out of the centre, and to take that place Yourself.
> Let me hold everything in open hands, and enjoy it all in You. Amen.

# The Gaze of the Soul

> Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth: for I am God, and there is none else. ISAIAH 45:22

What is faith? Few words are used more often by Christians, and few are understood less. Tozer noticed that the Bible itself offers only one formal definition, in the eleventh chapter of Hebrews, and that it describes what faith does rather than what it is. So in The Pursuit of God he went looking for the Scripture’s own picture of faith, and he found it in the language of sight. Faith, he concluded, is the soul looking to God — and keeping on looking.

## The serpent on the pole

He took his starting point from a strange story in the wilderness. Israel had sinned and been bitten by fiery serpents, and many were dying. Moses was told to make a serpent of brass and set it on a pole, and the promise was attached to a single act.

> And Moses made a serpent of brass, and put it upon a pole, and it came to pass, that if a serpent had bitten any man, when he beheld the serpent of brass, he lived. NUMBERS 21:9

The dying Israelite was not asked to do anything difficult. He was not asked to understand the remedy, or to feel a certain way about it, or to prove himself worthy of it. He was asked to look. And the Lord Jesus took this very story and applied it to Himself.

> And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up: that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have eternal life. JOHN 3:14–15

Tozer drew the conclusion simply. Looking and believing are the same thing. Faith is not a complicated religious performance. It is the turning of the heart’s eye away from everything else and towards God in Christ.

## Faith looks away from itself

This definition had an immediate practical consequence which Tozer pressed with great force. If faith is a gaze upon God, then faith is not occupied with itself. The anxious believer who keeps examining his own faith, wondering whether it is strong enough or real enough, has turned his eyes in exactly the wrong direction. He is looking at the look instead of at the One looked at. Faith, Tozer argued, is healthiest when it is least conscious of itself, as a man lost in admiration of a view is not thinking about his eyes.

> Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God. HEBREWS 12:2

This is a liberating truth for the introspective. The remedy for weak faith is not to stare harder at one’s faith but to look away to Christ. Strength of faith follows the clarity of the object, not the effort of the believer.

## A habit of the heart

Tozer insisted that this gaze is not meant to be occasional. The Christian is not to glance at God in a crisis and then return his attention to other things. He is to set his heart’s eye upon God as a settled habit, so that the inward look continues beneath all his ordinary business. He did not pretend this was easy for people crowded with work and noise. But he believed it could be learned, and that the ordinary believer, not only the cloistered saint, was invited to learn it.

> I have set the Lord always before me: because he is at my right hand, I shall not be moved. PSALM 16:8

> Mine eyes are ever toward the Lord; for he shall pluck my feet out of the net. PSALM 25:15

The first look saves. The continued looking transforms. Paul describes exactly this process: the believer, beholding the glory of the Lord, is gradually changed into the same image.

> But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord. 2 CORINTHIANS 3:18

## Confidence in God

In a sermon now in the Ochorus library, Faith, as Confidence in God, Tozer came at faith from another side. Preaching on the promise of 1 John 5:14–15, he lamented the great quantity of prayer offered in the churches that seemed to bring nothing back, and he traced it to the absence of real confidence in God. He made two further points there that belong with the gaze of the soul. The first was that unbelief is not chiefly an intellectual difficulty but a moral one: the heart refuses to trust because it does not want to depend. The second was that faith does not contradict reason but rises above it, laying hold of things that reason could never have discovered for itself.

> And this is the confidence that we have in him, that, if we ask any thing according to his will, he heareth us. 1 JOHN 5:14

A gaze upon God, in other words, is not vague. It is confidence in a Person who has made promises, and it expects Him to keep them.

## Weighing it

Tozer’s picture of faith as the gaze of the soul is one of his most helpful contributions, and it is well grounded in Scripture. Two notes of balance may be added. First, the gaze of faith is not a contentless staring into mystery; it is directed to the God who has spoken, and it is fed by His Word. Faith comes by hearing as well as by looking, and the two belong together.

> So then faith cometh by hearing, and hearing by the word of God. ROMANS 10:17

Second, in the confidence sermon Tozer spoke of a coming division in the church between those he called evangelical rationalists and evangelical mystics, and he placed himself firmly among the mystics. His concern was real: faith can be reduced to a set of arguments. But the word mystic carries a great deal of baggage, and a reader should take Tozer’s point without adopting his label. The faith of the New Testament is neither a system of proofs nor a private vision. It is trust in the crucified and risen Christ, grounded in the apostolic testimony, and lived out in the daily look of love.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read Numbers 21:4–9 and John 3:14–16 together. Notice how little the dying man was asked to do.
2. If you have been anxious about the strength of your faith, stop examining it this week. Each time the worry returns, deliberately look to Christ instead.
3. Choose a simple phrase of Scripture, such as Hebrews 12:2, and use it to turn your heart’s eye to God at set moments through the day.
4. Read Tozer’s sermon Faith, as Confidence in God in the Ochorus library. Ask where your prayer lacks real confidence.
5. Ask whether any unbelief in you is moral rather than intellectual — a refusal to depend rather than an honest doubt.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You were lifted up on the cross, and all who look to You live. I look to You now.
> Forgive me for looking at myself so long — at my failures, my feelings, and even my faith — and so seldom at You.
> Teach me the habit of the inward gaze. Let my eyes be ever toward You, through all the business and noise of the day.
> As I behold Your glory, change me into Your image, from glory to glory, by Your Spirit. Amen.

# The Speaking Voice

> In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. JOHN 1:1

Tozer loved the Bible and believed it without reservation to be the inspired Word of God. But he was troubled by the way many of his fellow believers handled it. They defended it, memorised it, outlined it and argued over it. What they rarely did, he thought, was listen to it. In The Pursuit of God he gave a chapter to the matter, and its title — The Speaking Voice — captures his whole approach to Scripture. God is not a God who once spoke and then fell silent. He is a God who speaks, and the Bible is the place where His voice is still to be heard.

## A God who speaks

Tozer began with creation. The world did not come into being by accident, or by a slow unfolding from some impersonal force. It was spoken into existence.

> By the word of the Lord were the heavens made; and all the host of them by the breath of his mouth. PSALM 33:6

The same Word by which the worlds were made became flesh and dwelt among us. God’s speech is not only something He did; it belongs to His nature. The eternal Son is the Word of God, the perfect expression of the Father. And so, Tozer argued, it is a mistake to think of the Word of God as a record of what He said long ago, preserved like a letter in a drawer. The Word of God is alive, because God is alive, and He goes on speaking through what He has spoken.

> For the word of God is quick, and powerful, and sharper than any twoedged sword, piercing even to the dividing asunder of soul and spirit, and of the joints and marrow, and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart. HEBREWS 4:12

He pointed out the tense of that verse. The Word is living and powerful — not was. It still pierces, still divides, still discerns. The believer who opens his Bible is not visiting a museum. He is entering a conversation.

## Reading to hear

This had a practical consequence for the way Tozer urged his readers to approach Scripture. They were not to read it merely as a textbook, still less as a collection of proof texts for their opinions. They were to read it expecting God to speak to them personally, and to read it slowly enough to let Him.

> Speak; for thy servant heareth. 1 SAMUEL 3:10

Samuel’s prayer, Tozer believed, should be the posture of every believer who opens the Bible. The question is not only what the passage means, though that must be asked and answered. It is what God is saying to me through it, now. Tozer was sure that the ordinary Christian, reading the plain text with a humble heart, could hear the voice of God there as truly as any scholar — and often more truly, because the scholar is sometimes too busy with his apparatus to listen.

In a sermon in the Ochorus library, A Call to Return to God, he developed a related thought. One of the tasks of Scripture, he said, is to find us — to show us where we actually stand before God, what is wrong with us, and also, he insisted, what is right with us. He urged his hearers to set aside a longer time than their hurried morning devotions, to lay aside their commentaries and study notes for a while, to take a plain text of Scripture and simply let it speak to them on their knees. He added that the King James Version would serve them well. The Bible, so read, becomes a mirror in which a man sees himself as God sees him.

> For if any be a hearer of the word, and not a doer, he is like unto a man beholding his natural face in a glass. JAMES 1:23

## In returning and rest

The same sermon took its text from the thirtieth chapter of Isaiah, where God reproves a nation that trusted in alliances and counsellors instead of in Him. Tozer drew a parallel with his own country and his own church, busy with conferences, programmes and advisers, and unwilling to be still before God. The voice of God, he suggested, is rarely heard in the middle of that noise. It is heard by those who stop and turn.

> For thus saith the Lord God, the Holy One of Israel; In returning and rest shall ye be saved; in quietness and in confidence shall be your strength: and ye would not. ISAIAH 30:15

The last three words of that verse are among the saddest in Scripture. God offered rest, and they would not. Tozer believed that a great part of the church’s weakness came from exactly this refusal to be quiet long enough to hear.

## The Word and the will

Hearing God’s voice, for Tozer, was never an end in itself. The voice of God always carries His will, and to hear it truly is to be faced with the demand to obey. He was blunt about the man who attends every meeting and studies every text while living in known rebellion against what God has told him to do. Such a man, he said in the same sermon, is only whistling in the dark. The Scripture is not given to satisfy curiosity but to direct life.

> And thine ears shall hear a word behind thee, saying, This is the way, walk ye in it, when ye turn to the right hand, and when ye turn to the left. ISAIAH 30:21

## Weighing it

This teaching is sound and much needed. One safeguard should be kept firmly in view. When Tozer spoke of the living voice of God, he meant the voice of God speaking through Scripture, not a voice speaking beside it or beyond it. Some who have taken up his language have come to treat every inward impression as a word from God, and to set their impressions above the plain sense of the text. That is the opposite of what he intended. The Spirit who speaks is the Spirit who inspired the written Word, and He does not contradict Himself.

> God, who at sundry times and in divers manners spake in time past unto the fathers by the prophets, hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son. HEBREWS 1:1–2

The Son is God’s final Word, and the Scriptures are the sure record of Him. Listen for God there, and you will not be misled.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Before you read the Bible each day this week, pray Samuel’s prayer: Speak; for thy servant heareth.
2. Set aside one longer time this month — an hour or more — to read a book of the Bible slowly on your knees, with no commentary.
3. As you read, write down one sentence each day that you believe God is saying to you. Then write down what you will do about it.
4. Read Tozer’s sermon A Call to Return to God in the Ochorus library, and ask what Scripture shows you about where you stand.
5. Is there anything God has plainly told you in His Word that you are refusing to do? Name it, and obey.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You spoke and the worlds were made. You spoke in Your Son, and You speak still through Your Word.
> Forgive me for reading Your Book without listening to You, for studying it without hearing You, and for hearing without obeying.
> Quiet my heart. Take away the noise within and around me, and let me hear the word behind me saying, This is the way.
> Speak, Lord; Your servant hears. And give me grace to do what You say. Amen.

# The Presence of God

> And Jacob awaked out of his sleep, and he said, Surely the Lord is in this place; and I knew it not. GENESIS 28:16

Jacob’s words at Bethel might stand as a summary of the human condition as Tozer saw it. God is here. He has always been here. And we did not know it. The whole purpose of the spiritual life, in one sense, is to wake up — to become aware of the God in whom we already live and move and have our being, and to live the rest of our days in the conscious enjoyment of His presence.

## Everywhere present

Tozer began with the doctrine of the omnipresence of God, which every orthodox Christian confesses. God is not located in one place and absent from another. He fills heaven and earth.

> Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there. PSALM 139:7–8

> That they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: for in him we live, and move, and have our being. ACTS 17:27–28

This, Tozer insisted, is not merely a doctrine to be believed but a fact to be enjoyed. The God who is everywhere is near to every one of us — nearer than our own thoughts. There is no need to travel to find Him, no need to climb up to heaven or to go down to the deep. He is here.

## Present and manifest

Yet if God is everywhere, why do so few people know it? Tozer answered by distinguishing between the presence of God and the manifestation of that presence. God is always present. He is not always perceived. The difference lies not in God but in us. In The Pursuit of God he argued that the soul has faculties by which it may know God — capacities for spiritual perception as real as the eyes and ears are for the physical world — but that in fallen man those faculties lie dormant and dulled by sin. The work of the Spirit in regeneration is to awaken them, and the work of a lifetime is to exercise them.

> But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil. HEBREWS 5:14

The manifest presence of God, then, is not a special visit from a God who is usually absent. It is the believer becoming aware of a God who was there all along. Moses would not go forward without it.

> And he said, My presence shall go with thee, and I will give thee rest. And he said unto him, If thy presence go not with me, carry us not up hence. EXODUS 33:14–15

## The veil

What hinders that awareness? Tozer’s answer was characteristically searching. It is not, in the first place, God’s distance, for He is near. Nor is it the barrier of guilt, for in Christ the way into the holiest has been opened. The veil in the temple was torn from top to bottom when Christ died.

> Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way, which he hath consecrated for us, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh. HEBREWS 10:19–20

The hindrance, he argued, is another veil, woven within us: the self-life, with its self-regard, self-confidence, self-pity, self-admiration and all the rest. It hangs between the believer and the conscious enjoyment of God, and it is not removed by instruction. It must be brought to the cross. Tozer did not pretend that this was pleasant. He spoke of it as something that hurts, because the self is not a garment we wear but a part of us, and its removal is felt in the living flesh. But he believed that on the other side lay the thing the heart was made for — an unclouded fellowship with God.

## Practising the presence

Tozer loved the old writers who had learned to live with God through the ordinary hours, and he often commended them. Among them was the seventeenth-century Carmelite lay brother known as Brother Lawrence, whose little collection of conversations and letters, The Practice of the Presence of God, taught that the kitchen could be as holy as the chapel for the one who did everything for the love of God. Tozer’s own counsel was similar. Cultivate a habit of inward attention. Turn the heart towards God often through the day. Do not wait for the special hour. God is here now.

> I have set the Lord always before me: because he is at my right hand, I shall not be moved. PSALM 16:8

## Weighing it

This is the chapter in which the reader must attend most carefully to the mystical streak in Tozer’s thought. He drew freely on writers of many centuries and traditions — medieval contemplatives such as the anonymous author of The Cloud of Unknowing and Nicholas of Cusa, the Englishwoman Julian of Norwich, the French quietist Madame Guyon, the hymn-writer Frederick Faber and others. Late in life he compiled an anthology, The Christian Book of Mystical Verse, from such sources. He commended them because they wanted God as he wanted God, and that was a real and genuine kinship.

But these writers carry with them doctrines and practices that Tozer did not share and did not always flag. Some spoke of union with God in language that blurs the distinction between Creator and creature. Some taught methods of prayer that aim at emptying the mind rather than filling it with Christ. Some placed private experience above the written Word. Tozer read them as a discerning evangelical, taking what he found true and leaving the rest. Not every reader who follows his recommendations will have his discernment.

The safeguard is simple and scriptural. The presence of God is enjoyed through Christ, by the Spirit, according to the Word. It is not reached by technique, and it does not require the believer to empty his mind or leave his theology at the door.

> For through him we both have access by one Spirit unto the Father. EPHESIANS 2:18

Read Tozer on the presence of God with gratitude; he awakens a hunger that is right. Read the mystics he commended, if at all, with the Bible open and your eyes wide.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Wherever you are as you read this, stop and say aloud: Surely the Lord is in this place. Sit quietly for two minutes with that truth.
2. Choose three fixed moments in your ordinary day — a meal, a journey, a task — and use each one to turn your heart deliberately towards God.
3. Ask God to show you one strand of the self-life that stands between you and a conscious sense of His presence. Bring it to the cross.
4. Read Psalm 139 slowly, and give thanks that there is nowhere you can go where God is not.
5. If you read devotional writers from other traditions, test one of them this week against Scripture, and note where you must part company.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You are here, and I have not known it. You fill heaven and earth, and I have lived as though You were far away.
> Thank You that the veil is torn, and that through the blood of Jesus the way is open. Take away the veil of self that still hangs in me.
> Awaken the eyes of my heart. Teach me to turn towards You through all the hours of the day.
> Let Your presence go with me, and give me rest. I do not want to go anywhere without You. Amen.

# The Old Cross and the New

> And he said to them all, If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross daily, and follow me. LUKE 9:23

Few things Tozer wrote have travelled further than a short essay called The Old Cross and the New, which has been reprinted as a tract many times. Its argument can be stated in a sentence. A new cross, he said, had crept into popular evangelicalism, looking very like the old one but doing the opposite work. The old cross killed. The new cross entertained. And the difference between them was the difference between the gospel and a pleasant imitation of it.

## What a cross is for

Tozer’s starting point was the plain historical fact of crucifixion. In a short piece now in the Ochorus library, The Cross Is a Radical Thing, he reminded his readers what a Roman cross actually was. It did not negotiate with its victim, or improve him, or give him a new outlook. It put him to death. Christ was alive when He was nailed to it and dead when He was taken down. That, Tozer insisted, is the cross the apostles preached, and it was this cross, carried into the world, that overturned paganism and turned persecutors into apostles.

> For the preaching of the cross is to them that perish foolishness; but unto us which are saved it is the power of God. 1 CORINTHIANS 1:18

The cross lost its power, he argued, when it was changed from an instrument of death into an object of beauty — when men made it an ornament to hang about the neck or a sign to ward off evil. Revered as a symbol, it was no longer feared as an executioner. And a cross that is not feared cannot save, because salvation comes precisely through death.

## The new cross

The new cross, as Tozer described it, does not slay the sinner. It redirects him. It leaves his old self largely intact and offers him a better way of living — cleaner, happier, more successful. It tells him that he can have Christ and keep the things he loves, only on a higher plane. It invites him to accept Jesus as a kind of addition to a life whose basic direction is unchanged. It promises him that he will enjoy the Christian life, and it delivers him into churches designed to make sure he does.

Tozer believed this was a counterfeit, and a deadly one, because it offered new life without the death that must precede it. It flattered the natural man instead of condemning him. It asked nothing of the self except that it be a little more religious. And it filled the churches with people who had never been told that the old life must end.

## The old cross

The old cross, by contrast, speaks with the voice of Scripture. It says that the old man, the self that has lived for itself, has no future. It must go to the cross with Christ. God does not propose to improve it but to end it, and then to raise the believer to a new life that is Christ’s own.

> Knowing this, that our old man is crucified with him, that the body of sin might be destroyed, that henceforth we should not serve sin. ROMANS 6:6

> I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me. GALATIANS 2:20

In the radical-cross piece Tozer put the choice starkly. There are only two things we can do with the cross: run from it, or die upon it. To modify it so that it no longer kills is to run from it while pretending to embrace it. He believed that a shallow and worldly leadership had done exactly this, trimming the cross to suit an entertainment-hungry age, and that the church would pay for it.

## Almost

Another sermon in the Ochorus library, The Bridge That Was Too Short, comes at the same truth from the side of the unconverted. Preaching on Paul before King Agrippa, Tozer dwelt on the king’s reply.

> Then Agrippa said unto Paul, Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian. ACTS 26:28

Agrippa was moved, and moved deeply. He was nearly persuaded. But nearly is not enough, and Tozer pressed the point with a vivid illustration of a bridge built almost all the way across a gorge. The man who comes close to the cross but will not die upon it is lost as surely as the man who never came near. The new cross, he believed, produced a great many almost-Christians — people who had come forward and signed their names and been counted, but who had never died.

## Weighing it

Tozer’s protest against a cross without death is one of the most necessary things he ever wrote. The Lord’s own words about self-denial and cross-bearing are unmistakable, and any gospel that omits them is not His gospel. But two notes of balance are worth sounding.

First, the cross in the New Testament is first of all the place where Christ died for us, and only then the place where we die with Him. Substitution comes before identification. The sinner is not saved by his willingness to die to himself; he is saved by Christ’s death in his place, received by faith. The call to take up the cross is the call of a Saviour who has already carried it for us.

> Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed. 1 PETER 2:24

Tozer believed this and preached it. But in his polemic against the new cross the order is not always plain, and an anxious reader can be left feeling that he must die hard enough to be saved.

Second, the old cross leads to resurrection. Tozer knew this well — he wrote that the God who raised Christ raises the believer to a new life — but the severity of his warnings can leave that joy in shadow. The cross is death, and it is also the door into life, liberty and a gladness that the new cross, for all its promises, can never supply.

> But God forbid that I should glory, save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by whom the world is crucified unto me, and I unto the world. GALATIANS 6:14

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read the short piece The Cross Is a Radical Thing in the Ochorus library. Ask honestly which cross you first heard preached.
2. Name one area of your life where you have tried to keep the old self and add Christ to it. Bring it to the cross.
3. Read Romans 6:1–11 slowly, and give thanks first for what Christ did for you before asking what you must do.
4. If you know someone who seems almost persuaded, pray for them by name this week.
5. Look at the way the gospel is presented in your own church or your own witness. Is anything of the cross missing? Speak or pray accordingly.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You bore my sins in Your own body on the tree. Before I ever took up a cross, You carried mine.
> Forgive me for wanting You without wanting to die, for asking You to improve my old life instead of ending it.
> I take up my cross today. Let the old self go down into Your death, and raise me into Your life.
> Keep me from being almost persuaded. Make me altogether Yours. Amen.

# The Spirit-Filled Life

> And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit. EPHESIANS 5:18

Tozer belonged to the Christian and Missionary Alliance, a movement whose founder, A. B. Simpson, had taught that Christ is not only Saviour but Sanctifier, and that the Holy Spirit is given to fill and empower every believer. Tozer inherited that teaching, wrote the life of Simpson, and preached on the Holy Spirit throughout his ministry. A short booklet of his on how to be filled with the Spirit has been widely read, and the same theme runs through Keys to the Deeper Life, The Divine Conquest and a great deal of his spoken ministry. He believed that the church of his day knew the doctrine of the Spirit and knew almost nothing of His power.

## The Spirit is a Person

He began, as he always did, with who God is. The Holy Spirit is not an influence, a force or an atmosphere. He is a Person — the third Person of the Trinity, fully God, with a mind, a will and affections. He may be grieved, resisted, quenched and obeyed. He is to be known, loved and worshipped.

> And grieve not the holy Spirit of God, whereby ye are sealed unto the day of redemption. EPHESIANS 4:30

Tozer believed that a great deal of confusion about the Spirit came from treating Him as a power to be used rather than a Lord to be yielded to. The question is not how I may get more of the Spirit, as though He were a substance, but how the Spirit may have more of me.

## For every believer

The second thing Tozer insisted upon was that the fulness of the Spirit is not the privilege of a spiritual elite. It is the normal provision of God for all His children.

> If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children: how much more shall your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him? LUKE 11:13

> In the last day, that great day of the feast, Jesus stood and cried, saying, If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink. JOHN 7:37

He did not think the filling of the Spirit was something to be earned. It is a gift, promised by the Father and purchased by the Son. But he did think that there were conditions for receiving it, and he set them out plainly. A man must be sure that it is God’s will to fill him, and Scripture makes that clear. He must want it, not as a thrill but as God’s answer to his need. He must be willing to be possessed, to hand over the whole of himself, for the Spirit will not fill what He is not permitted to rule. He must be ready to obey. And he must ask in faith and receive the gift as a gift.

## Why the church lacks power

Tozer was sure that much of the weakness of the church could be traced to a neglect of the Spirit. Churches had substituted organisation, method, publicity and talent for the power of God, and had grown so skilled at running their affairs by natural means that the absence of the Spirit could go unnoticed. He found this appalling. The church was born in a moment of divine power, and it was meant to live by that power still.

> But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth. ACTS 1:8

In a sermon in the Ochorus library, All with One Accord, preached on the day of Pentecost in Acts 2, he made a striking observation about the order of events. The disciples were of one accord before the Spirit came, not after. People pray that God will send His Spirit to make them one, he said in effect, but in Acts the unity came first and the Spirit came upon it. Division and strife in a church do not wait for the Spirit to heal them; they grieve Him and keep Him away.

> And when the day of Pentecost was fully come, they were all with one accord in one place. ACTS 2:1

In the same sermon he spoke of an inward witness of the Spirit — a confirmation in the heart that goes deeper than reasoning from texts — and he was careful to say that he did not believe any physical sign, such as speaking in tongues, was the necessary proof of it. The evidence of the Spirit’s fulness, as he taught it, is moral before it is anything else.

## The fruit of the Spirit

What does the Spirit-filled life look like? Tozer answered with Scripture. It looks like Christ.

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law. GALATIANS 5:22–23

The Spirit-filled believer is not necessarily the most excitable member of the congregation, nor the most gifted. He is the one in whom the character of Christ is being formed, whose witness carries weight, whose prayers are answered, and whose life has about it a quality that cannot be explained by his natural gifts. Tozer liked to say that a man who can be fully explained by his temperament and training has not yet shown what the Spirit can do.

## Weighing it

Tozer’s call to the Spirit-filled life is sound in its essentials, and the church needs it as much today as ever. But one feature of his teaching requires care. Following the tradition of his movement, he tended to speak of the fulness of the Spirit as a definite experience, distinct from and often later than conversion, into which a believer enters by a crisis of surrender. Many have found that pattern true to their own experience, and there is no reason to deny it. But the New Testament does not make it the rule. Every true believer has the Spirit.

> Now if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his. ROMANS 8:9

And the command to be filled is, in Paul’s grammar, a continuing one: go on being filled. The Spirit-filled life is not so much a second stage entered once for all as a daily yielding to the One who already dwells within. Where Tozer’s teaching is read as dividing the church into the filled and the unfilled, it can breed both pride and despair. Where it is read as a summons to every Christian to yield more fully, today, to the Spirit he already has, it is pure gain.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself honestly whether you have thought of the Holy Spirit as a Person or as a power. Speak to Him this week as the Lord He is.
2. Read Acts 1 and 2 at a sitting. Notice what the disciples did before the Spirit came.
3. Is there any disunity between you and another believer? Take one step this week to put it right.
4. Work through Tozer’s conditions — assurance of God’s will, desire, surrender, obedience, faith — and ask where you are holding back.
5. Read Galatians 5:16–26 and ask which fruit of the Spirit is most lacking in you. Pray specifically for it.

### A PRAYER

> Holy Spirit, You are God, and You dwell in me. Forgive me for treating You as a power to be used rather than a Lord to be obeyed.
> Forgive me for grieving You by my divisions, my self-will and my indifference.
> I give You the whole of myself — every room, every key. Fill me, not for my own sake, but that Christ may be seen in me.
> Let Your fruit grow in me, and let my witness carry Your power. Amen.

# The Sacrament of Living

> Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God. 1 CORINTHIANS 10:31

The Pursuit of God ends not in the prayer closet but in the kitchen, the office and the field. Its last chapter takes up a question that every earnest Christian eventually faces. If the pursuit of God is the great business of life, what are we to make of all the rest of life — the eating and sleeping, the work and the shopping, the long hours spent on tasks that seem to have nothing to do with God at all? Tozer’s answer was that the division itself is the problem, and that the believer who learns to overcome it will find the whole of his life becoming a kind of sacrament.

## The great divide

He observed that most Christians live, without ever quite deciding to, with their lives cut in two. On one side are the sacred things: prayer, Bible reading, church, giving, witnessing. On the other side are the secular things: work, meals, rest, family business, recreation. The sacred things are felt to please God; the secular things are tolerated as necessities. And since the secular side of life takes up by far the greater part of the day, the believer comes to feel that most of his time is spent away from God, and that only in brief, snatched moments is he really living as a Christian.

Tozer believed that this division caused a great deal of quiet misery. It left believers feeling guilty for the hours they could not avoid and strained by the effort of squeezing spiritual life into the margins. Worse, it had no foundation in Scripture. The New Testament knows nothing of a Christian life divided into holy and unholy compartments.

> And whatsoever ye do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus, giving thanks to God and the Father by him. COLOSSIANS 3:17

## The example of Christ

He pointed to the life of Christ Himself. The Lord did not divide His days into sacred and secular. He prayed on the mountain and ate with tax collectors; He taught in the temple and worked at a carpenter’s bench; He wept at a grave and turned water into wine at a wedding. All of it was done in perfect fellowship with the Father, and all of it was equally pleasing to Him.

> And he that sent me is with me: the Father hath not left me alone; for I do always those things that please him. JOHN 8:29

The same was true of Paul, who could make tents and preach the gospel in the same week without any sense that the one was less holy than the other. What made the difference, Tozer argued, was not the nature of the act but the motive and the fellowship in which it was done.

## It is the motive that sanctifies

This was the heart of the chapter. An act is not sacred or secular in itself. It becomes sacred when it is done by a child of God, in dependence on God, for the glory of God. The same work — a meal cooked, a field ploughed, a letter written, a sale made — may be common or holy depending on the heart of the one who does it. The believer, therefore, need not change his occupation to become spiritual. He needs to change the spirit in which he pursues it.

> And whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men. COLOSSIANS 3:23

Tozer found this truth beautifully foreshadowed in one of the last visions of the Old Testament prophets, where the inscription that was once engraved on the golden plate of the high priest’s mitre is written on the most ordinary objects of daily life.

> In that day shall there be upon the bells of the horses, HOLINESS UNTO THE LORD; and the pots in the Lord’s house shall be like the bowls before the altar. ZECHARIAH 14:20

In the kingdom of God, the pots and pans are as holy as the temple vessels. That, he believed, is the calling of every Christian now.

## Learning the habit

How does a believer move from a divided life to a unified one? Tozer did not suggest that it happens at once. He counselled a simple practice: begin to offer each ordinary act to God as it is done, deliberately and inwardly, until the habit of doing so becomes second nature. At first the effort will feel artificial and will often be forgotten. But over time the heart learns to live in a steady awareness that all its work is for God, and the feeling that God is absent from most of the day begins to fade. He commended once again the example of Brother Lawrence, whose pots and pans in the monastery kitchen became the place of his communion with God.

> I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. ROMANS 12:1

He added one caution of his own. This teaching does not mean that the believer may stop setting apart times for prayer and worship. Rather, it is those set times, faithfully kept, that give the rest of the day its savour. The hour of prayer and the hours of work are not rivals; each feeds the other.

## Weighing it

This is one of the most liberating things Tozer wrote, and it recovers a truth the Reformers held dear: that the calling of the farmer and the housewife is as truly a calling from God as the calling of the minister. Two notes may be added.

First, the principle sanctifies only what is lawful. It does not make sin holy by attaching a pious intention to it. The motive that sanctifies an act must include a willingness to have the act itself judged by the Word of God.

> Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might. ECCLESIASTES 9:10

Second, the ordinary life that Tozer invites us to offer to God includes, most ordinary of all, our life at home. The sacrament of living is celebrated not only at the desk and the bench but at the dinner table, in the conversation with a spouse, in the time given to a child. It is a sad irony that Tozer’s own family, by the account of his biographers, found him more present to God than to them. His teaching here is better than his practice, and it condemns the failure. A believer who takes this chapter seriously will find that the first place it sends him is home.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Make two lists: the parts of your day you consider sacred, and those you consider secular. Look at the second list and ask how each item might be offered to God.
2. Choose one ordinary task you do every day. For one week, offer it consciously to God each time you begin it.
3. Read Colossians 3:17–24 slowly, noticing how Paul applies it to husbands, wives, children and servants.
4. Keep your set time of prayer faithfully this week, and notice whether it colours the rest of the day.
5. Ask whether your family would say you are present to them. Offer them some of your attention this week as an act of worship.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have divided my life into Yours and mine, and I have given You the smaller part.
> Forgive me. Take the whole of it — my work and my rest, my meals and my conversations, my duties and my pleasures.
> Teach me to do everything in Your name and for Your glory, until there is no corner of my day where You are not.
> Let holiness to the Lord be written on the ordinary things of my life, and let it begin at home. Amen.

# The Church and the World

> Love not the world, neither the things that are in the world. If any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him. 1 JOHN 2:15

For more than forty years Tozer spoke to the church as a prophet speaks to Israel. He loved it, he served it, and he never stopped telling it what was wrong with it. Much of that ministry was carried on through his editorials in The Alliance Weekly, short pieces written week after week and afterwards gathered into books such as The Root of the Righteous, Of God and Men and That Incredible Christian. Again and again they return to one great complaint. The church, he said, had made its peace with the world, and in doing so had lost the very thing that made it the church.

## The world in the sanctuary

Tozer did not mean by worldliness chiefly the old list of forbidden amusements, though he held strict views on several of them. He meant something deeper: the adoption, by the people of God, of the spirit, values and methods of a society that does not know God. The world measures success by size, and so the church began to measure itself by numbers. The world runs on publicity, and so the church learned to advertise. The world worships celebrity, and so the church produced its own celebrities. The world must be entertained, and so the church began to entertain.

> And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God. ROMANS 12:2

He was especially sharp about the last of these. In a booklet on the religious film, and in many editorials, he protested against the drift of evangelical churches towards performance, spectacle and amusement as a means of attracting people and holding them. He believed it was not a harmless modernising of method but a change in the nature of the thing. A congregation that must be entertained to be held has not been gripped by God. In the sermon All with One Accord, in the Ochorus library, he put it plainly: turning a church into a music hall or a debating club does God no service. A church is a gathering of people who have been touched by heaven and who meet to hear the Word, to pray, to give and to sing to God — even if, he added, they sing a little off-key.

## Religion without God

Beneath the complaint lay the diagnosis that runs through all his teaching. The church had not lost its orthodoxy. It had lost its awareness of God. And a church that has lost God must fill the gap with something. Entertainment, programmes, organisation and activity are what rush in when the presence of God goes out.

> For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water. JEREMIAH 2:13

Tozer believed that many of the church’s leaders did not even notice what had happened, because the machinery went on running and the numbers went on rising. That was what made the situation so dangerous. The church at Laodicea was prosperous, busy and entirely content — and the Lord was outside the door.

> Because thou sayest, I am rich, and increased with goods, and have need of nothing; and knowest not that thou art wretched, and miserable, and poor, and blind, and naked. REVELATION 3:17

> Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me. REVELATION 3:20

## Separation and love

What was the remedy? Tozer called the church back to the New Testament. In All with One Accord he said that it is the business of every minister to see that his church is a New Testament church in its beliefs, its practices, its methods and its aims, because in every age some influence arises to draw God’s people away from that pattern. He called believers to a real separation from the world — not a withdrawal from the world’s people, whom Christ came to save, but a refusal of the world’s spirit.

> Come out from among them, and be ye separate, saith the Lord, and touch not the unclean thing; and I will receive you. 2 CORINTHIANS 6:17

He also knew that such separation is costly. A church that refuses the world’s methods will often be smaller, plainer and less admired. A preacher who says these things will not be popular. Tozer accepted both costs. In one of his sermons he said, in effect, that he would rather be true than be nice.

## Weighing it

Tozer’s critique of a worldly church is prophetic in the proper sense. Much of what he warned against came to pass on a larger scale after his death, and his words have a sharper edge now than when he wrote them. The church needs to hear them.

But here the reader meets the second of the cautions raised in the introduction. Tozer could be severe to the point of unfairness. His broad strokes sometimes fell on sincere believers who were doing their best with the gifts they had, and his scorn for fellow ministers could run beyond what charity allows. Some of the particular things he condemned — certain styles of music, certain uses of film — were matters on which Christians may honestly differ, and his own preferences were not always the same as the mind of God.

> Speaking the truth in love, may grow up into him in all things, which is the head, even Christ. EPHESIANS 4:15

The prophet’s task is necessary, but it is not the only task, and a prophet who never speaks tenderly is in danger of forgetting that the church he rebukes is the bride of Christ. Take Tozer’s diagnosis to heart. Test his particular judgements by Scripture. And rebuke, when you must, with tears.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read Revelation 3:14–22. Ask whether anything in your own church, or your own life, resembles Laodicea.
2. Name one way in which you measure your spiritual life by the world’s standards of success. Lay it aside.
3. Consider what draws you to church. Would you still go if there were nothing there but the Word, prayer and the praise of God?
4. Before you criticise another believer or church this week, pray for them first by name.
5. Read Tozer’s sermon All with One Accord in the Ochorus library, and ask what a New Testament church would look like where you are.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You loved the church and gave Yourself for it. Forgive us, Your people, for making friends with the world that crucified You.
> We have been busy and prosperous and content, and we have not noticed that You were standing outside. Come in.
> Deliver me from the spirit of the age, from the love of numbers and applause and amusement. Make me content with You.
> And when I see what is wrong in Your church, give me Your tears as well as Your truth. Amen.

# The Deeper Life

> Not as though I had already attained, either were already perfect: but I follow after, if that I may apprehend that for which also I am apprehended of Christ Jesus. PHILIPPIANS 3:12

The phrase the deeper life was common coin in the circles Tozer moved in, and he used it freely; one of his short books is called Keys to the Deeper Life. But he was uneasy with it too. He knew that it could suggest a special tier of Christianity, reserved for enthusiasts, above the ordinary faith of ordinary believers. What he meant by it was something simpler and more demanding. The deeper life, he said in effect, is nothing but the normal Christian life as the New Testament describes it. It is deeper only by comparison with the shallow thing that most Christians have settled for.

## Discontent as a gift

Where does such a life begin? Tozer’s answer, repeated in many forms, was that it begins with holy dissatisfaction. A man who is content with his present experience of God will not seek more, and God does not force Himself on the unwilling. But a man who has come to see the gap between what Scripture promises and what he actually knows, and who is troubled by it, is already on the way. That dissatisfaction, Tozer believed, is itself the work of the Spirit, and it should be welcomed rather than soothed away.

> Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness: for they shall be filled. MATTHEW 5:6

He was scathing about religious teachers who hurried to comfort every troubled conscience, assuring the seeker that he already had all there was and need not trouble himself further. Some troubles, he thought, are God’s own summons, and to quiet them is to silence God.

## Why believers go back

The reverse of the deeper life is backsliding, and Tozer preached on it with sober seriousness. His sermon Causes of Backsliding, in the Ochorus library, takes its text from Proverbs.

> The backslider in heart shall be filled with his own ways: and a good man shall be satisfied from himself. PROVERBS 14:14

He traced backsliding to two roots. The first is the fickleness of the human heart, which moves easily from one interest to another and holds firmly only to what instinct or necessity compels. The life of God in the soul is not an instinct of fallen nature; it runs against the grain, and so without steady attention it is easily neglected. The second root is the evil that still lives in the heart, which pulls continually away from God. Put the two together, he said, and the wonder is not that Christians go back but that any go on.

He made two further observations that are worth carrying. Backsliding begins in the heart, not in the circumstances a man blames for it. And it is discovered in a certain order: God knows it first, then the man himself, then the church, and only at the last the world. By the time anyone else can see it, it has been going on inwardly for a long time. Its symptoms, he said, are a loss of appetite for the things of God, prayer grown dull, church attendance grown dutiful, giving grown grudging — a religion kept up from habit after the love has cooled.

> Nevertheless I have somewhat against thee, because thou hast left thy first love. REVELATION 2:4

## Prepared in the garden

In another sermon in the Ochorus library, Prepare by Prayer, Tozer turned to Gethsemane and drew a lesson he considered vital for anyone who would go deeper with God. The Lord foresaw the crisis of the cross and prepared for it by prayer. The disciples did not. They slept. And the next morning one betrayed Him, one denied Him, and all forsook Him and fled.

> Watch and pray, that ye enter not into temptation: the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak. MATTHEW 26:41

Tozer’s point was that the outcome of a test is usually settled long before the test arrives. Peter did not fall in the courtyard; he fell in the garden, when he slept instead of praying. So it is with every believer. The crisis reveals what the quiet days have prepared or failed to prepare. The deeper life is not built in the moment of testing but in the unwitnessed hours before it.

## The way back and the way on

For the backslider, Tozer’s counsel was full of hope. Close to the end of the backsliding sermon he turned to Peter again, and to the look the Lord gave him across the courtyard after the third denial.

> And the Lord turned, and looked upon Peter. And Peter remembered the word of the Lord … And Peter went out, and wept bitterly. LUKE 22:61–62

That look did not destroy Peter. It restored him. Tozer longed for his hearers to be looked at in the same way, and to weep in the same way, because such tears are the beginning of return.

> Return, ye backsliding children, and I will heal your backslidings. JEREMIAH 3:22

For those who would go on, his counsel was simple and unspectacular. Seek God deliberately. Refuse to be content. Give Him time. Pray in advance of the battle. Surrender whatever He puts His finger on. And keep going.

## Weighing it

Here we meet the fourth caution raised in the introduction. Tozer sometimes wrote as though the deeper life were the possession of a few — a small, earnest minority set apart from the great mass of nominal and carnal believers. There was truth in his observation; not every professing Christian is pressing on. But the language of the spiritual few can breed a subtle pride in those who think themselves among them, and a hopeless discouragement in those who are sure they are not. The New Testament knows no spiritual aristocracy. Every believer is called to press on, and every believer who presses on is given grace to do it.

> Therefore leaving the principles of the doctrine of Christ, let us go on unto perfection. HEBREWS 6:1

Take Tozer’s summons as addressed to you, not as a description of an elite you may or may not belong to. The deeper life is simply the next step of obedience, taken today.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself whether you have been soothing a dissatisfaction that God put there. Stop soothing it, and bring it to Him.
2. Read Tozer’s sermon Causes of Backsliding in the Ochorus library. Test yourself honestly against its symptoms.
3. Identify one test you can see coming — a conversation, a decision, a season. Begin praying about it now, before it arrives.
4. If your first love has cooled, read Revelation 2:1–7 and do what it says: remember, repent, and do the first works.
5. If you have thought of the deeper life as for others, confess that, and take one concrete step of obedience this week.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, I have not already attained. I have settled for far less of You than You have offered.
> Keep me from the slow cooling of the heart. Where my love has grown cold, look on me as You looked on Peter, and let me weep and return.
> Teach me to watch and pray before the battle, and not to sleep when You call me to wake.
> I do not ask to be counted among a spiritual few. I ask only to follow You further, today. Amen.

# Conclusion

> Worthy is the Lamb that was slain to receive power, and riches, and wisdom, and strength, and honour, and glory, and blessing. REVELATION 5:12

We have covered a good deal of ground in fifteen short chapters, and it would be easy to close this book with the impression that Tozer taught many things. In truth he taught one thing, from many sides, for more than forty years. The one thing is this: God Himself is the soul’s true good, and He may be known. Everything else is commentary. The pursuit of God is that truth turned into desire. The attributes of God — His holiness, His infinitude, His sovereignty and love — are that truth turned into adoration. Worship is that truth turned into response. The surrender of our possessions, the gaze of faith, the listening ear and the practised presence are that truth turned into daily habit. The old cross is that truth turned against the self that stands in the way. The Spirit-filled life and the sacrament of living are that truth spread over the whole of life. And his long quarrel with a worldly church was simply that truth defending itself against its counterfeits.

## What to do with it

If you have read this far, you are in a position of some danger, and Tozer would have been the first to say so. The hazard of a book like this is that it can be enjoyed. A man may take real pleasure in reading about the pursuit of God while pursuing nothing but the next interesting book. He may admire Tozer’s hunger and remain entirely full of himself. He may learn to speak fluently about worship and never once fall silent before God. Nothing in these pages is of the slightest use as information.

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

So here is the plainest counsel this book can give. Go back to the one chapter that disturbed you. Not the one you found most interesting — the one you would rather not read again. That is likely to be where God is at work. Take its application points and do them, one at a time, over the coming weeks. Then take the next. And make room, somewhere in your life, for the thing Tozer most wanted his readers to have: unhurried, unscripted time alone with God, with the door shut, asking for nothing and simply worshipping.

## Two things to carry

Carry away, first, a large thought of God. Tozer was convinced that most of the church’s troubles, and most of ours, come from thinking of God as smaller than He is. The cure is not a technique but a vision. Read the Scriptures to see Him. Read the Gospels to see Him in the face of His Son. Let the old hymns and the great confessions lift your eyes. A believer who has seen God high and lifted up will find that a great many of his fears, sins and distractions shrink to their proper size.

Carry away, second, a holy discontent. Not with your circumstances, but with the distance between what the New Testament calls normal and what you have settled for. That discontent, honestly brought to God, is one of the surest signs that His Spirit is at work in you. Do not soothe it. Follow it.

> As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. PSALM 42:1

## A last word about the man

We have been honest in these pages about Tozer’s limitations. He leaned on the mystics more than was always safe and spoke of reason more slightingly than Scripture does. He could be severe to the point of unfairness with his fellow believers. He sometimes wrote as though the deeper life belonged to a few. And the man who wrote so tenderly of God’s presence was, by the account of those who knew him best, a distant presence in his own home, and those nearest him bore the cost.

None of this has been raised to diminish him. It has been raised because he would have insisted on it. No theme was dearer to him than the smallness of man before the majesty of God, and a book that set him on a pedestal would have contradicted everything he taught. He knew, as every true servant of God knows, that the treasure is carried in an earthen vessel.

> But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:7

In one of his Easter sermons, now in the Ochorus library under the title Three Great Days, Tozer read aloud the fifth chapter of Revelation — the Lamb in the midst of the throne, and all creation crying that He is worthy — and declined, in effect, to preach on it, because the passage was too great for any sermon to add to. It is a fitting place to leave him. His whole ministry was an attempt to point away from himself and towards that throne. The right response to him, then, is not admiration. It is worship — not of Tozer, but of the God he spent his life pursuing and the Lamb he spent his life adoring.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Return to the chapter of this book that most disturbed you. Read it again, and do the first of its application points this week.
2. Set a regular time — weekly, if not daily — for unhurried worship with no requests. Guard it.
3. Choose one of the attributes of God considered in this book, and meditate on it every day for a month.
4. Name the lesser thing you have settled for in your walk with God. Tell Him you are no longer content with it.
5. Read Revelation 4 and 5 aloud, slowly, and end in worship.

# A Reader’s Guide to A. W. Tozer

> O taste and see that the Lord is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him. PSALM 34:8

Tozer wrote only a handful of books with his own hand. Much of what now bears his name was gathered after his death by editors working from his magazine editorials, sermons and recorded addresses, and the shelf is still growing. The books he wrote himself are the surest guide to his mind; the posthumous collections vary in quality and should be read with that in view. His works remain in copyright and are available from their publishers. What follows is arranged for usefulness rather than chronology.

## Begin here

The Pursuit of God (1948). His best-known book and the natural starting place. Short, simple in structure and intensely searching, it gathers up the whole of his message: the pursuit of God for Himself, the surrender of possessions, faith as the gaze of the soul, the speaking voice of God, and the sacredness of ordinary life. Read it slowly, a chapter at a sitting.

The Knowledge of the Holy (1961). His meditations on the attributes of God, each chapter opening with a prayer. The finest thing he wrote, and the book behind chapters four to seven of this companion. Read it with the Gospels open beside it.

## For further reading

The Divine Conquest. An earlier and fuller treatment of the themes of The Pursuit of God, with more on the work of the Holy Spirit and the reality of spiritual experience. Later reissued under another title.

The Root of the Righteous, Of God and Men, and That Incredible Christian. Collections of his short editorials, the last published after his death. Sharp, quotable and often searching; read a few at a time, and allow for the severity of the prophet.

Man: The Dwelling Place of God. A posthumous collection of editorials on the inner life and the state of the church. Uneven, but containing some of his best short pieces.

Keys to the Deeper Life and Born After Midnight. Short works on spiritual hunger, surrender and the fulness of the Spirit. The first is a good companion to chapters fourteen and seventeen of this book.

Worship: The Missing Jewel. A short work on the theme of chapter eight, and a good summary of his lifelong burden.

The Best of A. W. Tozer. An anthology of passages from many of his books, compiled by Warren W. Wiersbe. A useful sampler, though no substitute for reading the books whole.

Wingspread. His life of A. B. Simpson, founder of the Christian and Missionary Alliance. It shows what Tozer admired in a servant of God, and it is a natural bridge to Simpson’s own works and to The Key Teachings of A. B. Simpson, both in the Ochorus library.

## His sermons in the Ochorus library

Eleven of his sermons and shorter pieces are in the Ochorus library, and several are drawn on in these pages: The Infinite God, God Made Man to Worship, God Is Our Refuge and Strength, Faith, as Confidence in God, A Call to Return to God, All with One Accord, Causes of Backsliding, Prepare by Prayer, The Cross Is a Radical Thing, The Bridge That Was Too Short, and the Easter message Three Great Days. They are transcripts of the spoken word, with the roughness that implies, but they let the reader hear the man.

## Approach with care

The Christian Book of Mystical Verse, which he compiled late in life, and the devotional writers he most often commended. Tozer read the medieval and later mystics as a discerning evangelical, taking what was true and leaving the rest. Not every reader will do the same. Read them, if at all, with the Bible open and chapter twelve of this book in mind.

His polemical editorials on church music, films and methods. Take his diagnosis seriously; test his particular judgements by Scripture.

## About Tozer

David J. Fant, A. W. Tozer: A Twentieth-Century Prophet (1964), is the earliest account, written soon after his death by a colleague; warm and admiring. James L. Snyder, In Pursuit of God: The Life of A. W. Tozer (1991), is a fuller popular life. Lyle W. Dorsett, A Passion for God: The Spiritual Journey of A. W. Tozer (2008), is the most balanced and candid, and the best place to see both the greatness of the man and the cost of it to his family. The Ochorus author page carries a shorter biography.

## Three counsels for reading him

Read him with the Bible open. Tozer claimed no authority but the Word of God, and he would have been the first to send you back to it. Where he is plainly scriptural, receive it. Where he goes beyond Scripture, weigh it.

Read him slowly. His sentences are compressed, and much of their force is lost when they are hurried. A page a day, prayed over, will do more than a book a week admired.

And read him on your knees. He wrote to bring his readers into the presence of God, not to fill their notebooks. Close the book, and seek the One he sought.
