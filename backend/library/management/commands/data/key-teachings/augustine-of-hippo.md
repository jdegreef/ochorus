  This is not a book by Augustine of Hippo. It is a companion to him — an independent work of exposition and appreciation, written by Ochorus, that gathers the heart of his teaching into twenty short chapters for the ordinary reader. It tells his life plainly, as the life of an African bishop in a Roman province, and it draws throughout on the two of his books that sit in the Ochorus library, the Confessions and the Enchiridion.

  Augustine’s one theme was the heart and its loves. We were made for God, and we cannot rest anywhere else; the tragedy of sin is love turned the wrong way, and the wonder of grace is that God Himself turns it back, going before the will He means to heal. This volume follows that thread through his conversion and his mother’s prayers, his teaching on evil, memory, time and the Trinity, his vision of the two cities, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body and the Sabbath rest, and it is honest about where he pressed too hard. Each chapter ends in a few questions and a prayer.

# Introduction

> O God, thou art my God; early will I seek thee: my soul thirsteth for thee, my flesh longeth for thee in a dry and thirsty land, where no water is. PSALM 63:1

There is hardly a Christian alive whose faith has not been shaped by Augustine of Hippo, and very few who have read him. He is quoted in sermons of every tradition, claimed by Catholics as a Doctor of the Church and by Protestants as the grandfather of the Reformation, studied by philosophers who do not share his faith, and remembered by almost everyone for a single sentence. It comes from the first paragraph of the Confessions, and it has never been bettered: “Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee.” If a person knows nothing else of Augustine, that sentence is not a bad place to have started. It is the key to everything he wrote.

He was an African. That needs saying at the outset, because the pictures of him in European churches have long made him look like a northern Italian. Aurelius Augustinus was born in 354 at Thagaste, a small inland town in the Roman province of Numidia, in what is now Algeria. He was educated at Madauros and Carthage, he spent the greater part of his life in North Africa, and he died in 430 as bishop of Hippo Regius, a port on the African coast, while a Vandal army besieged its walls. His mind was trained on Cicero and the Platonists; but his church was the ancient, argumentative, deeply rooted church of Africa, the church of Tertullian and Cyprian, and he never stopped belonging to it.

## Why he still matters

Augustine matters because he asked the questions that every serious believer eventually asks, and he asked them out of his own experience. Why do I do what I know is wrong, and love doing it? Where does evil come from, if God is good? Why can I see the truth and still not choose it? Where was God in all the years I did not want Him? Why does the world of power and applause, so solid and so impressive, fall down? Most theologians begin with a system. Augustine began with a heart that would not rest, and followed it to God.

He also matters because he shaped the language the Western church still speaks. Original sin, prevenient grace, the bondage of the will, the invisible and visible church, the two cities, the ordering of love — these are Augustine’s words or Augustine’s emphases. When the Reformers argued with the medieval church, both sides argued with his books open.

## The one thread

If you look for the single thread that runs through all his teaching, you find it in love. Augustine believed that human beings are not first of all thinking machines, but lovers. We are always moving towards something, pulled by what we love, the way a stone falls or a flame rises. “My weight, is my love,” he wrote near the end of the Confessions; wherever I am carried, it is my love that carries me. Sin is not simply breaking rules; it is love disordered — loving the gifts more than the Giver, loving ourselves more than God, loving lesser goods as if they could satisfy. Grace is God reordering our loves, turning the heart back to Himself, doing in us what we could never do for ourselves. And heaven is love at rest, the heart finally home.

> Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee. ISAIAH 26:3

## How this book is arranged

The second chapter tells his life. The twenty chapters that follow each take one of his key teachings, beginning with the restless heart and ending with the vision of God. The last four follow the closing articles of the Creed, as the Enchiridion does — the church, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body and the life everlasting. Wherever possible they are grounded in the two of his books that sit in the Ochorus library: the Confessions, in the nineteenth-century translation of Edward Pusey, and the Enchiridion, the little handbook on faith, hope and love he wrote for a friend. His other great works — The City of God, On Christian Doctrine, On the Trinity, his sermons and his writings against Pelagius — are named where they belong, and a guide to them is given at the end.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. Augustine would have wanted it so. The Confessions is itself one long prayer; he wrote it not to inform God, who knew it all, but, as he says in Book XI, to stir up his own and his readers’ devotion. So read a chapter, stop at the questions, and pray — his prayer if it helps, your own if it is truer.

## Reading him with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and Augustine would have been the first to object if it were. Near the end of his life he wrote the Retractations, going back through his books and correcting them. His readers must do the same where he did not.

Three matters in particular must be named plainly. First, in the long struggle with the Donatist church in Africa, Augustine came to approve the use of imperial force to bring schismatics back into the Catholic fold, and he defended it from the words of the parable, compel them to come in (Luke 14:23). He pleaded that no one should be put to death, and he meant well; but the argument he built was used for centuries afterwards to justify persecution, and it cannot be defended from the mind of Christ. Second, his teaching on sexual desire, shaped partly by his own long captivity to it, tied the passing on of original sin closely to the passion of the marriage bed, and it has left many Christians, especially women, with a shame that Scripture does not lay on them. Third, his conclusions on the fate of infants who die unbaptised were severe, and later church teaching has, in several traditions, drawn back from them.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21

And one more thing is worth saying to the Protestant reader, who will be most of the readers of this book. Augustine belongs to the whole church. He was a bishop who believed in the Catholic church of his day, its sacraments and its unity, and he prayed for his dead mother at the altar. He was also the great teacher of grace alone, the man who taught Luther and Calvin to read Paul. It is tempting for each side to take the half of him it likes and leave the other half to its opponents. This book tries to give you the whole man, fairly, and to let him speak where he is faithful to Scripture — which, on the restless heart, the bondage of the will, the sovereignty of grace, and the humility of Christ, he is, gloriously.

He was a sinner who was found, a proud man made humble, a lover whose loves God turned around. He would not want to be admired. He would want you to find the One he found.

# A Restless Heart from Thagaste

> I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek thy servant; for I do not forget thy commandments. PSALM 119:176

A boy of Numidia. Augustine was born on 13 November 354 at Thagaste, a modest town in the hills of Roman North Africa, in the province of Numidia — today Souk Ahras in Algeria. His father, Patricius, was a small landowner and town councillor, a pagan of quick temper who was baptised only near the end of his life. His mother, Monica, was a Christian of deep and stubborn faith. The family was African through and through; they belonged to the Latin-speaking townspeople of a province that had been Christian, in part, for a century and a half. They were not rich, and they stretched themselves to educate their clever son, first at nearby Madauros and then, when he was about seventeen, at Carthage, the great city of the African coast. In between there was an idle year at home, and a night when he and his friends stripped a neighbour’s pear tree and threw the fruit to the pigs. He never forgot it, and he would make it the most famous theft in Christian literature.

A cauldron of unholy loves. At Carthage he was brilliant and hungry — for learning, for applause, for love. He took a woman into his house, whom he never names, and lived with her faithfully for some fifteen years; she bore him a son, Adeodatus, whose name means given by God. In his nineteenth year he read Cicero’s Hortensius, an appeal to seek wisdom, and it set him on fire. He turned to the Bible and found its Latin crude beside Cicero’s, and he turned away. Instead he joined the Manichees, a sect that taught that the world was the battleground of two opposing powers, light and darkness, and that the evil in a man was not really his own doing. It suited him. For nine years, from nineteen to twenty-eight, he was one of their hearers. He taught rhetoric in Thagaste and in Carthage, and grew in reputation.

The son of these tears. Monica grieved as if he had died. For a time she would not let him eat at her table; then a dream, in which she saw him standing beside her on the same wooden rule, gave her hope, and she took him back. She begged a bishop to argue her son out of his errors. The bishop, himself once a Manichee, told her the young man was not yet teachable and she should pray. When she pressed him with tears, he sent her away with the words the Confessions has made immortal: “it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish.” She took it, Augustine says, as if it had sounded from heaven.

Rome and Milan. Disillusion came slowly. When the celebrated Manichee teacher Faustus came to Carthage, Augustine found him charming and shallow; his questions went unanswered. In 383 he sailed for Rome, deceiving his mother, who wished to come with him, by slipping away at night while she prayed in a chapel by the shore. In 384 he won the public post of professor of rhetoric at Milan, then the seat of the Western imperial court. It was the height of a provincial boy’s ambition. Monica followed him there.

Ambrose. At Milan two things worked on him. The first was Ambrose, the city’s bishop, whose sermons Augustine went to hear for their style and found he was listening to their substance. Ambrose showed him that the Old Testament he had despised could be read with depth and spiritual sense. The second was a handful of Platonist books, which freed him from the Manichaean habit of imagining God as a kind of bodily substance. His mind was changing. His will was not. At his mother’s urging a respectable marriage was arranged, and the mother of his son was sent back to Africa. He records the wound of it without excuse, and he records too that he promptly took another woman while he waited for his bride to come of age. He had long prayed, he tells us, for chastity — only not yet.

The garden. In August 386, after hearing of others who had given up everything to follow Christ, Augustine broke down in a garden at Milan. He flung himself under a fig tree, weeping, and heard from a neighbouring house a child’s voice chanting, “Take up and read; Take up and read.” He took up the letters of Paul that lay beside his friend Alypius and read the first words his eyes fell on, from Romans 13:13–14, a command to put on the Lord Jesus Christ. The struggle was over. He went in and told his mother, and she rejoiced.

Baptism, and Ostia. He gave up his professorship and spent the winter in a borrowed country house at Cassiciacum with his mother, his son and a group of friends, reading, talking and praying the Psalms. At Easter 387 Ambrose baptised him in Milan, together with Adeodatus and Alypius. Later that year, waiting at Ostia, the port of Rome, for a ship home to Africa, he and Monica stood at a window overlooking a garden and spoke together of the life of the saints, and were lifted, for a moment, beyond everything. Within days she fell ill. She died, in her fifty-sixth year and his thirty-third, asking only to be remembered at the Lord’s altar.

Hippo. Augustine returned to Africa in 388 and set up a small community of prayer and study at Thagaste. Adeodatus died there within a few years, still a young man. In 391, on a visit to the port city of Hippo Regius, he was seized by the congregation in church and presented to their bishop, Valerius, for ordination as a priest — a thing that happened in those days, and he wept at it. About 395 he was made Valerius’s fellow bishop and soon succeeded him. He remained bishop of Hippo for the rest of his life, some thirty-five years: preaching several times a week, hearing lawsuits, caring for the poor, living in common with his clergy, and writing without cease.

The controversies. His writing was forged in argument. Against the Manichees he defended the goodness of creation. Against the Donatists, a rival African church that held the Catholic clergy to be polluted by those who had handed over the Scriptures in the persecutions of a century earlier, he argued that the church is a mixed field of wheat and tares, and that the sacraments belong to Christ, not to the purity of the minister. That long struggle led him, in the end, to approve the empire’s coercion of the Donatists — the darkest page in his record. From about 411 he contended with the followers of the British monk Pelagius, who taught that human beings could keep God’s commandments by the strength of their own free will. Against them Augustine wrote the great books on grace that were to shape all Western theology after him.

The fall of Rome. In 410 the Goths under Alaric sacked the city of Rome. Pagans blamed the disaster on the Christians, who had abandoned the old gods. Augustine answered with The City of God, which he wrote over thirteen years and more: the story of two cities, built by two loves, running together through all of history. Around 421 he wrote the Enchiridion for a man named Laurentius. Near the end he reviewed all his writings in the Retractations, correcting what he thought mistaken.

The end. In 430 the Vandals, who had crossed from Spain, overran Roman Africa and besieged Hippo. Augustine fell ill in the third month of the siege. His friend and first biographer, Possidius, records that he had the penitential Psalms copied out and fixed to the wall beside his bed, and read them, weeping, in his last days. He died on 28 August 430, aged seventy-five. He left no will, Possidius says, because as one of God’s poor he had nothing to leave — nothing but a library. The library survived.

# Restless Until It Rests in Thee

> Return unto thy rest, O my soul; for the LORD hath dealt bountifully with thee. PSALM 116:7

The Confessions opens not with Augustine but with God. “Great art Thou, O Lord,” it begins, taking its words from the Psalms, and before a single fact of his life has been told, Augustine has set down the sentence that explains all the others: “Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee.” He was in his forties when he wrote it, a bishop looking back on thirty years of searching. He had tried nearly everything the ancient world could offer a clever young man — learning, reputation, pleasure, friendship, philosophy, a fashionable religion — and none of it had let him rest. The sentence is not a pious decoration. It is a conclusion, reached the hard way.

## Made for God

Notice where the sentence begins. It does not begin with our restlessness; it begins with our making. Thou madest us for Thyself. Before we are sinners, before we are seekers, we are creatures, and we were made with a direction built into us. We are not simply made by God; we are made for Him. He is not only our origin but our end, the place where our nature is meant to arrive.

> Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power: for thou hast created all things, and for thy pleasure they are and were created. REVELATION 4:11

This means that the longing in the human heart is not an accident or a disease. It is a sign of what we are. The Preacher saw it long before Augustine.

> He hath made every thing beautiful in his time: also he hath set the world in their heart, so that no man can find out the work that God maketh from the beginning to the end. ECCLESIASTES 3:11

The word translated world there carries the sense of the everlasting. God has set something of eternity in the human heart, and nothing that is merely temporal will fill the space. That is why the richest people are not the most content, why the most admired are often the most anxious, why a person can have everything he wanted at twenty and find at forty that he wants something else. We keep trying to fill an infinite space with finite things. Augustine’s claim is simple: the space has a shape, and the shape is God.

## Broken cisterns

The Confessions is, among other things, a long catalogue of the places Augustine tried to rest. As a boy he wanted to win at games and to be praised. As a student at Carthage he wanted to be loved, and to love. As a teacher he wanted fame; at Milan he wanted a post at court, a good marriage and money enough to live well. None of these things was evil in itself. Augustine was never a Manichee about creation after his conversion; he insisted all his life that the world God made is good. The trouble was not that the things were bad but that he asked them to be God. He asked a friend, a career, a woman, an idea to give him what only God can give, and each in turn failed under the weight.

> For my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water. JEREMIAH 2:13

Near the end of the tenth book Augustine gathers this up in one of the most beautiful passages he ever wrote. “Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new!” he says; he had been searching outside while God was within. “Thou wert with me, but I was not with Thee.” He had been plunging into the fair things God had made and missing the One who made them fair. It is worth sitting with that. The things we chase are not ugly. They are beautiful, and that is exactly why they are dangerous: they are lovely enough to be mistaken for the Loveliness they point to.

## The rest that Christ gives

If the heart was made for God, then the only cure for restlessness is to come to Him. Augustine did not find this out by philosophy. The Platonists, he says in Book VII, showed him something of God’s being, but they could not show him the way home; they saw the country from afar but did not know the road. The road is Christ.

> Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. MATTHEW 11:28

There is a great difference between being told where rest is and being given it. The Lord does not say, I will show you where rest may be found. He says, I will give it. The restless heart does not reach God by climbing; it is brought home by the One who came down. That is why Augustine’s great sentence is addressed to God, not to the reader. It is a prayer. He did not argue himself into rest; he confessed his way into it.

> Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again: but whosoever drinketh of the water that I shall give him shall never thirst. JOHN 4:13–14

## Restlessness that remains

It would be wrong to take Augustine’s sentence to mean that a converted person never feels restless again. He did not think so, and his life shows otherwise. The tenth book of the Confessions, written years after his baptism, is full of his present struggles — with pleasure in food, with the praise of men, with curiosity. He had come home, but he had not yet arrived. There is a restlessness that belongs to sin, the heart’s flight from God, and there is a restlessness that belongs to hope, the heart’s longing for more of Him. The first is cured by conversion. The second is only deepened by it.

> As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. PSALM 42:1

The Psalmist who wrote that was not unconverted. He was thirsty because he had tasted. That is the holy restlessness Augustine never lost, and it is why the Confessions ends, thirteen books later, with a prayer for the Sabbath rest that has no evening. The full repose the first paragraph promises is still to come.

> There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. HEBREWS 4:9

## Why it matters now

Our age has its own ways of silencing the heart’s question. We are busier and more distracted than Augustine could have imagined; we can fill every silent moment with noise. But the question does not go away because we refuse to hear it. It turns up at three in the morning, at a graveside, on the day we finally get what we wanted and find it is not enough. Augustine is a good companion at such moments, because he does not despise the longing. He honours it. He tells us it is the memory of home. And he tells us that the God we are longing for has been seeking us far longer than we have been seeking Him.

> Thou wilt shew me the path of life: in thy presence is fulness of joy; at thy right hand there are pleasures for evermore. PSALM 16:11

# The Pear Tree

> The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it? JEREMIAH 17:9

It is one of the oddest things in the Confessions. A bishop of the church, writing his life for God and for posterity, passes lightly over the years of his sexual sin and his false religion, and spends the better part of a book on a handful of stolen pears. He was sixteen. He and a gang of friends, out late in the streets, shook a neighbour’s tree, carried off the fruit, tasted a little, and threw the rest to the pigs. The pears were poor. He had better at home. He did not want them. And that, he says, was the whole point. “I loved mine own fault, not that for which I was faulty, but my fault itself.”

## Why a small sin

Augustine chose the pear tree because it was small. A great crime can always be explained. A man steals because he is hungry, kills because he is afraid, lies because he wants something. We understand those sins, even as we condemn them, because we can see the good thing the sinner was reaching for. But a boy who steals what he does not want, for no reason except that it is forbidden, has shown something more frightening. He has shown a will that takes pleasure in rebellion for its own sake. Augustine looked at that night and saw into the root of the human problem.

> Stolen waters are sweet, and bread eaten in secret is pleasant. PROVERBS 9:17

He did not let himself off by blaming his friends, though he notes honestly that he would never have done it alone. The company made it fun; the fun was the shared defiance. Much sin is like that. We do in a group what we would be ashamed to do by ourselves, and we call it belonging. Augustine calls it what it was.

## Love disordered

The pear tree led Augustine to his great insight about sin. Sin is not, at bottom, the love of evil things. There are no evil things, strictly speaking, in God’s creation; the pears themselves were good, he says, because God made them. Sin is love out of order. It is loving a lower good in place of a higher one, the creature in place of the Creator, my own will in place of God’s. Every sin, however ugly, is a counterfeit of something good. Pride imitates greatness; ambition imitates glory; even the pear-thief’s defiance, he says in Book II, was a crooked imitation of God’s own freedom, a creature trying to be a law to itself.

> Who changed the truth of God into a lie, and worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator, who is blessed for ever. Amen. ROMANS 1:25

This way of seeing sin is searching. It means that the question to ask of any temptation is not simply, is this thing wrong? but, what am I asking this thing to be? A meal, a friendship, a success, a pleasure can be received with thanks as a gift, or seized as a god. The thing is the same. The love is different.

## Born into it

Augustine did not think the pear tree was where his sin began. In the first book of the Confessions he looks back further, to infancy, and refuses the sentimental notion that babies are innocent. “Myself have seen and known even a baby envious,” he writes: too small to speak, it turned pale and glared at its foster-brother at the breast. The weakness of infant limbs, he says, is its innocence, not its will. What a baby lacks is not a selfish heart but the strength to act on it.

> Behold, I was shapen in iniquity; and in sin did my mother conceive me. PSALM 51:5

This became the ground of his teaching on original sin, which he set out most fully in the Enchiridion and in his writings against Pelagius. Adam, created good, turned from God by his own free will; and through him, Augustine taught, the whole race was corrupted and fell under the penalty of death. We do not start life neutral and then choose. We are born already bent.

> Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned. ROMANS 5:12

## Where he pressed too hard

The heart of this teaching is simply what Scripture says and what honest experience confirms. But Augustine’s way of explaining how original sin is passed on must be read with care. In the Enchiridion he describes Adam’s descendants as being the offspring of carnal lust, and elsewhere he tied the transmission of sin closely to the sexual passion by which children are conceived. Behind this lay his own long struggle; the desire that had enslaved him for years seemed to him the clearest proof of the soul’s disorder. But Scripture does not say that sexual desire within marriage is the channel of sin’s inheritance, and it calls the marriage bed honourable (Hebrews 13:4). Augustine’s view has left a long shadow of suspicion over the body and over marriage in the Western church, and many believers, especially women, have carried a shame God never laid on them. Hold fast to what he saw — that the heart is bent from birth — and let go of what he added.

## The only cure

Why dwell on all this? Because the doctor who does not diagnose cannot heal. Augustine’s unsparing account of sin was not the gloom of a man who hated himself. It was the relief of a man who had finally been told the truth, and found that the truth had a remedy. In Book II, right after the pear tree, he breaks into praise: God had forgiven him these heinous deeds, and even the sins he had not committed he owed to God’s keeping. That is how the knowledge of sin is meant to work. It drives us not to despair but to grace.

> Create in me a clean heart, O God; and renew a right spirit within me. PSALM 51:10

> But where sin abounded, grace did much more abound. ROMANS 5:20

The boy under the pear tree did not know it, but the God he was defying was already on his track. The very honesty with which the bishop could later tell the story was a sign of how thoroughly he had been found.

1. Recall a sin from your past that had no real reason behind it except that it was forbidden. What does it tell you about your heart?
2. Think of a sin you commit mostly in company. Ask whether you would do it alone, and decide what you will do next time the group goes that way.
3. Choose one good thing in your life and ask honestly whether you receive it as a gift or seize it as a god.
4. If you have carried a sense of shame about your body or your marriage that Scripture does not teach, bring it to God and to a wise believer, and let His Word set it right.
5. Read Psalm 51 aloud this week as your own prayer.

> Lord, I have loved my own way for its own sake, and taken pleasure in what You forbid simply because You forbid it.
> I was bent from the beginning, and I have bent myself further. I do not excuse it, and I cannot cure it.
> Create in me a clean heart. Put my loves back in their right order, with You above them all.
> Thank You that where my sin abounded, Your grace has abounded more, in Jesus Christ my Lord. Amen.

# The Absence of Good

> And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good. GENESIS 1:31

For nine years one question kept Augustine among the Manichees, and it was not a foolish question. Whence is evil? If God is good and made all things, where did the evil in the world come from? The Manichees had an answer that looked honest. There are two powers, they said, one of light and one of darkness, locked in an age-long struggle. The good in you belongs to the light; the evil in you belongs to the darkness, and it is not really you. It was a tidy explanation. It spared God the blame for evil, and it spared Augustine the blame for his own. It took him years to see that it did both at a terrible price.

## A question asked in an evil way

In Book VII of the Confessions Augustine describes his search with painful honesty. He sought whence evil came, he says, and sought in an evil way, and did not see the evil in his very search. He imagined the whole creation as a vast body, and God as a kind of boundless sea surrounding it, and he could not make the picture work. If God made all things good, was there some bad matter He could not master? Then He was not almighty. Had He no power to turn the whole to good? Then why create at all?

> Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right? GENESIS 18:25

What finally broke the deadlock was not a new argument about God but a new idea of what evil is. In Book III, looking back on his Manichaean days, he says that he did not yet know that evil was nothing but a privation of good. That phrase is the heart of his answer.

## What evil is not

Augustine sets it out simply in the Enchiridion. “For what is that which we call evil but the absence of good?” He takes an illustration from the body. When a wound is healed, the wound does not go and live somewhere else; it simply ceases to be. Disease is not a substance in its own right. It is the lack of health in a body that should be healthy. In the same way, evil is not a thing God made, or a rival power alongside Him. It is a spoiling, a lack, a falling-away from good in something that was made good.

> For every creature of God is good, and nothing to be refused, if it be received with thanksgiving. 1 TIMOTHY 4:4

This has two consequences that are easy to miss. First, it means that evil is always a parasite. It cannot exist on its own; it can only exist in something good, which it damages. A lie depends on the good gift of speech; cruelty depends on the good gift of strength; even the fallen angels, Augustine says, were made good, and what is evil in them is what they have lost. Second, it means that God is not the author of evil. Everything He made is good. Evil enters when a creature made good but changeable turns away from the unchangeable Good who made it — first an angel, Augustine says, and then a man.

> Let no man say when he is tempted, I am tempted of God: for God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man. JAMES 1:13

## Where evil begins

So the question whence is evil? leads back, in the end, to the will. Evil is not a place or a substance but a turning. The Manichees had placed the source of evil outside Augustine, in a dark power that overcame him. Augustine came to see that it was inside, in his own choosing. That was harder to accept, and far more hopeful. A man who is the victim of a dark power can only wait to be rescued. A man who has turned away can be turned back.

> God is light, and in him is no darkness at all. 1 JOHN 1:5

There is great pastoral wisdom here. We are always tempted to locate our evil somewhere else — in our upbringing, our circumstances, our genes, the people who hurt us. All of these are real and they matter. But the Christian cannot finally say, it was not me. Augustine’s long escape from Manichaeism was, at its core, an escape into responsibility. And responsibility, strangely, was freedom.

## Good out of evil

The privation account of evil does not mean that evil is unreal or small. A hole in the hull of a ship is a lack, but it can sink the ship. Augustine had watched friends die, had wept for his mother, and would die himself in a city under siege. He knew evil hurts. He never pretended otherwise, and no reader should use his idea to shrug at someone else’s pain.

What he did insist on was that evil does not have the last word, because God is greater than it. In one of the most important sentences of the Enchiridion he writes that God “judged it better to bring good out of evil, than not to permit any evil to exist.” God is so good and so powerful that He can take even the ruin creatures make and weave it into His purposes.

> But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive. GENESIS 50:20

> And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose. ROMANS 8:28

The supreme proof is the cross, where the worst evil ever done became the salvation of the world. That does not explain every sorrow, and Augustine does not pretend it does. In the same chapter of Book VII he says that some things belong to the depth of God’s just judgment, and that no creature has the right to demand, What is this? Why that? There are questions we will not answer this side of heaven. But we know enough of God’s character to trust Him with the rest.

## A caution and a hope

It must be said that Augustine’s account, however true, is not a complete answer to the problem of suffering, and it can sound cold if offered to someone in grief. It answers the philosopher’s question, where does evil come from? It does not answer the mourner’s question, why this, why me, why now? For that, Scripture gives us not an argument but a Person, who wept at a grave and then raised the dead, and a promise.

> And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away. REVELATION 21:4

2. Thank God this week for three good things in creation you have treated as ordinary: food, light, the body, friendship.
3. Recall a time God brought good out of something evil in your life. Write it down as a testimony.
4. If you are suffering now, bring God the mourner’s question rather than the philosopher’s. Tell Him plainly what hurts.
5. When you sit with someone in grief this month, resist explaining. Be present, and pray.

> Lord, You made all things good, and You are light with no darkness at all.
> I confess that the evil I have done came from my own turning away from You, and not from You.
> Where I have suffered evil at the hands of others, heal me, and bring good out of what was meant for harm, as You did for Joseph.
> Keep me trusting You in the questions I cannot answer, until the day You wipe away every tear. Amen.

# The Son of These Tears

> They that sow in tears shall reap in joy. PSALM 126:5

No account of Augustine can be told without his mother, and he would not have wanted it told any other way. Almost everything we know of Monica comes from the Confessions, written some ten years after her death, and it is a portrait drawn by a son who loved her and who knew he owed her, under God, his soul. It is not a flattering portrait. He shows her faults as well as her faith. But it is the fullest picture of a praying parent the ancient church has left us, and it has comforted mothers and fathers for sixteen centuries.

## A long obedience in prayer

Monica prayed for her son for something like seventeen years, counting from the time he first strayed to the day he was baptised. For nine of those years he was a Manichee, arguing cleverly against the faith she had taught him. He lived with a woman outside marriage. He mocked what she held dear. Nothing she said moved him. Augustine, looking back, speaks of her tears as though he could see them still. She wept for him, he says, more than mothers weep for the bodily death of their children, and God heard her, and did not despise her tears when they “watered the ground under her eyes in every place where she prayed.”

> And he spake a parable unto them to this end, that men ought always to pray, and not to faint. LUKE 18:1

There is no way to make those years sound easy. They were not a story yet. They were a mother going to God day after day about a son who was getting worse, with nothing to hold on to but a dream and a tired bishop’s word. That is what persevering prayer usually looks like. It does not feel like faith. It feels like going back to the same place with the same request and seeing no change. Monica’s example is not that she felt confident. It is that she did not stop.

> Thou tellest my wanderings: put thou my tears into thy bottle: are they not in thy book? PSALM 56:8

## The bishop and the promise

Augustine tells how, in the midst of those years, she went to a certain bishop, himself once a Manichee, and begged him to talk to her son and argue him out of his errors. The bishop wisely refused. The young man was not yet teachable, he said; he was too pleased with the novelty of his heresy. Let him alone, and pray. When she pressed him further with tears, the bishop, a little annoyed, sent her away with the words she kept for the rest of her life: “Go thy ways and God bless thee, for it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish.”

There is a lesson in the bishop’s refusal as well as in his promise. Monica wanted someone to fix her son with an argument. The bishop knew that arguments would not reach him yet. There are seasons when the most loving thing a parent can do is stop talking and keep praying. That is agony for a parent. It is sometimes the only way.

## When God says no

The most searching passage about Monica comes in Book V. When Augustine decided to leave Africa for Rome, she was determined to go with him, or to stop him. He lied to her. He told her he was only seeing off a friend, and while she spent the night praying in a chapel by the harbour — praying, Augustine says, that God would not let him sail — he sailed. She woke to find him gone.

Her prayer that night was refused. And yet Augustine, writing years later, saw in the refusal the deepest answer. “Thou, in the depth of Thy counsels and hearing the main point of her desire,” he prays, “regardest not what she then asked, that Thou mightest make me what she ever asked.” If Augustine had stayed in Carthage, he would never have gone to Milan, never have heard Ambrose, never have sat in the garden. Monica asked for the wrong thing in order to get the right one, and God, in His mercy, gave her the right one.

> For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. ISAIAH 55:8

Every praying parent needs this passage. We pray for particular outcomes — that a child would not move away, not take that job, not marry that person — and God sometimes refuses. We are tempted to think He has stopped listening. Monica’s story says He may be answering the deeper prayer, the one we have been praying all along, in a way we could never have chosen.

## Not a plaster saint

Augustine does not hide his mother’s weaknesses. As a young woman, he tells us, she had begun to drink more wine than was good for her, until a servant’s taunt shamed her out of it. She was anxious for her son’s worldly success as well as his soul, and she arranged the respectable marriage that led to the painful dismissal of the woman Augustine had lived with for fifteen years. Her love could be possessive; it followed him across the sea. None of this is said to diminish her. It is said because it is true, and because it means that her faith was the faith of an ordinary sinner, not of a heroine beyond our reach.

> The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much. JAMES 5:16

She was also, it should be said, a patient wife. Married young to Patricius, a man of fierce temper and unfaithful habits, she bore with him without quarrelling, and over many years won him to Christ, so that he was baptised before he died. She did in her marriage what she later did for her son: she outlasted unbelief with love.

> Likewise, ye wives, be in subjection to your own husbands; that, if any obey not the word, they also may without the word be won by the conversation of the wives. 1 PETER 3:1

## The window at Ostia

The prayers were answered. Monica saw her son baptised, and in the last days of her life they stood together at a window at Ostia, looking into a garden. “We were discoursing then together, alone, very sweetly,” Augustine says, about what the life of the saints would be, and their minds rose together beyond all created things to touch, for a moment, the Wisdom of God. When they came down again she told him that she had nothing left to live for; the one thing she had wanted, to see him a Christian before she died, God had given her in abundance.

Within days she fell ill. As she was dying she told her sons not to trouble about where she was buried: “let not the care for that any way disquiet you: this only I request, that you would remember me at the Lord’s altar, wherever you be.” Protestant readers will note that she asked for prayer after death, a practice Scripture does not teach and the Reformers rejected. It is fair to say so plainly, and fair also to see what the request shows: a woman who no longer cared where her body lay, because her hope was entirely in Christ’s sacrifice. She died in her fifty-sixth year, and her son wept for her in secret.

> When I call to remembrance the unfeigned faith that is in thee, which dwelt first in thy grandmother Lois, and thy mother Eunice. 2 TIMOTHY 1:5

1. Write down the name of one person you have stopped praying for because nothing seemed to change. Begin again today.
2. If you are a parent anxious about a child, ask whether this is a season to keep talking or to keep praying. Ask God for wisdom to tell the difference.
3. Think of a prayer God refused. Ask Him to show you what deeper prayer He may have been answering.
4. Thank God by name for the people who prayed for you before you prayed for yourself.
5. Look for someone in your church who has been praying for years with no visible answer, and encourage them this week.

### A PRAYER

> Father, You heard Monica’s tears and You did not despise them, though for years it seemed You were silent.
> I bring You those I love who are far from You. I do not know how to reach them, but You do.
> Where You refuse what I ask, give me grace to trust that You are answering what I most deeply desire.
> Keep me from fainting in prayer. Let me sow in tears, and let me live to reap in joy. Amen.

# Take Up and Read

> Awake, thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead, and Christ shall give thee light. EPHESIANS 5:14

By the summer of 386 Augustine’s mind had been won. He no longer believed the Manichees. The Platonists had taught him to think of God as spirit; Ambrose had shown him that the Scriptures were deep and true; he had heard the Christian faith explained and could find nothing against it. And still he did not come. Book VIII of the Confessions is the story of why, and it is one of the most honest accounts ever written of a soul that knows the truth and cannot bring itself to obey it.

## Only not yet

Augustine had been praying, after a fashion, for years. As a young man, he tells us, he had asked God for purity, and added a qualification: “Give me chastity and continency, only not yet.” He feared, he says, that God would answer too soon and cure a disease he wanted satisfied rather than removed. It is the most quoted prayer in the Confessions, and the reason is that almost everyone recognises it. We want to be holy — eventually. We want to follow Christ — once we have finished this, sorted that, enjoyed a little more of the other.

> To day if ye will hear his voice, harden not your hearts. HEBREWS 3:15

When God called him, Augustine says, he had nothing to answer but those dull and drowsy words, “Anon, anon,” “presently,” “leave me but a little.” But presently had no present, and his little while went on for a long while. There is a whole theology of delay in that sentence. The word not yet sounds modest and reasonable. It is in fact a refusal dressed as a postponement.

> For he saith, I have heard thee in a time accepted, and in the day of salvation have I succoured thee: behold, now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation. 2 CORINTHIANS 6:2

## The chain of habit

Why could he not simply decide? Augustine’s answer is one of his most important contributions to Christian understanding. The will, he saw, is not a free-floating switch that we can flip whenever we choose. It has a history. Our past choices build it into a shape. He puts it in a sentence that has become a classic of the moral life: he was bound, “not with another’s irons, but by my own iron will.” The enemy had held his will and made a chain of it. And he describes how the chain was forged, link by link: “For of a forward will, was a lust made; and a lust served, became custom; and custom not resisted, became necessity.”

> Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin. JOHN 8:34

This is profoundly true, and it is a warning to every young reader. The sins we indulge today are not only today’s sins. They are the forging of tomorrow’s chains. What begins as a choice becomes a habit, and what becomes a habit feels at last like a necessity — something we cannot help. We did it to ourselves, and we cannot undo it ourselves. That is exactly the condition from which Christ came to set people free.

## Two wills at war

The Manichees explained the inner struggle by saying that there were two souls in a man, one good and one evil. Augustine, having felt the struggle himself, rejected that. There were not two natures in him, but one will divided against itself. “Thus did my two wills, one new, and the other old, one carnal, the other spiritual, struggle within me,” he says, “and by their discord, undid my soul.” He wanted to want God, and he did not wholly want Him. The mind commands the hand to move, and it moves; but when the mind commands itself to will, it does not obey, because it does not command with its whole self.

> For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do. ROMANS 7:19

Augustine found himself in Paul’s words, and he found his way out in Paul’s cry.

> O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord. ROMANS 7:24–25

## The voice in the garden

The end came suddenly. A visitor named Pontitianus told Augustine and his friend Alypius about Antony of Egypt, who had given up everything to follow Christ, and of two court officials who had read Antony’s life and done the same on the spot. Augustine was stricken. Unlearned men were taking heaven by force while he, with all his learning, wallowed in flesh and blood. He rushed into the garden of their lodging, Alypius following, and the storm broke. He flung himself down under a fig tree and wept, crying, How long? Tomorrow and tomorrow? Why not now?

Then he heard from a neighbouring house a child’s voice, chanting over and over, “Take up and read; Take up and read.” He could not remember any children’s game with such words. He took it as a command from God to open the book and read the first passage he found, remembering that Antony had been converted by hearing a single verse of the Gospel. He went back to where Alypius sat, picked up the letters of Paul, and read in silence:

> Let us walk honestly, as in the day; not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying. But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof. ROMANS 13:13–14

“No further would I read; nor needed I,” he says: at the end of the sentence a light, as it were of serenity, flooded his heart, and all the darkness of doubt vanished away.

## What the garden teaches

It would be a mistake to make a method of this, as though believers should open the Bible at random and take whatever verse they find as God’s instruction. What happened in the garden was not magic. It was the end of a long work of God that had been going on for years — through a mother’s prayers, a bishop’s preaching, a friend’s example, the story of Antony. The child’s voice was simply the moment when God said, now.

And note what the verse actually said. It did not offer Augustine a feeling or an experience. It commanded him to put on a Person. He had been trying to become chaste by his own strength and had failed for years. The answer was not a better effort but Christ Himself, put on like a garment, making no room for the old desires. What his will could not do, grace did.

> If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed. JOHN 8:36

He went straight to tell his mother. She leapt for joy, he says, and blessed God, who is able to do far more than we ask or think. She had asked for a Christian son. She had received a son who would give up everything for Christ.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is there an area of your life where you are praying, in effect, “only not yet”? Name it before God today.
2. Trace one habit that now feels like a necessity back to the choices that forged it. Confess the choices, and ask Christ to break the chain.
3. Identify one provision you are making for the flesh — a place, a device, a relationship, a routine — and remove it this week.
4. Read Romans 13:11–14 each morning for a week, and consciously put on the Lord Jesus Christ as you dress.
5. If you are still standing on the edge of faith, ask yourself honestly: why not now?

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have said “not yet” to You more times than I can count, and called it caution.
> I am bound by chains I made myself, and my will is divided against itself. I cannot set myself free.
> But You have said, now is the day of salvation. Let it be today.
> I put on the Lord Jesus Christ. Make no room in me for what You hate, and make me wholly Yours. Amen.

# Give What Thou Enjoinest

> For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure. PHILIPPIANS 2:13

In the tenth book of the Confessions, confessing his present struggles with temptation, Augustine prays a short prayer four times over. “Give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt.” Command what You like, Lord, only give me what You command. It sounds like a simple prayer of dependence, and it is. It also turned out to be one of the most contested sentences in the history of the church. Augustine later recorded that when it was quoted in Rome in the hearing of a British monk named Pelagius, Pelagius objected to it with some heat. The long quarrel that followed shaped Western theology for the next fifteen hundred years.

## What Pelagius feared

Pelagius was a serious and moral man. He was troubled by the slackness of Roman Christians who excused their sins by saying that human nature was weak. God, he argued, would not command what we cannot do. If He tells us to be holy, we must be able to be holy. Our nature, created good, is still able to choose good; grace helps us, by giving us the law, the example of Christ and the forgiveness of past sins, but the choosing is ours. To pray, Give what Thou enjoinest, seemed to him to cut the nerve of moral effort.

It is worth taking Pelagius seriously, because his instinct is alive in every generation. It is the instinct that says, God helps those who help themselves. It is the instinct of every religion of self-improvement. And it seems so reasonable. Surely a command implies an ability?

## The will that destroyed itself

Augustine’s answer came out of his own experience and out of Paul. He had known what was right for years and could not do it. His will was not free in the sense Pelagius imagined; it was chained. In the Enchiridion he puts it with stark clarity. Man was created upright, with a will free to choose good; but it was “by the evil use of his free-will that man destroyed both it and himself.” As a man who kills himself was alive when he did it but cannot then give himself life again, so the will that turned from God cannot turn itself back.

> Jesus answered them, Verily, verily, I say unto you, Whosoever committeth sin is the servant of sin. JOHN 8:34

This does not mean that fallen people have no will, or that they are forced to sin. We choose freely what we want. The trouble is what we want. The slave of sin is free to sin; he is not free to love God, because he does not want to. The bondage is in the heart’s desires, and no command can change desire. The law, Augustine says in the Enchiridion, without the Holy Spirit through whom love is poured into our hearts, “can command, but it cannot assist.” It shows the target; it does not give the strength to hit it.

> No man can come to me, except the Father which hath sent me draw him. JOHN 6:44

## Grace that goes before

So grace, for Augustine, cannot be merely help offered to a will that is already willing. It must go first. It must make the will willing. In one of the finest sentences in the Enchiridion he takes two verses from the Psalms — that God’s mercy shall meet me, and that His mercy shall follow me — and draws out their meaning: “It goes before the unwilling to make him willing; it follows the willing to make his will effectual.”

> The God of my mercy shall prevent me. PSALM 59:10

> Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life. PSALM 23:6

To prevent, in the old English of the King James Bible, means to go before. This is prevenient grace: the grace that is there before we turn, that causes us to turn. Augustine drew the practical point himself. Why, he asks, are we taught to pray for our enemies, who plainly do not want to live a holy life, unless it is that God may work willingness in them? Every prayer for the conversion of another person is a confession that God can change a will from the inside.

> A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. EZEKIEL 36:26

## Nothing we did not receive

This leaves no room for boasting, and Augustine intended that. Even faith itself, he insists in the Enchiridion, is the gift of God, and he quotes Paul’s words to prove it.

> For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast. EPHESIANS 2:8–9

He does not say good works do not matter. He says they are fruit, not root. Eternal life may rightly be called a reward of good works, he says, but the good works were themselves given, so that “man’s good deserts are themselves the gift of God,” and when they are crowned with eternal life, it is simply grace given for grace. The verse he returned to again and again in these controversies was Paul’s question to the proud Corinthians.

> For who maketh thee to differ from another? and what hast thou that thou didst not receive? now if thou didst receive it, why dost thou glory, as if thou hadst not received it? 1 CORINTHIANS 4:7

## Honest questions

Augustine’s teaching on grace is among his greatest gifts to the church, and it is at the root of the Reformation’s rediscovery of salvation by grace alone. But it must be received with care at two points.

First, in his later writings he pressed his logic into a doctrine of predestination that many who love his teaching on grace find hard to square with the whole of Scripture. In the Enchiridion he reads Paul’s statement that God will have all men to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4) to mean either that no one is saved unless God wills it, or that God wills to save people of every rank and kind, rather than every single person, and he speaks of God making, out of one heap of perdition, one vessel to honour and another to dishonour. Scripture does teach God’s sovereign choice; it also teaches His sincere love for the world and His free offer to all. Hold both, as Scripture does, even where Augustine’s system seems to let one swallow the other.

Second, Protestant readers should know that when Augustine speaks of justification he usually means God making the sinner righteous by pouring love into the heart, not chiefly God declaring the sinner righteous for Christ’s sake. The Reformers drew on his doctrine of grace while making a sharper distinction here than he did. Both Catholics and Protestants have honestly found themselves in him, and both have had to go beyond him.

What no reader should lose is the prayer at the heart of it all. Give what You command. It is not a counsel of laziness. It is the only prayer that makes holiness possible.

> I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing. JOHN 15:5

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name a command of God you have been trying to keep in your own strength and failing. Turn it into Augustine’s prayer: give what You command.
2. Look for the traces of prevenient grace in your own story — ways God was drawing you before you wanted Him — and thank Him for them.
3. Pray this week for someone who plainly does not want God, asking that He would work willingness in them.
4. Catch yourself the next time you take credit for a spiritual strength. Ask, what have I that I did not receive?
5. Where a hard doctrine unsettles you, write down the Scriptures on both sides and hold them together before God rather than choosing one.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You command me to love You with all my heart, and I cannot do it by myself.
> Give what You command, and command what You will. Go before my unwilling heart and make it willing; follow my willing heart and make it strong.
> Take away all boasting from me. Everything I have that is good, I have received from You.
> Keep me abiding in Christ, for without Him I can do nothing. Amen.

# The Humble Mediator

> For there is one God, and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus. 1 TIMOTHY 2:5

Augustine’s road to Christ ran through the Platonists, and it is one of the most instructive things about him that he tells us exactly what they gave him and exactly what they could not. In Book VII of the Confessions he describes reading certain books of the Platonist philosophers in Latin translation. They set him free from the Manichaean habit of imagining God as a kind of physical stuff spread through space. They taught him to look inward and upward, to a God who is spirit and unchangeable light. He found in them, he says, much that was like the opening of John’s Gospel. But one thing he did not find.

## What the philosophers lacked

He sets it out in a careful list. He read there, in substance, that in the beginning was the Word, and that the Word was God, and that all things were made by Him. “But that the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, I read not there.” He read that the Son was in the form of God. He did not read that He emptied Himself, took the form of a servant, humbled Himself and became obedient unto death.

> And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth. JOHN 1:14

The philosophers could see the goal. They could not supply the road. Augustine has a memorable image for this. It is one thing, he says, to see the land of peace from a wooded mountain-top and find no way to it; it is another to keep to the road that leads there. The Platonists stood on the mountain and looked. They knew there was a homeland; they did not know how to reach it, and they were too proud to take the road God had made, because the road was a crucified man.

> I am the way, the truth, and the life: no man cometh unto the Father, but by me. JOHN 14:6

## Pride and its cure

For Augustine the root of all sin was pride — the creature’s refusal to be a creature, the wish to be one’s own god. And pride is precisely what keeps the clever from coming to Christ. The Platonists, he says, scorned to learn of Him who is meek and lowly in heart. Augustine himself had once despised the Scriptures because their style was so plain beside Cicero’s. He had wanted a religion that flattered his mind. God gave him one that humbled it.

> Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. MATTHEW 11:29

The confession he makes about this period is one of the most searching in the book. He had begun to understand God, but he did not yet cling to Christ, because he did not yet want to be humble. “For I did not hold to my Lord Jesus Christ, I, humbled, to the Humble,” he writes, “nor knew I yet whereto His infirmity would guide us.” Only a humbled man can take hold of a humble Saviour. The way to God is not up, by the strength of our minds, but down, by the humility of His Son.

In the same book he describes a moment when, lifted briefly towards God, he found himself far off, in what he calls the region of unlikeness, and seemed to hear a voice from on high: “I am the food of grown men, grow, and thou shalt feed upon Me; nor shalt thou convert Me, like the food of thy flesh into thee, but thou shalt be converted into Me.” But how could a creature so unlike God feed upon Him? Only if God made Himself food fit for us. That, Augustine says, is why the Word was made flesh: so that the Wisdom by which all things were made might become milk for our infancy.

## Why the Mediator must be God and man

Augustine returned to Christ the Mediator throughout the Enchiridion, and he is exact. A mediator stands between two parties and reconciles them. Adam, before he sinned, needed no mediator. But when sin had opened a great gulf between God and the human race, it was fitting that One who alone of all humanity was born, lived and died without sin should reconcile us to God. And He could not do it unless He were both. We could not be redeemed, Augustine insists, even through the one Mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, if He were not also God.

> Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus: who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men. PHILIPPIANS 2:5–7

Then he gives the reason in a phrase that sums up his whole understanding of salvation. God became man, he says, “in order that the pride of man might be exposed and cured through the humility of God.” Our disease was pride. The medicine was a God who stooped. There is no more striking remedy in all of theology: the infinite God healing the arrogance of creatures by becoming lowlier than any of them.

## Victor because Victim

At the end of Book X of the Confessions, after the long and painful inventory of his continuing temptations, Augustine turns again to Christ, and he writes of Him in one of his most concentrated sentences. Christ was, he says, “for us to Thee both Victor and Victim, and therefore Victor, because the Victim.” He won by losing, conquered by dying, prevailed by being sacrificed. And Augustine draws the conclusion for his own weary soul: his hope is strong in Him, because otherwise he would despair.

> Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them. HEBREWS 7:25

It is worth pausing here, because this is the Augustine who is often forgotten. He is remembered as the great theologian of sin and grace, and he was. But sin and grace are not abstract categories in him. Grace has a face. It is the face of a Man who took the lowest place, and Augustine never tired of looking at it. When he was most conscious of his own continuing weakness, it was not to his conversion or his office or his learning that he turned. It was to Christ, the Mediator, who had paid the price and was standing for him still.

> God resisteth the proud, and giveth grace to the humble. 1 PETER 5:5

1. Ask yourself honestly whether there is any part of the gospel you find beneath you — its plainness, its people, its demands. Confess that pride to God.
2. Read Philippians 2:5–11 slowly each day this week, and choose one concrete act of humility in your household or work.
3. When you are most aware of your failures, deliberately turn your eyes from yourself to Christ the Mediator, as Augustine did at the end of Book X.
4. If you are drawn to spiritual ideas but reluctant to commit yourself to Christ, ask what it would mean for you to take the road rather than admire the view.
5. Thank God specifically that the Son became what you are so that you could be brought to Him.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You were in the form of God, and You took the form of a servant for me.
> I confess the pride that has kept me from You, and the pride that still wants to reach God by my own strength.
> Humble me, that I may hold on to You, the Humble One. Be my way, for I cannot find the road home alone.
> You are Victor because You were Victim. Let my hope rest wholly in You. Amen.

# Confession as Praise

> Great is the LORD, and greatly to be praised; and his greatness is unsearchable. PSALM 145:3

We use the word confession almost entirely for the admission of sin. Augustine used it in two senses, and his great book cannot be understood without both. To confess, in the Latin Bible he read, was to acknowledge the truth before God — the truth about ourselves, which is our sin, and the truth about God, which is His glory. Confession of sin and confession of praise were, for him, a single act seen from two sides. The Confessions is therefore not simply an autobiography, still less a scandalous memoir. It is a hymn in which a sinner tells the truth about himself in order to praise the God who saved him.

## Speaking to God, not about Him

The first thing a reader notices is that the whole book is addressed to God. Augustine does not say, I did this, and then that happened. He says, Thou knowest, Lord, what I did; Thou wert there; Thou heardest my mother. It is a strange way to write a life, and it changes everything. A memoir invites the reader to admire or pity the writer. A confession addressed to God invites the reader to look past the writer to the One he is speaking to.

Augustine was well aware that God did not need the information. At the start of Book XI he asks the question himself. Since God is eternal and knows all things, why does he lay so many stories before Him? Not, he answers, so that God might learn them through him, but to stir up his own and his readers’ love for God, so that together they might all say, Great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised. Confession is not a report. It is worship.

> I acknowledged my sin unto thee, and mine iniquity have I not hid. I said, I will confess my transgressions unto the LORD; and thou forgavest the iniquity of my sin. PSALM 32:5

## Why confess past sins

A reader might ask why a bishop, respected and forgiven, should rake over the sins of his youth in public. Augustine asks that question too, in Book X, and his answer is pastoral. Other people, he says, when they hear the story of past evils now forgiven, are not to marvel at the sinner but to take heart for themselves. Such confessions, read and heard, stir up the heart so that it does not sleep in despair and say, I cannot, but wakes in the love of God’s mercy and the sweetness of His grace. The purpose of telling the truth about sin is to make grace visible. A church in which no one confesses anything is a church in which no one can see what grace has done.

He was equally honest that his struggle was not over. Book X is the confession not of the young sinner but of the bishop, and it is a humbling list: the pleasure he still took in food, the snares of the eyes and ears, the love of praise that he could not wholly shake off. He prays there the prayer we met in an earlier chapter: give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt. The mature Christian, on Augustine’s showing, does not stop confessing. He only confesses more truly.

> If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. 1 JOHN 1:9

## Knowing and being known

Behind the whole practice lies a simple conviction: God already knows. There is nothing to hide from Him, and therefore nothing to gain by hiding. Book X opens with a prayer taken from Paul. “Let me know Thee, O Lord, who knowest me: let me know Thee, as I am known.” The one who is fully known by God can dare to be honest, because the knowing is not a threat but a mercy.

> For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known. 1 CORINTHIANS 13:12

This is liberating. Much of our spiritual exhaustion comes from managing appearances — before others, before ourselves, even, absurdly, before God. Augustine invites us to stop. He prays in Book I that God would enlarge the narrow house of his soul, repair its ruins, and forgive what in it offends His eyes: “Narrow is the mansion of my soul; enlarge Thou it, that Thou mayest enter in.” It is the prayer of a man who has given up pretending.

> Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: and see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting. PSALM 139:23–24

## The Psalms as his school of prayer

Augustine learned this double confession from the Psalms, which are full of both. In the winter after his conversion, at the country house at Cassiciacum, he read them as a new believer, and he writes of the effect with unforgettable warmth. He read the fourth Psalm, he says, and it wrought upon him; he wished the Manichees could have been there to see his face and hear him.

> Hear me when I call, O God of my righteousness: thou hast enlarged me when I was in distress; have mercy upon me, and hear my prayer. PSALM 4:1

He learned too from the singing of the church. At Milan, where Ambrose had lately brought in the singing of hymns and psalms by the whole congregation, he found himself overwhelmed. “How did I weep, in Thy Hymns and Canticles, touched to the quick by the voices of Thy sweet-attuned Church!” The truth, he says, distilled into his heart through his ears, and tears ran down, and he was happy in them.

> Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly in all wisdom; teaching and admonishing one another in psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, singing with grace in your hearts to the Lord. COLOSSIANS 3:16

As a bishop he preached through the whole Psalter over many years, in the sermons now gathered as his Expositions of the Psalms. He taught his people to hear in the Psalms the voice of Christ — sometimes Christ the Head speaking to the Father, sometimes Christ speaking in and for His body, the church, in its sorrow and sin. And at the very end, as Possidius tells us, it was the penitential Psalms he had fixed to the wall of his sickroom. The man who began his great book with a verse of praise from the Psalms ended his life reading the Psalms of confession. The two had never been far apart.

> If thou, LORD, shouldest mark iniquities, O Lord, who shall stand? But there is forgiveness with thee, that thou mayest be feared. PSALM 130:3–4

2. Each time you confess a sin this week, follow it immediately with a sentence of praise for the God who forgives.
3. Consider whether there is a story of forgiven sin in your life that, told wisely, could encourage someone else not to despair.
4. Choose a Psalm and pray it every day for a week, letting it give you words for both confession and praise.
5. Sing this Sunday as though the words were your own prayer, not only the congregation’s.

> Great are You, Lord, and greatly to be praised. You know me better than I know myself.
> I confess my sin to You, not to inform You, but because I no longer wish to hide.
> Enlarge the narrow house of my soul, repair what is ruined, and come in.
> Let every confession of my sin become a confession of Your mercy, until my whole life is praise. Amen.

# The Fields and Palaces of Memory

> Bless the LORD, O my soul, and forget not all his benefits. PSALM 103:2

The first nine books of the Confessions tell a story. The tenth stops telling it. Having brought his life up to his mother’s death, Augustine turns from what he was to what he is, and the question he asks is startling: where, in myself, do I find God? The answer takes him into the long meditation on memory that fills most of Book X. It is one of the strangest and most original things he ever wrote, and it has a great deal to teach a hurried age that has almost forgotten how to remember.

## I asked the earth

He begins by looking outward. What is it that I love, he asks, when I love my God? Not the beauty of bodies, nor the brightness of light, nor sweet melodies, nor fragrance, nor manna, nor limbs made for embracing. And yet there is a kind of light and melody and fragrance and embrace he loves when he loves God — the light and voice and embrace of his inner self. So he goes to the creation and questions it. “I asked the earth,” he says, “and it answered me, ‘I am not He’.” He asks the sea and the deeps and the creeping things, and they answer, “We are not thy God, seek above us.” The air, the heavens, sun, moon and stars all make the same reply. Their answer, he says, was their beauty. They point beyond themselves.

> The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament sheweth his handywork. PSALM 19:1

This is Augustine at his most balanced. The creation is not God, and he will not worship it. But neither is it silent. Everything beautiful is a signpost with the same words on it: not here — higher. The trouble with fallen people is not that they love beautiful things, but that they stop at the signpost.

## The vast palace within

So he turns inward. Passing beyond the senses, he comes, he says, “to the fields and spacious palaces of my memory, where are the treasures of innumerable images.” He is astonished by what he finds. Everything he has ever seen, heard, learned or felt is stored there, in some fashion, ready to be called out. He can picture colours in the dark and hear songs with his mouth shut. He can remember being happy when he is sad, and remember being afraid without fear. He can even remember that he has forgotten.

“Great is the power of memory,” he exclaims, “a fearful thing, O my God, a deep and boundless manifoldness; and this thing is the mind, and this am I myself.” He had spent years looking at the stars and the sea; now he finds a greater wonder inside his own head, and he marvels that men go abroad to admire mountains and oceans and pass themselves by without wonder.

> I will praise thee; for I am fearfully and wonderfully made: marvellous are thy works; and that my soul knoweth right well. PSALM 139:14

## In Thee above me

But Augustine is not writing psychology for its own sake. He is looking for God. Where in all these halls is He? God is not an image stored among images, nor a feeling, nor a thought like other thoughts. Yet Augustine knows that since he came to know God he has never forgotten Him; God is somehow in his memory. And how did He get there?

He reaches for an illustration from the Gospel. The woman who lost her coin, he says, and searched for it with a lamp, would never have found it unless she had remembered it; how else would she recognise it? Every search presupposes some knowledge of what we are looking for. And all people, he observes, want the happy life. They may not agree what it is, but they know they want it, and they would all rather rejoice in truth than be deceived. “A happy life is joy in the truth,” he says: and that joy is a joy in God, who is the Truth. Somewhere in the deep places of the soul there is a memory of home, however clouded.

> That they should seek the Lord, if haply they might feel after him, and find him, though he be not far from every one of us: for in him we live, and move, and have our being. ACTS 17:27–28

Yet when he asks where exactly he first found God, he comes to a surprising answer. Not in his memory, for God was not there before he learned Him. “Where then did I find Thee, that I might learn Thee, but in Thee above me?” The search inward does not end in the self. It ends in God, of whom Augustine says elsewhere in the Confessions, “Thou wert more inward to me than my most inward part; and higher than my highest.” We do not find God by digging Him out of ourselves; He finds us, and in the finding we discover that He was closer all along than we were to ourselves.

## Too late loved I Thee

It is at exactly this point that Augustine breaks into the famous prayer we met at the start of this book: too late loved I Thee, O Beauty ever ancient, ever new. Thou wert within, and I abroad. And he goes on to describe God’s pursuit of him through all five senses. “Thou calledst, and shoutedst, and burstest my deafness.” God flashed and shone and scattered his blindness; breathed fragrance and he drew in breath and panted; he tasted, and now hungers and thirsts; God touched him, and he burned for His peace.

> O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him. PSALM 34:8

## Remembering rightly

What does this mean for us? At least this: memory is not a filing cabinet. It is the place where we meet God, and it can be trained for good or ill. Scripture commands Israel again and again to remember — the exodus, the wilderness, the covenant — because a people who forget what God has done will soon forget God.

> And thou shalt remember all the way which the LORD thy God led thee these forty years in the wilderness, to humble thee, and to prove thee, to know what was in thine heart. DEUTERONOMY 8:2

The Confessions itself is an act of Christian memory: a man going back over his life, not to wallow in it or to boast of it, but to trace the hand of God in it. We can do the same. The past is not only a place of regret. Looked at in God’s light, it is a record of mercy. And our remembering is meant to become thanksgiving.

> I will remember the works of the LORD: surely I will remember thy wonders of old. PSALM 77:11

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Spend ten minutes this week in silence simply remembering — not planning, not reviewing — and ask God to show you where He was in what you recall.
2. Walk outside and look at something beautiful. Let it say to you what it said to Augustine: not here — higher. Then pray.
3. Write a list of ten mercies of God from your past that you had begun to forget, and thank Him for each.
4. If your memory holds wounds rather than blessings, bring one of them to God in prayer and ask Him to show you how He was present even there.
5. At the end of each day, before sleep, recall where God met you that day, and give thanks.

> Lord, You are more inward to me than my inmost self, and I went looking for You everywhere but where You were.
> Thank You for the great and fearful gift of memory. Let it be a place where I meet You and not a place where I hide from You.
> Call, and break my deafness; shine, and scatter my blindness. Let me taste and see that You are good.
> Teach me to remember all the way You have led me, and to turn every memory into praise. Amen.

# What Then Is Time?

> Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, even from everlasting to everlasting, thou art God. PSALM 90:2

Book XI of the Confessions begins with the first verse of the Bible, In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth, and it very quickly runs into a question that has troubled thinkers ever since. If God made the world at a beginning, what was He doing before? Augustine records the answer some wit had given — that God was preparing hell for people who pry into mysteries — and refuses it. It is one thing, he says, to answer a question and another to make fun of the questioner; he would rather say I do not know than raise a laugh at a serious enquirer. So he sets out, prayerfully, to think about time. Few pages in all of Christian writing have been read more often by philosophers. But Augustine wrote them as a prayer, and they are meant for ordinary believers too.

## The question no one can answer

He admits at once that he is out of his depth. “What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not.” We speak of time constantly. We plan, remember, wait, hurry. And yet when we try to say what it is, it slips through our fingers. The past is no longer; the future is not yet; and the present, if it lasted, would not be time at all but eternity. It seems to have no width. How can we measure something that is always vanishing?

Augustine’s answer is that we measure time in the mind. There are not really three times, past, present and future, but three presents: a present of things past, which is memory; a present of things present, which is attention; and a present of things future, which is expectation. “It is in thee, my mind, that I measure times,” he says. Time, for a creature, is a kind of stretching of the soul, reaching back in memory and forward in hope.

> For what is your life? It is even a vapour, that appeareth for a little time, and then vanisheth away. JAMES 4:14

## God’s To-day

Against this fragile, stretched-out creaturely time, Augustine sets the eternity of God. The answer to the question, what was God doing before creation? is that there was no before. Time itself is something God made. “For there was no ‘then,’ when there was no time.” God does not live through a very long succession of moments. He does not have a past He has left behind or a future He is waiting for. He simply is.

> And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM. EXODUS 3:14

Augustine puts it in lines of great beauty. “Thy years are one day; and Thy day is not daily, but To-day, seeing Thy To-day gives not place unto to-morrow, for neither doth it replace yesterday. Thy To-day, is Eternity.” Every moment of history is present to God at once. He is not hurried by our deadlines nor delayed by our waiting.

> But, beloved, be not ignorant of this one thing, that one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day. 2 PETER 3:8

This is not a cold, abstract idea. It is profoundly comforting. The God who is eternal is never taken by surprise, never runs out of time, never arrives too late. Our yesterdays and tomorrows lie open before Him. The prayer we prayed ten years ago and the need we will have ten years from now are both in His today.

> But thou art the same, and thy years shall have no end. PSALM 102:27

## A life scattered and gathered

Then Augustine turns from speculation to his own soul, and the tone changes. Having spoken of the stretching of the mind across past, present and future, he confesses that his own life feels less like a stretching than a scattering. “Behold, my life is but a distraction,” he says: pulled this way and that by many things, torn apart by the passing of time. It is a description that many modern people, with their divided attention and crowded calendars, will recognise at once.

The remedy he finds is Christ, the Mediator between the One God and us many. By Him, Augustine prays, he may be gathered up from his old way of life to follow the One, forgetting what is behind, not scattered but stretched forward, not distractedly but intently, towards the prize of his heavenly calling. He is borrowing the words of Paul, and he has turned a philosophical puzzle into a way of life.

> Brethren, I count not myself to have apprehended: but this one thing I do, forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before, I press toward the mark for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus. PHILIPPIANS 3:13–14

## Living in time before eternity

Augustine’s reflection leaves us with several practical wisdoms. First, humility. If we cannot explain even time, which we live in every moment, we should hold our theories about God’s ways lightly. Second, peace. Our times are in the hand of One who is not subject to time, and so we need not be frantic.

> My times are in thy hand. PSALM 31:15

Third, attention. Since the present is the only point at which we touch time, it is the only place we can obey God. We cannot live in the past, and we cannot live in the future. We can only live, and love, and pray today. Fourth, hope. Our scattered lives are not meant to stay scattered. The eternal God has entered time in Jesus Christ, so that creatures of time might at last share His everlasting day.

> So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom. PSALM 90:12

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Notice this week how often your mind is in the past (regret) or the future (worry). Each time, bring it back to the present and pray.
2. Take one anxiety about the future and consciously place it in the hands of the God for whom tomorrow is already today.
3. Identify one source of distraction that is scattering your life, and set a limit on it for a month.
4. Choose one thing you will stop looking back on, and, like Paul, deliberately forget it before God.
5. Read Psalm 90 aloud at the start of the week and ask God to teach you to number your days.

### A PRAYER

> Eternal God, You were before the mountains, and You will be when all our days are done. Your years are one day, and Your day is always today.
> My life is scattered among many things. Gather me by Christ, the Mediator, and set me on the one thing needful.
> I place my past and my future in Your hands. Teach me to live and love and obey You now.
> And bring me at last into Your everlasting day, where there is no more waiting. Amen.

# Believe That You May Understand

> Through faith we understand that the worlds were framed by the word of God, so that things which are seen were not made of things which do appear. HEBREWS 11:3

Augustine joined the Manichees partly because they promised him knowledge without faith. The Catholic church, they said, asked people to believe things on authority; they would lead their hearers to truth by reason alone. It was an attractive offer to a proud young intellectual. It took him nine years to discover that the Manichees, too, demanded belief in a great many things they could not prove — and far stranger things than the church had ever asked. When he finally came to Christ, he came with a new understanding of how faith and reason belong together, and it became one of the most influential principles of Christian thought.

## Everyone believes

The turning point, as he tells it in Book VI of the Confessions, was a simple observation. God, he says, gently persuaded him by making him consider “what innumerable things I believed, which I saw not, nor was present while they were done” — the events of history, the existence of distant cities, the word of friends and physicians. He believed, without having seen it, who his own parents were. Without trusting others, he realised, we could do nothing at all in this life. Faith is not a peculiar religious weakness. It is the ordinary way human beings come to know most of what they know.

> Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen. HEBREWS 11:1

This freed him from the arrogance that assumes a thinking person may accept nothing he has not personally proved. The real question is not whether to trust, but whom. And the Scriptures, he came to see, carried an authority that commended itself — spread through all the world, humble in style yet deep in meaning, open to the simple and yet unexhausted by the learned.

## Faith first, then understanding

From this grew the principle for which Augustine is remembered: believe, that you may understand. He found it in the prophet Isaiah, in the old Latin version he read, which rendered a verse of chapter 7, Unless you believe, you shall not understand. The King James Version, following the Hebrew, reads differently.

> If ye will not believe, surely ye shall not be established. ISAIAH 7:9

Augustine’s text was imperfect, but the truth he drew from it is thoroughly scriptural, and he could have found it in the words of Christ.

> If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether I speak of myself. JOHN 7:17

Faith, for Augustine, is not the enemy of thinking. It is where thinking starts. We do not first understand God and then decide to trust Him; we trust Him, and then begin to understand. In the Enchiridion he sums up the whole Christian life in a phrase: “we begin in faith, and are made perfect by sight.” Faith is the road; understanding grows along it; sight is the home at the end.

But notice that he wants us to understand. Augustine never used faith as an excuse to stop thinking. He wrote more than almost anyone in the ancient church, and he wrestled with every question he could find. His principle is not believe instead of understanding, but believe in order to understand. Faith that never seeks understanding has not taken God seriously. Understanding that despises faith has not taken itself seriously enough.

## The Trinity: faith seeking understanding

The great example of this in his work is On the Trinity, which he laboured over for some twenty years. He begins where faith must begin — with Scripture — and spends the first books gathering what the Bible says of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. He sets it out briefly in the Enchiridion too: the one true God is the Trinity, the Father, the Son begotten of the Father, and the Holy Spirit, one and the same Spirit of Father and Son.

> Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost. MATTHEW 28:19

Only then does he go looking for understanding. He looks for traces of the Trinity in the human soul, which was made in God’s image. In the last book of the Confessions he offers a simple one: I am, I know, and I will. These are three, and yet one life, one mind; they cannot be separated, and yet they are distinct. In On the Trinity he develops others — memory, understanding and will; the lover, the beloved and the love between them. He is careful to say that these images fall far short. In the Confessions he calls them things “far other than the Trinity.” They are not proofs. They are the attempts of a believing mind to glimpse a little of what it already confesses.

> God is love; and he that dwelleth in love dwelleth in God, and God in him. 1 JOHN 4:16

His image of the Spirit as the bond of love between Father and Son has shaped Western worship and prayer ever since. It is fair to add that the Eastern churches have been uneasy with the way this was later expressed in the Western creed, and readers will meet that long debate if they read further.

## The humility of the enquirer

Augustine was frank about how little any of us can grasp. He said in On the Trinity, in effect, that we speak of three persons not because the phrase says all that should be said, but so as not to be silent. His great mind ends, again and again, not in a solution but in worship.

> O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out! ROMANS 11:33

That is the pattern he offers every thinking believer. Start with what God has said. Believe it. Then think as hard as you can, pray as you think, and when you reach the edge of what can be understood, bow down. The end of theology is doxology.

> For we walk by faith, not by sight. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:7

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. List some of the things you believe on the testimony of others. Consider what this shows about the reasonableness of trusting God’s testimony.
2. Choose one doctrine you believe but do not understand well, and spend an hour this month studying what Scripture says about it.
3. If you have doubts, bring them honestly to God and to a wise believer rather than hiding them. Faith seeks understanding; it does not fear questions.
4. Read Matthew 28:19 and 2 Corinthians 13:14, and pray this week consciously to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit.
5. When your thinking reaches its limit, stop and worship, as Paul does in Romans 11:33–36.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I believe; help me to understand. And where I cannot understand, keep me believing.
> Deliver me from the pride that will trust nothing it has not proved, and from the laziness that will not think at all.
> Father, Son and Holy Spirit, one God, You are far beyond my mind. Yet You have made Yourself known.
> Lead me by faith, grow me in understanding, and bring me at last to sight. Amen.

# My Weight Is My Love

> Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life. PROVERBS 4:23

Near the end of the Confessions, reflecting on the Spirit who moved upon the waters at creation, Augustine pauses on an image from the physics of his day. Every body, the ancients believed, is drawn by its weight towards its proper place. A stone falls; fire rises. Oil poured under water rises to the top; water poured on oil sinks beneath it. “They are urged by their own weights to seek their own places,” he writes. “When out of their order, they are restless; restored to order, they are at rest.” And then he turns the image on himself: “My weight, is my love; thereby am I borne, whithersoever I am borne.”

## We are what we love

It is one of his most important insights. Human beings are not chiefly defined by what we think, or even by what we do, but by what we love. Love is the weight of the soul. It pulls us, and we move where it pulls. Two people may hold the same opinions and live utterly different lives, because their loves are different. And two people may disagree about many things and yet be heading in the same direction, because at bottom they love the same God.

> For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also. MATTHEW 6:21

This means that the Christian life cannot be reduced to right belief, necessary as that is, or to right behaviour, necessary as that is. It is a matter of the heart’s direction. The question God asks of each of us is the question the risen Christ asked Peter: lovest thou me? Augustine says the same in the Enchiridion. When we ask whether a man is good, “one does not ask what he believes, or what he hopes, but what he loves.”

## Love set in order

But Augustine did not think that the problem with sinners is that they love too much. The problem is that they love in the wrong order. God made a world full of good things, and every one of them is worth loving in its place. The trouble begins when we love a lesser good more than a greater one — when we love money more than people, people more than God, ourselves more than all. In The City of God he gives a brief definition of virtue that has become famous: it is the right ordering of love. A good life is one in which each thing is loved as much as it deserves, no more and no less, and God above all.

He found this, as he read it, in the Song of Songs, where the bride prays that the Lord would set love in order within her. The King James Version renders the verse differently, but the thought is everywhere in Scripture, and nowhere more plainly than in the words of Christ.

> Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. MATTHEW 22:37–39

Notice the order. God first, with everything. Then the neighbour, as oneself. A love for family that crowds out God is disordered, however admirable it looks. So is a love for God that has no room for the neighbour.

> He that loveth father or mother more than me is not worthy of me: and he that loveth son or daughter more than me is not worthy of me. MATTHEW 10:37

## Enjoying and using

In On Christian Doctrine Augustine develops this through a distinction that has sometimes been misunderstood. Some things, he says, are to be enjoyed — loved for their own sake, rested in. Other things are to be used — loved for the sake of something beyond them. Only God is to be enjoyed in the full sense; everything else is to be loved in relation to Him. It can sound cold, as though our friends and families were mere instruments. That is not what he meant. He meant that we love people best when we love them in God and for God, wanting for them what God wants, and not asking them to be what only God can be. A husband who demands that his wife be his heaven will crush her. A husband who loves her in God can love her without idolatry.

> Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not highminded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy. 1 TIMOTHY 6:17

It must be admitted that the language of using people can mislead a modern reader, and it should never be taken as permission to treat anyone as a means to an end. The heart of his point stands: all things find their right place only when God has His.

## Love, and do what you will

This is the setting for the saying from his homilies on the First Epistle of John for which he is often quoted: love, and do what you will. It is frequently misread as a licence. He meant the opposite. If the root of your life is the love of God, then what grows from it will be good; whether you speak or keep silent, correct or spare, let it all come from love. The saying is not a loosening of the law but a going to its root.

> Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law. ROMANS 13:10

## The Gift that reorders love

Here we come back to grace. If love is the weight of the soul, we cannot change it by an act of will, any more than a stone can decide to rise. Our loves must be changed from outside. That is why Augustine, reflecting on his weight, speaks immediately of God’s Gift. “We are inflamed, by Thy Gift we are kindled; and are carried upwards,” he writes. The Gift is the Holy Spirit, who pours the love of God into our hearts and gives the soul a new weight, pulling it upward towards home.

> Because the love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us. ROMANS 5:5

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Look at how you spent your time, money and attention last week. What do they reveal about the actual order of your loves?
2. Name one good thing you love more than it deserves. Pray that God would put it back in its place without taking it from you.
3. Is there someone you are asking to be your heaven? Release them from that weight by asking God to be your first love.
4. Before a difficult conversation this week, examine whether what you intend to say comes from love. If not, wait.
5. Pray daily for the Holy Spirit to shed the love of God abroad in your heart, since you cannot reorder your loves yourself.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, my love is my weight, and I have been carried by it into many places far from You.
> I have loved good things in the wrong order, and asked creatures to be what only You can be.
> Set love in order within me. Be first in my heart, and let every other love find its right place beneath You.
> Kindle me with Your Gift, the Holy Spirit, and carry me upward to You. Amen.

# Two Loves, Two Cities

> Glorious things are spoken of thee, O city of God. PSALM 87:3

In August 410 the unthinkable happened. An army of Goths under their king Alaric entered the city of Rome and sacked it for three days. Rome had not fallen to a foreign enemy in some eight hundred years. It was not the capital of the empire any longer in practice, but it was its heart and symbol, the Eternal City. Refugees poured across the sea to Africa, some of them to Hippo, with terrible stories. And an old accusation was heard again. Rome had prospered, pagans said, while she worshipped her ancient gods. Now that she had become Christian, and the altars were deserted, she had fallen. The Christians were to blame.

## A book for a falling world

Augustine’s answer took him thirteen years and more, and it became The City of God, twenty-two books long, his largest work. He began by answering the pagans on their own ground: Rome had suffered disasters long before Christ, her gods had never protected her, and her glory had always been stained with cruelty and pride. He is remembered for asking what kingdoms are, if justice is taken away, but great robberies. But he soon went far beyond a defence of Christianity. He set out to tell the story of the whole world, from creation to the last judgment, as the story of two cities.

> For here have we no continuing city, but we seek one to come. HEBREWS 13:14

## Two loves have made two cities

The heart of the book is a single sentence in its fourteenth book, which may be summarised this way: two loves have made two cities. The love of self, reaching even to contempt of God, has made the earthly city. The love of God, reaching even to contempt of self, has made the heavenly city. The one glories in itself; the other glories in the Lord. The one seeks glory from men; the other finds its greatest glory in God, the witness of its conscience.

This is the same insight we met in the last chapter, now written across the whole of history. What makes a people is what it loves. The two cities are not two places on a map. They are not simply church and state, though they are sometimes confused with them. They are two communities defined by the direction of their love, running together through every nation and every age, from Cain and Abel until the end.

> For men shall be lovers of their own selves, covetous, boasters, proud, blasphemers, disobedient to parents, unthankful, unholy. 2 TIMOTHY 3:2

> For he looked for a city which hath foundations, whose builder and maker is God. HEBREWS 11:10

## Mingled until the harvest

For now, the two cities are tangled together. Augustine insisted on this against those who wanted a pure church. Not everyone in the visible church belongs to the city of God, and some who seem to be outside it will one day be found within. The wheat and the tares grow in the same field, and only the Lord of the harvest can separate them.

> Let both grow together until the harvest: and in the time of harvest I will say to the reapers, Gather ye together first the tares, and bind them in bundles to burn them: but gather the wheat into my barn. MATTHEW 13:30

The separation will come. The Enchiridion describes it plainly: after the resurrection and the final judgment “there shall be two kingdoms, each with its own distinct boundaries, the one Christ’s, the other the devil’s.” Until then, believers live as pilgrims. They are citizens of the heavenly city, living for a time as resident aliens in the earthly one.

> Dearly beloved, I beseech you as strangers and pilgrims, abstain from fleshly lusts, which war against the soul. 1 PETER 2:11

## Using the peace of Babylon

But pilgrims are not to despise the city they pass through. One of the wisest parts of The City of God, in its nineteenth book, explains how the two cities share the things of this life. Both need food and shelter, order and peace. The earthly city seeks an earthly peace, and the heavenly city, while it is on pilgrimage, makes use of that peace and is glad of it. Christians obey laws, pay taxes, serve the common good, and pray for their rulers, not because they expect the earthly city to save them but because God commands them to seek its welfare while they sojourn in it. Augustine reached for the words Jeremiah sent to the exiles in Babylon.

> And seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it: for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace. JEREMIAH 29:7

This is a balanced and enduring vision. It forbids Christians to despise the world and withdraw from it. It equally forbids them to put their hope in any political order, any party, any empire or nation. Rome was not the city of God. Neither is any other.

## When empires fall

The sack of Rome shook many believers, as the fall of familiar institutions shakes believers today. Augustine’s great book is a word to all such moments. Earthly cities rise and fall; this is their nature. The city of God does not fall. Augustine lived to see his own province invaded, and died in a besieged city. He did not pretend it did not matter. But he had set his hope where no army could reach.

> And I John saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down from God out of heaven, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband. REVELATION 21:2

A word of caution belongs here. In the centuries after Augustine, rulers and churchmen sometimes used his ideas to identify the church’s earthly power with the city of God and to claim that power over nations. That was not his teaching; he refused to equate any earthly institution, even the visible church, with the heavenly city. Read him for what he said, not for what others made of him.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask which city your daily loves are building. Where does your love of self shade into contempt of God?
2. Identify one way you have put your hope in a political cause, leader or nation. Give that hope back to God.
3. Pray this week by name for your local and national rulers, and look for one practical way to seek the welfare of your town.
4. When news of decline or disaster unsettles you, read Hebrews 11:10–16 and remind yourself which city you belong to.
5. Resist the urge to sort people too quickly into wheat and tares. Leave the harvest to the Lord.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, glorious things are spoken of Your city, and I long to be found among its citizens.
> Turn my love from myself to You, that I may build what lasts and not what falls.
> Teach me to seek the peace of the place where You have set me, to pray for its rulers and serve its people, without making it my hope.
> And when the cities of this world shake, keep my eyes on the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God. Amen.

# Love the Key to Scripture

> Now the end of the commandment is charity out of a pure heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned. 1 TIMOTHY 1:5

The young Augustine did not think much of the Bible. When Cicero’s Hortensius had set him longing for wisdom, he turned to the Scriptures to see what they offered, and was disappointed. They seemed to him, he says in Book III of the Confessions, unworthy to be compared to “the stateliness of Tully” — Tully being Cicero. The style was plain, the stories crude, the Latin of the old translation rough. But he adds at once that the fault was his. His swelling pride shrank from their lowliness, and his sharp wit could not pierce their inner meaning. They were a book that would grow up with a little one, and he disdained to be a little one.

## Learning to read

The man who later preached through the Psalms and the Gospel of John and wrote commentary after commentary on Genesis had to be taught to read Scripture. His first teacher was Ambrose. At Milan, Augustine says, he heard Ambrose again and again commend to the people the rule that the letter killeth, but the Spirit giveth life, drawing aside the veil from passages that, taken woodenly, had seemed absurd. The Manichees had mocked the Old Testament. Ambrose showed Augustine that it was deeper than its mockers.

> Who also hath made us able ministers of the new testament; not of the letter, but of the spirit: for the letter killeth, but the spirit giveth life. 2 CORINTHIANS 3:6

He also watched Ambrose read. In Book VI he gives a small portrait that has fascinated readers ever since: when Ambrose read, “his eye glided over the pages, and his heart searched out the sense, but his voice and tongue were at rest.” People would come to his room and find him reading in silence, and sit and watch, not daring to disturb him. Here was a man for whom the Bible was not a text to perform but a word to ponder.

## The rule of love

Years later, as a bishop, Augustine wrote a handbook on how to understand and teach Scripture. On Christian Doctrine is one of the most influential books on interpretation ever written, and at its heart is one simple principle. The whole of Scripture, he says, aims at love: the love of God for His own sake, and the love of our neighbour for God’s sake.

> On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets. MATTHEW 22:40

From this he draws a bold conclusion. Anyone who thinks he has understood the Scriptures, or any part of them, but whose understanding does not build up this double love of God and neighbour, has not yet understood them as he ought. And anyone who draws from a passage a meaning that builds up love, even if it is not what the writer meant, is not dangerously deceived — though he should be corrected, as a traveller who has left the road, even if he has reached the right place across the fields, needs setting back on the road before he wanders further.

It is a remarkable rule, and it cuts two ways. It rebukes the scholar whose learning has made him proud and cold; however accurate his readings, if they do not lead him to love God and people more, he has missed the point of the book. And it comforts the humble believer who reads with little learning but much love; the Spirit who wrote the Scriptures is not stingy with those who come to them to love Him better.

> Knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth. 1 CORINTHIANS 8:1

## Charity among interpreters

Augustine practised what he taught. In Book XII of the Confessions, working through the first verses of Genesis, he surveys several possible meanings and admits that he cannot be certain which one Moses intended. Rather than attack those who differ, he pleads for peace. “Let us not then be puffed up for one against another, above that which is written,” he says: let us love the Lord our God with all our heart and our neighbour as ourselves. It would be foolish, he says, to quarrel so bitterly over the meaning of the words as “with pernicious contentions to offend charity itself, for whose sake he spake every thing, whose words we go about to expound.”

That sentence deserves to be written over the door of every seminary and every church Bible study. We are tempted to fight hardest over Scripture precisely where Scripture most commands us to love. Augustine did not believe that every interpretation was as good as every other; he argued fiercely when the gospel was at stake. But he knew the difference between the essentials of faith and the places where good believers read differently, and he refused to break charity over the second.

## Christ in all the Scriptures

Augustine also taught that the whole Bible speaks of Christ. In one famous formula of his, the New Testament lies hidden in the Old, and the Old is laid open in the New. He found Christ in the Psalms, in the patriarchs, in the tabernacle, in the story of creation. Some of his allegories strain modern readers, and not all of them should be followed. But the conviction behind them is the Lord’s own.

> And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself. LUKE 24:27

## Humility in reading

Above all, Augustine read as a pray-er. The Confessions is full of Scripture, woven so closely into his own words that it is often hard to tell where the Bible ends and Augustine begins. That is what a lifetime of loving reading does. The words of God become the words in which we think and pray.

> Open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law. PSALM 119:18

He knew too that the plainness he had once despised was a mercy. The Scriptures are lowly in access and lofty in their depths, he says, like a door low enough for a child and a house too great for any scholar to fill. They humble the proud and feed the humble, which is exactly what they were meant to do.

> And though I have the gift of prophecy, and understand all mysteries, and all knowledge; and though I have all faith, so that I could remove mountains, and have not charity, I am nothing. 1 CORINTHIANS 13:2

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Before you read the Bible this week, pray that it would make you love God and your neighbour more — and afterwards ask whether it has.
2. Is there a part of Scripture you have despised as dull or crude? Read it slowly this month and ask God to show you its depth.
3. Think of a disagreement over the Bible in which you have been more concerned to win than to love. Seek to put it right.
4. Try reading a short passage slowly and silently, as Ambrose did, letting your heart search out the sense.
5. Learn by heart one verse this week, so that Scripture begins to become the language of your prayers.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You have given me Your Word, and I have sometimes read it proudly and coldly, and sometimes not at all.
> Open my eyes to behold wondrous things in it. Humble me, that I may enter by its lowly door.
> Let everything I learn from it make me love You more and my neighbour better, and keep me from breaking charity over the words You gave to teach it.
> Show me Christ in all the Scriptures, until my heart burns within me. Amen.

# Faith, Hope and Love

> And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity. 1 CORINTHIANS 13:13

Some time around 421, an elderly and very busy bishop received a letter from a layman named Laurentius, asking for a short book he could keep always beside him. What should be a man’s chief aim in life? What should he chiefly avoid? How far does reason support religion? What is the beginning of it all, and the end? What is the sum of the whole body of doctrine? It was the kind of letter any pastor receives: a sincere believer wanting the whole faith in a nutshell. Augustine answered it with the Enchiridion, which means handbook — “that is, one that can be carried in the hand,” he says with a smile, “not one to load your shelves.” It is the nearest thing he ever wrote to a summary of what he believed, and it is in the Ochorus library.

## The whole faith in three words

His answer to all of Laurentius’s questions is disarmingly simple. God is to be worshipped, he says, with faith, hope and love. Know what to believe, what to hope for and what to love, and you have the whole of religion. Everything else is commentary. And he gives Laurentius two texts that every Christian already has by heart: the Creed, for faith, and the Lord’s Prayer, for hope and love.

“The true wisdom of man is piety,” Augustine begins, and he finds it in the book of Job.

> Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom; and to depart from evil is understanding. JOB 28:28

He will not pretend that a short book can do what only a changed heart can. Proper instruction in the faith, he says, comes not from having a short treatise put into one’s hands, but from having a great zeal kindled in one’s heart. The handbook is a map, not a journey.

## How the three hold together

Augustine is careful to show how the three graces differ and yet depend on one another. Faith can have for its object things past, present and future, and things good and evil; we believe that Christ died, that He reigns, that He will come, and we believe in the punishment of the wicked, though we do not hope for it. Hope is only of good things, only of things future, and only of things that concern the one who hopes. Love embraces both God and neighbour.

> For we are saved by hope: but hope that is seen is not hope: for what a man seeth, why doth he yet hope for? But if we hope for that we see not, then do we with patience wait for it. ROMANS 8:24–25

And yet they cannot be pulled apart. Even the devils believe, he notes, but they have neither hope nor love, and so their belief only makes them tremble. The faith Paul commends is the faith that works by love. “Wherefore there is no love without hope, no hope without love, and neither love nor hope without faith.”

> For in Jesus Christ neither circumcision availeth any thing, nor uncircumcision; but faith which worketh by love. GALATIANS 5:6

He sums up their relationship in one lovely phrase, reflecting on the prophet’s promise that whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved, and Paul’s question, how shall they call on Him in whom they have not believed? “In these two you have those three graces exemplified: faith believes, hope and love pray.” Faith stands behind the Creed; hope and love kneel in the Lord’s Prayer. And since no one prays to a God he does not believe in, faith also prays.

## Christ the foundation

For all its simplicity, the Enchiridion is not vague. Its long central section, the part on faith, walks through the Creed and covers creation and the goodness of all things, evil as the absence of good, the fall, grace, the incarnation, Christ the Mediator, the Holy Spirit and the church, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection and the judgment. We have met much of it already in this book. And Augustine names the foundation of it all at the very start.

> For other foundation can no man lay than that is laid, which is Jesus Christ. 1 CORINTHIANS 3:11

He also gives Laurentius the shape of the whole Christian life: “we begin in faith, and are made perfect by sight.” Near the end he traces four stages of the soul — living by the flesh without struggle, then under the law and defeated by sin, then under grace and fighting by the power of love, and finally at rest in perfect peace — and sees the same four stages in the history of God’s people. We live now in the third: not yet at rest, but no longer alone in the fight.

## Hope in the Lord’s Prayer

The section on hope is short, for Augustine says everything that belongs to hope is contained in the Lord’s Prayer. He walks through its petitions, noticing that the first three ask for things that will be perfected in eternity, while the last four concern our needs in this life.

> After this manner therefore pray ye: Our Father which art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven. MATTHEW 6:9–10

There is great practical wisdom in this. If you want to know what a Christian may hope for, look at what Christ taught us to pray for. Hope is not wishful thinking about anything we happen to want. It is confident expectation of what God has promised, shaped and disciplined by prayer.

## The greatest of these

The last section is on love, and it is where the book finds its end. All the commandments, Augustine says, are embraced in love, and he quotes Paul: the end of the commandment is charity. Whatever is done from fear of punishment or any other low motive, and not from love of God and neighbour, is not yet done as it ought to be. And love is not something we can generate. It is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Spirit. Without that gift, the law can command, but it cannot help.

> And this commandment have we from him, That he who loveth God love his brother also. 1 JOHN 4:21

He ends characteristically. He has written the book, he tells Laurentius, loving him very much as one of the members of Christ’s body. It is for Laurentius to judge whether to call it a handbook, or to use it as one. And then, with the dry humour of a man who has written a longer answer than he meant to: “May its value be equal to its length.”

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Try to write down, in a paragraph each, what you believe, what you hope for and what you love. What does the exercise reveal?
2. Recite the Apostles’ Creed slowly each morning this week, pausing on each line to affirm it as your own.
3. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly each evening, letting it correct and shape what you hope for.
4. Ask whether your faith is working by love. Choose one concrete act of love this week that flows from what you believe.
5. If you know a young believer who wants the faith in a nutshell, sit down with them and go through the Creed and the Lord’s Prayer together.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You have given me faith to believe, hope to pray, and love to fulfil Your law.
> Strengthen my faith in all You have revealed in Your Son. Discipline my hope by the prayer He taught me. Kindle love in me by Your Spirit.
> Let my faith never be the faith of devils that tremble, but faith that works by love.
> Begin in me by faith what You will perfect by sight, and make love the end of all I do. Amen.

# His Friend in Thee

> A friend loveth at all times, and a brother is born for adversity. PROVERBS 17:17

Augustine was never alone if he could help it. The Confessions is crowded with friends — Alypius, who followed him from Thagaste to Carthage to Milan and into the garden; Nebridius, who left home and fortune to seek wisdom with him; the circle who talked through the winter at Cassiciacum; the clergy who lived with him in common at Hippo. He needed friends the way some people need solitude. And it was through a friend that he first learned how dangerous a gift love can be, and through God that he learned how to love a friend rightly.

## A friend lost

In Book IV he tells of a young man from his home town, a friend since boyhood, who had become dearer to him than anyone in the year after Augustine returned to teach at Thagaste. Augustine had drawn him into the Manichees. The friend fell gravely ill with fever, and while he lay unconscious his family had him baptised. When he recovered a little, Augustine tried to joke with him about the baptism he had received without knowing it. The friend drew back from him as from an enemy, and told him that if he wanted to remain his friend he must stop talking that way. Augustine was astonished, and decided to wait until his friend was well again. A few days later, in Augustine’s absence, the fever returned, and the young man died.

What follows is one of the most piercing descriptions of grief ever written. “At this grief my heart was utterly darkened; and whatever I beheld was death.” His home town became a torment, his father’s house a strange unhappiness. His eyes looked for his friend everywhere, and every place was hateful because it did not contain him. “I became a great riddle to myself,” he says, and he asked his soul why it was so sad and so disquieted, and it had no answer.

> Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him for the help of his countenance. PSALM 42:5

He quotes an old poet who called his friend half of his soul, and says that he felt his soul and his friend’s had been one soul in two bodies. Years later, in the Retractations, he judged that line more a piece of rhetoric than a sober confession. But the grief was real, and anyone who has lost someone they loved will recognise every sentence.

## Loving the mortal as if immortal

What Augustine learned from this grief is the heart of the chapter. Why, he asks, had the sorrow gone so deep? Because, he says, “I had poured out my soul upon the dust, in loving one that must die, as if he would never die.” The fault was not in loving his friend. The fault was in loving him as if he were God — as if he could not be lost, as if he could bear the whole weight of Augustine’s heart.

> Jesus wept. JOHN 11:35

Augustine is not saying that we should love our friends less, or protect ourselves from grief by holding people at arm’s length. The Lord Himself wept at the grave of His friend, knowing He was about to raise him. Augustine is saying that there is only one love that cannot be lost, and every other love is safe only when it rests in that one. And so he writes one of his most beautiful sentences: “Blessed whoso loveth Thee, and his friend in Thee, and his enemy for Thee. For he alone loses none dear to him, to whom all are dear in Him who cannot be lost.”

## The joys of friendship

It would be quite wrong to read Augustine as suspicious of friendship. Few ancient writers describe its pleasures so tenderly. In the same book he lists what drew him to his friends: “to talk and jest together, to do kind offices by turns; to read together honied books; to play the fool or be earnest together; to dissent at times without discontent, as a man might with his own self.” He goes on — to teach and to learn from one another, to long for the absent and welcome them home with joy. These are not the pleasures of a man who thought people were distractions from God. They are the pleasures of a man who knew friendship to be one of God’s best gifts.

> Two are better than one; because they have a good reward for their labour. For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow: but woe to him that is alone when he falleth; for he hath not another to help him up. ECCLESIASTES 4:9–10

After his conversion, friendship did not end; it was baptised. Alypius was converted in the same hour in the garden, taking the next verse of Romans for himself. They were baptised together. Alypius became bishop of their home town, Thagaste, and the two laboured together for the African church for decades. At Hippo, Augustine lived with his clergy in a common household, sharing meals, prayer and possessions. The Rule that bears his name, a short guide to common life, begins with the command that those who live together should be of one mind and one heart in God.

> And the multitude of them that believed were of one heart and of one soul. ACTS 4:32

## Grieving as a Christian

Augustine’s grief at his mother’s death, told in Book IX, is very different from his grief for his friend. He held back his tears at her funeral, thinking it unfitting to weep for one who had died so well. But later, alone, remembering her, he let the tears come, and his heart found rest in them, he says, because they were poured out in God’s hearing. He had learned not to grieve less, but to grieve with hope.

> But I would not have you to be ignorant, brethren, concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope. 1 THESSALONIANS 4:13

Christian grief is still grief. It still darkens the heart and makes familiar places strange. But it is grief that has somewhere to go. It is poured out in the ears of the One who cannot be lost, in whom all who are His are kept.

> Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends. JOHN 15:13

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Thank God this week, by name, for three friends who have been His gift to you. Tell one of them so.
2. Is there someone you are loving as if they could never be lost? Ask God to help you love them in Him, more safely and more freely.
3. If you are grieving, let yourself weep before God, as Augustine finally did for his mother. Do not pretend.
4. Consider whether your friendships include reading, praying and speaking of God together. Suggest it to one friend this month.
5. Pray for an enemy, loving them for God’s sake, as Augustine’s sentence commands.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You have given me friends, and I thank You for every one of them.
> Forgive me where I have loved them as if they were You, and asked them to carry what only You can carry.
> Teach me to love my friends in You and my enemies for You, so that I lose none dear to me, because all are dear in You who cannot be lost.
> When I grieve, let me grieve with hope, pouring out my tears in Your hearing. Amen.

# The Church and the Sacraments

> Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it; that he might sanctify and cleanse it with the washing of water by the word. EPHESIANS 5:25–26

This is the chapter on which Augustine’s readers most often part company. Catholics read him as a bishop of the one Catholic church, devoted to its unity, its sacraments and its altar, who could say that he would not believe the gospel unless the authority of the church moved him. Protestants read him as the great teacher of grace alone, who taught Luther and Calvin how to read Paul. Both are reading the same man. An old saying among Protestant scholars puts it well: the Reformation was, in a sense, the triumph of Augustine’s doctrine of grace over Augustine’s doctrine of the church. That is a Protestant way of putting it, and a Catholic would put it differently. But it names the tension honestly, and the fairest thing this book can do is to let the whole Augustine speak.

## A mixed field

Augustine’s thinking about the church was hammered out in a bitter African quarrel. In the great persecution of the early fourth century, some clergy had handed over copies of the Scriptures to the authorities to be burned. When one of them was said to have taken part in consecrating a bishop of Carthage, a party of rigorists refused to recognise him and set up a rival church. They came to be called Donatists, after one of their leaders. By Augustine’s day they were a large and powerful church across Africa, in many towns the larger of the two. They held that they alone were the pure church, that Catholic clergy were polluted, and that sacraments given by unworthy ministers were no sacraments at all. They rebaptised Catholics who joined them.

Augustine answered with two convictions that have shaped Christian thinking ever since. The first is that the church on earth is a mixed body. It contains wheat and tares, and it always will until the harvest. The church is holy, not because every member is holy, but because Christ is holy and is making it holy.

> That he might present it to himself a glorious church, not having spot, or wrinkle, or any such thing; but that it should be holy and without blemish. EPHESIANS 5:27

The second is that the sacraments belong to Christ, not to the minister. In his homilies on John’s Gospel he makes the point unforgettably: whether Peter baptises or Judas baptises, it is Christ who baptises. The grace does not depend on the purity of the hand that pours the water. This is a great comfort to any believer who has discovered, painfully, that the minister who baptised him or taught him was not what he seemed. Christ was never absent.

> Endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. There is one body, and one Spirit, even as ye are called in one hope of your calling; one Lord, one faith, one baptism. EPHESIANS 4:3–5

## Signs and grace

Augustine gave the Western church much of its language about sacraments. A sacrament, for him, is a sacred sign: a visible thing that points to and conveys an invisible grace. He is remembered for saying, in his homilies on John, that when the word is added to the element, it becomes a sacrament — the water of baptism is not just water, because the word of Christ comes to it. In the Enchiridion he explains that baptism signifies our death to sin and our rising with Christ to new life, whatever the age of the one baptised.

> Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life. ROMANS 6:4

On the Lord’s Supper, he spoke both of receiving the body and blood of Christ and of the sacrament as a sign whose reality is the unity of Christ’s body, the church. In a famous sermon to the newly baptised he told them, in effect, that they were receiving on the altar the mystery of what they themselves were. Catholic readers have found in him a strong sense of the real presence; Reformed readers have found in him their sacramental signs. Each has something to point to, and a fair reader will admit that he did not frame the question in the terms of the sixteenth century.

> For we being many are one bread, and one body: for we are all partakers of that one bread. 1 CORINTHIANS 10:17

## Where Protestants will hesitate

There are places where Protestant readers will not follow him, and should say so without rancour. In the Enchiridion he teaches that the souls of the faithful dead may be helped by the offering of the sacrifice of the altar and by alms given on their behalf, and he raises, tentatively, the possibility that some believers pass through a kind of purgatorial fire after death — though he calls it a matter that may be inquired into and left doubtful. The Reformers rejected these teachings as lacking warrant in Scripture, and they were right to test them there. It is worth noticing, all the same, how tentative Augustine was, and that in the very same pages he insists that everything rests on the one Mediator and the free grace of God.

## Two dark pages

Two parts of his teaching on the church must be named as plainly as possible. The first concerns infants. Believing that every child is born guilty in Adam, and that baptism washes away that guilt, Augustine concluded that infants who die unbaptised are lost. He softened it as far as he felt he could: the Enchiridion says that “the mildest punishment of all” will fall upon them. But it remains a hard conclusion, and many Christians in every tradition, including later Catholic teaching, have drawn back from it, trusting the mercy of the Lord who took children in His arms.

> Suffer the little children to come unto me, and forbid them not: for of such is the kingdom of God. MARK 10:14

The second concerns coercion. For years Augustine held that the Donatists should be won by argument alone. Then, after imperial laws were passed against them and he saw towns return to Catholic unity, he changed his mind. In his letters he defended the use of fines, confiscations and exile, and he appealed to the words of the parable, compel them to come in (Luke 14:23). He pleaded with officials not to put Donatists to death, and he did not approve of cruelty. But the argument he built was used for a thousand years to justify the persecution of dissenters, and it is simply wrong. Christ does not build His church with the sword.

> Jesus answered, My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight. JOHN 18:36

## What remains

Take away what must be taken away, and a great deal remains. Augustine loved the church as a mother, bore with her imperfections, refused to leave her for a purer sect, and taught that her holiness is Christ’s. In an age of endless splits, that has something to say to every tradition.

> Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwelleth in you? 1 CORINTHIANS 3:16

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Have you been tempted to leave a church because its members are not holy enough? Weigh that against Christ’s patience with His mixed field.
2. If you were baptised or taught by someone who later failed, thank God that it was Christ who baptised you and Christ who was at work.
3. Come to the Lord’s Table next time remembering that you receive not only Christ but your belonging to His one body.
4. Where your tradition differs from Augustine’s, state the difference to yourself charitably and test it by Scripture rather than by party.
5. Examine whether you have ever wished to see those you disagree with forced into line. Repent of it, and pray for them instead.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You loved the church and gave Yourself for it, and You are making it holy though it is not holy yet.
> Keep me from despising Your people for their faults, and from trusting in the purity of any minister rather than in You.
> Forgive the church where it has tried to build Your kingdom with force, and keep me from that spirit.
> Make us one bread and one body, washed and fed by You, until You present us to Yourself without spot or wrinkle. Amen.

# Forgive Us Our Debts

> And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. MATTHEW 6:12

When Augustine came, in the Enchiridion, to the article of the Creed that follows the holy church, he paused over the order of the words. After the church comes the forgiveness of sins, and he did not think the order was an accident. “For it is by this that the Church on earth stands,” he writes. The church is not a society of people who have stopped needing mercy. It is the company of the forgiven, who go on being forgiven, and who can only live together because they do. The church at Hippo was not held up by the holiness of its members. It was held up by pardon.

## Not free from sin

Augustine begins with baptism, in which, he taught, all guilt is washed away. But he will not let anyone think that the need for forgiveness ends at the font. The rest of our life, he says, gives constant occasion for the forgiveness of sins, however far we advance in righteousness.

He draws a careful line here. “For although every crime is a sin, every sin is not a crime.” A believer’s life may, by grace, be kept free of the gross offences that scandalise the church and cut a man off from the body of Christ. It will not be kept free of sin. The word spoken in irritation, the duty left undone, the daily coldness of love — these stay with us to the end.

> If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us. 1 JOHN 1:8

There is a realism here that is a mercy to anxious consciences. Expect to need forgiveness every day, Augustine says in effect, and come for it every day.

## The daily prayer

The place where he comes for it is the Lord’s Prayer. The daily prayer of the believer, he says, takes away the small sins of daily life. Forgive us our debts is a petition no Christian outgrows; there is, he remarks drily, no want of debts to be forgiven.

But the petition has a condition, and Augustine presses it hard. We ask to be forgiven as we forgive. He calls forgiveness a kind of almsgiving — “to forgive a man who asks for pardon, is really to give alms” — and then he goes further. Of all the alms we can give, none is greater than to forgive from the heart a sin that has been committed against us. To love the one who wishes us ill, and do him good when we can, is the mark of the mature children of God.

> But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you. MATTHEW 5:44

He knows that few reach that height. The least the petition asks, he says, is that when someone who has wronged us begs our pardon, we forgive him from the heart. But he will not soften the Lord’s own warning, which follows the prayer in Matthew’s Gospel.

> For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you: but if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses. MATTHEW 6:14–15

“The man whom the thunder of this warning does not awaken is not asleep, but dead,” Augustine writes; and then, with the hope that never quite leaves him, he adds that the voice is so powerful it can awaken even the dead.

## Sins we grow used to

One of the most searching passages in the Enchiridion concerns sins that come to seem small. Some sins, however great, look trivial simply because we are used to them. They are no longer hidden; they are boasted of. He tells how, expounding Galatians some years before, he had been driven to cry out: “Woe to the sins of men! for it is only when we are not accustomed to them that we shrink from them.”

> Woe unto them that call evil good, and good evil; that put darkness for light, and light for darkness. ISAIAH 5:20

Every age has its accustomed sins, and Augustine admits that in his own day some were practised so openly that the church dared not discipline even its clergy for them. The first step towards being forgiven is to see that there is something to forgive. A conscience dulled by custom has to be woken before it can be washed.

## Mercy that makes us repent

Where does such a waking come from? Not from ourselves, Augustine says. Even repentance needs grace. Shame holds us back from humbling ourselves, because the good opinion of others gives us more pleasure than righteousness does; and so the mercy of God is needed not only when a man repents, but to bring him to repentance at all. His proof is Peter in the high priest’s courtyard.

> And the Lord turned, and looked upon Peter. And Peter remembered the word of the Lord, how he had said unto him, Before the cock crow, thou shalt deny me thrice. And Peter went out, and wept bitterly. LUKE 22:61–62

Peter did not turn first. The Lord turned, and looked. Every true repentance begins with that look. And because it begins with God, no one who truly repents need ever despair. However great the sin, Augustine says, the mercy of God is never to be despaired of by those who truly repent; and in weighing repentance we should look less at the length of time than at the depth of sorrow, for a broken and contrite heart God does not despise.

> The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit: a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise. PSALM 51:17

The one sin that cannot be forgiven, on his reading, is the refusal of forgiveness itself: to despise God’s mercy, not believing that sins are forgiven, and to persist in that hardness to the last day of one’s life. The unpardonable sin is not a slip that a tender conscience falls into unawares. It is a settled no to pardon.

## Where to read with care

Protestant readers will find some things here to question. Augustine speaks of alms as making satisfaction for sins, of times of penance fixed by the church, and of sins being forgiven only within the church. The Reformers, reading Scripture, insisted that the one satisfaction for sin is Christ’s own sacrifice, and that works of mercy are the fruit of forgiveness, never its price. Augustine himself, it should be said, was fierce against anyone who thought alms could buy leave to go on sinning; the life must be changed, he says, and God has given no man licence to sin. What no reader should miss is the heart of it: the church lives by pardon, pardon is daily, and those who receive it must give it.

> And be ye kind one to another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God for Christ’s sake hath forgiven you. EPHESIANS 4:32

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Pray the Lord’s Prayer slowly each day this week, and at the words forgive us our debts, name before God the sins of that day.
2. Is there someone who has asked your pardon and not truly received it? Forgive them from the heart, and tell them so.
3. Name one sin that has come to seem small to you because you, or those around you, are used to it. Ask God to let you see it as He does.
4. If you have despaired of being forgiven, read Luke 22:54–62 and notice who turned first.
5. Examine whether you have treated any good work — giving, serving, praying — as a payment for sin. Thank God that Christ has paid, and give freely.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I come to You as one who needs forgiving every day, and I thank You that there is forgiveness with You.
> Forgive me my debts, the ones I see and the ones I have grown used to, and give me a heart that forgives as freely as I am forgiven.
> When I will not turn, turn and look on me, as You looked on Peter, and lead me to repent.
> Keep Your church standing on Your pardon, not on our goodness, for the sake of Jesus Christ, who has paid what we could never pay. Amen.

# The Resurrection of the Flesh

> Jesus said unto her, I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live. JOHN 11:25

The Enchiridion follows the Creed, and after the forgiveness of sins comes the resurrection of the body. Augustine admits that he cannot treat it briefly and still answer every question people ask. But on the main point he is not in the least uncertain. He does not mean a resurrection like that of Lazarus, who came back to life for a time and died again, but a resurrection to eternal life, as the body of Christ Himself rose. And that the bodies of all people, those who have died and those who will die, shall be raised, “no Christian ought to have the shadow of a doubt.”

## Death is an enemy

This was not a small thing for Augustine to say. He had been formed by the Platonists, for whom the body was at best a lodging and at worst a prison, and death a release of the soul into its proper freedom. Who would want the prison back? Augustine learned a different account from the Scriptures. The body is God’s good work. Death, the tearing of soul from body, is not a natural release but a penalty. Neither the first death, he says, when the soul is forced to leave the body, nor the second death, would ever have been inflicted if no one had sinned.

> For since by man came death, by man came also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:21–22

So death may be named honestly as an enemy. The Christian need not pretend it is beautiful. It is the last enemy, and it will be destroyed.

> The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:26

## Nothing is far to God

Augustine had seen this faith lived out in his mother. Monica had long planned to be buried in Africa beside her husband. But at Ostia, in her last days, her friends asked whether she was not afraid to leave her body so far from her own city. Her answer is one of the quiet glories of the Confessions: “Nothing is far to God; nor was it to be feared lest at the end of the world, He should not recognise whence He were to raise me up.” She no longer cared where she lay, because she knew who would raise her.

When she died, his friend Euodius took up the Psalter, and the whole house sang, I will sing of mercy and judgments to Thee, O Lord. Years later, writing of the resurrection in the Enchiridion, Augustine returned to that verse: only on that day, he says, shall the saints see fully what it means.

> I will sing of mercy and judgment: unto thee, O LORD, will I sing. PSALM 101:1

## The great Artist

Augustine’s people asked what people still ask. What of a body burned, or lost at sea, or turned to dust and scattered? He answers with an illustration. If a statue of some metal were melted, or broken into dust, a sculptor could make it again from the same metal, and it would not matter which particle went into which part, so long as the whole was restored. So, he says, “God, the Artificer of marvellous and unspeakable power,” will restore our bodies, and nothing of them will be lost to Him. The great Artist, he says, will see that nothing is unbecoming or out of place.

> Who shall change our vile body, that it may be fashioned like unto his glorious body, according to the working whereby he is able even to subdue all things unto himself. PHILIPPIANS 3:21

Some of this is speculation. But under it lie two convictions that are pure comfort. The first is that we shall be ourselves. He thinks it likely that each will keep his own features and a recognisable likeness to his former self, and that whatever differences remain will be like “the voices that make up a full harmony.” The second is that what is broken will be mended. Whatever time has marred, he says, will be renewed, and whatever time never had the chance to complete will be completed. Of children who died before birth he will not affirm more than he knows; but he cannot see, he says, why anyone who has once lived should be denied a share in the resurrection.

## A body, not a ghost

Paul calls it a spiritual body, and Augustine guards the phrase. The bodies of the saints will be free from every defect and corruption, from all heaviness and hindrance; they will be called spiritual, he says, “though undoubtedly they shall be bodies and not spirits.” A spiritual body is not a body made of spirit, but one wholly at the service of the spirit. Even after the resurrection, he reminds us, the body of Christ was called flesh.

> It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: it is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power: it is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:42–44

> Behold my hands and my feet, that it is I myself: handle me, and see; for a spirit hath not flesh and bones, as ye see me have. LUKE 24:39

Augustine is often blamed, with some reason, for a suspicion of the body that has troubled the church. Here he is at his most biblical. The body is not the enemy. It is to be raised, glorified and brought into perfect harmony with the spirit, so that the war he knew so well will be over. The gospel does not promise to free us from our bodies. It promises to give them back, made new.

## Mercy and judgment

Augustine does not hide the other side. The lost, too, will rise, each with his own body, to what Scripture calls the second death.

> Marvel not at this: for the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice, and shall come forth; they that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation. JOHN 5:28–29

In the following pages he presses on into the mysteries of election, where the reader will need the care urged in the chapter on grace. But his summary of what the saints will understand on that day is one no believer should quarrel with: “it is only of unmerited mercy that any is redeemed, and only in well-merited judgment that any is condemned.” No one in the resurrection of life will think that he earned his place there.

What should this hope do to us now? Paul’s answer is not speculation but work.

> Therefore, my beloved brethren, be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:58

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Read 1 Corinthians 15 in one sitting this week, and mark every phrase that speaks of the body.
2. Ask yourself whether you have thought of heaven as an escape from the body rather than the raising of it. How would the resurrection change the way you treat your body now?
3. If you have buried someone who died in Christ, give thanks by name for the day God will raise them, and let Monica’s words, nothing is far to God, comfort you.
4. If you live with a body that is broken or in pain, bring it to God in prayer as a body He has promised to renew.
5. Choose one task in the work of the Lord that you have let go slack, and take it up again this week, knowing that your labour is not in vain.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You are the resurrection and the life, and You were raised in the body that was nailed to the cross.
> Thank You that nothing is far from You, and that not one of those who sleep in You is lost to Your sight.
> Teach me to honour this body You have made and will raise, and to name death honestly as the enemy You have conquered.
> Keep me stedfast in Your work until the day You change this lowly body to be like Your glorious body, and I sing of mercy and judgment to You for ever. Amen.

# The Sabbath Which Hath No Evening

> And on the seventh day God ended his work which he had made; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had made. GENESIS 2:2

The Confessions begins with a restless heart and ends with rest. After thirteen books — the story of a life, the long search through memory, the puzzle of time, the slow meditation on the days of creation — Augustine comes at last to the seventh day. Every other day in Genesis, he notices, has an evening and a morning. The seventh day has no evening. And he turns that detail into a prayer: “O Lord God, give peace unto us: (for Thou hast given us all things;) the peace of rest, the peace of the Sabbath, which hath no evening.” The first paragraph of the book said that our hearts are restless until they rest in God. The last pages show where that rest is finally found.

## The rest that remains

Augustine reads the Sabbath of creation as a promise. God rested after His works, not because He was tired, but to tell us beforehand that we too, after our works in this life, “shall rest in Thee also in the Sabbath of eternal life.” All the good things of this world, he says, have their morning and their evening; they come to be and pass away. The eternal rest does not pass away.

> There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. For he that is entered into his rest, he also hath ceased from his own works, as God did from his. HEBREWS 4:9–10

And then he adds something unexpected. “For then shalt Thou rest in us, as now Thou workest in us.” Our rest in God will be God’s rest in us. The whole of the Christian life, on this view, is God working in us now, so that at the end He may rest in us for ever. It is a remarkable thought: heaven is not only our home in God, but God at home in us.

## A foretaste at a window

Augustine had tasted something of this once, and he never forgot it. In Book IX he describes the evening at Ostia, shortly before his mother died, when the two of them stood at a window looking into a garden and talked together of what the eternal life of the saints would be. Their thoughts rose step by step above all bodily things, above sun and moon and stars. “We came to our own minds, and went beyond them,” he says, towards the Wisdom by whom all things are made. And for a moment, he says, “we slightly touched on her with the whole effort of our heart.” Then they sighed, and came back to the sound of their own voices, to words that have a beginning and an end.

He asks what it would be like if that moment were not taken away — if all the noise of the world fell silent, and every voice that passes, and God Himself spoke, not through anything created, but directly, and we heard Him and were caught up into joy. Would not that be, he asks, what the Lord meant when He said, Enter into thy Master’s joy?

> His lord said unto him, Well done, thou good and faithful servant: thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things: enter thou into the joy of thy lord. MATTHEW 25:21

## Seeing God

At the heart of Augustine’s hope was the vision of God. The Enchiridion says that we begin in faith and are made perfect by sight, and that the pure in heart will see that unspeakable beauty, the full vision of which is supreme happiness. Everything else in heaven — the absence of sorrow, the reunion with those we love, the resurrection of the body — flows from this. We shall see Him.

> Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God. MATTHEW 5:8

> Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is. 1 JOHN 3:2

In the last chapter of The City of God he gathers it all up in words that have been remembered ever since: there we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise. That is what shall be in the end without end. For what other end do we have, he asks, but to reach the kingdom that has no end?

## Free at last

The Enchiridion adds two things that ought to comfort any struggling believer. The first is that the inner war will be over. In the resurrection, Augustine says, no part of our nature will be at odds with another, “but as we shall be free from enemies without, so we shall not have ourselves for enemies within.” The man who described his two wills tearing his soul apart in the garden at Milan looked forward, above all, to being whole.

The second concerns freedom. Some fear that in heaven, unable to sin, we shall be less free. Augustine says the opposite. In that life it will not be in our power to will evil, “and yet this will constitute no restriction on the freedom of his will. On the contrary, his will shall be much freer when it shall be wholly impossible for him to be the slave of sin.” True freedom is not the ability to choose ruin. It is the settled delight of a will that loves only good. God is supremely free, and He cannot sin.

> And they shall see his face; and his name shall be in their foreheads. And there shall be no night there. REVELATION 22:4–5

## Living towards the Sabbath

The hope of this rest is not an escape from present duty. Augustine worked harder than almost anyone in his age. He preached, judged, wrote and cared for his people until the Vandals were at the gates. But he worked as a man who knew where the work was going. Every good work of ours, he says at the close of the Confessions, is God’s gift, and after it we hope to rest in His great hallowing. And he ends the book not with an argument but with an invitation to ask, seek and knock: what no man or angel can teach another, God alone will give to those who ask Him.

> As for me, I will behold thy face in righteousness: I shall be satisfied, when I awake, with thy likeness. PSALM 17:15

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Keep one day this week as a real Sabbath — a day of worship and rest — and treat it as a foretaste of the rest to come.
2. Think about what you most look forward to in heaven. Is it God Himself? Ask Him to make it so.
3. When the inner war with sin wearies you, remind yourself of the day when you will not have yourself for an enemy within.
4. Read Revelation 21:1–7 and 22:1–5 slowly, and give thanks for what is promised.
5. Consider whether your hope of heaven makes you idle or faithful. Choose one task you will do this week as work offered towards that rest.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, give us peace, the peace of rest, the peace of the Sabbath which has no evening.
> Work in me now, that You may rest in me for ever. Make me pure in heart, that I may see You.
> When I am weary of the war within, remind me that the day is coming when I shall be whole and free.
> Bring me at last to the place where we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise, in the end that has no end. Amen.

# Conclusion

> For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever. Amen. ROMANS 11:36

Twenty chapters may give the impression that Augustine taught many things. In one sense he did; he wrote more than any other writer of the ancient church, on more subjects. But at the centre of it all is one simple, searching claim, and it is the claim of his first paragraph. We were made for God, and our hearts are restless until they rest in Him. Everything else in his teaching is that sentence followed out.

Follow the thread and the whole shape appears. We are lovers, carried by our loves as a stone is carried by its weight. Sin is love turned the wrong way — the pear-thief loving his own fault, the creature loving itself in place of its Creator. Evil is not a rival power but the absence of good, a falling away from the God who made all things well. Left to ourselves, we cannot turn back; the will that turned away destroyed its own freedom. So grace must go before, making the unwilling willing, giving what it commands. It comes to us in Christ, the humble Mediator, who cured our pride by His humility and won the victory by becoming the victim. It meets us in the Scriptures, whose whole aim is love, and in the church, a mixed field that Christ is making holy, which stands by the daily forgiveness of sins. It reorders our loves, until we love God first and everything else in Him. It builds a city out of that love, running through all of history beside the city of self-love, and it will raise that city, body and soul, and bring it at last to the Sabbath which has no evening. Faith begins; hope prays; love is the end; and sight is the home.

## What he got wrong

This book has not hidden Augustine’s errors, and it would be a poor tribute to pretend he had none. He approved the use of force against the Donatists, and his arguments outlived his gentleness. He tied the inheritance of sin too closely to sexual desire, and left a long shadow on the church’s view of the body. He consigned unbaptised infants to punishment, however mild, and pressed his doctrine of predestination further than many of his most loving readers can follow. Protestant readers will also hesitate over his prayers for the dead and his tentative purgatorial fire.

None of this is raised to diminish him. It is raised because he himself taught us to expect it. No one wrote more powerfully about the deceitfulness of the heart, the blindness of pride, the way we fail to see our own sins. And no great writer did more to correct himself. At the end of his life he sat down and reviewed every book he had written, noting what he now thought mistaken. His Retractations are a model of intellectual honesty. He would want us to read him the same way: gratefully, critically, with the Bible open.

> Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts. PSALM 139:23

## What to carry away

If you carry nothing else from this book, carry two things.

First, take your restlessness to God. Do not numb it, and do not despise it. It is the memory of home. Every time you find that something you wanted has not satisfied you, hear it as Augustine heard the voices of the earth and sea and sky: not here — higher. And go to the One who said, Come unto me, and I will give you rest.

> Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. MATTHEW 11:28

Second, pray his prayer. Give what Thou enjoinest, and enjoin what Thou wilt. It is the prayer of a man who had discovered that he could not make himself holy, and that God could. It is not a counsel of passivity; Augustine was one of the busiest men who ever lived. It is the recognition that every good thing in us is a gift, and the confidence that the Giver is generous. If you are fighting a battle you keep losing, stop fighting alone. Ask.

> For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure. PHILIPPIANS 2:13

## A last word about the man

Augustine died in August 430, in a city surrounded by an invading army, with the civilisation he had known collapsing around him. He was seventy-five. He had spent the last days of his life reading the penitential Psalms fixed to the wall beside his bed, weeping over them. He died, as he had lived since the garden, a confessing man: a sinner who knew himself forgiven, a bishop who knew himself a beggar before God.

He left no will, because he had nothing to leave but his books. Those books have outlasted the Vandals, the empire, and every one of the controversies in which they were written. They have been read by monks and Reformers, by philosophers and children, in Latin and English and in languages Augustine never heard. And the sentence that begins them has lost none of its force. The heart is still restless. God is still its rest.

> Thou wilt shew me the path of life: in thy presence is fulness of joy; at thy right hand there are pleasures for evermore. PSALM 16:11

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Go back to the chapter of this book that unsettled you most, not the one you enjoyed most, and work through its application points again.
2. Begin reading the Confessions for yourself this month, a few pages a day, with a pencil and a prayerful heart.
3. Write down one error of your own thinking that you have come to see. Thank God for correcting you, and be humble about the errors you have not yet seen.
4. Make Augustine’s prayer, give what You command, part of your daily prayer for the next month.
5. Tell one other person what you have learned from Augustine, and invite them to read him with you.

### A PRAYER

> Lord God, You made us for Yourself, and our hearts are restless until they rest in You.
> Thank You for Your servant Augustine: for his honesty, his hunger for You, his witness to Your grace. Forgive us where we have followed him into error, and keep us where he followed You.
> Give what You command, and command what You will. Reorder our loves, humble our pride, and bring us home.
> Let us rest and see, see and love, love and praise, in the Sabbath that has no evening. Amen.

# A Reader’s Guide to Augustine of Hippo

> Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path. PSALM 119:105

Augustine left more writing than any other author of the ancient church: over a hundred books and treatises, hundreds of letters and hundreds of sermons. No one reads it all, and no one needs to. All of it is in the public domain in its original Latin, and a great deal is freely available in older English translations, above all in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, the series edited by Philip Schaff in the 1880s, whose first eight volumes are given to Augustine. The difficulty is not access but choice. What follows is a short map.

## Begin here

Confessions. Begin here, and come back here. Thirteen books: the first nine tell his life to the death of his mother; the tenth examines his memory and his present heart; the last three meditate on time, eternity and the opening of Genesis. The Pusey translation is in the Ochorus library. It is old-fashioned but faithful. Among modern translations, Henry Chadwick’s (1991) is exact and readable, and F. J. Sheed’s (1943) is warm and much loved. Many readers leave the last books for a second reading.

Enchiridion. His own handbook of the faith, written for a layman, arranged under faith, hope and love. It is in the Ochorus library in J. F. Shaw’s translation from the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers. Short, clear and the best single summary of his mature theology.

## The great works

The City of God. Twenty-two books, written after the sack of Rome: the story of the two cities from creation to the end. The first ten books answer pagan objections and can be skimmed. Books XI to XIV and XIX to XXII are the heart. The translation by Marcus Dods in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers is widely available.

On Christian Doctrine. His handbook on reading and preaching Scripture, and the source of the rule of love. The first book is short and repays slow reading.

On the Trinity. His most demanding work, written over some twenty years. Read the first books for his handling of Scripture and the eighth to tenth for his search for the image of God in the soul. Not a place to start.

## Sermons and commentary

Homilies on the Gospel of John and on the First Epistle of John. Sermons preached to his people at Hippo, plain and vivid. The homilies on First John, which contain the saying love, and do what you will, are the best introduction to Augustine the preacher.

Expositions of the Psalms. His longest work, on every Psalm. Dip in rather than read through.

## On grace

The anti-Pelagian writings, gathered in the fifth volume of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers: especially On the Spirit and the Letter, on the difference between the law that commands and the grace that gives, and On Grace and Free Will, a shorter, pastoral piece written for monks troubled by the controversy. These are the books the Reformers read most closely.

## Approach with care

His late writings on predestination, On the Predestination of the Saints and On the Gift of Perseverance, and his letters defending the coercion of the Donatists, are part of the record and should be read, but read with Scripture open and with the cautions raised in this book in mind.

## Shorter things

The Rule that bears his name, a brief guide to common life, still followed by religious communities today. The Retractations, his late review and correction of his own books. And his letters, which show the bishop at work.

## About Augustine

Possidius, his friend and fellow bishop, wrote a short Life soon after his death; it is the only account by someone who knew him, and it is simple and moving. Peter Brown’s Augustine of Hippo: A Biography (1967, with a revised edition in 2000) is the standard modern life — learned, humane and beautifully written, and not afraid of the hard questions. Henry Chadwick’s brief Augustine (1986) is an excellent short introduction by a great scholar.

## Three counsels for reading him

Read him as prayer. Much of what he wrote was spoken to God before it was written for us.

Read him whole. Do not take only the Augustine your tradition likes. The teacher of grace alone and the bishop of the Catholic church are the same man.

Read him under Scripture. He would insist on it. He placed his own writings far below the Bible, and spent his last years correcting them. Hold fast what agrees with the Word of God, let go what does not, and give thanks for the one who taught so many to say, with him, that our hearts are restless until they rest in God.

> All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness. 2 TIMOTHY 3:16
