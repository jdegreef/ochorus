This volume follows that thread from his searching directions for killing sin, through his writing on communion with the Trinity, temptation, indwelling sin, the atonement, the spiritual mind and the church, and it is honest about where he must be read with care: his dense style, his disputed doctrine of definite atonement, and his part in the Commonwealth.

# Introduction

> That which we have seen and heard declare we unto you, that ye also may have fellowship with us: and truly our fellowship is with the Father, and with his Son Jesus Christ. 1 JOHN 1:3

Most Christians who know the name of John Owen know one sentence of his, and it is a hard one. It comes from a little book of 1656, The Mortification of Sin in Believers, and it has been quoted in pulpits and books for more than three centuries: “be killing sin or it will be killing you.” It sounds like the motto of a grim man. It is not. The same man spent the best of his life writing about the love of the Father, the beauty of Christ and the comfort of the Holy Spirit, and he died dictating a book on the glory of the Saviour he expected to see within days. The sentence about killing sin is true, and Owen meant every word of it. But it is only half of him.

He was the greatest theologian of the English Puritans, and very likely the most learned. Born in 1616, he rose from a country parsonage to be chaplain to Oliver Cromwell, Dean of Christ Church and Vice-Chancellor of the University of Oxford. When the monarchy returned and the Act of Uniformity drove some two thousand ministers out of the Church of England in 1662, he lost everything he had held and spent the rest of his life as a Nonconformist pastor in London, preaching to gathered congregations, writing without pause, and burying nearly all his children.

## Why he still matters

Owen matters because he understood the inner life of a Christian with a precision that almost no one else has matched. Other writers tell you that you ought to be holy. Owen shows you, with the patience of a physician, how sin actually behaves in a believing heart: how it hides when it is watched, how it trades one form for another, how it borrows the language of grace to excuse itself, how it grows by inches until a tender conscience has become a hard one.

He matters, too, because he never lets that honesty end in despair. Every searching page in Owen turns sooner or later to Christ — to His blood, His fulness, His tenderness as a High Priest who was tempted like us.

## The one thread

If you are looking for the single thread that runs through everything Owen wrote, you find it in the word communion. God has made Himself known as Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and in Christ He has brought sinners into real fellowship with Himself. That fellowship is the whole point of the Christian life. Sin is dreadful chiefly because it spoils it; holiness is precious chiefly because it is the atmosphere in which it flourishes; heaven is glorious chiefly because there it will be complete.

Owen made the connection himself. In the preface to The Mortification of Sin he explains that he had long promised some friends a treatise on communion with God, and had not yet written it; he offers them this smaller book in the meantime, calling it “this discourse of variance with themselves, as interest for their forbearance of that of peace and communion with God.” The war against sin and the enjoyment of God were, for him, two halves of one life. You fight the one because you want the other.

> Blessed are the pure in heart: for they shall see God. MATTHEW 5:8

## How this book is arranged

The second chapter tells the story of his life. The fifteen chapters that follow each take one of his key teachings. The first eight stay close to The Mortification of Sin in Believers, which is in the Ochorus library, and follow its argument from the necessity of the work, through the Spirit’s part in it, to the marks of a dangerous sin and the directions Owen gives for dealing with it, ending where he ends, with faith fixed on Christ. The remaining seven turn to his other great works: Communion with God, The Glory of Christ, Of Temptation, Indwelling Sin, The Death of Death in the Death of Christ, The Grace and Duty of Being Spiritually Minded, and his writings on the church and liberty of conscience. A short conclusion and a reader’s guide close the book.

Where Owen is quoted directly, the words come from The Mortification of Sin as Ochorus hosts it. His other books are described in plain modern prose, and not quoted, because his seventeenth-century English, long sentences and piled-up clauses are better served by a faithful summary than by a fragment torn from its setting. Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer.

## Reading him with care

This is not a book of hero-worship. Owen was a great man and a sinner, and he would have said so first. Four cautions in particular should be named at the start.

The first is his style. Owen is hard to read. His sentences run on, his arguments divide and subdivide, and his vocabulary belongs to the lecture halls of seventeenth-century Oxford. But the difficulty is mostly on the surface, and it is worth persevering.

The second is his doctrine of the atonement. In The Death of Death in the Death of Christ Owen argued that Christ died with the definite intention of saving His elect, and actually secured their salvation. Many faithful Christians, including other Puritans who loved him, have read the Scriptures differently, and a chapter of this book tries to set out his case fairly and to be fair to theirs.

The third is his part in the Commonwealth. Owen preached before Parliament the day after King Charles I was beheaded, went with Cromwell’s army to Ireland and to Scotland, and held high office under a government that many of his fellow Christians regarded as illegitimate. He used his power at Oxford fairly; but he stood close to power in a violent time, and his readers should not look away from that.

The fourth is his tone towards Roman Catholics, which is often harsh in the manner of his age, and his view of toleration, which was generous for his century but narrower than ours. Where he is simply unkind, we need not follow him.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21

None of this is said to put you off. It is said so that you can read him the way he read everyone — with the Bible open. Where he is faithful to it, and on the deceitfulness of sin, the sufficiency of Christ and the sweetness of communion with God he is wonderfully faithful, let him search you, and then let him lead you to the Saviour he loved.

2. Ask yourself honestly whether your picture of the Christian life has more to do with fighting sin or enjoying God. Owen would say that you need both, held together.
3. Read the preface and first chapter of The Mortification of Sin in Believers this week, slowly, and note one sentence that stops you.
4. If you have avoided Owen because he seemed too difficult, resolve to read him in small portions, a few paragraphs at a time, with prayer.
5. Think of one Christian teacher you admire and one point on which you believe he or she was wrong. Practise holding both judgements without letting go of either.

# A Poor Under-Rower

> I know both how to be abased, and I know how to abound: every where and in all things I am instructed both to be full and to be hungry, both to abound and to suffer need. PHILIPPIANS 4:12

A parsonage in Oxfordshire. John Owen was born in 1616 at Stadhampton, a village a few miles south-east of Oxford, where his father, Henry Owen, was the minister. Henry was a Puritan, one of those clergy who stayed within the Church of England while longing to see it more thoroughly reformed. John was the second son, and at about twelve years of age he was sent up to Queen’s College, Oxford. He took his first degree at sixteen and his master’s degree in 1635. He studied with a ferocity that became a legend; he is said to have allowed himself only a few hours’ sleep a night for years, a habit he later regretted as having harmed his health.

A conscience that would not bend. In the 1630s Archbishop William Laud, as Chancellor of Oxford, was tightening the university’s statutes and ceremonies. Owen could not in conscience submit to the new requirements, and in 1637 he left the university rather than conform. It cost him his prospects of an academic career. He became a private chaplain and tutor in the households of gentry, and when the Civil War broke out and his employer took the King’s side, the young Owen, whose sympathies lay with Parliament, moved to London.

The unknown preacher. For all his learning, Owen spent these years under a heavy spiritual darkness. He believed the gospel and could not rest in it. The turning point, as he later told it to friends, came in London around 1642. He went to hear the famous preacher Edmund Calamy at St Mary Aldermanbury, but Calamy did not come, and a country minister whom nobody there knew went up into the pulpit instead. Owen stayed. The stranger preached on the words of Christ to His frightened disciples in the storm, Why are ye fearful, O ye of little faith? (Matthew 8:26). Under that plain sermon the burden lifted, and Owen came into the peace with God that he had long been seeking. He tried afterwards to find out who the preacher had been, and never could.

> Why are ye fearful, O ye of little faith? MATTHEW 8:26

A country pastor. Owen’s first book, A Display of Arminianism, appeared in 1643, and it brought him the living of Fordham in Essex. In 1644 he married Mary Rooke. In 1646 he moved to the market town of Coggeshall, where large congregations came to hear him, and where his study of the New Testament led him away from the Presbyterian view of church government towards the Congregational, or Independent, view that each gathered church of believers is a true church under Christ. In the same years he wrote The Death of Death in the Death of Christ, published in 1647, his great defence of the particular redemption that he believed the cross secured.

Into the storm. Owen was now asked to preach before Parliament, and on 31 January 1649, the day after the execution of King Charles I, he preached before the House of Commons. Soon afterwards Oliver Cromwell heard him and took him as a chaplain on the army’s campaign in Ireland in 1649, and later in Scotland. Owen came back from Ireland pleading with Parliament that the country needed preachers of the gospel and not only soldiers. His admirers cannot pass over this lightly: he stood close to a government that had killed a king and fought a savage war, and he lent it his voice.

Oxford again. In 1651 Parliament made Owen Dean of Christ Church, and in 1652 Cromwell, as Chancellor, made him Vice-Chancellor of the University. Until 1657 he restored discipline to a university torn by war, preached regularly to the students, and protected scholars whose views differed from his own, including some who worshipped by the Prayer Book in private. It was to these young men, many of them Christians making no headway against habits they hated, that he preached the sermons which became The Mortification of Sin in Believers, published in 1656. Of Communion with God followed in 1657 and Of Temptation in 1658. In 1658 he was among the leaders of the Savoy Assembly, which drew up a declaration of faith and order for the Congregational churches.

> Wherewithal shall a young man cleanse his way? by taking heed thereto according to thy word. PSALM 119:9

The tide goes out. Owen had opposed the plan to make Cromwell king, and after Cromwell’s death in 1658 he was caught up in the confused politics that brought down his son Richard. With the return of Charles II in 1660 he lost his deanery, and the Act of Uniformity in 1662 excluded him, with some two thousand others, from the ministry of the national Church. For the rest of his life he was a Nonconformist. He preached to gathered congregations in London at a time when such meetings were illegal, and in 1673 his church united with that of the late Joseph Caryl. He was invited to become minister of a church in Boston, in New England, and declined, choosing to stay with the persecuted churches at home. He wrote and wrote: Indwelling Sin in 1667, the great commentary on the Epistle to the Hebrews from 1668 onwards, the long work on the Holy Spirit in 1674, and a stream of pleas for liberty of conscience for Dissenters.

A friend to a tinker. One story from these years has become part of the legend. Owen valued the preaching of John Bunyan, the Bedford tinker who spent years in prison for preaching without a licence, and he is credibly said to have helped to secure Bunyan’s release. Tradition also reports that when King Charles II asked him why a learned man would go to hear an uneducated tinker preach, Owen answered that he would gladly exchange all his learning for the tinker’s power to touch the heart. The words come only by tradition.

A house of sorrows. The public career hid a private grief that is hard to take in. Mary Owen bore him eleven children, and according to his early biographers all but one died in infancy or childhood. The one daughter who grew up married unhappily, came home, and died of consumption. Mary herself died in 1676. The next year Owen married Dorothy D’Oyley, a widow, who cared for him through his last years of illness. His words on affliction and glory were paid for.

> For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:17

The long looked-for day. In his last years Owen’s health failed, and his writing turned more and more towards heaven. In 1681 he published The Grace and Duty of Being Spiritually Minded, begun during a serious illness, and he spent his final months on Meditations on the Glory of Christ. Days before his death he wrote to his old friend Charles Fleetwood that he was going to the One whom his soul had loved, or rather who had loved him with an everlasting love, and that though he was leaving the ship of the church in a storm, the great Pilot was still aboard, and the loss of one poor under-rower would not be missed. On the last morning a friend, William Payne, came to tell him that the book on the glory of Christ was at the printers. Owen is reported to have replied that he was glad of it, but that the long-looked-for day had come at last, when he would see that glory in another way than he had ever seen it, or could see it, in this world.

He died at Ealing on 24 August 1683, St Bartholomew’s Day, the anniversary of the day in 1662 on which he and his brethren had been put out of the Church. He was buried in Bunhill Fields, the burial ground of the London Dissenters, where Bunyan would be laid five years later. Through all of it he had been learning the two lessons he wrote about best: how deep sin runs, and how much deeper runs the love of God in Christ.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Owen came to assurance through a sermon by a preacher whose name he never learned. Thank God for someone unknown or unremarked who has helped your faith, and if you can, tell them.
2. He left Oxford rather than act against his conscience, and returned years later to govern it. Is there a point where your conscience is being asked to bend? Pray for the courage to keep it.
3. Owen’s closeness to power in a violent time is a warning. Ask where your own loyalties to a party, a cause or a leader may be blunting your Christian judgement.
4. He buried nearly all his children and still wrote of the love of God with joy. Bring one grief you carry to God this week, honestly, and ask Him to meet you in it.
5. Read the story of the disciples in the storm in Matthew 8:23–27, and hear Christ’s question as addressed to you.

> Thank You for Your servant John Owen, for the peace You gave him through a stranger’s sermon, and for the long faithfulness of his life through honour and loss.

# Be Killing Sin

> For if ye live after the flesh, ye shall die: but if ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body, ye shall live. ROMANS 8:13

The Mortification of Sin in Believers is an exposition of one verse. Owen takes Paul’s words in Romans 8:13 and finds in them a whole map of the Christian’s inner war: a duty (mortify the deeds of the body), the people to whom it is given (ye, believers), the power by which it is done (through the Spirit), and a promise attached to it (ye shall live). To mortify, he explains, is simply to put to death. Sin in the believer is pictured as a living thing, an old man with his own strength and cunning, and the command is to take away its life.

The word sounds old-fashioned now, and to many ears it suggests hair shirts and cold baths. Owen means nothing of the kind, as later chapters will show. He means the daily, deliberate, Spirit-given work of weakening the sin that still lives in a Christian, so that it loses its power to rule.

## Even the best believers

The first thing Owen establishes is the most surprising: this work is for Christians, and for the best of them. He puts it as a thesis at the head of his argument: “The choicest believers, who are assuredly freed from the condemning power of sin, ought yet to make it their business all their days to mortify the indwelling power of sin.”

He proves it from Paul’s letter to the Colossians. The people told to mortify their members which are upon the earth are the very people who are risen with Christ, dead with Him, and hidden with Him in God.

> Mortify therefore your members which are upon the earth; fornication, uncleanness, inordinate affection, evil concupiscence, and covetousness, which is idolatry. COLOSSIANS 3:5

Then comes the sentence for which Owen is remembered. Being dead with Christ, he says, will not excuse you from this work: “Do you mortify; do you make it your daily work; be always at it whilst you live; cease not a day from this work; be killing sin or it will be killing you.”

Owen first spoke it to young Christians at Oxford. He was not frightening unbelievers; he was telling believers that forgiveness does not end the fight. It begins it.

## Why the fight never stops

Owen gives his reasons with characteristic thoroughness, and they are worth hearing slowly.

First, sin remains. There is no perfection in this life. Paul himself said he had not yet attained, and the flesh still lusts against the Spirit in every Christian.

> For the flesh lusteth against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh: and these are contrary the one to the other: so that ye cannot do the things that ye would. GALATIANS 5:17

Second, sin is active. It does not merely sit in the heart; it works. Owen has a memorable image for its stillness: “sin is never less quiet than when it seems to be most quiet.” Its waters are deepest where they are calmest. And so, he concludes, “There is not a day but sin foils or is foiled, prevails or is prevailed on.” There are no neutral days.

Third, sin aims at the worst. Every temptation, if it had its way, would go to the limit of its kind. “Every unclean thought or glance would be adultery if it could; every covetous desire would be oppression, every thought of unbelief would be atheism, might it grow to its head.” Sin starts modestly, asking only a little ground, but it never means to stop there.

> But every man is tempted, when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed. Then when lust hath conceived, it bringeth forth sin: and sin, when it is finished, bringeth forth death. JAMES 1:14–15

Fourth, God has given the Spirit and the new nature precisely so that we may fight. To leave that gift idle, Owen says, is like binding the arms of the one who fights for you while leaving your enemy free to strike.

Fifth, neglect leads to decline. Grace withers when it is not exercised, and the heart grows hard. Owen had watched it happen. He describes Christians he had known as humble, tender and zealous who, through neglecting this duty, had become earthly, cold and quick-tempered.

> But exhort one another daily, while it is called To day; lest any of you be hardened through the deceitfulness of sin. HEBREWS 3:13

## Life, vigour and comfort

What is promised to those who do this work? Ye shall live. Owen is careful here. Mortification is not the cause of eternal life, which is the free gift of God; it is the appointed way to it, the means God has joined to the end. But he also hears in the promise something for this life: a vigour and comfort in walking with God. “The vigour, and power, and comfort of our spiritual life depends on the mortification of the deeds of the flesh.”

He uses a gardener’s picture. Two plants of the same kind are set in two plots. One plot is left to the weeds, and the plant lives but can hardly be found. The other is weeded daily, and the plant flourishes. The graces of the Spirit, Owen says, are like that. They may be truly present in a neglected heart and yet so choked that faith, love and zeal can scarcely be seen.

> Every branch in me that beareth not fruit he taketh away: and every branch that beareth fruit, he purgeth it, that it may bring forth more fruit. JOHN 15:2

He is honest that the connection is not mechanical. He points to Heman, the writer of the eighty-eighth Psalm, who walked with God faithfully and yet had terrors for his portion all his days. God keeps it in His own hand to speak peace and comfort. But in the ordinary course of things, an unmortified sin will weaken the soul and darken it, and nothing but mortification will clear the sky.

## A word of caution

Owen can be misread in two directions. Some readers, sensitive and scrupulous, hear his warnings and turn them on themselves until every flicker of temptation seems proof that they are lost. Owen did not intend this, and later in the book he says plainly that finding sin in yourself proves nothing either way about whether you are a Christian. The work of mortification is done by people who are already forgiven, in the light of that forgiveness. If you are reading him in a way that leaves you further from Christ, you are reading him wrongly.

Others hear the call and make it a programme of self-improvement, as if holiness were a matter of discipline and technique. Owen will close that door firmly in the next chapter. The fight is real, and it is ours; but it is fought through the Spirit, or not at all.

> Though our outward man perish, yet the inward man is renewed day by day. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:16

Between those two errors lies the plain path of Romans 8:13. There is sin in you. It is alive and it means your harm. God has given you His Spirit to put it to death. Do not make peace with it for a single day.

# Through the Spirit

> A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. EZEKIEL 36:26

If the first word of Romans 8:13 that Owen presses on us is mortify, the second is Spirit. If ye through the Spirit do mortify the deeds of the body. Everything turns on that little phrase. It is what divides Christian holiness from every other kind of moral effort in the world, and Owen will not let his readers pass over it.

He states the principle bluntly. The Holy Spirit alone is sufficient for this work; all other ways and means without Him are nothing. And then he adds a sentence that could stand over the door of every church: “Mortification from a self-strength, carried on by ways of self-invention, unto the end of a self-righteousness, is the soul and substance of all false religion in the world.”

## The remedies that do not heal

Owen knew the false remedies well, because the seventeenth century was full of them. He looks first at the Roman Catholic system of his day, with its vows, penances, rough garments and monastic disciplines. His complaint is that they had mistaken the enemy. They attacked the natural body instead of the corrupt heart, and so wore themselves out fighting the wrong foe. They also took things God has appointed, such as prayer and fasting, and treated them as if they worked by their own power, so that they were always mortifying and never came to any sound mortification.

Owen’s language here is often harsh, as Protestant polemic of his age commonly was, and a modern reader need not share his contempt for men and women who were, many of them, seeking God with great seriousness. But his theological point stands, and he turns it at once on his own side. There is, he says, a natural popery in every heart. A man falls into a sin, is ashamed, promises God he will never do it again, watches himself for a while, and prays hard until the sense of guilt fades; then the resolution fades with it and the sin returns. Owen’s verdict is one of his best lines: “Duties are excellent food for an unhealthy soul; they are no physic for a sick soul.” Prayer, reading and self-denial nourish a healthy Christian. They cannot, by themselves, cure a diseased one.

> Wherefore if ye be dead with Christ from the rudiments of the world, why, as though living in the world, are ye subject to ordinances, (Touch not; taste not; handle not; … Which things have indeed a shew of wisdom in will worship, and humility, and neglecting of the body; not in any honour to the satisfying of the flesh. COLOSSIANS 2:20–23

## How the Spirit does it

How, then, does the Spirit put sin to death? Owen answers in three ways.

First, He makes the heart abound in grace. The fruit of the Spirit is the opposite of the works of the flesh, and as love, joy, peace, patience and self-control grow, the lusts that oppose them lose room to live. Pride is weakened by humility, anger by patience, love of the world by a heavenly mind. The Spirit kills sin partly by crowding it out.

> But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law. And they that are Christ’s have crucified the flesh with the affections and lusts. GALATIANS 5:22–24

Second, He works directly on the root of sin itself. Owen calls the Spirit a fire that burns up the very root of lust. The same almighty power that gave the new heart in the first place goes on weakening the old habit by degrees.

Third, He brings the cross of Christ into the heart by faith, and gives the believer fellowship with Christ in His death. This is the deepest of the three, and Owen keeps it for the last chapter of his book, to which the tenth chapter of this one returns.

Many years later, in the long work usually called Pneumatologia, Owen traced the Spirit’s work from creation to the new creation. The Mortification of Sin shows that same Spirit at work in the most private place of the Christian life.

## Still our work

If only the Spirit can do it, Owen imagines someone asking, why are we told to do it? Why not leave it to Him? His answer is balanced and important. The Spirit works in us as all His graces work in us. He works in us to will and to do, and yet we are commanded to work out our salvation. He is the Spirit of prayer, and yet we are commanded to pray. He does not treat us as stones. In Owen’s phrase, “he works in us and with us, not against us or without us.”

> Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure. PHILIPPIANS 2:12–13

So the Christian neither sits back waiting to be made holy nor strains to make himself holy. He fights, prays, watches and refuses, and he does all of it leaning on a strength that is not his own.

## Believers only

This leads Owen to his first general rule, which he states without softening: unless a man is a believer, truly joined to Christ, he can never mortify any sin. An unbeliever may do something that looks like it, as the ancient philosophers did with their fine discourses on self-control; but Owen says their maxims differed from true mortification as much as the sun painted on a signboard differs from the sun in the sky. “There is no death of sin without the death of Christ.”

> But ye are not in the flesh, but in the Spirit, if so be that the Spirit of God dwell in you. Now if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his. ROMANS 8:9

What, then, should an unconverted person do who is troubled by a particular sin? Owen’s answer is pastoral and searching. Conversion comes first. When the crowd at Pentecost were cut to the heart and asked what they should do, Peter did not tell them to go and master their pride or their anger. He called them to repent and turn to Christ. To press a man to fix one habit while his whole heart remains unchanged is to deal with a sore finger when he has a fever. “Thou settest thyself against a particular sin, and dost not consider that thou art nothing but sin.” Owen has a word here for preachers too: it does no good, he says, to beat a man off his drunkenness into a sober formality. The axe must be laid to the root.

> Now when they heard this, they were pricked in their heart, and said unto Peter and to the rest of the apostles, Men and brethren, what shall we do? Then Peter said unto them, Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost. ACTS 2:37–38

Owen thanks God for every restraint that keeps the world from being worse; but he refuses to let restraint pass for renewal. “To kill sin is the work of living men.” Only those made alive in Christ can do it, and they can do it only through His Spirit.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think back over your last serious attempt to overcome a sin. Was it fuelled mainly by shame and resolution, or by dependence on the Spirit? What happened when the shame wore off?
2. Name one good practice, such as a fast, a rule or a promise, that you have been relying on as though it had power in itself. Keep the practice if it helps, but move your trust to God.
3. Pray each morning this week for the fruit of the Spirit opposite to your most troublesome sin: patience against anger, humility against pride, contentment against covetousness.
4. If you are not sure that you belong to Christ, stop fighting single sins for a moment and settle the greater question first. Read Acts 2:37–41 and respond to it.

> You alone can put sin to death. Make my heart abound in the graces that crowd it out, burn at the root of what is evil in me, and bring the cross of Christ close to my heart.

# What It Is to Mortify a Sin

> Knowing this, that our old man is crucified with him, that the body of sin might be destroyed, that henceforth we should not serve sin. ROMANS 6:6

Having laid his foundations, Owen turns to the case that the rest of his book is written to answer. He describes it with care, and many readers have found it describes them exactly. Suppose, he says, a true believer finds in himself a powerful sin, leading him captive, filling his heart with trouble, weakening him in prayer, disturbing his peace and perhaps defiling his conscience. What is he to do? How is he to deal with this sin so that, even if it is not utterly destroyed, he may still keep strength and peace in his walk with God?

Before he gives directions, Owen stops to define terms, because a great deal of misery comes from mistaking what mortification is. He does it first negatively, then positively.

## What it is not

It is not, first, the complete destruction of a sin in this life. That is what every honest Christian aims at, Owen says; he wants the sin never to stir again. But it is not to be expected on this side of glory. Paul himself, a pattern for believers, said he had not yet attained. God has seen fit that we should be complete in nothing in ourselves, so that we may be complete in Christ. A believer who thinks mortification has failed because a sin still stirs has misunderstood the goal.

> Not as though I had already attained, either were already perfect: but I follow after, if that I may apprehend that for which also I am apprehended of Christ Jesus. PHILIPPIANS 3:12

It is not, second, the hiding of a sin. A man may give up the outward practice of some sin for reasons of reputation or advantage and be taken for a changed man. God knows better. Owen’s judgement is crisp: “He hath got another heart than he had, that is more cunning; not a new heart, that is more holy.”

It is not, third, the cultivation of a calm temperament. Some people are naturally placid, and by discipline and good sense they may come to seem very mortified, while their hearts are full of hidden pride, envy or unbelief. Owen observes that a man who struggles with a violent temper every day may have made more progress against it than another man who has never been troubled by anger at all. He tells the naturally calm to test themselves not by the sins their temperament spares them but by the spiritual sins it does not.

It is not, fourth, the diversion of a sin into another channel. Simon Magus gave up his sorcery when he professed faith, but the covetousness and ambition that had driven it remained, and Peter saw it.

> Thou hast neither part nor lot in this matter: for thy heart is not right in the sight of God. Repent therefore of this thy wickedness, and pray God, if perhaps the thought of thine heart may be forgiven thee. For I perceive that thou art in the gall of bitterness, and in the bond of iniquity. ACTS 8:21–23

A sin can be exchanged for another as easily as a coat. Owen notes, with a dry realism, that “Men in age do not usually persist in the pursuit of youthful lusts, although they have never mortified any one of them.” Circumstances change; the heart does not. The man who swaps sensuality for self-righteousness, or vanity for contempt of others, should not imagine he has made progress. “He hath changed his master, but is a servant still.”

It is not, fifth, an occasional victory. Owen describes two such seasons. The first comes after a sin has broken out badly and shamed a man. Everything in him is roused against it; he hates it, prays, weeps and resolves. The sin lies still, like an enemy who has killed a sentry in the night and then lies low while the camp is searched. But when the hue and cry has passed, “the thief appears again alive, and is as busy as ever at his work.” The second comes in affliction. Trouble drives a man to God, he gives up his sin in the hope of relief, and while the fear lasts the sin is quiet; when the trouble lifts, it returns. Owen finds exactly this in Israel’s history.

> When he slew them, then they sought him: and they returned and enquired early after God. And they remembered that God was their rock, and the high God their redeemer. Nevertheless they did flatter him with their mouth, and they lied unto him with their tongues. For their heart was not right with him, neither were they stedfast in his covenant. PSALM 78:34–37

## What it is

What, then, is mortification? Owen answers in three parts.

It is, first, the habitual weakening of a sin. Every lust is a settled bent of the heart towards some evil, and the first thing the Spirit does is to weaken that bent, so that it rises less often and less violently. Owen compares it to a man nailed to a cross. At first he struggles and cries out with great strength; as his strength drains away his struggles grow faint and his cries hoarse. So a sin, dealt with steadily, may still have its dying pangs, but it moves more seldom and more weakly. And this must be done at the root. “A man may beat down the bitter fruit from an evil tree until he is weary; whilst the root abides in strength and vigour, the beating down of the present fruit will not hinder it from bringing forth more.”

It is, second, constant fighting. This means knowing that you have such an enemy, and taking it seriously. Owen calls it knowing the plague of your own heart, borrowing Solomon’s phrase.

> What prayer and supplication soever be made by any man, or by all thy people Israel, which shall know every man the plague of his own heart, and spread forth his hands toward this house. 1 KINGS 8:38

It means studying the enemy’s ways: what occasions it uses, what excuses it offers, what disguises it wears, so that when it moves you can say to it, I know this old trick of yours and where it is going. And it means loading it daily with everything that wounds it, never supposing it dead merely because it is quiet.

> For I acknowledge my transgressions: and my sin is ever before me. PSALM 51:3

It is, third, success. Not merely the disappointment of a sin, but victory over it: catching it at work, bringing it before the law of God and the love of Christ, and condemning it. When a sin has been weakened at the root, when its motions are fewer and fainter, when a man can meet it calmly and overcome it, then, Owen says, it is mortified in good measure, and he may have peace with God all his days.

## Why the definitions matter

This careful sorting can seem pedantic. It is in fact deeply merciful. It protects the sincere believer from despair, because it tells him that the continued presence of sin is not proof of failure; the question is whether the sin is being weakened, fought and overcome. And it protects the complacent from false comfort, because it strips away the counterfeits: the calm temperament, the traded sin, the resolution made in a crisis.

> Examine yourselves, whether ye be in the faith; prove your own selves. 2 CORINTHIANS 13:5

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Go through Owen’s five counterfeits one by one and ask whether any of them describes your dealing with your besetting sin.
2. If you are naturally calm or disciplined, test yourself this week against a spiritual sin such as envy, unbelief or self-righteousness, rather than the sins your temperament spares you.
3. Consider whether a sin you think you have left behind has only changed its clothes. Where has that old desire gone?
4. Write down the usual path by which your besetting sin approaches you: the time, the place, the mood, the excuse. Learn its old tricks so you can name them.

> Lord, You see my heart as it is, and not as it appears.
> Forgive me for mistaking a quiet temperament for holiness, a hidden sin for a conquered one, and a crisis resolution for a changed heart.
> Teach me to know the plague of my own heart, to learn its ways, and to fight it daily at the root.
> Weaken what is sinful in me, little by little, until it moves seldom and faintly, and let me walk before You in peace. Amen.

# The Marks of a Dangerous Sin

> Having therefore these promises, dearly beloved, let us cleanse ourselves from all filthiness of the flesh and spirit, perfecting holiness in the fear of God. 2 CORINTHIANS 7:1

A good physician does not treat every illness the same way. Some complaints yield to ordinary care; others carry signs that call for urgent and unusual measures. Owen approaches sin in the same spirit. Before he gives particular directions for dealing with a troublesome sin, he lays down a second general rule, and then asks his reader to take the pulse of his own soul.

## Hating sin as sin

The general rule is this: there is no mortifying one sin while you are careless about the rest. Owen imagines a man tormented by some particular lust. He prays against it, groans under it, longs to be free; but in other things, in prayer, in reading Scripture, in the ordinary duties of the Christian life, he is loose and negligent. Such a man, Owen says, will never overcome the sin that troubles him.

Why not? Because his fight against it springs from the wrong root. He hates this sin because it hurts him, not because it offends God. If he hated sin as sin, he would be just as watchful against the neglect of prayer, which does not trouble his conscience, as against the lust that does. Owen names the true foundation of the whole work: “Hatred of sin as sin, not only as galling or disquieting, a sense of the love of Christ in the cross, lie at the bottom of all true spiritual mortification.” And then, addressing the troubled man directly, he gives one of his most piercing sentences: “Did it not disquiet thee, it should not be disquieted by thee.”

Much of what we call fighting sin is really an attempt to get rid of discomfort. God, Owen says, will not simply relieve us of the one sin that bothers us so that we may be at ease in the others that bother Him. “Let not any man think to do his own work that will not do God’s.”

He adds a further thought, gently. Sometimes God allows a particular sin to gain strength in a believer precisely to chasten him for a general carelessness, or to wake him up. Paul was given a thorn in the flesh lest he be lifted up; Peter was left to deny his Master as a correction to his self-confidence. If that is so, it is no use seeking to remove the symptom while leaving the cause.

## The dangerous symptoms

Owen then gives his first particular direction: consider whether your sin has any dangerous symptoms. If it has, ordinary remedies will not be enough. He names six.

The first is long standing. A sin that has been allowed to lie in the heart for a long season, unresisted, grows familiar. The conscience stops being startled by it. It weaves itself into the affections. Owen quotes David’s complaint that his wounds stank and were corrupt through his foolishness, and he draws the lesson in two short sentences: “Old neglected wounds are often mortal, always dangerous.” And again: “As it never dies of itself, so if it be not daily killed it will always gather strength.”

> My wounds stink and are corrupt because of my foolishness. PSALM 38:5

The second is the heart’s secret pleading for peace while the sin remains. Owen describes two forms of it. In one, a man troubled by his sin, instead of dealing with it, turns to count up his evidences of grace, reassuring himself that he is a Christian after all, so that his conscience may be quiet without the sin being touched. In the other, he applies the promises of mercy to a sin he is not really fighting. He is like Naaman, who asked leave to go on bowing in the house of Rimmon: in everything else I will walk with God, but in this one thing may the Lord pardon me.

> In this thing the LORD pardon thy servant, that when my master goeth into the house of Rimmon to worship there, and he leaneth on my hand, and I bow myself in the house of Rimmon … the LORD pardon thy servant in this thing. 2 KINGS 5:18

Owen’s verdict on this is severe and exact: “To apply mercy, then, to a sin not vigorously mortified is to fulfil the end of the flesh upon the gospel.” It is to turn grace into an excuse, which is exactly what Paul refused.

> What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound? God forbid. How shall we, that are dead to sin, live any longer therein? ROMANS 6:1–2

The third is frequent success in winning the will’s consent. A sin may not be carried into outward action, for fear of consequences, and yet the will has agreed to it with pleasure.

The fourth is fighting a sin only with arguments from its punishment. If the only thing a man can say to his temptation is that it will bring shame or hell, then, Owen says, he has in effect decided he would do it if there were no penalty. Those who belong to Christ have better weapons: the love of God, the death of Christ, the preciousness of communion with Him, a hatred of sin as sin. Owen points to Joseph, who met temptation not with a calculation of risk but with a question about God.

> How then can I do this great wickedness, and sin against God? GENESIS 39:9

A man who has been driven back from these gospel defences to the law alone is, Owen warns, in a weak place, and he will not hold it long. “What gospel principles do not, legal motives cannot do.”

> For sin shall not have dominion over you: for ye are not under the law, but under grace. ROMANS 6:14

The fifth is the possibility that the sin is a chastening from God for earlier neglect. Owen asks the troubled believer to look back. What was the state of your soul before this sin took hold? Had you grown careless in prayer? Had you received some great mercy and been unthankful? Had you drifted into the ways of the world?

The sixth is a sin that has already withstood God’s particular dealings with it: warnings in Scripture, in preaching, in affliction, which touched the conscience for a while and were then shaken off. Owen finds this in Isaiah.

> For the iniquity of his covetousness was I wroth, and smote him: I hid me, and was wroth, and he went on frowardly in the way of his heart. I have seen his ways, and will heal him. ISAIAH 57:17–18

Where these signs are present, Owen says, borrowing the words of Christ about a stubborn evil spirit, this kind goes out only by prayer and fasting. An ordinary course will not do; extraordinary seriousness is required.

## A necessary caution

Owen ends this section with a caution that is easy to miss and important to keep. He has said that true believers may fall into these conditions. But no one should conclude from finding them in himself that he is therefore a believer. A man might as well reason that because David committed adultery, every adulterer is a David. The evidence that a person is a Christian must come from what makes a Christian: faith in Christ, the Spirit’s work, love to God. The marks in this chapter are not badges of grace. They are warnings. If you find them, do not take comfort from them; run from them to Christ.

> For the love of Christ constraineth us. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:14

> Where my sin is old and familiar, and has shaken off Your warnings, do the extraordinary work that only You can do.
> Let the love of Christ constrain me, so that I fight sin as sin, for Your sake and in Your strength. Amen.

# Load the Conscience

> And grieve not the holy Spirit of God, whereby ye are sealed unto the day of redemption. EPHESIANS 4:30

One of the first things a sin does, once it has settled in the heart, is to make itself look small. Owen knew its voice well. Is it not a little one? Others have done far worse. Many godly people have struggled with this. God is merciful. Together such thoughts work like a fog. The middle chapters of The Mortification of Sin are Owen’s answer to that fog. He gives a series of directions, and the first of them is to get a clear and lasting sense of what the sin really is.

## Guilt, danger and evil

He asks us to consider three things.

Consider, first, its guilt. Owen makes a point that turns our usual reckoning upside down. We tend to think that a Christian’s sins are less serious than an unbeliever’s, because the Christian is forgiven and is trying. Owen argues the opposite. A believer sins against more love, more light and more help than anyone else, and so there is more guilt, not less, in the sin that remains in him. God sees more beauty in the faint desires of His children than in the grand deeds of the world; and for the same reason He sees more evil in their cherished sins.

Consider, second, its danger. The chief danger is hardening. Owen warns the tender Christian that if he tolerates his sin he will lose his tenderness. He borrows a phrase he had heard used, that such a person grows “sermon-proof and sickness-proof.” Warnings that once made him tremble will leave him untouched; he will pray, read and hear without being moved; sin will grow a light thing to him. There is also the danger of God’s fatherly rod, as David found; the danger of losing all peace and strength in the Christian life; and, for one who continues under the power of sin, the threat of final destruction, which Owen insists must be held out honestly. He is careful to say that this is a judgement on a man’s ways rather than a verdict on his person. A believer is not being told that he is damned; he is being told where the road he is on leads.

> Take heed, brethren, lest there be in any of you an evil heart of unbelief, in departing from the living God. HEBREWS 3:12

Consider, third, its present evil. It grieves the Holy Spirit, who has chosen the believer’s heart as His dwelling, as a loving friend is grieved by a friend’s unkindness. It wounds Christ afresh. And it takes away a person’s usefulness. Owen thought that many barren lives in the church could be explained by hidden sins lying like worms at the root.

## Law, then gospel

Owen’s next direction is stronger still: load your conscience with the guilt of it. He means that the believer should deliberately bring his sin before God’s law and God’s gospel, and not let it escape either.

Bring it first to the law. Let the holiness and severity of God’s commandments speak to it. If the conscience objects that the Christian is no longer under the law’s condemnation, Owen replies that a man who shelters a cherished sin behind that doctrine has no right to its comfort.

Then bring it to the gospel, not for relief, he says, but for deeper conviction. Look on Him whom you have pierced, and mourn.

> And they shall look upon me whom they have pierced, and they shall mourn for him, as one mourneth for his only son. ZECHARIAH 12:10

Owen writes out the words such a person might say to himself, and they are among the most moving in the book: “Is this the return I make to the Father for his love, to the Son for his blood, to the Holy Ghost for his grace?” He goes on to God’s patience with repeated failures and all His kindnesses in providence. Let the conscience dwell on these, he says, until it is humbled in the dust. As long as the conscience can find some way to lighten the guilt, the soul will never seriously fight the sin.

## Longing, temperament and occasions

The directions that follow are short and practical.

Long for deliverance. Owen observes that in ordinary life a desire is worth only what it moves us to do; but in spiritual things longing itself is a grace, with power to shape the soul towards the thing it longs for. Paul cried out for deliverance from the body of this death. “Assure thyself, unless thou longest for deliverance thou shalt not have it.”

> O wretched man that I am! who shall deliver me from the body of this death? ROMANS 7:24

Consider whether your sin is rooted in your natural temperament. Some people are by constitution more prone to anger, others to melancholy, others to sensual indulgence. Owen will not allow this to excuse the sin; it is, he says, simply the particular form original sin has taken in you, and it should humble you. But it does call for particular measures. Here, somewhat against the grain of his polemic against monastic discipline, he commends Paul’s practice of keeping his body under, by fasting and watchfulness and restraint of appetite, so long as these are used only as means through which the Spirit works and never as though they had power in themselves. He adds a sharp warning against making bodily discipline an end: “A man may have leanness of body and soul together.”

> But I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection: lest that by any means, when I have preached to others, I myself should be a castaway. 1 CORINTHIANS 9:27

Watch the occasions of your sin. Consider what company, what places, what studies, what moods have given it opportunity, and set yourself against them. “Know that he that dares to dally with occasions of sin will dare to sin.” Owen reminds us of Hazael, who was horrified when the prophet foretold his cruelty, but who would soon be king of Syria with every temptation to cruelty in his hands.

> And Hazael said, But what, is thy servant a dog, that he should do this great thing? And Elisha answered, The LORD hath shewed me that thou shalt be king over Syria. 2 KINGS 8:13

Rise mightily against the first motions of sin. Do not say, it shall go this far and no further. Once a sin has taken one step, it will take another, like water through a breach in a bank. “It is impossible to fix bounds to sin.” Meet an unclean thought, Owen says, with as much indignation as if it had already done all it wanted to do.

## A word on proportion

A modern reader may find these chapters heavy, and may worry that so much dwelling on guilt and danger will crush rather than heal. Owen’s answer is that this is preparation, not the cure. He is clearing away the fog so that the sin can be seen clearly enough to be brought to Christ. It is also worth noticing how much of his loading of the conscience is done with the gospel rather than the law: the love of the Father, the blood of the Son, the grace of the Spirit. The deepest conviction, for Owen, does not come from fear of punishment. It comes from seeing what our sin has done to the One who loved us.

> I was also upright before him, and I kept myself from mine iniquity. PSALM 18:23

# Think Greatly of God

> I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes. JOB 42:5–6

In the middle of a practical manual on fighting sin, Owen does something unexpected. He stops giving tactics and asks his reader to think about God. Not about God’s law, or God’s judgement, though he has spoken of those; about God Himself, His majesty, His greatness, and how little even the best of us know of Him. The chapter is one of the finest in the book, and it is easy to wonder at first why it is there at all.

The reason is simple and profound. Sin thrives on a small God. A heart that thinks of God as manageable, familiar and near to its own size will find its temptations large by comparison. A heart that is filled with the greatness of God will find them shrinking. Owen puts his direction in seven words: “Think greatly of the greatness of God.”

## The distance between

His first counsel is to dwell on the excellency of God’s majesty and our infinite distance from Him. When Job at last saw God, he abhorred himself. When Habakkuk heard the Lord, his belly trembled. The Scriptures compare the inhabitants of the earth to grasshoppers, to the small dust of the balance, to vanity. Owen says that such thoughts strike deep at the root of sin, because they humble the pride on which sin feeds, and there is no frame of heart less easily deceived than a humble one.

> It is he that sitteth upon the circle of the earth, and the inhabitants thereof are as grasshoppers. ISAIAH 40:22

His second counsel is more surprising: think much of how little you know of God. It might seem an odd thing for the Vice-Chancellor of Oxford to press on a congregation of students, but Owen meant it with all his heart. Even Moses, who spoke with God as a friend, was shown only His back parts. God proclaimed His name, the Lord merciful and gracious, and even that glorious revelation was, compared to the fulness of His glory, only a glimpse.

> And I will take away mine hand, and thou shalt see my back parts: but my face shall not be seen. EXODUS 33:23

## Through a glass, darkly

Someone will object, Owen says, that this was true under the law, when God hid Himself in clouds and shadows, but that now, with Christ come and the gospel shining, we see His face. Owen grants a great deal of this. The difference between our knowledge of God and that of the saints before Christ is almost beyond measure; the sun has risen and the shadows are gone. And yet Paul, who saw more than any of us, said that he saw through a glass, darkly, and knew only in part.

> For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known. 1 CORINTHIANS 13:12

Owen draws out the picture. It is not a telescope that brings far things near; it is an old mirror, giving only dim images. Our best thoughts of God are like a child’s notions of the world, which the grown man remembers with a smile. “We lisp and babble, and say we know not what, for the most part, in our most accurate, as we think, conceptions and notions of God.” The Queen of Sheba heard great reports of Solomon, and when she saw him she said the half had not been told her. So, Owen says, when we come into God’s presence we shall confess that we never knew Him as He is.

> Who only hath immortality, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto; whom no man hath seen, nor can see: to whom be honour and power everlasting. Amen. 1 TIMOTHY 6:16

He goes on to show why this must be so. God has described Himself largely by what He is not: invisible, immortal, infinite. We can repeat the words, but we cannot comprehend the things. To form an image of God’s being in our minds and suppose we have grasped it would be to make an idol as surely as if we carved one from wood. And all that we do know, we know by faith, which rests on His testimony and not on sight.

> But without faith it is impossible to please him: for he that cometh to God must believe that he is, and that he is a rewarder of them that diligently seek him. HEBREWS 11:6

## Not less than we need

Owen is careful not to let this humility become an excuse. We know little of God, he says, but we know enough. The honest truth is that “we all of us know enough of him to love him more than we do,” to trust Him, serve Him and obey Him far beyond anything we have yet attained. Our darkness is no plea for our disobedience. If we had used well what light we had, we might have been trusted with more.

And he adds something that every Christian who is not a scholar should treasure. The difference between a believer and an unbeliever lies less in how much they know than in how they know it. An unbeliever may know and say more about God than many Christians. “The excellency of a believer is, not that he hath a large apprehension of things, but that what he doth apprehend, which perhaps may be very little, he sees it in the light of the Spirit of God.” The humble reader who knows little theology but knows God truly through Christ has what the learned sceptic lacks.

## Awe as a guard

Why does all this belong in a book about mortification? Owen answers at the close of the chapter. A soul kept in reverent awe of God’s greatness and presence will be watchful over itself. It will find the ground hostile to the growth of any lust. Our God is a consuming fire.

> Wherefore we receiving a kingdom which cannot be moved, let us have grace, whereby we may serve God acceptably with reverence and godly fear: for our God is a consuming fire. HEBREWS 12:28–29

Owen is aware of a danger here, and he names it. He wants this awe to be consistent with the child-like boldness that is given us in Christ to come to the throne of grace. It is not the fear of a slave before a tyrant. It is the reverence of a child before a Father whose greatness he will spend eternity discovering. A Christian who has lost that reverence has lost something vital; a Christian who has lost the boldness has lost something vital too. The gospel holds both.

This may be the most neglected of all Owen’s counsels. We live in an age that speaks easily of God, often cheerfully and familiarly, and seldom with trembling. It would do most of us good to take his advice literally: to set aside time not to ask God for anything, nor to examine ourselves, but simply to think about Him, until our sins look as small and as ugly as they are.

> Be still, and know that I am God. PSALM 46:10

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Set aside fifteen minutes this week to read Isaiah 40:12–31 slowly, and then sit in silence before God without asking Him for anything.
2. Notice how you speak of God in ordinary conversation. Is there any reverence in it? Ask Him for a truer sense of His greatness.
3. Owen says we all know enough of God to love Him more than we do. Name one thing you know about Him that you have not yet acted on, and act on it.
4. If you have felt that you know too little theology to walk closely with God, take comfort from Owen’s words about the believer who sees little but sees it in the Spirit’s light.

> Great and holy God, You dwell in light that no one can approach, and I have treated You as if You were small.

> Show me enough of Your glory to humble me, and enough of Your grace in Christ to draw me near with the boldness of a child.
> Keep me in reverent awe all my days, until I see You face to face and know even as I am known. Amen.

# Speak No Peace Until God Speaks

> I will hear what God the LORD will speak: for he will speak peace unto his people, and to his saints: but let them not turn again to folly. PSALM 85:8

There is a moment in the life of every Christian who takes sin seriously when the conscience is troubled and the heart is desperate for relief. A sin has broken out, or an old sin has been brought to light, and the soul cannot rest. It is at this moment, Owen believed, that one of the most dangerous deceptions of the Christian life does its work. The troubled believer reaches for comfort, finds it, and applies it to himself before God has given it. The wound is covered; it is not healed.

Owen’s ninth direction addresses this directly: when God disquiets your heart about sin, take heed that you do not speak peace to yourself before He speaks it. Listen for what He says.

## Peace is God’s to give

He begins with two observations. First, it is God’s sovereign right to speak peace to whom He pleases and when He pleases. Just as He gives grace freely to sinners, so among His children He keeps in His own hand the giving of comfort. Owen finds this in Isaiah, where God, speaking of the backsliding He will heal, says that He Himself creates peace.

> I create the fruit of the lips; Peace, peace to him that is far off, and to him that is near, saith the LORD; and I will heal him. ISAIAH 57:19

Second, it is Christ’s particular office to speak that peace home to the conscience. When He addressed the church at Laodicea, which had spoken peace to itself falsely, He called Himself the Amen, the faithful and true witness. We may flatter ourselves or trouble ourselves in vain; He tells the truth about our condition.

> And unto the angel of the church of the Laodiceans write; These things saith the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the beginning of the creation of God. REVELATION 3:14

## The marks of self-made peace

Owen then gives rules by which a person may tell whether the peace he feels is God’s or his own.

We speak peace to ourselves, first, when it is not joined to a deep hatred of the sin concerned. A man may go to the right Physician, look to the right promises, and still come away with a false peace, if he has not come to loathe the sin that wounded him. When people truly look on Christ whom they have pierced, they mourn, and they detest the sin that pierced Him. Anything else, Owen says, “is to heal themselves, and not to be healed of God.” It is like skin growing over a wound while the infection remains underneath, to break out again later.

We speak peace to ourselves, second, when we reason our way to it from the promises by our own logic, without the Spirit applying them. Owen draws a vivid picture of a troubled man who searches the Bible for a promise whose words fit his case, and says to himself, “here I will take myself a plaster as long and broad as my wound.” The promise is true, and the man may even be a Christian. But the Lord is not in it. It is the reasoning mind at work, not the Spirit of God. Owen compares it to the wind that passed before Elijah on the mountain: the Lord was near, but the Lord was not in the wind.

> And, behold, the LORD passed by, and a great and strong wind rent the mountains, and brake in pieces the rocks before the LORD; but the LORD was not in the wind. 1 KINGS 19:11

We speak peace to ourselves, third, when we do it in haste. God sometimes comforts at once; more often He calls for waiting. Owen puts it tenderly: “God will have his children lie a while at his door when they have run from his house.” Those who heal themselves are always in a hurry.

> And I will wait upon the LORD, that hideth his face from the house of Jacob, and I will look for him. ISAIAH 8:17

We speak peace to ourselves, fourth, when it does not change the life. When God speaks peace, it keeps the soul from returning to folly. When we speak it, the heart is not weaned from the sin at all; indeed, Owen warns, this is the quickest road into a habit of backsliding. And we do it, fifth, when we heal ourselves slightly, with a glance of faith at a promise, rather than mixing the word with faith until it has done its work.

> For they have healed the hurt of the daughter of my people slightly, saying, Peace, peace; when there is no peace. JEREMIAH 8:11

Sixth, he warns that a man who seeks and finds peace about one sin while another, equally serious, lies untouched in his heart has no peace from God at all. “God will justify us from our sins, but he will not justify the least sin in us.” And seventh, true peace always humbles. Owen points to David, who was never so deeply humbled as when Nathan brought him the news of his pardon. “God’s peace is humbling peace, melting peace.”

## How to know His voice

All this raises an anxious question, and Owen does not dodge it. How are we to know when God is speaking? He gives two answers.

The first is that faith has, as he puts it, a secret instinct by which it knows the voice of Christ when He speaks. Christ’s sheep know His voice. The bride in the Song of Solomon, half asleep, knew at once that it was her beloved knocking. A believer who exercises himself in daily communion with Christ will learn to tell His voice from a stranger’s. When He speaks, He speaks as no one else speaks; He makes the heart burn, as He did on the road to Emmaus.

> And the sheep follow him: for they know his voice. JOHN 10:4

The second answer is simpler and more practical: if the word does your soul good, He is speaking it. If it humbles, cleanses, melts and binds you to obedience, that is His voice. God’s own question in Micah points the same way.

> Do not my words do good to him that walketh uprightly? MICAH 2:7

## A balancing word

Owen’s warning here can be misused by anxious souls, who may refuse all comfort for fear that it is self-made. He anticipates this. When God speaks peace, sooner or later, it must be received; and he says that there is hardly anything with which the Lord is more troubled in His people than their unbelieving fears that keep them from the comfort He is willing to give. The point of this direction is not to keep believers in suspense. It is to make sure that the peace they rest in is real. False peace leaves the wound to fester. True peace, given by God, heals it, and leaves the soul humbled, grateful and done with its sin.

> Now the God of peace … make you perfect in every good work to do his will. HEBREWS 13:20–21

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think back to the last time you felt forgiven after a particular sin. Did the peace humble you and change you, or did it simply relieve you?
2. If there is a sin you have been quickly soothing with promises, slow down. Spend time before God about it, and wait for Him.
3. Ask whether you have sought peace about one sin while tolerating another. Bring both to God together.
4. Read John 10:1–16 and ask God to train your ear to know the voice of the Good Shepherd.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You are the Amen, the faithful and true witness. Tell me the truth about my soul.
> Forgive me for the times I have hurried to comfort myself, taking a promise as a plaster for a wound I did not want to feel.
> Speak peace to me Yourself, and let it be a humbling peace, a melting peace, that turns me from my folly.
> And when You speak, give me faith to receive Your comfort without fear. Amen.

# Set Faith at Work on Christ

> For we have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin. HEBREWS 4:15

Everything Owen has said so far, he tells us at the start of his last chapter, has been preparation. He has cleared the ground, exposed the counterfeits, loaded the conscience, humbled the pride. Now he comes to the work itself, and his directions for it, he says, are very few. In truth there is only one. “Set faith at work on Christ for the killing of thy sin. His blood is the great sovereign remedy for sin-sick souls. Live in this, and thou wilt die a conqueror.”

After all the searching analysis, this is where the book arrives. Not at a technique, but at a Person.

## The fulness that is in Him

Owen first asks the struggling believer to fill his mind, by faith, with the provision laid up in Christ. You may be utterly unable to master your sin; you may be weary of the fight and ready to faint; but there is enough in Christ to relieve you. He reminds us of the prodigal son, who was sustained in the far country by the simple thought that there was bread enough in his father’s house.

> For it pleased the Father that in him should all fulness dwell. COLOSSIANS 1:19

> And of his fulness have all we received, and grace for grace. JOHN 1:16

He then writes out, as he likes to do, the words such a believer might say to himself. They begin in honest despair: I am weak and unstable as water; this corruption is too hard for me; I have made promises and broken them; I see that without help I am lost. And then they turn: but the Lord Christ has all fulness of grace in His heart and all fulness of power in His hand, and He is able to slay these enemies. Owen has his reader take up the fortieth chapter of Isaiah, and it is worth hearing again in this setting.

> He giveth power to the faint; and to them that have no might he increaseth strength. Even the youths shall faint and be weary, and the young men shall utterly fall: but they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint. ISAIAH 40:29–31



## Expect relief from Him

Second, raise your heart to an expectation of relief from Christ. Owen compares it to the vision in Habakkuk, which was for an appointed time and would surely come though it tarried. Look to Christ as the eyes of a servant look to his master’s hand. Expect Him to deal with your sin, in His time and His way.

What grounds do we have for such an expectation? Owen gives two. One is Christ’s mercifulness as our High Priest. He was made like His brethren in all things, He suffered being tempted, and so He is able, and willing, to help those who are tempted.

> For in that he himself hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted. HEBREWS 2:18

The other is His faithfulness. He has promised, and He will do it, as surely as the morning comes to one who watches for it.

Owen then makes a claim so bold that it takes the breath away. This settled expectation of help from Christ, he says, will do more to ruin a lust than all the harshest self-punishment ever devised. And he goes further: “never any soul did or shall perish by the power of any lust, sin, or corruption, who could raise his soul by faith to an expectation of relief from Jesus Christ.”

> Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need. HEBREWS 4:16

Such expectation, Owen adds, also makes the believer diligent in the means by which Christ ordinarily gives Himself: prayer, the Scriptures, the Lord’s Supper. A beggar who expects alms waits at the door.

## Look to the cross

Third, and most particularly, Owen directs faith to the death of Christ. Mortification, he says, flows especially from the cross. One great purpose of Christ’s death was to free His people not only from the guilt of sin but from its power.

> Who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity, and purify unto himself a peculiar people, zealous of good works. TITUS 2:14

He turns to the sixth chapter of Romans. Those who were baptised into Christ were baptised into His death; the old man was crucified with Him, that the body of sin might be destroyed. Owen explains that we are crucified with Christ in three senses: He purchased the Spirit who mortifies sin in us; His death is the source of the power that works in us; and His death is the pattern to which we are conformed.

> Therefore we are buried with him by baptism into death: that like as Christ was raised up from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life. ROMANS 6:4

And then comes the simple, daily practice that crowns the book. “Look on him under the weight of our sins, praying, bleeding, dying; bring him in that condition into thy heart by faith; apply his blood so shed to thy corruptions: do this daily.” Nothing else Owen says is more practical, and nothing else is more neglected.

## The Spirit does it all

Finally Owen gathers everything up in the work of the Holy Spirit. The whole process he has described, he says, is carried out by the Spirit’s power. The Spirit alone convinces the heart of the evil of its sin. The Spirit alone reveals the fulness of Christ for our relief. The Spirit alone establishes the heart in expectation of help from Him. The Spirit alone brings the cross into the heart with its sin-killing power. The Spirit gives fresh supplies of grace, and helps us in prayer with groanings that cannot be uttered.

> Likewise the Spirit also helpeth our infirmities: for we know not what we should pray for as we ought: but the Spirit itself maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered. ROMANS 8:26

The book ends there, almost abruptly, with the Spirit helping a weak believer to pray. It is a fitting close. The man who began by telling Christians to be killing sin ends by telling them that they cannot do it, that Christ has done what makes it possible, and that the Spirit will do it in them as they look to Him.

## Why this matters

Readers who come looking for a method may be disappointed that the final answer is faith in Christ. But this is the genius of the book. The earlier chapters show that no method will work on its own. The last chapter shows why: sin is too strong, too deep and too cunning for anything less than the crucified Christ, applied by the Spirit, received by faith. The fight is real, the effort is real, the watchfulness is real; but the victory is His.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Each day this week, take a few minutes to follow Owen’s practice literally: picture Christ under the weight of your sins, praying, bleeding, dying, and bring that sight to bear on your besetting sin.
2. Write out Isaiah 40:28–31 and keep it with you for the moments when you feel too weak to fight.
3. Ask whether you actually expect Christ to deliver you from your sin. If not, confess your unbelief and ask Him for faith.
4. Come to the Lord’s Supper next time with this particular sin in mind, looking to Christ’s death for power against it.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus Christ, I have tried everything except looking steadily to You.
> I am weak and unstable as water, and my sin is too hard for me. But all fulness dwells in You, and You are able to help those who are tempted, because You were tempted too.
> Let me see You under the weight of my sins, praying, bleeding, dying, and let the sight put my sin to death.
> Holy Spirit, bring the cross into my heart, help me to pray when I have no words, and give me faith to wait for the deliverance that will surely come. Amen.

# Communion with Father, Son and Spirit

> The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost, be with you all. Amen. 2 CORINTHIANS 13:14

In the preface to The Mortification of Sin, Owen admitted that he owed some friends a book on communion with God, and offered them the smaller work as interest on the debt. The following year, 1657, he paid it. Of Communion with God the Father, Son and Holy Ghost is, for many who love Owen, the warmest thing he ever wrote. If The Mortification of Sin shows the surgeon, this book shows the lover. And it explains why the surgery is worth enduring.

## What communion is

Owen defines communion as a mutual giving and receiving between God and His people, grounded in their union with Christ. God gives Himself to us, and we return ourselves to Him in faith and love. It is not a feeling that comes and goes, though it has feelings in it. It is a real relationship, with things given and things returned on both sides.

His great and distinctive claim is that believers have this communion with each Person of the Trinity distinctly. He does not mean that the Father, Son and Spirit can be separated, or that one acts without the others; Owen was an orthodox theologian and insisted that all three are at work in everything God does. But Scripture ascribes particular things to each Person, and the believer is invited to know and enjoy each of them in His own way. Owen built the whole book on Paul’s benediction: the love of God the Father, the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, the communion of the Holy Spirit.

> If a man love me, he will keep my words: and my Father will love him, and we will come unto him, and make our abode with him. JOHN 14:23

## The Father in love

The Father’s particular gift, Owen says, is love. And here he makes an observation that has set many Christians free. A great many believers, he notices, think of the Father as stern and reluctant, and of the Son as the kind one who persuades Him to be merciful. Owen says this is exactly backwards. The Son came to reveal the Father’s love, not to create it. The Father’s love is the fountain from which all the rest flows. Jesus Himself told His disciples that He would not need to plead with the Father on their behalf, because the Father Himself loved them.

> At that day ye shall ask in my name: and I say not unto you, that I will pray the Father for you: for the Father himself loveth you, because ye have loved me. JOHN 16:26–27

What is the believer’s part in this communion? Owen says it is, first, to receive the Father’s love: to believe it, to rest in it, to let it be what it is. And then to return it, with a love of delight and reverence. He urges his readers to dwell on the Father’s love until it melts them. Many Christians, he says, walk in fear of the Father for years when they might have been enjoying Him. The remedy is to go to Him directly as the One who loved them first.

> Herein is love, not that we loved God, but that he loved us, and sent his Son to be the propitiation for our sins. 1 JOHN 4:10

## The Son in grace

With the Son, the believer’s communion is in grace. Owen divides this into two parts.

There is, first, the personal grace of Christ: His beauty, His fitness to be our Saviour as God and man in one Person, His fulness, His excellence. Owen lingers here, and draws on the Song of Solomon, which he reads, as many Christians before him had done, as a picture of the love between Christ and His people. He describes Christ as the Bridegroom who delights in His bride, and the believer as one who learns to delight in Him in return. Modern readers may find this use of the Song unfamiliar, and some interpreters read the book differently; but Owen’s point does not depend on it. The New Testament itself speaks of Christ as the Bridegroom, and the believer’s relation to Him is one of love, not only of duty.

> And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us, (and we beheld his glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father,) full of grace and truth. JOHN 1:14

There is, second, the purchased grace of Christ: all that He won for His people by His life, death and intercession. Owen gathers it under three headings. There is acceptance with God, the free forgiveness and righteousness that are ours in Christ. There is holiness, the new life He gives by His Spirit. And there is adoption, the privilege of being received into God’s family as sons and daughters. The believer has communion with Christ in all these, by receiving them from Him with thankfulness and giving himself back to Him.

## The Spirit in comfort

With the Holy Spirit, Owen says, the believer’s communion is in comfort, or consolation. Christ promised His disciples another Comforter who would abide with them for ever. Owen draws out what the Spirit does as Comforter: He brings to remembrance the words of Christ; He glorifies Christ; He sheds abroad the love of God in the heart; He bears witness with our spirit that we are the children of God; He seals us; He is the earnest, or down payment, of our inheritance; He anoints us to understand.

> And I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever. JOHN 14:16

> The Spirit itself beareth witness with our spirit, that we are the children of God. ROMANS 8:16

The believer’s part, Owen says, is to receive these gifts with gratitude, to pray for them, and to take care not to grieve, quench or resist the Spirit who gives them. Here the two books meet. The sin that The Mortification of Sin teaches us to kill is the very thing that grieves the Comforter and clouds our communion with Him.

## Why this belongs at the centre

Owen believed that most Christians live far below their privileges. They know about the Trinity as a doctrine but have little conscious fellowship with the Father, the Son and the Spirit. They serve God but do not enjoy Him. This book was written to change that, and it still can.

It also corrects a false impression of Owen. He is often remembered as a severe writer about sin, and he was. But his severity served his joy. He wanted his readers to kill sin because sin robbed them of fellowship with a God who loved them, a Saviour who delighted in them, and a Spirit who longed to comfort them. The Mortification of Sin is the surgery; Communion with God is the health it restores.

> Whom having not seen, ye love; in whom, though now ye see him not, yet believing, ye rejoice with joy unspeakable and full of glory. 1 PETER 1:8

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself honestly how you picture God the Father. If you picture Him as stern while Christ is kind, read John 16:26–27 and 1 John 4:9–10, and let them correct you.
2. This week, give one period of prayer to each Person of the Trinity in turn: thank the Father for His love, the Son for His grace, the Spirit for His comfort.
3. Make a list of the things Christ has purchased for you: forgiveness, righteousness, new life, adoption. Thank Him for each one by name.
4. Consider whether any sin in your life is grieving the Spirit and clouding your communion with God. Bring it to Him.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I have too often thought of You as distant and severe. Thank You that You Yourself love me, and that Your love is the fountain of everything Christ has done.
> Lord Jesus, You are full of grace and truth. Let me see Your beauty, receive Your gifts, and give myself back to You in love.
> Holy Spirit, Comforter, bear witness with my spirit that I am a child of God, and forgive me for the ways I have grieved You.
> Draw me into real fellowship with You, Father, Son and Spirit, and let me enjoy You as well as serve You. Amen.

# The Glory of Christ

> Father, I will that they also, whom thou hast given me, be with me where I am; that they may behold my glory, which thou hast given me: for thou lovedst me before the foundation of the world. JOHN 17:24

Owen’s last book was about the glory of Christ, and he did not live to see it printed. He worked on it through his final illness, and it was published the year after his death as Meditations and Discourses on the Glory of Christ. It is not a work of controversy, like so much of what he wrote, nor a manual of practice, like The Mortification of Sin. It is the work of an old man looking steadily at the Saviour he expected soon to see, and inviting his readers to look with him.

Its text is the prayer of Jesus in John 17, that His people might be with Him where He is and behold His glory. Owen draws from it a single conviction that shapes the whole book. To behold the glory of Christ is the great privilege of believers in heaven; and the way to be ready for it is to begin beholding it now, by faith.

## Why we must look now

Owen presses this with some urgency. No one, he argues in effect, should expect to delight in seeing Christ’s glory in heaven who takes no delight in it on earth. Heaven is not a reward arbitrarily attached to the Christian life; it is the completion of what the Christian life already is. If the sight of Christ by faith seems dull to us now, if we would rather think about almost anything else, we should ask what we imagine we will do with eternity.

This is not said to frighten but to awaken. Owen believed that Christians in his day thought too little about Christ Himself, and that looking at Him has a power nothing else has.

> But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord. 2 CORINTHIANS 3:18

To behold Christ is to be changed into His likeness. The Mortification of Sin ended by telling believers to look daily on Christ crucified; the Meditations take that counsel and widen it to the whole of who He is.

## Glory in His person

What, then, is there to see? Owen leads his reader through many aspects of Christ’s glory, and a few of the chief ones can be named.

There is the glory of His person, as God and man in one. Owen never tired of this mystery: that the eternal Son, the brightness of the Father’s glory, took our nature into union with Himself, so that in Christ we see God in a way we could never see Him otherwise. He is the image of the invisible God. To look at Him is to look at the Father made visible.

> Who being the brightness of his glory, and the express image of his person, and upholding all things by the word of his power, when he had by himself purged our sins, sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high. HEBREWS 1:3

There is the glory of His humiliation: that one so high should stoop so low. Owen asks his readers to consider the condescension of the Son in taking a servant’s form, and to see in that very lowliness a greater glory than any display of power.

> But made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant, and was made in the likeness of men: and being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross. PHILIPPIANS 2:7–8

There is the glory of His love, a love that passes knowledge, that moved Him to undertake our salvation and bear our sin. There is the glory of His offices, as the Prophet who reveals God, the Priest who offered Himself and still intercedes, and the King who rules His church. There is the glory of His exaltation, now seated at the right hand of the Father, all authority His. And there is the glory of the union between Christ and His church, the Head and the members, which Owen regarded as one of the deepest mysteries of the gospel.

## Faith now, sight then

Owen spends much of the book on the difference between beholding Christ by faith now and beholding Him by sight in heaven. The difference is not that one is real and the other imaginary; both are real. But the sight of faith is partial, dim and interrupted. It comes in glimpses, often clouded by sin and trouble, and it cannot last long without the mind wandering. The sight of glory will be complete, clear and unbroken, and it will not weary us, because we shall be made fit for it.

> Beloved, now are we the sons of God, and it doth not yet appear what we shall be: but we know that, when he shall appear, we shall be like him; for we shall see him as he is. 1 JOHN 3:2

This is the thought that sustained Owen at the end. The story his friends told, of his reply when he was told the book was at the printers, is a summary of the whole work: he rejoiced, but said that the day had come when he would see that glory in another manner than he had ever done or could do in this world.

## Counsels for looking

The Meditations close with practical counsel for those who want to grow in beholding Christ. Owen urges that such contemplation be grounded always in the Scriptures, since it is there that Christ is set forth; he was wary of imaginations that went beyond what God has revealed. He urges that it be frequent, woven into ordinary days, so that thoughts of Christ become as natural to the mind as thoughts of the things we most love. He urges that it be joined to love and to obedience, since the sight that does not change the life is not the sight of faith. And he says frankly that it will be a struggle, because our minds are weak and easily distracted, and because the world is always pressing in.

> Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God. HEBREWS 12:2

Some readers find the book slow, and it is; Owen circles his subject rather than marching through it. But it is meant to be read a little at a time, by someone who wants to learn to look.

## The end of it all

It is fitting that Owen’s life of study and controversy should have ended here. All of it, in the end, was for this: that he and his readers might see the glory of Christ. The war against sin, the communion with God, the careful doctrine, the patient suffering, all come home in the prayer of John 17:24. The Son wants His people to be with Him and to see His glory. Owen wanted it too, and he spent his last strength preparing for it.

> Thine eyes shall see the king in his beauty: they shall behold the land that is very far off. ISAIAH 33:17

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask yourself honestly whether thinking about Christ Himself is a delight to you or a duty you seldom perform. What does your answer suggest?
2. Choose one passage that sets forth Christ’s glory, such as Hebrews 1:1–4, Colossians 1:15–20 or Philippians 2:5–11, and read it slowly every day this week.
3. Take one of the glories Owen names, His person, His humiliation, His love, His offices, His exaltation, and spend a quiet quarter of an hour simply thinking about it.
4. Notice what your mind naturally turns to when it is free. Pray that thoughts of Christ would become more natural to you.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You prayed that I might be with You where You are and behold Your glory. Thank You that You want me there.
> Forgive me for how seldom I look at You, and how easily my mind turns to lesser things.
> Open my eyes by faith to see Your glory now: Your person, Your humility, Your love, Your reign. And as I look, change me into Your likeness.
> Prepare me for the day when faith gives way to sight, and I shall see You as You are. Amen.

# Entering into Temptation

> Watch and pray, that ye enter not into temptation: the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak. MATTHEW 26:41

In the middle of his directions for mortifying sin, Owen tells his readers to watch against the occasions of their sin, and then breaks off. This subject, he says, is too large to handle there: “I have at large in another treatise, about entering into temptations, treated of it.” That treatise appeared in 1658 as Of Temptation: The Nature and Power of It, and it belongs beside The Mortification of Sin as its twin. The one deals with the enemy inside; the other with the way that enemy finds help from outside.

Its text is the word of Christ to His sleeping disciples in Gethsemane. Watch and pray, that ye enter not into temptation. Within the hour Peter would deny his Lord three times. The warning was not theoretical.

## Tempted, and entering in

Owen begins by making a distinction that is simple and illuminating. To be tempted is one thing; to enter into temptation is another. Every Christian is tempted, and Christ Himself was tempted, without sin. Temptation in itself is anything that tends to draw a person away from obedience to God, whether from the devil, the world or our own hearts. We cannot avoid it. Christ did not tell His disciples to pray that they would never be tempted.

To enter into temptation, Owen explains, is something more. It is for a temptation to get such a hold on the mind and heart that the soul becomes entangled with it. The person begins to entertain it, to weigh it, to parley with it. He may not yet have sinned outwardly, but he is inside the net. Owen compares it to a man who has walked into a snare. He may still struggle free, but he is no longer walking freely.

> Wherefore let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall. There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it. 1 CORINTHIANS 10:12–13

## The signs that we are entering

How does a person know that he is entering into temptation? Owen gives several signs, and they are uncomfortably recognisable.

One sign is a secret pleasure in the matter of the temptation. The heart begins to find the thing attractive, to return to it in thought, to enjoy imagining it, even while the will still says no.

Another is a growing slackness in the ordinary duties of the Christian life. Prayer becomes formal and brief; the Scriptures are read without appetite; the heart is cold in worship. Owen regarded this deadness not only as a result of temptation but as one of its chief doorways.

Another is when the soul begins to reason with the temptation, to argue its case, to consider exceptions. Eve’s conversation with the serpent is the pattern. The moment we start debating whether a sin might be allowable in this particular case, we are already inside the gate.

Owen also names seasons when temptation is especially dangerous. Seasons of great prosperity, when the heart grows easy. Seasons of spiritual deadness. Seasons of unusual spiritual privilege, when a person has received much and is in danger of being lifted up, as Paul was given a thorn lest he be exalted above measure. And seasons of self-confidence, which is the case of Peter.

> And the Lord said, Simon, Simon, behold, Satan hath desired to have you, that he may sift you as wheat: but I have prayed for thee, that thy faith fail not. LUKE 22:31–32

Owen also reminds us of Hezekiah, a good king, whom God left to himself for a moment in the matter of the Babylonian envoys, to try him and to show him what was in his heart. No one, Owen insists, is beyond this danger. Every believer has, as it were, an hour of temptation appointed.

> Howbeit in the business of the ambassadors of the princes of Babylon, who sent unto him to enquire of the wonder that was done in the land, God left him, to try him, that he might know all that was in his heart. 2 CHRONICLES 32:31

## Watch and pray

What, then, are we to do? Christ’s answer is twofold, and Owen follows it.

Pray. It is Christ Himself who taught His disciples to ask, Lead us not into temptation. Owen regarded this as a prayer to be prayed daily and seriously, not as a formality. We should ask God to keep us out of the paths where we would be exposed, and to keep us when we are exposed.

Watch. Owen gives a good deal of practical counsel about watching. Know your own heart, and especially the particular weaknesses of your temperament and circumstances, so that you know where you are likely to be attacked. Watch over the first approaches of temptation, when it is weakest and you are strongest. Consider the occasions and companies and situations that have led you into trouble before. Keep your heart with all diligence.

> Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life. PROVERBS 4:23

But Owen’s deepest counsel about watching is not a technique at all. He believed that the best defence against temptation is a heart filled with a sense of the love of God in Christ. A soul that is busy enjoying God, that is often meditating on the cross, that is aware of the Father’s love and the Spirit’s presence, has little room for the pleasures temptation offers. The things that tempt us are always offering us some satisfaction; the heart already satisfied in Christ is not easily bought.

He draws a particular encouragement from Christ’s words to the church at Philadelphia. Those who keep the word of His patience will be kept by Him in the hour of temptation. Faithfulness in ordinary days is the best preparation for extraordinary ones.

> Because thou hast kept the word of my patience, I also will keep thee from the hour of temptation, which shall come upon all the world, to try them that dwell upon the earth. REVELATION 3:10

## An honest word

Owen’s treatment of temptation is searching, and some readers will find it heavy going. He has a tendency to multiply signs and warnings until the reader feels that nearly everything is dangerous. That is partly the style of his age, and partly the man. But his basic insight is one that every Christian needs and few are taught: the battle is usually lost before the sin is committed, at the point where we let the temptation in and began to entertain it. The fight against sin, therefore, is largely a fight at the door. And the strongest guard at that door is not fear, but love.

> Blessed is the man that endureth temptation: for when he is tried, he shall receive the crown of life, which the Lord hath promised to them that love him. JAMES 1:12

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think of a recent time you gave way to sin. Can you identify the moment, perhaps long before, when you began to enter into the temptation?
2. Ask whether you are in one of Owen’s dangerous seasons now: prosperity, spiritual deadness, unusual privilege, or self-confidence. Take appropriate care.
3. Pray the petition Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil, slowly and deliberately each day this week.
4. Notice if you have begun to reason with a particular sin, looking for exceptions. Stop the conversation.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You watched and prayed in the garden while Your disciples slept. Forgive me for my sleepiness when temptation is near.
> Lead me not into temptation, and deliver me from evil. Keep me out of the paths where I would be exposed, and hold me fast when I cannot avoid them.
> Show me the doors by which temptation enters my heart, and give me grace to shut them early.
> Above all, fill my heart with Your love, so that I am too satisfied in You to be bought by anything else. Amen.

# The Deceit of Indwelling Sin

> I find then a law, that, when I would do good, evil is present with me. ROMANS 7:21

In 1667, five years after the Great Ejection, Owen published a book with a characteristically long title: The Nature, Power, Deceit, and Prevalency of the Remainders of Indwelling Sin in Believers. It is usually called simply Indwelling Sin. If The Mortification of Sin tells a Christian what to do about the sin within him, this book tells him what that sin is and how it works. It is Owen at his most analytical, and to many readers it has been the most unsettling of all his writings, because it describes what goes on in their own hearts with such accuracy.

Its text is Paul’s confession in Romans 7. I find then a law, that, when I would do good, evil is present with me. Owen takes Paul to be speaking as a believer, and he builds the whole book on the word law.

## Sin as a law

Why does Paul call indwelling sin a law? Owen answers that a law is a power that moves and inclines a person in a settled direction. Indwelling sin is not a series of isolated lapses; it is a steady pressure, a bent in the soul, always pulling the same way. The believer finds it, Paul says, especially when he would do good. It is present exactly when he least wants it. It presses hardest against the things that matter most.

> But I see another law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity to the law of sin which is in my members. ROMANS 7:23

Owen observes that this law has its seat in the heart, and he takes the heart in the full biblical sense: the mind, the will and the affections together, the centre from which a person lives. And the heart is not easily known. It is deep, and it is deceitful.

> The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it? JEREMIAH 17:9

## Enmity against God

What is the nature of this law? Owen’s answer is stark: it is enmity against God. Not merely weakness, not merely imperfection, but hostility. The carnal mind, Paul says, is enmity against God. Even in a believer, what remains of sin has this character.

> Because the carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be. ROMANS 8:7

Owen draws out the implications with care. Enmity is universal; it opposes God in everything, not only in one area. It is constant; it never takes a day off. And it shows itself especially in an aversion to God Himself, and to those duties that bring the soul nearest to Him. Owen notices, with a kind of grim precision, that indwelling sin tends to resist most strongly the most spiritual duties: private prayer, meditation, self-examination. A person can often manage public religion more easily than secret communion, because the flesh has less to gain in the closet. The reluctance many Christians feel towards private prayer is, Owen suggests, exactly what Paul was describing.

## How deceit works

The heart of the book is its analysis of the deceit of sin. Owen takes the pattern from the Epistle of James: every man is tempted when he is drawn away of his own lust, and enticed; then lust conceives and brings forth sin. He sees in this a progression through the faculties of the soul.

First, sin draws the mind away. Its first aim is not to make us do evil, but to make us stop paying attention: to distract the mind from its watch, to make it careless of God, forgetful of the danger, occupied with other things. Owen believed that a great many falls begin here, long before any temptation is felt.

Second, sin entices the affections. Once the mind is off its guard, sin presents its object attractively to the desires, dressing it up, making it seem sweet. In The Mortification of Sin Owen had already warned that sin is subtle in how it begins: “it is modest, as it were, in its first motions and proposals, but having once got footing in the heart by them, it constantly makes good its ground.”

Third, sin conceives in the will. The will consents, and the sin is conceived, even if it has not yet come to birth in action. And fourth, sin brings forth; the inward consent becomes outward deed, and the deed, if it runs its course, brings death.

> That ye put off concerning the former conversation the old man, which is corrupt according to the deceitful lusts. EPHESIANS 4:22

Owen shows how sin uses every tool. It abuses the doctrine of grace, whispering that since God forgives, sin is less serious. It takes advantage of weariness in duty. It works by degrees, so that each step seems small. And, as Owen had put it in the earlier book, the two processes feed each other: “As sin gets ground in the affections to delight in, it gets also upon the understanding to slight it.” The more we enjoy a sin, the less serious it seems.

## Decline and great falls

Two of Owen’s observations in this book are particularly sobering. The first is that indwelling sin explains the decline that is sometimes seen in older Christians. Men and women who were zealous and tender in their youth grow cold, worldly and formal in their age, not through any single great sin, but through the slow, steady working of this law against which they have stopped watching.

> Nevertheless I have somewhat against thee, because thou hast left thy first love. REVELATION 2:4

The second is that it explains the great falls of great saints. Noah drunk, Lot in the cave, David with Bathsheba, Peter at the fire. Owen does not treat these as inexplicable. They are what indwelling sin does when it is not watched and mortified. No one is beyond it.

## Why this is good news

It may seem strange to call such a book encouraging, but in an important sense it is. Many Christians are bewildered by their own hearts. They have trusted Christ, they want to please Him, and yet they find a stubborn resistance within them, a reluctance to pray, a pull towards things they hate. They wonder whether they were ever converted at all. Owen tells them that this is exactly what Scripture leads us to expect. Paul felt it. The struggle is not a sign that grace is absent; it is a sign that grace is present and at war.

> Who can understand his errors? cleanse thou me from secret faults. Keep back thy servant also from presumptuous sins; let them not have dominion over me. PSALM 19:12–13

At the same time Owen will not let anyone rest in the struggle as if it were the whole story. The law of sin is real, but so is the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus, which has set the believer free from the law of sin and death. To understand the enemy is the first step to fighting him. And the fight, as the last chapters of The Mortification of Sin have shown, is won by looking to Christ.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Notice this week which spiritual duties you find hardest to begin. Owen suggests that resistance to secret prayer is a sign of the law of sin at work. Pray anyway.
2. Trace a recent sin back through Owen’s stages: when was your mind first drawn away, when were your affections enticed, when did your will consent?
3. Ask whether you have grown colder than you once were. If so, what small, steady neglects have brought you there?
4. If you have been troubled by the persistence of sin in your heart, read Romans 7:14–8:4 and take comfort that Paul knew the same struggle and the same deliverance.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I find a law in me that when I would do good, evil is present with me. You know my heart better than I do, and it is deceitful above all things.
> Keep my mind awake and watchful. Guard my affections from the things that entice them. Hold my will fast to You.
> Cleanse me from secret faults, and keep me back from presumptuous sins. Do not let me grow cold as the years go by.
> Thank You that the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has set me free from the law of sin and death. Let that freedom be seen in my life. Amen.

# The Death of Death

> And she shall bring forth a son, and thou shalt call his name JESUS: for he shall save his people from their sins. MATTHEW 1:21

In 1647, when he was about thirty years old and a country pastor in Essex, Owen published a large and closely argued book with a striking title: Salus Electorum, Sanguis Jesu; or, The Death of Death in the Death of Christ. Its subject was the design and effect of the atonement. For whom did Christ die, and what did His death achieve? Owen’s answer, which he defended with enormous learning and relentless logic, is often called particular redemption, or definite atonement. It has been one of the most influential and one of the most disputed things he ever wrote.

This chapter tries to do two things: to set out Owen’s case fairly and in his own terms, and to be fair to the many faithful Christians who have not been persuaded by it.

## What Owen argued

Owen’s starting point is that the work of salvation is the work of the whole Trinity acting in one purpose. The Father chose a people and gave them to the Son; the Son came to redeem them; the Spirit applies that redemption to them. These three cannot be at cross purposes. The Son did not die with one intention while the Father elected with another.

From this he argues that Christ died with the definite intention of saving those the Father had given Him, and that His death actually secured their salvation. It did not merely make salvation possible for everyone, leaving it uncertain whether anyone would be saved; it purchased everything needed for the salvation of His people, including the faith by which they would receive it. The Good Shepherd laid down His life for the sheep.

> I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep. JOHN 10:11

Owen gathered a great weight of Scripture to support this. Christ came to save His people from their sins. He loved the church and gave Himself for it. He purchased the church of God with His own blood. He prayed, on the night before He died, not for the world but for those the Father had given Him; and Owen argued that Christ’s death and His intercession cannot be separated, so that those for whom He died are those for whom He intercedes.

> Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it. EPHESIANS 5:25

> I pray for them: I pray not for the world, but for them which thou hast given me; for they are thine. JOHN 17:9

He also pressed a famous argument, which may be put simply. Either Christ bore the punishment for all the sins of all people, or for all the sins of some people, or for some of the sins of all people. If only some sins of all, then everyone still has sins to answer for, and no one can be saved. If all the sins of all, why is anyone lost? If the answer is that they are lost because of their unbelief, Owen asked, is unbelief a sin or not? If it is, Christ bore it too. So, he concluded, Christ bore all the sins of some people: His elect.

## What Owen did not argue

It is important to be clear about what Owen did not say, because his view is often caricatured.

He did not say that Christ’s death was too small to save everyone. He affirmed, with the older theologians, that the death of Christ was of infinite value and sufficient in itself for the whole world; its limitation lay in God’s purpose, not in its worth.

He did not say that the gospel should be offered only to the elect. He insisted that the gospel is to be preached freely to all people, and that everyone who hears it is commanded to repent and believe. No one is told to find out whether he is elect before coming to Christ.

He did not say that the universal language of the New Testament is meaningless. He argued that words such as world and all have various senses in Scripture, and that in many places they refer to people of every nation and kind, Gentiles as well as Jews, rather than to every individual without exception.

> And he is the propitiation for our sins: and not for ours only, but also for the sins of the whole world. 1 JOHN 2:2

## Those who disagree

Many Christians who love Christ and revere Scripture have read these texts differently. They point to verses that seem to them to speak plainly of a love and provision extending to all.

> For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. JOHN 3:16

> Who will have all men to be saved, and to come unto the knowledge of the truth. … Who gave himself a ransom for all, to be testified in due time. 1 TIMOTHY 2:4–6

Some, often called Arminians after the Dutch theologian Jacob Arminius, whom Owen had written against in his very first book, hold that Christ died for all people in the same way, making salvation available to all, and that it becomes effective for those who believe. Others, including many Reformed theologians of Owen’s own day, took a middle path: they held that Christ died in one sense for all, providing a genuine basis for the universal offer, and in a special sense for the elect, securing their salvation. Owen’s contemporary Richard Baxter, a fellow Puritan and a man of equal devotion, disagreed with him sharply on these matters, and the two men argued in print. Later, the Wesleys and a great host of evangelical preachers held that Christ died for all, and preached it with extraordinary fruit.

This book will not pretend to settle these differences. Owen’s logic is powerful, and those who hold his view find in it a great comfort. Those who do not often feel that the system presses some verses harder than they will bear. What the reader should do is what Owen himself would urge: go to the Scriptures, weigh the texts on every side, and hold what the Word of God teaches, even where it does not fit neatly into a system.

## What all may learn

Whatever one concludes about the extent of the atonement, there is something in Owen’s book that every Christian can gain. He insisted that the cross actually accomplished something. Christ did not die to make a salvation that might or might not come to anything. He died to save. He paid a real debt, bore a real penalty, and won a real redemption. The believer’s assurance does not rest on the strength of his own faith or the success of his own efforts, but on a finished work.

> Ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold, … But with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot. 1 PETER 1:18–19

This is why Owen’s doctrine of the atonement is not separate from his teaching on mortification. In the last chapter of The Mortification of Sin he told believers to look to Christ’s death as the source of power against sin, because Christ died not only to free His people from guilt but to purify them. The same Christ who secured their pardon secured their holiness. The title of his book says it all: in the death of Christ, death itself was put to death.

> O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? 1 CORINTHIANS 15:55

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Before deciding what you think of Owen’s view, read carefully John 10:11–30, John 17:1–26, 1 Timothy 2:1–6 and 1 John 2:1–2, noting what each passage says.
2. If you hold a view different from Owen’s, try to state his argument fairly in your own words. If you share it, try to do the same for those who disagree.
3. Whatever your conclusion, thank God that Christ’s death actually saves, and that your hope rests on His finished work, not on your own.
4. Ask whether your convictions on this subject have ever made you proud, harsh or less eager to share the gospel with everyone. If so, repent.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, Good Shepherd, You laid down Your life for Your sheep, and You saved Your people from their sins.
> Thank You that Your death was not a mere possibility but a finished work, and that nothing can be added to it.
> Where Christians disagree about the depths of Your purpose, give us humility, patience and charity, and keep us all close to Your Word.
> And make us bold to call everyone to come to You, knowing that You cast out none who come. Amen.

# Spiritually Minded

> For to be carnally minded is death; but to be spiritually minded is life and peace. ROMANS 8:6

Two years before his death, Owen published a book that grew, he explained, out of meditations he had first prepared for his own soul during a season of weakness, when he did not expect to live much longer. He called it The Grace and Duty of Being Spiritually Minded. It is one of the most personal of his writings, and one of the most searching. It asks a single question and presses it on every reader: what is your mind set on?

Its text is Romans 8:6. To be carnally minded is death, but to be spiritually minded is life and peace. Owen notes that Paul does not say that to be spiritually minded brings life and peace, as if it were a condition that earned a reward. He says it is life and peace. The spiritual mind is itself the life of the soul, and the peace it enjoys.

## The test of the thoughts

How can a person know whether he is spiritually minded? Owen’s first answer is characteristically practical: look at your thoughts. Not at the thoughts you have when you are in church, or when you are praying, or when duty requires them, but at the thoughts that come of their own accord, when your mind is free. What does your mind run to when nothing is pressing on it? What do you think about while walking, or waiting, or lying awake?

Owen believed that these voluntary thoughts are among the surest indicators of the true state of the heart. We think most naturally about what we most love. A person whose free thoughts run constantly to money, to reputation, to grievances, to pleasures, is showing where his treasure is. A person whose free thoughts turn often and gladly to God, to Christ, to the things of the Spirit, is showing that his mind has been renewed.

> For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also. LUKE 12:34

He adds that spiritual thoughts must be habitual, not occasional; the question is what the mind returns to and feels at home with.

This is a hard test, and Owen knew it. Many sincere Christians, applying it honestly, will be dismayed. But his purpose is not to condemn them. It is to show them where to begin.

## Things above

The positive counsel of the book is summed up in Paul’s command to the Colossians. If ye then be risen with Christ, seek those things which are above. Owen spends much of the book on what it means to set the mind on heavenly things.

> If ye then be risen with Christ, seek those things which are above, where Christ sitteth on the right hand of God. Set your affection on things above, not on things on the earth. COLOSSIANS 3:1–2

He urges meditation on the glory to come: on the presence of God, the sight of Christ, the company of the redeemed, the freedom from sin. He did not mean vague daydreams about heaven, but thoughtful reflection on what Scripture reveals. Such meditation, he says, puts the things of this world in their true proportions. The man who has thought seriously about eternity is less likely to be enslaved by the next promotion or the next pleasure.

He urges, above all, meditation on God Himself, and on Christ, which connects this book with his earlier work on communion with God and his later one on the glory of Christ. The spiritual mind is not simply a mind full of religious ideas; it is a mind turned towards a Person.

> Finally, brethren, whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on these things. PHILIPPIANS 4:8

## Renewed affections

The second half of the book turns from thoughts to affections: our loves, desires, joys, fears and hopes. Owen argues that being spiritually minded means not only thinking about spiritual things but loving them. And here he makes an important distinction.

There is a difference, he says, between affections that are merely stirred and affections that are truly renewed. A moving sermon, a powerful piece of music, a crisis or a bereavement can stir strong religious feelings in anyone, converted or not. Such feelings may be real, but they are often short-lived and leave the heart unchanged. Renewed affections, by contrast, are the work of the Spirit, who changes what a person loves. They are steadier, humbler, and they bear fruit in obedience.

Owen gives several marks of renewed affections. They are set on God for His own sake, not only for His gifts. They delight in spiritual duties, not merely endure them. They are grieved by sin, and especially by the sin that dishonours God. They long for more of God and are never fully satisfied in this life.

> As the hart panteth after the water brooks, so panteth my soul after thee, O God. PSALM 42:1

## Decline and fruit in old age

Owen wrote this book as an old man, and some of its most moving passages deal with the spiritual life in age. He was well aware that some Christians decline as they grow older: their zeal cools, their affections dry up, their thoughts become occupied with comfort and complaint. He had written about this in Indwelling Sin, and he returns to it here.

But he also sets before his readers the promise of the Psalm. The righteous shall still bring forth fruit in old age. Owen believed that the last years of a Christian’s life could be the most fruitful, if the mind were kept spiritual: if, as bodily strength failed, the soul grew in its sense of God and its longing for glory.

> They shall still bring forth fruit in old age; they shall be fat and flourishing. PSALM 92:14

It is hard to read these pages without remembering Owen’s own circumstances: ill, bereaved of nearly all his children, excluded from the ministry of the national Church, his great public career long over. He was not writing theory. He was describing what he was trying, by grace, to become.

## A balancing word

Some readers have worried that this kind of spirituality might make Christians neglect their earthly duties, lost in thoughts of heaven while their neighbours go unhelped. Owen did not think so. He expected believers to work diligently at their callings and to love their neighbours in practical ways. What he opposed was not earthly business but earthly-mindedness: letting the things of this world fill the heart and crowd out God. A person can work hard at his trade with his mind set on things above. Indeed, Owen would say, that is the only way to work at it without being enslaved by it.

> Seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you. MATTHEW 6:33

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. For three days, pay attention to where your mind goes when it is free: while walking, waiting, or lying awake. Write down what you notice, without excuses.
2. Choose one truth about God or about the glory to come, and make it the subject of your free thoughts for a week.
3. Ask whether your religious feelings are mostly stirred by outward things or steadily renewed by the Spirit. Pray for the second.
4. If you are older, read Psalm 92 and ask God to make your last years your most fruitful ones. If you are younger, pray for an older believer you know.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You have said that to be spiritually minded is life and peace. You know how often my mind runs to other things.
> Renew my thoughts, so that when my mind is free it turns to You. Renew my affections, so that I love You for Yourself and delight in the things of Your Spirit.
> Set my heart on things above, where Christ is seated at Your right hand, and let that keep me free in the midst of my work and my cares.
> Let me bring forth fruit even in old age, and let my last years be spent in growing nearer to You. Amen.

# The Church and Liberty of Conscience

> And he is the head of the body, the church: who is the beginning, the firstborn from the dead; that in all things he might have the preeminence. COLOSSIANS 1:18

Owen is remembered today mostly for his writing on the inner life. In his own time he was just as well known as a churchman: a leader of the Congregational churches, a framer of their confession, and one of the most persistent advocates of liberty of conscience in seventeenth-century England. These were not side interests. They grew from the same root as everything else he taught: the conviction that Christ alone is Lord of His church and of the conscience.

This chapter gathers what he wrote on these subjects across many works, including his treatises on the nature of a gospel church, his writing on schism, and the many pamphlets he wrote after 1662 pleading for toleration for Dissenters.

## A gathered church

As a young pastor at Coggeshall, Owen’s study of the New Testament led him to the Congregational, or Independent, view of the church. In brief, he came to believe that a local church is a company of believers who have voluntarily joined together, under Christ, to worship Him and keep His ordinances; that each such congregation has from Christ all it needs to govern itself, choosing its own officers and exercising its own discipline; and that churches should hold fellowship with one another in counsel and love, without being ruled by any authority above them except Christ and His Word.

> For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them. MATTHEW 18:20

This was a minority view. Most English Puritans were Presbyterians, who wanted a national church governed by assemblies of elders, and the Church of England was governed by bishops. Owen argued his case with learning and some warmth, and in 1658 he was among the leaders of the assembly at the Savoy Palace which produced the Savoy Declaration, a confession of faith and order for the Congregational churches, closely based on the Westminster Confession but with its own account of church government.

## Christ alone appoints worship

Closely tied to this was Owen’s conviction that Christ alone has the right to appoint how He is to be worshipped. The church may order things of mere convenience, such as the time and place of meeting; but it may not add ceremonies or rites of its own devising and impose them on the consciences of believers as part of the worship of God. This was the heart of the Puritan objection to the imposed ceremonies of the Church of England, and it was one reason Owen could not conform in 1662.

In The Mortification of Sin he makes a related point in a different setting. Speaking of devotional practices that God has never appointed, he applies to them God’s question through Isaiah: “Who hath required these things at your hand?”

> When ye come to appear before me, who hath required this at your hand, to tread my courts? ISAIAH 1:12

Readers who worship in liturgical traditions will not share all Owen’s objections, and many devout Christians have found set forms of prayer a great help to their devotion. But his underlying concern deserves a hearing in every church: that nothing be made a condition of fellowship which Christ Himself has not required.

## Liberty of conscience

Owen wrote on toleration as early as 1649, in a short treatise attached to one of his sermons before Parliament, and he returned to it repeatedly for the rest of his life. His arguments were drawn from Scripture and from long experience.

Conscience, he argued, is subject to God alone. The civil power has its proper sphere, and may punish crimes against the peace of society; but it cannot compel belief, and when it tries, it produces not converts but hypocrites. Christ’s kingdom is not of this world, and it does not advance by the sword.

> Jesus answered, My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight. JOHN 18:36

After 1662, when he and his brethren were themselves the persecuted, he wrote a stream of pleas for indulgence. He argued that the Dissenters were loyal subjects who asked only to worship God according to their consciences; that persecuting them was unjust, unwise and contrary to the spirit of the gospel; and that the peace and prosperity of the nation would be better served by liberty than by force.

> Not for that we have dominion over your faith, but are helpers of your joy: for by faith ye stand. 2 CORINTHIANS 1:24

## The limits of his view

Honesty requires two qualifications. First, Owen’s idea of toleration was generous for his century but narrower than most Christians would hold today. During the Commonwealth, when he had influence, he supported liberty for orthodox Protestants of different kinds, but he did not extend it to those who denied the Trinity, and he did not favour the public practice of Roman Catholic worship. He believed the magistrate had a duty to uphold the fundamentals of the Christian faith. His arguments for liberty were, in the end, broader than his own application of them.

Second, it must be said plainly that Owen argued most eloquently for liberty when he and his friends were the ones denied it. That is not a charge of hypocrisy; his early treatise shows that his convictions on the matter predated his own persecution. But it is a reminder that every Christian, and every church, finds it easier to see the value of liberty from below than from above.

## Charity among the churches

There is another side to Owen’s churchmanship that is easily forgotten. In his writing on schism, he argued that the New Testament word refers to divisions within a church, among those who ought to be united, rather than to separation from a national establishment; and he pleaded for peace and charity among Protestants who differed on church order. He believed firmly in his own view of the church, but he did not unchurch those who disagreed.

> Endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace. There is one body, and one Spirit, even as ye are called in one hope of your calling. EPHESIANS 4:3–4

His friendship with John Bunyan, a Baptist who differed from him on baptism and on church membership, is a small picture of this. The learned Oxford divine and the unschooled tinker disagreed on important matters of church order, and yet Owen went to hear him preach, valued his ministry, and is said to have worked for his release from prison.

## What we may learn

Few Christians today will need to argue for toleration before a king. But Owen’s concerns remain. Christ is still the only Head of the church. Churches are still tempted to bind consciences with rules Christ never gave. Believers are still tempted to despise those who worship differently. And Christians who have known what it is to be in the majority still need to remember how it feels to be in the minority. Owen’s example, flawed as it was, points in a good direction: hold your convictions firmly, let others hold theirs, and do not ask the sword to do what only the Spirit can.

> We ought to obey God rather than men. ACTS 5:29

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Ask whether your church, or you yourself, has made anything a condition of fellowship that Christ has not required.
2. Think of a Christian tradition different from your own. Name one thing you could learn from it, and pray for its members this week.
3. Consider whether you have ever supported pressure on others’ consciences that you would resent if applied to your own.
4. If you belong to a local church, thank God for it, and consider what you can do to strengthen its fellowship and its care for its members.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus Christ, You are the Head of the church, and You alone are Lord of the conscience.
> Forgive us where we have bound one another with burdens You never laid on us, and where we have despised brothers and sisters who worship You differently.
> Keep Your church faithful to Your Word, and give us grace to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace.
> Strengthen those who suffer for conscience’ sake, and let Your kingdom advance, not by force, but by Your truth and Your Spirit. Amen.

# Conclusion

> Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy, to the only wise God our Saviour, be glory and majesty, dominion and power, both now and ever. Amen. JUDE 1:24–25

Fifteen chapters may leave the impression of a man with many subjects, and Owen did write on almost everything: on the atonement and the Trinity, on the Spirit and the church, on temptation and toleration, on Hebrews verse by verse. But underneath the mountain of his work there is one simple shape. God has called sinners into fellowship with Himself, Father, Son and Spirit. Sin spoils that fellowship, so sin must die. Christ has died to make both the fellowship and the dying possible. And the end of it all is to see His glory.

Follow the thread and the parts fall into place. Be killing sin, because the best of believers still carry a living enemy. Kill it through the Spirit, because every other remedy is self-made religion. Know what mortification is and is not, so as not to be fooled by a calm temperament or a traded vice. Hate sin as sin, and watch for the marks of danger. Load the conscience with the love you have wronged. Think greatly of God. Do not speak peace to yourself before He speaks it. And then look to Christ, crucified and full of grace, and expect Him to deliver you. Meanwhile enjoy the Father’s love, the Son’s grace and the Spirit’s comfort, and set your mind on things above.

## Where he must be read with care

This book has not hidden Owen’s limits. His style is heavy, and he could multiply distinctions until a reader loses the way. His view of the atonement, argued with great power, is not shared by many faithful Christians, and ought to be held, by those who hold it, with humility. He stood close to Cromwell’s government in a violent time and preached before a Parliament that had just killed its king. His polemic against Roman Catholics was often harsh, and his idea of toleration, broad for his age, was narrower than his own best arguments. And his searching analysis of the heart, taken the wrong way, can drive tender consciences to despair.

Owen himself saw that last danger, and ended his most searching book not with a demand but with Christ.

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21

## What to carry away

If you remember nothing else, remember two things.

First, do not make peace with your sin. There is no neutral day. Owen’s famous sentence is not the whole of him, but it is true: “be killing sin or it will be killing you.”

> And every man that hath this hope in him purifieth himself, even as he is pure. 1 JOHN 3:3

Second, do not fight alone, and do not fight in your own strength. The book that begins with the call to kill sin ends by sending the reader to Christ: to His fulness, His tenderness as a tempted High Priest, His blood, His Spirit. You will not win by resolutions. You will win by looking to Him, daily, and expecting Him to do what He has promised.

> Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever. PSALM 73:25–26

And hold these two together. Owen wanted his readers to kill sin because he wanted them to enjoy God. The surgery is for the sake of health, and the health is communion with a Father who loves, a Saviour who delights in His people and a Spirit who comforts.

## A last word about the man

Owen died at Ealing in August 1683, an old man by the standards of his day, worn out by illness and labour. He had governed Oxford and been excluded from every pulpit of the national Church. He had buried his first wife and nearly all his children. His last book was on the glory of Christ, and his last recorded words expressed joy that the day had come when he would see that glory as he never had.

He would not want to be admired. He called himself a poor under-rower in the ship of the church. What he would want is for his readers to go on where he pointed: to put sin to death, through the Spirit, by looking to Christ, and so to grow in fellowship with the God who had loved him with an everlasting love.

> But grow in grace, and in the knowledge of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ. To him be glory both now and for ever. Amen. 2 PETER 3:18

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Go back to the sin you wrote down at the start of this book. What has changed? What will you do next?
2. Choose the chapter of this book that searched you most, and work through its application points again.
3. Begin reading The Mortification of Sin in Believers for yourself, a few pages a day, with Scripture open and a prayer before you start.
4. Set aside a regular time each week, not for self-examination, but simply for enjoying God, Father, Son and Spirit.
5. Tell one other person what you have learned from Owen, and invite them to read him with you.

### A PRAYER

> Father, Son and Holy Spirit, thank You for calling us into fellowship with Yourself, and for Your servant John Owen, who knew both the depth of sin and the greater depth of Your grace.
> Keep us from making peace with any sin. Through Your Spirit, put to death what is evil in us, and make us look daily to Christ crucified for the power to do it.
> Let us not only fight but enjoy You: the Father’s love, the Son’s grace, the Spirit’s comfort.
> And bring us at last where Owen longed to be, to see the glory of Christ, not by faith but by sight, for ever. Amen.

# A Reader’s Guide to John Owen

> Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path. PSALM 119:105

Owen wrote an enormous amount. The standard collected edition, edited by William Goold in the middle of the nineteenth century, runs to some two dozen large volumes, seven of them on the Epistle to the Hebrews alone. All of it is in the public domain. No one needs to read it all, and very few have. What follows is a short map for the ordinary reader.

## Begin here

The Mortification of Sin in Believers (1656). The best place to start, and the book from which most of this companion is drawn. It is short, practical and searching, and it ends with Christ. It is in the Ochorus library. Read it slowly, a few pages at a time; it rewards patience more than speed.

Of Temptation: The Nature and Power of It (1658). The natural companion to the first, on Christ’s words in Gethsemane about entering into temptation.

Indwelling Sin (1667). The fullest of the three, and the hardest, on the deceit and power of the sin that remains in believers. These three works are often printed together, and a modern edition with helpful introductions and notes, Overcoming Sin and Temptation, edited by Kelly Kapic and Justin Taylor (2006), is a good way in.

## The warm centre

Of Communion with God the Father, Son and Holy Ghost (1657). For many readers this is Owen’s best book, and it corrects any impression that he was only a stern physician of the soul. Kapic and Taylor have also edited it in a modern edition, Communion with the Triune God (2007).

Meditations and Discourses on the Glory of Christ (1684). His last book, published after his death. Slow, meditative and deeply moving, it is best read a chapter at a time over many weeks.

The Grace and Duty of Being Spiritually Minded (1681). Written out of illness and old age, on the thoughts and affections of a renewed heart. Its test of the free thoughts is worth the price of the book.

## The larger works

Pneumatologia, or A Discourse Concerning the Holy Spirit (1674). A massive treatment of the person and work of the Spirit, and one of the most important works on the subject in English. Not for beginners, but the sections on regeneration and sanctification repay study.

The Exposition of the Epistle to the Hebrews (1668–1684). Owen’s great commentary, the labour of many years. It is a treasury for anyone studying Hebrews.

## Approach with care

The Death of Death in the Death of Christ (1647). Owen’s defence of particular redemption. It is the classic statement of that view, and many are glad of it; many others are not persuaded. Read it with the Scriptures open and with the cautions of this book in mind. J. I. Packer’s introductory essay, written for a mid-twentieth-century reprint, is a well-known and sympathetic introduction to it.

His controversial writings against the Arminians, the Socinians and the Roman Catholic Church belong to their age. They are learned and often acute, but they are harsh in tone, and the ordinary reader may leave them aside. The same is true of his sermons before Parliament during the Commonwealth, which should be read, if at all, with the history in view.

## About Owen

Andrew Thomson’s Life of Dr Owen, prefixed to the Goold edition of the Works (1850), is the classic older account, warm and still useful. Peter Toon’s God’s Statesman: The Life and Work of John Owen (1971) is a careful modern biography. Crawford Gribben’s John Owen and English Puritanism: Experiences of Defeat (2016) is a scholarly study that places Owen firmly in his troubled times and is frank about his politics. For his teaching on the Christian life, Sinclair Ferguson’s John Owen on the Christian Life (1987) is clear and faithful, and J. I. Packer’s A Quest for Godliness (1990) contains several fine chapters on Owen among the Puritans.

## Counsels for reading him

Read him slowly. Owen’s sentences are long and his arguments are layered. A page a day, understood, is worth more than a chapter skimmed.

Read him with a pencil. Mark the sentences that strike you, and copy the best of them. Many readers find that a handful of Owen’s sentences stay with them for life.

Read him on your knees. Owen did not write to inform but to change. The right response to a page of The Mortification of Sin is not to agree with it but to pray about it.

Read him under Scripture. He would insist on it. Every page he wrote is thick with the Bible, and he wanted his readers to go past him to the Word. Hold fast what agrees with it, and let go what does not.

> Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me. JOHN 5:39
