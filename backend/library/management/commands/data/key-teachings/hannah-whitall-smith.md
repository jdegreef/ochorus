# Introduction

> For ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God. COLOSSIANS 3:3

Hannah Whitall Smith wrote one of the best-loved devotional books of the last two centuries, and she wrote it for people who were tired. Not tired of God, exactly, but tired of themselves: tired of resolving and failing, of promising and forgetting, of carrying a religion that felt, as an agnostic once told her, like a man carries a headache. The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life appeared in 1875 and has not been out of print since. It has been read by Anglicans and Baptists, Methodists and Brethren, Catholics and Quakers, and it has been quietly pressed into the hands of the discouraged by people who remembered what it once did for them.

She was a Philadelphia Quaker, a wife and mother, a Bible teacher who never trained for the work, and for a few years in the 1870s one of the best-known Christian speakers in the English-speaking world. She was also a woman of unusual candour. She wrote plainly about her own spiritual failures, her long years of darkness, her impatience with pious language, and her griefs. She buried children. She watched her husband’s public ministry collapse and his faith drain away. She did not hide these things, and neither will this book.

## The one thread

Her teaching is easy to summarise because she summarised it herself, in a sentence near the start of her most famous book: man’s part is to trust and God’s part is to work. Everything else is that sentence worked out. The Christian cannot make himself holy any more than a lump of clay can make itself into a vessel. What he can do is put himself in the Potter’s hands, and keep himself there, and let the Potter work. Consecration is the handing over. Faith is the believing that God has taken what was handed over. The will is the part of us that does both, whatever the feelings may be doing at the time.

> For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure. PHILIPPIANS 2:13

But beneath that thread lies another, and it took her most of her life to see it clearly. Trust is only as sound as the one trusted. A soul cannot rest in a God it secretly suspects of being harsh, distant or selfish. So the later books — The God of All Comfort and her spiritual autobiography, The Unselfishness of God — turn from our side to God’s side. They ask what God is actually like, and they answer with the Shepherd, the Father who has a mother’s heart, the God whose will is good, and at last the God who is simply enough. The early Hannah tells you how to trust. The later Hannah tells you why you can. Read together, they make one argument: the believer rests because God is good.

> O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him. PSALM 34:8

That is the question to carry through these pages. Not, how do I feel about God today? but, what is God like, and have I handed myself over to Him?

## How this book is arranged

After a chapter on her life, eighteen short chapters set out her teaching. The first group follows The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life: the life hid with Christ in God, God’s side and man’s side, consecration, faith, the will, and the difference between facts and feelings. The second group takes up the difficulties she was famous for addressing — whether God is in everything, trials as chariots, guidance, temptation and failure, doubt, growth, obedience and oneness with Christ. The last group follows her later books into the character of God: the Shepherd, the God of all comfort, the unselfishness of God, and the God who is enough. A conclusion and a reader’s guide close the book.

Each chapter ends with application points and a prayer. She would have wanted that. She had little patience with religion that stayed in the head, and she measured every truth by whether it made an actual person in an actual kitchen more restful, more loving and more obedient. Read a chapter at a time. Stop at the application points. Pray the prayer, or better, pray your own.

## Where she must be read with care

This is not a book of hero-worship, and there are places where Hannah Whitall Smith must be read with care. It is kinder to say so at the start.

First, the movement she and her husband led was badly wounded in 1875, when Robert Pearsall Smith withdrew from public ministry amid accusations of improper conduct, and in later years he lost his faith altogether. None of this touches her own integrity, but it is a sober warning about a teaching of rapid victory that can run ahead of character, and chapter two tells the story without either sensation or evasion.

Second, in The Unselfishness of God she describes her conviction that God will at last restore every human being — what she called the restitution of all things. Many readers, reading the same Scriptures, will not share that hope, and the historic churches have not taught it. Chapter nineteen sets out what she believed and why, and where it outruns what is written.

Third, her own temperament was steady, cheerful and, by her own account, not naturally emotional. She sometimes wrote as though feelings could simply be set aside, and as though every doubt and discouragement came straight from the devil. That counsel has freed thousands. It has also, pressed too hard, made grieving or depressed believers feel that their pain is a sin. We will honour what she got right and say plainly where she is too brisk.

Fourth, she moved among holiness circles in which impressions, ecstasies and dramatic experiences were prized, and she saw where that could lead. To her great credit, she warned against it herself — sharply and often. On guidance, on emotion and on fanaticism she is one of the most sensible guides that movement produced, and we will lean on her own cautions.

So the counsel of this book is the counsel of Scripture:

> Prove all things; hold fast that which is good. 1 THESSALONIANS 5:21

Where she is faithful — on the sufficiency of Christ, on the folly of self-effort, on the goodness of God, on handing our burdens over and leaving them there — receive it gladly and put it into practice. Where she presses beyond what is written, mark it and set it aside. You will lose nothing, and she herself, who hated anything that smelled of a party or a system, would not have asked you to swallow her whole.

A last word. She wrote in the voice of a friend at the kitchen table, not a scholar at a desk, and her books are full of housekeeping and nurseries, servants and children, trains and bedrooms. That plainness was deliberate. She believed the Christian religion was meant to work on a wet Monday morning or it was not worth much. If this companion does anything, it should send you back to her own pages, and through them to the God she kept insisting was far better than we fear.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Write down, honestly, what you think God is like when things go wrong. Keep the page; you will return to it at the end of this book.
2. Name the burden you have been carrying longest by your own effort — a habit, a fear, a person, a failure.
3. Decide now how you will read: one chapter a day or a week, with a Bible open beside it.
4. Get hold of one of her own books, in the Ochorus library or elsewhere, and read it alongside this one.
5. Ask God to show you, as you read, where you have been trusting yourself for what only He can do.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I have called You good and lived as though You were not quite to be trusted.
> I have carried my own burdens, managed my own soul, and wondered why I am so tired.
> Teach me, through these pages and through Your Word, what You are really like, and give me the grace to hand myself over to You.
> Keep me from swallowing any teacher whole, and keep me from refusing any truth that comes from You. Amen.

# A Quaker in Search of God

> My soul, wait thou only upon God; for my expectation is from him. PSALM 62:5

A Quaker childhood. Hannah Tatum Whitall was born in Philadelphia on the seventh of February, 1832, into a prosperous and devout family of the Society of Friends. Her father, John Mickle Whitall, had gone to sea as a young man and sailed to China before settling into the glass business; her mother, Mary Tatum Whitall, came of the same plain, earnest stock. The home was Quaker through and through — the plain dress, the plain speech, the silent meetings, the thee and thy — and it was a happy one. In The Unselfishness of God, written when she was past seventy, she called her parents the most delightful any child ever had, and said that her first notions of her heavenly Father were learned from her earthly one. That childhood left her with two lasting gifts: a conviction that goodness is meant to be practical, and a readiness to believe that love at its best is a picture of God.

The long search. At sixteen something woke in her. It was not, she said, a sense of sin but a glimpse of the sheer magnificence of God, caught while a teacher read to her class about the stars. From that day her search began, and for ten years it went nowhere. She had been taught to look for God in the light within, and she turned her gaze inward on her own feelings, trying to work them up to the pitch she supposed God required. Her diary from those years is, by her own frank description, a long record of wrestling with hardly a ray of light. She could not repent to order, could not feel what she thought she ought to feel, and concluded that God must be a distant and exacting Being who would take notice of her only if she made herself worthy. She married Robert Pearsall Smith, of another Quaker family, in 1851, when she was nineteen. The widening of her life drew her out of her self-absorption, but not into faith. By her early twenties she had reasoned herself into scepticism, unable to reconcile a good and powerful God with the misery of the world.

The first discovery. In 1858 two things happened together. A little daughter of five died, and Hannah could not bear to think of her gone out alone into a godless universe. And that same year, the great noonday prayer meetings of the revival were crowding the business streets of American cities. She went into one out of curiosity. She heard nothing she could remember afterwards, but she came out convinced that God was a fact — the bottom fact of all facts — and that the one thing to do was to find out about Him. She took a Bible to the seashore that summer and nothing else, read Romans with a small explanatory book a visitor gave her, and discovered that salvation was the work of Another, received simply by believing what God had said. A Plymouth Brother had to tell her that this discovery meant she was a Christian. She had supposed she had merely found out some wonderful news. From then on she told it to everyone, in corners and behind doors, to the alarm of the quieter Friends.

The Brethren and the questions. For some years her teachers were the Plymouth Brethren. They gave her clarity, a love of the Bible and an orderly doctrine of justification, and she became a confident teacher of it. But their strict Calvinism troubled her deeply. She could not rest in a salvation that seemed to her narrow and selfish while so many had never had a fair chance, and she came in time to the hope that God would at last restore every soul He had made. That hope, which she held for the rest of her life, will be weighed in its place.

Millville and the second discovery. In 1865 family business took the Smiths from Germantown to Millville, New Jersey, where the Whitall glassworks stood. Hannah hated the move and knew she ought not to. She had been forgiven, she said, but she was not delivered: her inner life was full of irritation, coldness, anxiety and rebellion, and every older Christian she consulted told her that constant sinning and repenting was all anyone could expect. It was a dressmaker who first told her otherwise, and a little Methodist meeting of factory workers that showed her. There she heard a woman with a shawl over her head say that her whole horizon had once been filled with the great big Me of hers, and that it had shrivelled to nothing when she saw Christ as her perfect Saviour. Hannah went to teach and stayed to learn. What she learned she summed up in four words — not I, but Christ — and it became the fourth and, as she called it, the most captivating epoch of her life. Robert came into the same experience soon afterwards, in a more emotional way, at a holiness camp meeting.

The angel of the churches. The Smiths began to speak and write. Robert edited a paper, and Hannah’s Bible readings, plain and practical and unexpectedly witty, drew people who distrusted revivalists. In 1873 Robert went to England; Hannah followed. In a London drawing room she was questioned by evangelical ladies who had heard of her unorthodox hope, and she answered honestly. One of them, Mrs Cowper-Temple, later Lady Mount Temple, kissed her and invited her to Broadlands, the family’s house in Hampshire. The meetings there opened the way to larger conferences at Oxford in 1874 and Brighton in 1875, where thousands gathered from across Britain and the Continent. People called her the angel of the churches. Out of those meetings grew the convention at Keswick, first held that same summer of 1875, which has gathered Christians for the deepening of spiritual life ever since. That year, too, The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life was published as a book.

The fall. Within weeks of Brighton the work was in pieces. Robert had been reported by others for improper conduct toward a young woman, bound up with loose talk about spiritual experience, and the leaders of the movement quietly required him to withdraw from public work. He returned to America broken in health and never recovered his place. Over the following years he drifted from the faith altogether. Hannah stood by him and kept her own convictions, but the marriage never recovered its early happiness. It is right to say no more than is known, and right not to say less.

Graves and griefs. The 1870s were full of other sorrows. Her eldest son, Frank, died in 1872 at eighteen, and she wrote a short memoir of him. Later their daughter Rachel, still a child, died too. Of the seven children Hannah bore, four died before her. The three who lived — Mary, Logan and Alys — grew into gifted adults who moved far from their mother’s faith. She loved them without condition and did not pretend to be untouched.

Reform and England. Hannah was never only a devotional writer. She was active in the temperance cause and among the early leaders of the Woman’s Christian Temperance Union, spoke for the rights of women, and liked nothing better than an argument. In 1888 the Smiths moved to England to be near their married daughter, and there she spent the rest of her life, writing, entertaining a large circle of family and friends, and corresponding with anyone who asked for help. Robert died in 1898. In 1903 she published The Unselfishness of God, the story of her soul told in four epochs, and in 1906 The God of All Comfort, the ripest statement of her teaching. She had also been collecting, over many years, accounts of religious fanaticism she had witnessed, as a warning to others.

The end. In old age arthritis confined her to a wheelchair. She wrote that knowing God is and that God is enough made old age a resting place and death something to look forward to, and those who knew her said her cheerfulness never failed. She died on the first of May, 1911, aged seventy-nine. She had lost much — children, a husband’s faith, a public reputation, the hopes of her early ministry — and she was not defeated by any of it. What she left was not a movement or an institution but a handful of books that have gone on quietly telling tired Christians that the burden was never theirs to carry.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Hannah searched for ten years in the wrong place — her own feelings. Where have you been looking for assurance?
2. She learned her deepest lesson from factory workers she had meant to teach. Whom have you written off as having nothing to teach you?
3. Consider a grief or disappointment of your own. Has it pushed you away from God or toward the question of what He is really like?
4. Pray by name for someone close to you who has walked away from the faith, as her husband and children did, without despair and without pretending.
5. Read the first chapters of The Unselfishness of God, where she tells this story in her own words.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You were patient with a young woman who looked for You in all the wrong places, and You are patient with me.
> Thank You that You are a fact before You are a feeling, and that You find us before we find You.
> For those I love who have wandered, I ask not that You would make them like me, but that You would bring them home to You.
> Keep me faithful when public hopes fall and private griefs come, and let me finish, as she did, cheerful and unafraid. Amen.

# The Life Hid with Christ in God

> Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. MATTHEW 11:28

Hannah Whitall Smith’s movement went by many names. The Methodists spoke of the second blessing, others of the higher Christian life, and she herself preferred the plain phrase the life of faith. But the name she reached for when she wanted to describe it at its best was Paul’s: the life hid with Christ in God. In The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life she defines it with a picture rather than a formula, and the picture has outlived nearly everything else she wrote.

> For ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God. COLOSSIANS 3:3

## The man in the wagon

A man is toiling along a road with a heavy load on his back. A wagon comes by, and the driver kindly offers him a lift. He climbs up gratefully and sits down — and keeps the load on his shoulders. When the driver asks why he does not put it down, he answers that it is almost too much to ask to be carried himself; he could not think of letting the driver carry his burden too.

The story is funny, and it is meant to be. Its point is that most Christians have climbed into the wagon. They have trusted Christ with their eternal future, and they know they cannot keep their own souls. But they have kept the burdens of this life on their own backs: the health, the children, the business, the reputation, the future, the habits they cannot break. They sit in the wagon and sweat.

> Cast thy burden upon the LORD, and he shall sustain thee: he shall never suffer the righteous to be moved. PSALM 55:22

The first burden to lay down, she says, is not our circumstances but ourselves. “The greatest burden we have to carry in life is self.” Our moods, our temperaments, our temptations, our peculiar faults — these weigh more than any outward trouble, because they come with us everywhere. So the first act of the hidden life is to hand oneself over: here, Lord, is this self I have tried and failed to manage; You made it, You understand it, You take charge of it. Then everything else follows, down to the servants and the house.

## Taking the burden and leaving it

She tells of a woman worn down by a heavy trouble who picked up a tract about an old woman who had endured great sorrow. Asked how she bore it all, the old woman said she did not bear it; the Lord bore it for her. Most people, she added, take their burdens to the Lord and then bring them back again. She took hers and left them. When the worry returned, she took it back to Him, as often as it took, until it stayed.

> Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you. 1 PETER 5:7

There is nothing mystical in this. It is the difference between saying a prayer about something and actually letting go of it. Hannah did not claim that the feeling of anxiety vanished for ever the first time. She taught a habit: to hand the thing over, and when it creeps back, to hand it over again, without scolding oneself, until the handing over becomes the natural movement of the soul.

## The child in the Father’s house

Her second picture is the one Jesus Himself chose.

> And said, Verily I say unto you, Except ye be converted, and become as little children, ye shall not enter into the kingdom of heaven. MATTHEW 18:3

A little child, she says, provides nothing for himself and yet receives everything. He does not lie awake over tomorrow’s dinner or next year’s clothes. He runs in and out of his father’s house as though it were his own, because it is. Plague and famine may be spoken of in the streets, but under his father’s care he is at peace. She sums it up: “This life of faith, then, about which I am writing, consists in this: being a child in the Father’s house.”

> Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof. MATTHEW 6:34

She adds a picture of bedtime. After a long day, how good it is to lie down and let the bed hold us up, relaxing every muscle. But suppose we did not trust the bed and lay rigid all night in case it gave way — we would rise more tired than if we had never gone to bed at all. That, she says, is how many Christians rest in God: lying in His arms with every muscle clenched.

## What the hidden life is not

It needs saying, because Hannah’s pictures can be misread, that the life hid with Christ is not carelessness. The child in her illustration is thoughtless about his own provision, not about his father’s wishes; he is free because he is under authority. A Christian who hands his business over to God still opens the shop in the morning. A mother who hands her children over to God still feeds them and teaches them. The burden laid down is the anxious weight of ultimate responsibility, not the ordinary work of love. Hannah herself was an energetic reformer, a hardworking mother and a busy correspondent. She never imagined that rest in God meant lying in bed.

> Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus. PHILIPPIANS 4:6–7

Nor is it an escape from trouble. Paul’s word is that our life is hid — not that our troubles are. The Christian in the wagon still travels the same rough road. What changes is who carries the weight. The old woman in the tract had endured great sorrow; she was not spared it, she was carried through it.

## A rest that remains

The honest reader will ask whether this is realistic. Hannah would answer that it is the only realistic thing, because the alternative — managing an unmanageable self and an uncontrollable world by our own anxiety — has never worked for anyone. She had tried it for years. It did not make her holier; it only made her tired.

> There remaineth therefore a rest to the people of God. For he that is entered into his rest, he also hath ceased from his own works, as God did from his. HEBREWS 4:9–10

The rest she offered is not the rest of doing nothing but of ceasing from a particular kind of work: the work of being our own saviour, our own sanctifier and our own providence. That work belongs to God. When we give it back to Him, we find, often to our surprise, that we have more strength for the work that is really ours.

> Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because he trusteth in thee. ISAIAH 26:3

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name the burden you are carrying in the wagon. Say out loud to God that you are handing it over.
2. When the worry returns this week, do not scold yourself. Simply hand it over again, as often as it takes, and note how many times.
3. Begin with yourself: tell God plainly which part of your own temperament you have failed to manage, and ask Him to take charge of it.
4. Distinguish your duties from your burdens. Write down one thing you must still do and one weight you must stop carrying.
5. Tonight, as you lie down, use the bed as a lesson: let it hold you, and let God hold the day.

> I give You the people and the things I have been anxiously managing, and I leave them with You.

# God’s Side and Man’s Side

> But now, O LORD, thou art our father; we are the clay, and thou our potter; and we all are the work of thy hand. ISAIAH 64:8

The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life opens not with an exhortation but with a clarification, and it is one of the most useful things Hannah Whitall Smith ever wrote. She had watched two teachers of the higher Christian life hold meetings in the same place at alternate hours. One spoke only of what God does; the other spoke only of what the believer does. They were in perfect agreement with each other, and a good many of their hearers were thoroughly confused. One woman said she could not understand it at all: two preachers were teaching the same truth and seemed to contradict each other.

## Two sides of one building

Hannah’s answer was an illustration. Two friends visit a famous building. One walks round the north side, the other the south, and they come home and argue about what it looks like, until they realise they have described different sides of the same thing. So it is, she said, with sanctification. There is God’s side and there is man’s side. They are distinct and even contrasting, but they are not contradictory, and the Christian who sees only one will have a lopsided faith.

Then she gave the sentence that became the motto of her life: “In brief, I would say that man’s part is to trust and God’s part is to work.”

> For it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure. PHILIPPIANS 2:13

The reasoning is simple. A real work has to be done in us: sins conquered, habits broken, tempers made gentle, a character changed into the likeness of Christ. Someone must do it. Either we do it ourselves, or another does it for us. Most of us try the first way and fail. Scripture and experience agree that we cannot. But Christ came to do it, and He does it for those who trust Him. Trusting and doing are opposites when one person is involved; when two are involved, one trusting and the other doing, there is no contradiction at all.

## The clay and the potter

Her governing image is the potter’s house.

> And the vessel that he made of clay was marred in the hand of the potter: so he made it again another vessel, as seemed good to the potter to make it. JEREMIAH 18:4

If you describe to a beginner what the clay must do to become a vessel, there is only one thing to say: it must be put into the potter’s hands and lie there, yielding to his turning. That is all the clay can do. But no intelligent hearer concludes that nothing more happens. The potter kneads and presses, wets the clay and lets it dry, works it on the wheel, sets it aside for days, smooths it, bakes it, and brings it out at last a vessel fit for use. The clay’s part is yieldedness. The potter’s part is everything else.

> If a man therefore purge himself from these, he shall be a vessel unto honour, sanctified, and meet for the master’s use, and prepared unto every good work. 2 TIMOTHY 2:21

She insisted that critics who accuse the teachers of faith of encouraging idleness have simply not heard the other side. When we trust, the Lord works — and His work is very active indeed. He disciplines, trains and refines through inward exercises and outward providences, and He uses every circumstance of our lives to make us like Christ. A soul that thought faith meant a religious easy chair, she said, had understood nothing.

## A step and a process

Here she makes a distinction that rescues her teaching from its most common misreading. “Now, sanctification is both a sudden step of faith and a gradual process of works.” The step is ours: the moment of handing ourselves over, which can be as definite as a signature. The process is God’s, and it takes a lifetime. By a step of faith we get into Christ; by a process we grow up into Him in all things.

> But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory, even as by the Spirit of the Lord. 2 CORINTHIANS 3:18

To explain how a person can be pleasing to God and still very unfinished, she used the image of fruit. The apple in June is a perfect apple for June. It is not the apple of October. God’s works are perfect at every stage of their growth; man’s works are never perfect until they are complete. A new believer who has put himself wholly into God’s hands is, at that hour, what the Potter wants him to be — and still very far from what the Potter means to make.

## When the Potter’s way surprises us

The most searching part of the chapter is a pair of stories. A woman entered the life of faith with a great flood of joy and supposed she was being prepared for some large service. Instead her husband lost his money, and she was shut up at home with housework that left no time for anything else. She accepted it, and swept and baked and sewed as gladly as she would have preached, and the Lord made her through it a vessel fit for His use. Another woman, blessed in the same way, found herself confined to nursing two peevish invalid relations. She chafed, then rebelled, and fell into a cold misery. She had understood her part at the start, Hannah says, but not the Potter’s ways, and she took herself out of His hands.

> Whereunto I also labour, striving according to his working, which worketh in me mightily. COLOSSIANS 1:29

The lesson is that once we have handed ourselves over, we must expect God to begin working, and we must not be surprised if His methods are not ours. The wheel may turn in a kitchen rather than a pulpit.

## A necessary qualification

Hannah was writing, as she says herself, about man’s side, and she deliberately described that side in the barest terms — surrender and trust. The reader should notice that Scripture also speaks of our effort, and speaks of it without embarrassment.

> Wherefore, my beloved, as ye have always obeyed, not as in my presence only, but now much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling. PHILIPPIANS 2:12

> And beside this, giving all diligence, add to your faith virtue; and to virtue knowledge; 2 PETER 1:5

Paul does not tell the Philippians to lie still; he tells them to work, because God is working in them. The language of passivity — the clay lying still — is a true picture of dependence, but it is not the whole picture of obedience. Hannah knew this. Her own illustration of the saw and the carpenter makes the point: the saw cuts the log, and yet the carpenter cut it. We act, and our acting is His. She quotes the apostle himself.

> But by the grace of God I am what I am: and his grace which was bestowed upon me was not in vain; but I laboured more abundantly than they all: yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me. 1 CORINTHIANS 15:10

That is the balance: I laboured — yet not I. The reader who hears only the second half will become idle; the reader who hears only the first will become exhausted. Hannah’s gift to tired Christians was to shout the second half, because most of them had heard nothing else.

> For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them. EPHESIANS 2:10

1. Which side of the building have you mostly heard preached — God’s part or yours? What has that done to your faith?
2. Name one area where you have been trying to be the potter rather than the clay.
3. Take the step if you have never taken it: put yourself definitely into God’s hands for the whole work of your sanctification.
4. Look at your present circumstances, however ordinary, and ask whether this is the wheel on which God is shaping you.
5. Guard the balance: this week, do one hard duty diligently, consciously depending on God for the strength to do it.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I am the clay and You are the potter. I have spent years trying to shape myself, and I am tired and misshapen.
> I put myself into Your hands now, the whole of me, to be made into whatever You please.
> When Your ways surprise me, when the wheel turns in a kitchen and not a pulpit, keep me from taking myself back out of Your hands.
> Work in me to will and to do, and let me labour gladly, knowing it is not I but Your grace. Amen.

# Entire Consecration

> I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service. ROMANS 12:1

When Hannah Whitall Smith describes how to enter the life of faith, she makes a surprising move before she says anything about effort. She insists that this life is not something we reach but something we receive. In her words, we must not look upon it “as an attainment but as an obtainment.” It is a gift of God in Christ, and the only thing to do with a gift is to take it and thank the giver. Then she names the two conditions on which the gift is received. The first is consecration. The second is faith. This chapter is about the first.

## Why the whole case

Consecration, she says, is not a price we pay. It is simply the removal of the obstacle. A potter cannot make a vessel of clay he is not allowed to hold. So the soul that wants God to work in it must put itself wholly into His hands.

She tells of trying to explain this to a doctor who could not see the point. At last she asked him what he would do with a patient who begged to be cured but would not tell him all his symptoms, and who proposed to follow some of his directions and ignore the rest. The doctor answered indignantly that he would leave such a man to his own devices; a patient must obey implicitly or he could do nothing for him. That, she said, is consecration. “God must have the whole case, without reserves.” The doctor saw it at once, and said God should have His way with him from then on.

> My son, give me thine heart, and let thine eyes observe my ways. PROVERBS 23:26

She was aware that the word consecration can sound grim — a religious name for giving up everything we love. So she set out to show what she called the privilege side of it.

## The lovely will of God

The real reason Christians hesitate to hand themselves over, she believed, is that they suspect God’s will. They think that if they say Thy will be done, God will take the opportunity to make them miserable, to take away every comfort and send them to the hardest place on earth. She was ashamed, she wrote, even to set the thought down, and yet it was making hundreds of lives wretched.

Her answer was a story. A mother who feared to say Thy will be done was asked to imagine her only little boy running to her and saying that from now on he would let her have her way with him, because he trusted her love. Would she seize the chance to fill his life with hard and disagreeable things? The mother protested that she would hug him and fill his life with every good thing. Was she more loving than God? She saw her mistake.

> Saying, Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done. LUKE 22:42

The reader should notice that the prayer of Gethsemane is not a small thing. Our Lord prayed it in agony. Hannah does not pretend that surrender never costs; she insists that it never costs what we fear. Near the end of her life, in The Unselfishness of God, she went further still: once she had seen how good God is, consecration seemed to her not a lofty achievement for a devout few but a privilege that any sensible soul would seize. If a guide offered to lead you out of a trackless wilderness, would it feel like a sacrifice to say, lead on?

> I delight to do thy will, O my God: yea, thy law is within my heart. PSALM 40:8

## When nothing seems to happen

Having urged surrender, Hannah turned at once to the difficulty that follows it. A believer consecrates himself, as honestly as he knows how, and feels no different. Nothing seems changed. So he wonders whether he has really done it, and does it again the next day, and the next, for months.

Her diagnosis was blunt. The temptation at this point is always to wait for a feeling. But God’s rule, she wrote, is “faith first, feelings second, in all things.” She asked her readers to imagine giving an estate to a friend and then, because they felt no different the next morning, giving it again, and again the day after, for months. The friend would begin to doubt they had ever meant it, and the giver would no longer know whose the estate was. That, she said, is exactly how many Christians treat God.

> Notwithstanding no devoted thing, that a man shall devote unto the LORD of all that he hath, both of man and beast, and of the field of his possession, shall be sold or redeemed: every devoted thing is most holy unto the LORD. LEVITICUS 27:28

Under the old law, a thing once laid on the altar belonged to the Lord. It was not the giver’s sincerity that made the offering holy but the altar.

> Ye fools and blind: for whether is greater, the gift, or the altar that sanctifieth the gift? MATTHEW 23:19

So, she counselled, surrender yourself as fully as you know how. Ask the Holy Spirit to show you anything held back, and yield it when He shows it. Then count the transaction done. Do not reopen it every morning. Believe that God has taken what you gave, because He asked you to give it, and He does not ask for what He will not receive.

## A caution and a comfort

One of her rules needs care. She advised that if, on asking, the Spirit showed you nothing held back, you should conclude there was nothing. That can be a great mercy to a scrupulous conscience that invents new sins every hour. But it can also make us too quickly satisfied. Hannah’s own chapter on failure tells of a sealed cask left for years in her cellar that turned out to be breeding moths through the whole house. Consecration covers what we can see; God will show us more as we are able to bear it, and the handing over will be required again on new ground. That is not a failure of the first surrender. It is the Potter continuing His work.

> Neither yield ye your members as instruments of unrighteousness unto sin: but yield yourselves unto God, as those that are alive from the dead, and your members as instruments of righteousness unto God. ROMANS 6:13

And here is the comfort. The soul that has once given itself to God does not belong to itself any longer. That is not a demand; it is a relief.

> What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own? For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God’s. 1 CORINTHIANS 6:19–20

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. What do you secretly fear God would do with you if you gave Him the whole case? Write it down and look at it in the light of the mother and her boy.
2. Make a definite surrender, in words, and date it. Then refuse to reopen it tomorrow morning.
3. Ask the Spirit plainly: is anything held back? Yield what He shows you at once.
4. When feelings do not follow, say aloud, I am the Lord’s, because I gave myself and He has taken me.
5. Think of one concrete thing — a plan, a relationship, a possession — and say Thy will be done over it today.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have feared Your will as though You were waiting to make me miserable.
> Forgive me for thinking myself more loving than You. I give You the whole case, without reserves: body, soul and spirit, and all that belongs to me.
> I lay myself on Your altar, and I will not keep taking myself back because I do not feel different.
> Show me, as I am able to bear it, what I have still kept back, and give me grace to yield it gladly. Amen.

# Faith Is Only Believing

> He staggered not at the promise of God through unbelief; but was strong in faith, giving glory to God; And being fully persuaded that, what he had promised, he was able also to perform. ROMANS 4:20–21

After consecration, Hannah Whitall Smith says, comes faith. And here the earnest seeker often gets stuck in a peculiar way. Every teacher tells him that the life of trust is received by faith, and he agrees; but that is exactly his difficulty. He has no faith, he says. He does not know what it is or how to get it. He has prayed for it for years, and nothing has come.

## Faith is not a thing

Hannah’s answer, in The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life, is one of her most liberating. The trouble, she says, is that we imagine faith to be a kind of substance — a religious feeling or a gracious disposition, something we could look inside and find, and then offer to God like a coin. We pray for this thing, and when we cannot find it inside us, we conclude we have none.

But faith is not that sort of thing at all. “It is simply believing God, and, like sight, it is nothing apart from its object.” You might as well shut your eyes and look inside to see whether you have sight. You know you can see because you see something. You know you believe because you believe something. And then comes the sentence that has set many anxious souls free: “The virtue does not lie in your believing, but in the thing you believe.”

> Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness. ROMANS 4:3

Faith, then, is only the link. It is not the Saviour; it joins us to the Saviour. A weak faith in a strong Christ is worth more than a strong faith in anything else. The question to ask is never, how strong is my faith? but, how trustworthy is the one I am trusting?

## We trust all day long

She makes the point with gentle ridicule. We trust other people constantly and never think it remarkable. We eat what the cook has prepared without suspecting poison. We board a train and trust our lives to a driver we have never met. We read the newspaper and believe there is such a place as Ireland, though we have never been. Imagine a day, she says, in which we refused to trust anyone because we had no faith. We would starve at breakfast, walk everywhere, insult our friends, and end the day ridiculous and exhausted.

> If we receive the witness of men, the witness of God is greater: for this is the witness of God which he hath testified of his Son. 1 JOHN 5:9

Then she turns it round. If we can trust our fellow creatures, who may fail us at any moment, how is it that we say without embarrassment that we cannot trust God? She asked her readers, whenever they said I have no faith, to finish the sentence honestly: I have no faith in God. Said plainly, it sounds as terrible as it is.

> He that believeth on the Son of God hath the witness in himself: he that believeth not God hath made him a liar; because he believeth not the record that God gave of his Son. 1 JOHN 5:10

## The fact, the faith, the feeling

Her most memorable illustration came from the recent history of her own country. Emancipation had been proclaimed; the enslaved were free in law. But she told of a woman in a remote place who, years after the war, had never been sure of it. She had heard she was free, and asked her master, who said she was not; she had asked an officer, who told her to stay. So she lived between hope and fear. When at last someone told her plainly that it was true, and explained how it had come about, she believed it — and walked out free.

Nothing in her circumstances had changed. What changed was that she believed the proclamation. From this Hannah drew her famous order: first the fact, then the faith, then the feeling. Human nature, she said, reverses it and waits to feel free before believing it. But the fact came first; faith took hold of it; feeling followed.

> For unto us was the gospel preached, as well as unto them: but the word preached did not profit them, not being mixed with faith in them that heard it. HEBREWS 4:2

God has proclaimed our freedom. He has told us that sin shall not have dominion over us, that Christ is able to keep us, that we are His. Faith is simply taking Him at His word, and acting as though it were true, because it is.

> If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed. JOHN 8:36

## Trust and worry

Hannah added a test that is uncomfortably practical. “And remember, trust and worry are more incompatible than oil and water.” If we give a matter to a friend and then lie awake all night wondering whether he will manage it, we have not really trusted him. If we give our soul to the Lord and then spend our days anxious about it, the anxiety is the proof that we have not handed it over. By this rule, she said, there is very little real trust in the church.

> Nevertheless when the Son of man cometh, shall he find faith on the earth? LUKE 18:8

## A word for those who struggle

It must be said that Hannah can make faith sound easier than many people find it. She calls it the simplest thing in the world, and in one sense it is. But the man who cried out to Jesus for his son did not find it simple.

> And straightway the father of the child cried out, and said with tears, Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief. MARK 9:24

Jesus did not rebuke him for the tears or the mixture. He healed the boy. There are seasons — of illness, depression, bereavement — when believing is not a simple act of will but a long holding on, and a reader in such a season should not hear Hannah as saying that his struggle proves he does not care. Her real point survives even there: the strength of our grip matters less than the strength of the one we are gripping. A drowning man who clutches the rope feebly is still pulled out by the rope.

> Let us hold fast the profession of our faith without wavering; (for he is faithful that promised;) HEBREWS 10:23

Notice where the writer to the Hebrews puts the weight: not on the firmness of our holding but on the faithfulness of the one who promised.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Stop asking whether you have enough faith. Ask instead whether God is trustworthy, and answer from Scripture.
2. Finish the sentence honestly the next time you say I have no faith: I have no faith in God. Then take it back.
3. Choose one promise and act on it this week as a fact, before you feel it.
4. Use the worry test: what are you still lying awake over that you claim to have given to God?
5. If believing is hard for you just now, pray the father’s prayer and leave the rest to Christ.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have been looking inside myself for faith as though it were a coin I could offer You.
> Turn my eyes from my believing to You, the one I believe. You have proclaimed my freedom; let me walk out free.
> Where I trust other people more readily than I trust You, forgive me, and teach me to take You at Your word.
> I believe; help Thou mine unbelief. Amen.

# The Will Is the King

> Choose you this day whom ye will serve … but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD. JOSHUA 24:15

There is a difficulty, Hannah Whitall Smith observed, that comes to many people soon after they have given themselves to God. The first glow fades. They go on saying that they belong to the Lord, but it all feels unreal. Their surrender seems a matter of words; their faith seems shallow; they begin to fear that they are hypocrites, claiming what their hearts do not feel. In The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life she devotes a whole chapter to this, and her answer is one of the pillars of her teaching.

## Not in the emotions

The mistake, she says, is to think that the life hid with Christ is lived in the feelings. “Now, the truth is, this life is not to be lived in emotions. It is to be lived in the will.” She cites Fénelon, the French archbishop whose writings her father had given her as a girl, to the effect that pure religion resides in the will. By the will she does not mean a passing wish but the deciding power in us — the part that chooses, and to which everything else must finally submit. It is, she says, the man himself, the I that we recognise as our real self behind all our moods.

Our emotions belong to us, and we enjoy and suffer them, but they are not the self. They rise and fall with health, weather and circumstance. If God is to possess us, He must possess the centre, and the centre is the will. As the will is, so is the man.

> I have chosen the way of truth: thy judgments have I laid before me. PSALM 119:30

## The mother in the nursery

Her illustration is domestic, as usual. “The will is like a wise mother in a nursery; the feelings are like a set of clamoring, crying children.” The mother decides on a course she knows to be right. The children howl and protest. She does not panic or give way; she calmly holds her course, soothing them where she can, and in time they settle. But if she once believed the children were in charge, the nursery would descend into chaos. And so, Hannah says, do many souls, because they let their feelings govern.

She tells of a young man, a slave to doubting, to whom nothing in religion ever felt real. He was told that he could not control his emotions but could control his will, and that he could put his will on the believing side by saying I will believe, I do believe, and leave his feelings to follow. For some days his feelings accused him of hypocrisy at every turn, and he answered each accusation by choosing again. Then the struggle ended, and he became, she says, one of the most direct and powerful Christians she knew.

> If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether I speak of myself. JOHN 7:17

## Choosing against our own feelings

Two further stories show how this works under pressure. A woman met a great trial against which every feeling rebelled. Instead of waiting to feel submissive, she met each rebellious thought with the words Thy will be done, repeated as an act of choice, and before long even her feelings came round. Another woman had a besetting sin which, as she put it, she loved in her emotions and hated in her will. She knelt and told the Lord just that: with one part of me I love this, with my true self I hate it, and I put my will on Your side. She found herself delivered, not by an outward rule but by an inward power.

> Know ye not, that to whom ye yield yourselves servants to obey, his servants ye are to whom ye obey; whether of sin unto death, or of obedience unto righteousness? ROMANS 6:16

The great relief of this teaching is that it puts the Christian life within reach. Nobody can command his feelings, and a religion that required the right feelings would drive sensitive people to despair. But anyone can choose. The transaction with God, Hannah says, is as real when only the will acts as when every emotion agrees. It does not seem as real to us, but in God’s sight it is.

At the end of the chapter she printed a letter from a Presbyterian minister, written years before, who had covenanted to walk by faith and not by feelings, and found that God kept him in steady victory whether his feelings were high or low. The line has helped many people since.

> My heart is fixed, O God, my heart is fixed: I will sing and give praise. PSALM 57:7

## Where she needs balance

This chapter is Hannah at her most useful, and it also shows her most characteristic weakness. She says that throughout the Bible the heart means the will rather than the emotions. That is only partly right. The heart in Scripture is the whole inner person — thinking, choosing, desiring and feeling — and God asks for all of it.

> Jesus said unto him, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. MATTHEW 22:37

Feelings are not the governor, but neither are they mere noise. The psalms are full of feeling brought honestly before God: fear, longing, grief, delight. Sometimes our feelings tell us true things — that we are exhausted, that we are grieving, that something is wrong and needs attention. The psalmist does not ignore his downcast soul; he speaks to it.

> Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted within me? hope thou in God: for I shall yet praise him, who is the health of my countenance, and my God. PSALM 42:11

That is the right use of Hannah’s teaching. The will does not pretend the feelings are absent. It hears them, names them, and then turns them toward God and chooses His way anyway. Held like that, her counsel is a lifeline for anyone who has thought that unreal feelings meant an unreal faith.

> Keep thy heart with all diligence; for out of it are the issues of life. PROVERBS 4:23

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Where have you mistaken a lack of feeling for a lack of faith or surrender?
2. Put your will on God’s side in one matter today, in words: I choose Thy will in this, whatever I feel.
3. Name a sin you love in your emotions and hate in your will, and tell God so honestly.
4. When a feeling protests this week, speak to it as the psalmist did, rather than obeying it or pretending it is not there.
5. Write down the minister’s covenant — to walk by faith and not by feelings — and keep it where you will see it.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, my feelings are loud and changeable, and I have let them rule me.
> I give You the centre of myself, my will, and I choose You. I choose to believe, and I choose to obey.
> Where my emotions protest, let me hear them honestly and bring them to You, but not let them govern.
> Take the self You have made and, in Your time, bring every feeling into harmony with Your good will. Amen.

# Facts, Not Feelings

> For we walk by faith, not by sight. 2 CORINTHIANS 5:7

If one conviction runs through every book Hannah Whitall Smith wrote, it is this: the facts of the Christian faith matter more than our feelings about them. She learned it the hard way, over ten miserable years, and she spent the rest of her life trying to spare others the same mistake.

## A religion of trying to feel

In The Unselfishness of God she describes the religion of her youth as nothing but a religion of trying to feel. She had been taught to look within for the light of God, and so she watched her emotions constantly, trying to work them up into what she supposed God required. She never succeeded. Her question every day was not what God had said, but how she felt. She compared herself to a prisoner before a judge who spent all his energy examining his own feelings about the judge, instead of trying to learn what the judge felt about him. Or to a traveller who boarded the first train he saw and then shut his eyes to try to feel whether it was the right one.

The change came when she discovered that there were facts to be found out — that God had spoken, and that what He had said was true whether she felt it or not. Her own summary is worth quoting at length.

“You may feel good or you may feel bad, but neither the good feeling nor the bad feeling affects the real thing.” If God loves you, she went on, it makes no difference to the fact whether you feel it; it only affects your comfort. And after she had learned that the facts of religion were far more important than her feelings about them, she found she was always happy in her religious life, and that the very feelings she had failed to manufacture came of their own accord.

> But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name. JOHN 20:31

She loved to tell how Luther, when the devil asked whether he felt himself a child of God, answered that he did not feel it at all, but knew it.

## The map and the country

In The God of All Comfort she put the same point with a child’s joke. A boy who had been learning about Columbus told his father that he would not have gone to all that trouble; he would simply have looked on the map and found America. The boy had not understood that maps describe countries that already exist. America was not real because it was on the map; it was on the map because it was real. So with the Bible. Things are not true because they are in the Bible; they are in the Bible because they are true. When it says God loves us, it is describing a fact as solid as a coastline.

> Jesus saith unto him, Thomas, because thou hast seen me, thou hast believed: blessed are they that have not seen, and yet have believed. JOHN 20:29

A fact once seen becomes a conviction, and a conviction, she said, is sturdier than any experience. Once you know that two and two make four, no illness or bad weather can shake it.

## Conviction and emotion

Her time among the holiness camp meetings sharpened this. She went forward to the altar again and again seeking the overwhelming blessing others described, and it never came. “I am not naturally emotional,” she wrote. Her husband, of a warmer temperament, received the blessing in floods of joy. She was jealous at first. Then she came to see that the difference was not in God’s favour but in the two natures receiving the same truth, and she noticed that the emotional experiences of her friends were often less lasting than her plainer convictions. When trouble came, their feelings failed, and some were glad at last to stand on the ground of conviction they had once thought barren.

> For if our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart, and knoweth all things. 1 JOHN 3:20

This is sound and freeing teaching, and it has rescued many. The Christian whose assurance depends on a glow will lose it every time the glow fades. The Christian who rests on what God has said can walk through a very dark valley and still know whose he is.

> Who is among you that feareth the LORD, that obeyeth the voice of his servant, that walketh in darkness, and hath no light? let him trust in the name of the LORD, and stay upon his God. ISAIAH 50:10

## Where her temperament shows

Yet here more than anywhere the reader should notice her own temperament speaking. She once wrote that if she had her way, the whole subject of feelings in the religious life would be absolutely ignored. That is too much. A cheerful, steady nature can easily treat feelings as a nuisance to be stepped over; a person who has buried a child, or lives with depression, cannot simply ignore what he feels, and should not be made to think he ought to.

Scripture does not ignore feelings. It gives them a voice.

> How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me? How long shall I take counsel in my soul, having sorrow in my heart daily? how long shall mine enemy be exalted over me? PSALM 13:1–2

The psalmist does not hide his sorrow, and God does not rebuke him for it. Our Lord Himself wept at a grave. What the psalm does — and this is Hannah’s truth rightly held — is bring the feeling to God and then stand on the fact.

> But I have trusted in thy mercy; my heart shall rejoice in thy salvation. PSALM 13:5

So the right lesson is not that feelings are unimportant, but that they are not the foundation. They are weather; the facts are the ground. The weather is real, and a wise person dresses for it. But no one decides whether the ground is there by looking at the sky.

> We have not an high priest which cannot be touched with the feeling of our infirmities; but was in all points tempted like as we are, yet without sin. HEBREWS 4:15

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Where do you still ask, how do I feel? before you ask, what has God said?
2. Pick one fact of the gospel you find hard to feel — that God loves you, that you are forgiven — and write out the Scripture that states it.
3. If you are in a dark season, pray one of the lament psalms aloud, holding nothing back, and end where the psalm ends.
4. Consider your own temperament. Are you more likely to be ruled by feelings, or to dismiss them in others? Adjust accordingly.
5. Be gentle with a grieving or depressed friend this week; do not tell them to ignore what they feel. Remind them of what is true.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have lived by the weather of my feelings and wondered why my faith was so unsteady.
> Thank You that Your love is a fact, written down and sealed in Your Son, and that it does not rise and fall with my moods.
> When my heart condemns me, You are greater than my heart. When I walk in darkness, let me stay myself upon You.
> Let me neither despise my feelings nor obey them, but bring them honestly to You and stand on what You have said. Amen.

# Is God in Everything?

> But as for you, ye thought evil against me; but God meant it unto good, to bring to pass, as it is this day, to save much people alive. GENESIS 50:20

Hannah Whitall Smith knew that one objection brings more people to a halt in the life of trust than any other. It is easy enough, they say, to submit to what comes from God. But most of my trials come from people — from their carelessness, their ignorance, their unkindness, their sin. God cannot be the author of those things. So how can I say Thy will be done about them? And what is the use of trusting my affairs to God if any foolish or malicious person can walk in and spoil them?

She took the objection seriously. In The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life she wrote that if it could not be answered, the whole life of faith would become an impossible theory, because nearly everything in life reaches us through human hands.

## No second causes

Her answer was that, for the children of God, there are no second causes. Nothing reaches them except by their Father’s permission. She piled up the Scriptures: not a sparrow falls without Him, the hairs of our head are numbered, the Lord is our keeper, He shuts the mouths of lions.

> Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing? and one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father. But the very hairs of your head are all numbered. MATTHEW 10:29–30

Then she drew a careful line. “Man’s sin may cause the action. So, it can’t be called God’s will. But by the time it reaches us, it is God’s will.” The sin remains sin, and those who commit it are answerable for it. But it cannot touch us without first passing through the hands of God, and if He permits it, He means to use it for our good.

Joseph is her great example. Nothing could look more like the work of wicked men than his being sold into slavery by his brothers. And yet Joseph could look back and say:

> So now it was not you that sent me hither, but God: and he hath made me a father to Pharaoh, and lord of all his house, and a ruler throughout all the land of Egypt. GENESIS 45:8

## The presence that surrounds

She told of a woman at a meeting who had struggled with exactly this question and asked God to show her whether He was really in everything. She saw, as in a vision, a great light advancing toward her and wrapping itself round everything, and seemed to hear, this is the presence of God. Within it she saw armies, storms, wild beasts and wicked men; but every one of them was enveloped by that presence, and nothing could reach her unless the presence parted to let it through. Then she saw the small annoyances of daily life — a cross look, a sharp word — and they too were wrapped in God.

Hannah did not build doctrine on the vision; she used it as a picture of what Scripture already says. She pointed to Elisha’s servant, terrified by the Syrian army round the city, until his eyes were opened.

> And he answered, Fear not: for they that be with us are more than they that be with them. And Elisha prayed, and said, LORD, I pray thee, open his eyes, that he may see. And the LORD opened the eyes of the young man; and he saw: and, behold, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha. 2 KINGS 6:16–17

## I never supposes

Her favourite illustration of this teaching in practice was a poor woman named Nancy, who earned an uncertain living by daily labour and was always cheerful. A gloomy acquaintance tried to sober her: suppose you fell ill, suppose your employers moved away, suppose — and Nancy stopped her. “I never supposes.” The Lord was her shepherd, and she would not want; it was the supposing, she said, that was making her friend so miserable.

> Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee. So that we may boldly say, The Lord is my helper, and I will not fear what man shall do unto me. HEBREWS 13:5–6

Hannah claimed two practical fruits for this way of seeing. It makes us patient with those who annoy us, because we see them as instruments in the hands of a wise and loving Father. And it puts an end to grumbling, because Christians who would never dare to murmur against God feel quite free to murmur against people — until they realise that nothing reaches them from people that God has not allowed.

> And we know that all things work together for good to them that love God, to them who are the called according to his purpose. ROMANS 8:28

## Where care is needed

This teaching is strong medicine, and it must be taken rightly.

First, it does not make God the author of evil. Hannah said so plainly, and Scripture says so more plainly still.

> Let no man say when he is tempted, I am tempted of God: for God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man: JAMES 1:13

The cross is the supreme case: wicked hands crucified the Lord, and the same event was by the determinate counsel of God. Both are true, and neither cancels the other.

Second, accepting a trial from God’s hand does not mean accepting injustice passively. Joseph was a faithful servant in Potiphar’s house and in prison, but he also asked the butler to remember him and get him out. Seeing God in a situation does not forbid us from leaving an abusive home, reporting a crime, or fighting for the oppressed. Hannah herself spent years campaigning against drink and for the rights of women. God’s sovereignty over a wrong is not His approval of it, and it does not excuse us from resisting it.

> Learn to do well; seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow. ISAIAH 1:17

Held with those two cautions, her teaching is a fortress. The soul that believes nothing can touch it except through the hands of its Father can face almost anything.

> The LORD shall preserve thee from all evil: he shall preserve thy soul. The LORD shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in from this time forth, and even for evermore. PSALM 121:7–8

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think of a present trial that has come through another person. Say over it: this has passed through my Father’s hands.
2. Notice where you allow yourself to grumble about people that you would never grumble about God.
3. Follow Nancy’s rule for a week: when you catch yourself supposing the worst about tomorrow, stop.
4. Ask whether there is a wrong in your life you should resist or report rather than simply endure. Seeing God in it does not mean doing nothing.
5. Pray for the person who has hurt you, as an instrument in God’s hand and as someone accountable to Him.

### A PRAYER

> Father, open my eyes, as You opened the eyes of Elisha’s servant, to see that Your presence surrounds me.
> Nothing can reach me except through Your hands. What others mean for evil, You can turn to good, and I trust You to do it.
> Keep me from grumbling at people I would never dare to grumble at You, and give me patience with those who try me.
> Where I should resist wrong, give me courage; where I must endure, give me peace; and in both, let me find You. Amen.

# The Chariots of God

> The chariots of God are twenty thousand, even thousands of angels: the Lord is among them, as in Sinai, in the holy place. PSALM 68:17

Of all Hannah Whitall Smith’s images, the one that has travelled furthest is the chariot. It appears first in a few lines of The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life — “Our trials may be our chariots” — and then fills a whole chapter of its own. The idea is simple, a little startling, and very practical.

## Chariots in disguise

Chariots, she says, are for carrying people. Earthly chariots carry their passengers over rough roads to where they want to go. God’s chariots carry souls, and they carry them to heights they could never reach on foot. And they are everywhere around us — but they do not look like chariots. They look like enemies, disappointments, losses, misunderstandings and unkindnesses. They look like great cars of misery waiting to crush us.

So her prayer is Elisha’s prayer for his servant: Lord, open our eyes, that we may see. And then comes the sentence on which the whole chapter turns: “We may make out of each event in our lives either a Juggernaut car to crush us, or a chariot in which to ride to heights of victory.” It all depends, she says, on whether we lie down under our trials and let them roll over us, or climb up into them and let them carry us.

> Now no chastening for the present seemeth to be joyous, but grievous: nevertheless afterward it yieldeth the peaceable fruit of righteousness unto them which are exercised thereby. HEBREWS 12:11

## The slow servant

As always, she brought the idea down to the kitchen. She knew a woman of a naturally quick temper who had a servant, excellent in every way, but maddeningly slow. The mistress lost her temper twenty times a day, repented twenty times, and made no progress at all. Then it occurred to her that she had long been praying for patience, and that perhaps this slow girl was the very chariot the Lord had sent to carry her into it. She accepted her as such — and found a patience that no one’s slowness could disturb again.

Another woman, at one of their crowded conventions, had to share a room with two others who wanted to talk late into the night when she wanted to sleep. The first night she lay fuming. The next day she heard about God’s chariots, and that night she decided the talkers were hers. She lay peaceful. At last, very late, she said quietly into the dark, “Sisters, I’m lying here riding in a chariot.” There was complete silence, and she won her victory inside and out.

The point is not that trials are pleasant. It is that the trial we are praying to be rid of may be the very answer to what we have been praying for. If we ask for patience, God rarely sends a parcel of it; He sends a slow servant.

> My brethren, count it all joy when ye fall into divers temptations; Knowing this, that the trying of your faith worketh patience. But let patience have her perfect work, that ye may be perfect and entire, wanting nothing. JAMES 1:2–4

## Paved with love

She loved a detail from the Song of Solomon, where the king’s chariot is described as paved with love.

> He made the pillars thereof of silver, the bottom thereof of gold, the covering of it of purple, the midst thereof being paved with love, for the daughters of Jerusalem. SONG OF SOLOMON 3:10

Your own chariot, she said, may look very unlovely. It may be a difficult relative, or the result of someone’s neglect or malice. But any chariot God sends must be paved with love, because God is love, and it is His love that sends it.

## Our chariots and His

Then she turned to a harder truth. We are always tempted to trust in chariots we can see — a friend who helps us, a favourite preacher, a Bible class, our own religious routine. These are good gifts, but we lean our whole weight on them, and so God often has to take them away before we will climb into His.

> Some trust in chariots, and some in horses: but we will remember the name of the LORD our God. PSALM 20:7

She put it memorably. We say, He and something else — He and my experience, He and my church, He and my Christian work. Everything after the and must be taken away, or shown to be useless, before we can say, He only. As long as visible chariots are at hand, the soul will not climb into the invisible ones.

## How to climb in

How, then, does one get into the chariot? Her answer was plain: “Find out where God is in each one of them, and hide yourself in Him.” Do what a small child does when trouble comes — run to its mother and hide in her arms. The real chariot, in the end, is the carrying of God.

> And even to your old age I am he; and even to hoar hairs will I carry you: I have made, and I will bear; even I will carry, and will deliver you. ISAIAH 46:4

## A word of care

This teaching can be misused. Said glibly to someone in fresh grief — your bereavement is a chariot, climb in — it is cruel. Hannah buried four children; she knew what suffering was, and her words were meant for use on oneself, not as a verdict on others. Paul, whose thorn she cites, prayed three times for it to be taken away before he learned to glory in it. There is a time for asking God to remove the trial, and a time for weeping, before there is a time for riding.

> Therefore I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ’s sake: for when I am weak, then am I strong. 2 CORINTHIANS 12:10

But when the time comes, the invitation stands. The very thing that looks set to crush us can carry us somewhere we could not otherwise have gone.

> In all their affliction he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them: in his love and in his pity he redeemed them; and he bare them, and carried them all the days of old. ISAIAH 63:9

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. What have you been praying for — patience, humility, trust? Look for the chariot God may have sent in answer.
2. Name the difficult person or circumstance in your life, and say, deliberately, this is my chariot.
3. Ask what visible chariots you are leaning your whole weight on, and whether God is loosening your grip on them.
4. In your next trial, before anything else, ask: where is God in this? Then hide yourself in Him.
5. Resolve never to tell someone in fresh grief that their sorrow is a chariot. Weep with them, and pray.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, open my eyes, that I may see Your chariots where I have seen only enemies and misery.
> I have asked You for patience and complained at the slow servant You sent. Forgive me, and help me climb in.
> Take away the chariots I trust in more than You, until I can say, He only.
> Carry me, as You have carried Your people all the days of old, and let me find that even this is paved with love. Amen.

# The Four Voices of Guidance

> I will instruct thee and teach thee in the way which thou shalt go: I will guide thee with mine eye. PSALM 32:8

Hannah Whitall Smith grew up among people who took divine guidance with absolute seriousness. In The Unselfishness of God she describes how, among the Friends, a child who said she felt a leading from God was treated with tender respect, and how an inward concern could silence every objection. She loved that inheritance. She also saw its dangers, and her chapter on guidance in The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life is one of the wisest things she wrote, precisely because it is written by someone who believed in guidance and had watched it go wrong.

## Guidance is promised

She begins with confidence. God has promised to guide His people, and the soul that is wholly surrendered — she stresses wholly, since any secret resistance clouds the question — may expect Him to do it.

> And when he putteth forth his own sheep, he goeth before them, and the sheep follow him: for they know his voice. JOHN 10:4

> If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God, that giveth to all men liberally, and upbraideth not; and it shall be given him. JAMES 1:5

She adds a warning that is easy to skip. A true leading may sometimes take us against the wishes of those we love, even of older and wiser Christians, because God sees further than they do. But when it does, she says, it will always be carried out in the Master’s spirit — with gentleness, patience and love toward those we are obliged to disappoint. A leading that makes us harsh is suspect from the start.

## The four voices

Then comes the heart of her teaching. God speaks, she says, in four ways: through Scripture, through the inward impressions of the Holy Spirit, through our own enlightened judgement, and through providential circumstances. And because God cannot contradict Himself, His voice in any one of these will always agree with His voice in the others. If the voices conflict, the speaker is not the same.

So her rule was to test every impression. Does it agree with Scripture? Does my sanctified common sense approve it? And — as the Quakers say — does way open to carry it out? If any test fails, it is not safe to go forward. Wait quietly until the Lord brings the voices into harmony, as He will, if it was His voice at all.

> To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them. ISAIAH 8:20

## Other voices

Why such caution? Because, she says, God is not the only one who speaks to the soul. There is the loud and clamouring voice of self. And there are evil spirits who communicate, as the Holy Spirit does, by inward impressions — and who do not sign their names. An impression, taken by itself, proves nothing about its source.

> Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God: because many false prophets are gone out into the world. 1 JOHN 4:1

She had seen what happens when this is forgotten. Earnest Christians, meaning to honour the Spirit, have disregarded the plain teaching of the Bible, ignored clear providences and outraged their own judgement, and though God forgives the sincere, the consequences in this life are often sad. “I have met Christians who overlooked it and became fanatical,” she wrote. She told of a woman at a meeting who took the text we walk by faith, not by sight as a literal command, blindfolded herself, walked up and down the aisle to illustrate it, stumbled against the stove and burned herself — and then wondered at the mysterious dispensation.

## Scripture first, and common sense

Her first test is always Scripture. We need no special revelation to tell us not to steal; God has already spoken. And the Bible, she says, is fuller of practical direction than most people imagine — on speech, on forgiveness, on money, on anxiety, on dress. Many who seek a private leading have simply not read what is written.

> Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path. PSALM 119:105

But she adds that the Bible is a book of principles, not of isolated sayings, and a single text torn from its setting can be made to sanction almost anything. That is why her second test is common sense. She asked a clear-headed Christian woman how she told the Holy Spirit from false spirits, and received the reply: “I rap them over the head and see if they have any common sense.” God guides us through the faculties He gave us, not independently of them. We must use our eyes to walk, however great our faith.

> For God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind. 2 TIMOTHY 1:7

Her third test is providence. If the Lord goes before His sheep, He opens the way; the Christian who has to batter down every door to follow a leading should suspect it. Her fourth voice is the inward one, and she did not despise it. One Christian gave her a simple secret: “I always mind the checks.” A sense of hesitation, a stop in the mind, should make us wait.

## The sense of oughtness

When all four voices agree, she says, there comes a quiet, settled sense of oughtness, and that is the only safe foundation for action. Where doubt remains, wait; whatsoever is not of faith is sin. And where the path is clear, go at once, before hesitation turns into reasoning and reasoning into disobedience.

> For God is not the author of confusion, but of peace, as in all churches of the saints. 1 CORINTHIANS 14:33

Hannah’s own movement was later troubled by exactly the dangers she describes here: impressions treated as oracles, experiences prized above Scripture, strong personalities claiming direct leadings. Her chapter was a warning written in advance, and it remains one of the best short guides to the subject. Guidance is a wonderful privilege, she says at the end: that God should care enough about the details of our lives to direct them is proof of His love. But love that guides is never frantic, never contrary to His Word, and never at war with a sound mind.

> Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths. PROVERBS 3:5–6

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Think of a decision in front of you. Test it by the four voices: Scripture, enlightened judgement, providence, and the inward witness.
2. Before seeking a special leading, ask whether the Bible has already spoken on the matter.
3. Recall a time you or someone you know acted on an impression that proved false. What test was skipped?
4. Mind the checks: if you feel a stop in your mind about something, wait rather than push through.
5. If a leading means disappointing someone you love, examine whether you are carrying it out in the Master’s spirit.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You have promised to guide me, and You are not the author of confusion.
> Keep me from mistaking the loud voice of my own wishes for Yours, and from every spirit that is not of You.
> Speak to me through Your Word, through the mind You have given me, through the circumstances You arrange, and in the quiet of my heart.
> Where the way is clear, make me quick to obey; where it is not, make me willing to wait. Amen.

# Temptation and Failure

> There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man: but God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape, that ye may be able to bear it. 1 CORINTHIANS 10:13

Every teaching about victory over sin has to face two awkward facts. Christians who have given themselves wholly to God are still tempted, and sometimes they still fall. Hannah Whitall Smith faced both squarely in The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life, in two chapters that have probably helped more people than anything else she wrote, because they meet us exactly where the brighter chapters leave off.

## Temptation is not sin

The first mistake, she says, is to expect that after we have entered the rest of faith, temptation will stop. When it comes back — often stronger than before — the soul concludes that it must have been mistaken, and that this cannot be the promised land after all. But Israel met far more enemies inside Canaan than in the wilderness: walled cities, giants and thirty-one kings. Strong temptations, she says, are more often a sign of great grace than of little.

The second mistake is worse: to think that being tempted is itself a sin. The enemy whispers some evil thought, and then accuses us of it — how wicked you must be to think such a thing! — like a burglar who, when the owner resists him, shouts that the owner is the thief. So the soul feels condemned, grows discouraged, and a discouraged soul falls easily.

Her answer was plain. “It is no more a sin to hear evil whispers in our souls than to hear the swearing of bad men as we pass along the street.” The sin comes only when we stop, listen and join in. She told of a sheltered woman who was horrified by the dreadful thoughts that rushed in whenever she knelt to pray, and concluded she could never have been born again. When she learned to recognise them as the enemy’s and not her own, she turned her back on them, told him to take them to the Lord, and was set free.

> Blessed is the man that endureth temptation: for when he is tried, he shall receive the crown of life, which the Lord hath promised to them that love him. JAMES 1:12

“The power of temptation is in the fainting of our own hearts,” she wrote, and so she prized cheerfulness as a weapon. She passed on an allegory of the devil’s council: tempting a good man with pleasure will not work, nor with the hardness of virtue — but discourage him, and he is ours.

## When we fall

Then she turned to failure. She did not pretend that trusting Christians never sin. “We are not preaching a state, but a walk.” Holiness is not a place we reach and then own for ever; it is a road we walk day by day, and we may step off it. What matters is what we do next.

A believer who falls is tempted to one of two responses: to give up, concluding that the whole thing was a delusion; or to hide the sin, calling it an infirmity to protect his doctrine. Both are fatal. The only way is honesty and an instant return. “Our sin is no reason for ceasing to trust, but only an unanswerable argument why we must trust more fully than ever.”

> If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins, and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness. 1 JOHN 1:9

When Israel was defeated at Ai, Joshua lay on his face in despair until evening. God’s response was brisk.

> And the LORD said unto Joshua, Get thee up; wherefore liest thou thus upon thy face? JOSHUA 7:10

Hannah’s gloss is memorable: “Up, sanctify the people,” is always God’s command; “Lie down and be discouraged” is always the enemy’s temptation.

## Three years and five minutes

She told two stories side by side. A man who had known a time of real victory was overcome by a temptation to treat a brother unkindly. He concluded he had never truly trusted, then that he had never been born again, and spent three years in misery, drifting from sin to sin. At last a woman asked him whether he had confessed it. He had done nothing but confess it for three years. Had he ever believed God forgave him? No, he had never felt that He did. She read him First John, and in a few minutes he saw that God had said it all along.

The other was a woman, two weeks into her new life, who was suddenly overtaken by a violent fit of anger. She ran to her room still boiling, knelt and confessed it — I am sinning even now, I hate it and cannot get rid of it — and said aloud that she believed God did forgive and cleanse. Deliverance came as she spoke. The sin and its cure had taken five minutes.

> Rejoice not against me, O mine enemy: when I fall, I shall arise; when I sit in darkness, the LORD shall be a light unto me. MICAH 7:8

## The cask in the cellar

But she also pressed the reader to ask why a failure happened. Israel fell at Ai because of hidden sin in the camp. So she told of a clean-looking cask she had left unopened in a new house because it was too much trouble to carry up the stairs, though her conscience twinged every spring. Years later her house was overrun by moths. She suspected everything except the cask. When at last she had it opened, out they poured. Some small indulgence, left unexamined because it seemed harmless, lies behind many repeated failures.

> Search me, O God, and know my heart: try me, and know my thoughts: And see if there be any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting. PSALM 139:23–24

## Where she needs filling out

Two cautions. First, she repeats an old saying that all discouragement is from the devil. As a weapon against morbid self-accusation, it is useful. As a rule, it is too sweeping. Some discouragement is simple tiredness, and some is the right grief of a conscience that has done wrong.

> For godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death. 2 CORINTHIANS 7:10

Second, instant forgiveness from God does not cancel what we owe to people. If the sin wronged someone, returning to God includes making it right with them. Her stories are true to experience, but they are not a formula; Peter’s restoration after his fall took more than five minutes, and the Lord prayed for him through it.

> Now unto him that is able to keep you from falling, and to present you faultless before the presence of his glory with exceeding joy, JUDE 1:24

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Distinguish this week between the thought that comes and the thought you entertain. Refuse to feel guilty for the first; turn away from it at once.
2. Watch for discouragement after temptation, and treat it as a second temptation.
3. Next time you fail, confess immediately — within the minute — and believe you are forgiven because God says so.
4. Open your cask: name one small indulgence your conscience has twinged about for years, and bring it into the light.
5. If your failure hurt someone, go and put it right before you consider the matter closed.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have mistaken the enemy’s whispers for my own heart and let him discourage me.
> When I am tempted, let me turn at once to You. When I fall, keep me from lying on my face, and from hiding my sin.
> I confess it now, and I believe that You are faithful and just to forgive me and to cleanse me.
> Search me and show me whatever I have left unopened, and give me the courage to bring it out before You. Amen.

# The Luxury of Doubting

> Let not your heart be troubled: ye believe in God, believe also in me. JOHN 14:1

It is strange, Hannah Whitall Smith remarked, that people whose very name is believers should have to confess to so much doubting. The habit is so common that if the church were renamed by its most obvious characteristic, doubters might be the fitting word. In The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life she gave a whole chapter to doubt, and it is the most vigorous thing in the book. She did not console doubters. She told them to stop.

## The kind of doubt she meant

It matters which doubts she had in view. She was not writing about honest intellectual questions; she had passed through years of scepticism herself and wrote about them with sympathy in The Unselfishness of God. Her target was something else: the settled habit of doubting God’s love and faithfulness toward oneself. It is the Christian who says, yes, I believe my sins are forgiven — but then; and after the but comes a doubt about every promise God has made. These believers, she says, see themselves as interesting cases of rare spiritual conflict. She thought a truer name would be spiritual rebellion.

> And immediately Jesus stretched forth his hand, and caught him, and said unto him, O thou of little faith, wherefore didst thou doubt? MATTHEW 14:31

## A luxury, not a trial

Her most searching claim was this: “I am convinced that, for many, doubting is a luxury.” Most of us would protest that our doubts are a misery, not an indulgence. But try giving them up, she said, and see. Do our doubts not come to us like sympathetic friends who understand our troubles? Is it not a luxury to sit with them and listen to their condolences? She compared it to brooding over an injury someone has done us — making us wretched, and yet a wretchedness we find strangely hard to give up.

And the most subtle doubts wear the costume of humility. We do not accuse God of injustice; we simply feel that we are too wicked for Him to care, or too complicated for Him to manage. But this, she says, is still a hard thought about the Lord. He came to save sinners. Our unworthiness is not a bar to His love; it is our claim on it.

> But when Jesus heard that, he said unto them, They that be whole need not a physician, but they that are sick. MATTHEW 9:12

> What man of you, having an hundred sheep, if he lose one of them, doth not leave the ninety and nine in the wilderness, and go after that which is lost, until he find it? And when he hath found it, he layeth it on his shoulders, rejoicing. LUKE 15:4–5

## A mother’s indignation

She told of a mother who left two little girls at Hannah’s house while she did errands. One played happily all afternoon. The other sat in a corner and worked herself into a frenzy, sure that her mother would forget her, or would be glad to be rid of so naughty a child. When the mother returned and heard it, Hannah watched grief, wounded love, pity and indignation fight for her face — and indignation won. That scene, Hannah said, had come back to her a hundred times, and it taught her to refuse the doubts that knocked at the door of her own heart.

> Yea, they spake against God; they said, Can God furnish a table in the wilderness? PSALM 78:19

## Taking the pledge

How, then, is one delivered? The same way as from any sin, she said: by handing it to the Lord. And because the stronghold is in the will, she advised something like a temperance pledge. Many people say, I hope I shall not doubt any more. Few say, I will not. But no surrender is effectual until it reaches the point of I will not. The liberty to doubt must be given up for ever.

Then, when the first doubt comes, do not argue with it or try to disprove it. Lift the shield of faith at once, and say something true. Her own formula was three words, repeated as often as needed: Jesus saves me. She said she had tried it more times than she could count and had never known it to fail.

> Above all, taking the shield of faith, wherewith ye shall be able to quench all the fiery darts of the wicked. EPHESIANS 6:16

The best illustration in the chapter is her own little daughter, now with the Lord, who announced one night at bedtime that she had had her first doubt: Satan had told her the Bible was not true, and she had told him she would believe it anyway. The next night he told her she was too naughty for Jesus to love. What did she say this time? “Satan, shut your mouth!” — and he could not make her unhappy one bit. A grander battle, said her mother, no soul ever fought.

> When the enemy shall come in like a flood, the Spirit of the LORD shall lift up a standard against him. ISAIAH 59:19

## Where to be gentle

The reader should take Hannah’s vigour for what it is: strong medicine for a habit, not a verdict on everyone who struggles. She wrote that doubts are all from the devil and always untrue, and that is too strong. Some doubts are honest questions that deserve honest answers. The Lord did not rebuke Thomas for wanting evidence; He offered it, and then called him to believe.

> Then saith he to Thomas, Reach hither thy finger, and behold my hands; and reach hither thy hand, and thrust it into my side: and be not faithless, but believing. JOHN 20:27

And some doubting comes from depression, trauma or long grief, where the will is not the only thing at work and a pledge alone will not mend it. Scripture tells us to deal differently with different people.

> And of some have compassion, making a difference: JUDE 1:22

But with those cautions, her central point stands. Many Christians have made a home for doubts that deserve eviction. They are not a sign of depth. They grieve a Father who has given every proof of His love, and they rob His children of the peace He means them to have.

> Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you. Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid. JOHN 14:27

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Name the doubt you return to most often about God’s love for you. Is it an honest question or a comfortable habit?
2. Try going one week without entertaining it, and notice whether giving it up feels like a loss.
3. If you are ready, write out a pledge: I will not doubt my Father’s love. Sign it before God.
4. Choose a short true sentence to say whenever doubt knocks — Jesus saves me, or a verse — and use it without arguing.
5. If you are struggling with honest questions or with depression, tell a wise Christian friend rather than fighting alone.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I have entertained doubts about Your love and called them humility.
> Forgive me for grieving You like a child in the corner who thinks her mother will forget her.
> I give You my liberty to doubt. When the first doubt comes, let me lift the shield of faith and look at You.
> Where my questions are honest, answer them; where my heart is weary, carry me; and let me rest in the love You have proved. Amen.

# Consider the Lilies

> But grow in grace, and in the knowledge of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ. To him be glory both now and for ever. Amen. 2 PETER 3:18

Critics of the higher life teaching often said that it left no room for growth. If a Christian could enter into rest by a single act of faith, what was left to do? Had these teachers not abolished the long, slow work of sanctification? Hannah Whitall Smith answered the charge directly in The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life. Entering the life of trust, she said, is not the end but the beginning. No one believed in growth more than she did. What she denied was that most Christians were actually growing.

## Twenty-five years of growing

She told of urging a group of Christians to take a definite step of faith, when an intelligent woman interrupted her with what she thought was a decisive reply: but I believe in growing in grace. How long had she been growing? About twenty-five years. And how much more devoted to the Lord was she now than when she began? The woman was honest. She feared she was not nearly as devoted as at the start.

Hannah’s diagnosis was a single phrase: “they are trying to grow into grace, instead of in it.” They are like a rosebush planted in a stony path in the hope that it will grow its way into the flower bed. It will wither first. Or like Israel in the wilderness, walking for forty years and ending no nearer the land than at the start — indeed, with a river to cross that had not been there at Kadesh.

> Blessed is the man that trusteth in the LORD, and whose hope the LORD is. For he shall be as a tree planted by the waters, and that spreadeth out her roots by the river, and shall not see when heat cometh, but her leaf shall be green; and shall not be careful in the year of drought, neither shall cease from yielding fruit. JEREMIAH 17:7–8

To grow in grace, one must first be planted in it. And grace, she says, is not a dry doctrine of unmerited favour but the boundless love of God poured out without measure. To be planted in grace is to live in that love, to steep oneself in it, and to trust it to order all things well. Once planted there, the soul grows more in a month than in years in any other soil.

## How the lilies grow

Her text is the Sermon on the Mount.

> And why take ye thought for raiment? Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow; they toil not, neither do they spin: And yet I say unto you, That even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these. MATTHEW 6:28–29

A lily does not strain to grow. It does not stretch its leaves or try to manage the clouds. It grows because it is alive and planted, and the sun and rain do their work. She asked her readers to imagine a child so anxious about his height that he rigged up ropes and pulleys to stretch himself every day. He would waste his youth and add nothing to his stature. That, she said, is exactly how many Christians go about their sanctification — wearing themselves out with effort that achieves nothing.

> So is the kingdom of God, as if a man should cast seed into the ground; And should sleep, and rise night and day, and the seed should spring and grow up, he knoweth not how. MARK 4:26–27

Her counsel is to put our growing into God’s hands as completely as we have put everything else there. Our part is to abide in the Vine, to keep the sky clear between our souls and the Sun of Righteousness, and to let the Gardener do His work in His own way and time.

> Abide in me, and I in you. As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me. JOHN 15:4

## A potato as gladly as a rose

Part of letting God be the Gardener is letting Him choose what He grows. We are not to pick our own soil, set our own borders or demand our own seasons. “If He wills it, let Him grow a potato as gladly as a rose.”

She knew this from experience. In The God of All Comfort she tells how, as a plain-spoken and energetic young woman, she envied a sister who looked and sounded so saintly, and often complained to God for making her as she was. Then she read a sentence in an old book telling her to be content to be what God had made her, and saw that if God had made her a potato vine, she must be satisfied to grow potatoes and not pine to be a rosebush. Much restless striving is simply refusing to be the plant God has sown.

> I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase. So then neither is he that planteth any thing, neither he that watereth; but God that giveth the increase. 1 CORINTHIANS 3:6–7

## Growth and discipline

One caution. The picture of effortless growth is true about the source of growth — it is God’s life in us, not our straining. It is not true that growth needs no attention. Scripture also speaks of exercise and practice.

> But refuse profane and old wives’ fables, and exercise thyself rather unto godliness. 1 TIMOTHY 4:7

> But strong meat belongeth to them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil. HEBREWS 5:14

A lily does nothing to make itself grow, but a gardener does a great deal: he waters, weeds and prunes. Hannah’s own counsel to expose ourselves to God’s presence, to bask in His love and keep the sky clear, requires daily choices — to open the Bible, to pray, to gather with His people, to obey. Those are not the ropes and pulleys; they are how the plant turns its face to the sun. The mistake she was attacking was not discipline but anxious self-effort that puts its trust in itself.

> And he shall be like a tree planted by the rivers of water, that bringeth forth his fruit in his season; his leaf also shall not wither; and whatsoever he doeth shall prosper. PSALM 1:3

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Compare yourself honestly with five years ago. Have you grown in love and devotion, or only in knowledge and activity?
2. Where have you been using ropes and pulleys — anxious effort — to make yourself grow?
3. Make the step: put your growing into God’s hands, and give up measuring it daily.
4. Keep the sky clear: name anything that stands between your soul and God’s light, and remove it.
5. Is there a way in which God has made you a potato vine while you have longed to be a rose? Thank Him for what He has made you.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have tried to grow into grace by my own straining, and I am no nearer than when I began.
> Plant me in Your love, and let me grow there as the lilies grow, without anxiety.
> I give You my growing, my soil and my seasons. Make of me whatever plant You please, and let me be glad of it.
> Keep me turned toward You, and make me fruitful in my season, for Your glory and not my own. Amen.

# The Joy of Obedience

> He that hath my commandments, and keepeth them, he it is that loveth me: and he that loveth me shall be loved of my Father, and I will love him, and will manifest myself to him. JOHN 14:21

Hannah Whitall Smith is sometimes read as though her teaching were all about rest, and obedience were an afterthought. Her chapter on the joy of obedience in The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life shows the opposite. Rest in God and obedience to God were, for her, two sides of one love. The chapter opens with a sentence she had read somewhere and never forgot: “Perfect obedience would bring perfect happiness if we had perfect confidence in the power we obeyed.”

The key is the last clause. Obedience to someone we distrust is slavery. Obedience to someone we trust completely is freedom.

> Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light. MATTHEW 11:29–30

## Why must I when others need not

She addressed a particular kind of reader: the Christian who has sensed God asking for a complete surrender and who shrinks from it. Such a person looks round and sees other believers walking with easy consciences in a wider path. Why, he asks, must I give up what they are free to keep? Why must I do what they need not do? It seems hard.

Her answer was to change the frame. That very difference, she said, is your privilege. Our Lord says that He manifests Himself to those who have His commandments and keep them. If He has shown you His mind on matters where others are still in the dark, it is because you are close enough for Him to tell you. Is that a burden or an honour?

> Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you. Henceforth I call you not servants; for the servant knoweth not what his lord doeth: but I have called you friends; for all things that I have heard of my Father I have made known unto you. JOHN 15:14–15

## Friendship and love

She illustrated it from ordinary human relationships. In a friendship there is a proper reserve. Friends have separate lives and separate interests; they enjoy each other’s company, but they do not expect to know one another’s private wishes or to be governed by them. But when friendship becomes love, all that changes. The two hearts give themselves to each other, and separate paths are no longer possible. Things that were lawful before become unlawful, not because a rule has been imposed but because love has drawn so close. Each longs to know the other’s secret wishes, and to fulfil them.

Do such lovers resent the obligations love lays on them, she asked, or envy the cool friendships they see around them? They glory in them. So it is, she said, with the Christian whom Christ has drawn near. “What to them is lawful, love has made unlawful for you.” And far from being a hardship, this becomes the sweetest privilege of the soul.

> As the bridegroom rejoiceth over the bride, so shall thy God rejoice over thee. ISAIAH 62:5

## His joy in us

She pressed further, to something she admitted she was hardly fit to speak of. We can understand that we need Christ; it is harder to believe that He desires us. But Scripture says it over and over. He rejoices over His people. Our love and devotion are, in some real sense, His reward for all He has suffered for us.

> The LORD thy God in the midst of thee is mighty; he will save, he will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing. ZEPHANIAH 3:17

So she urged her readers to let Him have all: to begin each day by asking how to order it to please Him, to give Him their reading, their friendships, their thinking, their time. And she promised that such a life would gild even the dullest existence. Watching a poor woman at her washtub one day, she wondered why such a hard and dreary life had to exist — and then saw that even that life, lived with Christ, could be glorious in every hour.

> For whosoever shall do the will of my Father which is in heaven, the same is my brother, and sister, and mother. MATTHEW 12:50

## Two cautions

This chapter must be read with two guard rails, both of which Hannah herself would have granted.

First, the language of a closer path than other Christians can breed pride. It is a short step from I am called to more to I am better than those far-off ones, and some holiness circles took that step. The Lord’s own word to those who have obeyed everything is humbling.

> So likewise ye, when ye shall have done all those things which are commanded you, say, We are unprofitable servants: we have done that which was our duty to do. LUKE 17:10

Second, the idea that God shows some believers requirements He does not show others can turn into scrupulosity — a conscience burdened with rules God never gave. Hannah grew up among Quakers whose scruples over hats, bonnets and forms of speech she later described with affectionate irony in The Unselfishness of God. Personal convictions are real, but they are personal. We must not lay them on others, and we must not let the enemy lay false ones on us.

> Who art thou that judgest another man’s servant? to his own master he standeth or falleth. Yea, he shall be holden up: for God is able to make him stand. ROMANS 14:4

The test is love. Obedience that grows out of intimacy with Christ is joyful and humble. Obedience that grows out of fear or comparison is neither.

> For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments: and his commandments are not grievous. 1 JOHN 5:3

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Is there something Christ has shown you that you have resisted because other Christians seem free of it? Ask whether it is His word to you.
2. Begin tomorrow by asking, Lord, how shall I order this day so as to please You?
3. Offer Him one specific area you have kept separate — your reading, your money, a friendship.
4. Examine your convictions: are any of them rules you now expect others to keep? Hold them more humbly.
5. Find joy in one dull duty this week by doing it consciously with Christ.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You have drawn me near and shown me Your mind. Forgive me for envying those who stand further off.
> I give You all of myself — my days, my thoughts, my pleasures, my friendships — not as a burden, but as love gives to love.
> Keep me from pride in my obedience and from rules You never gave.
> Let my obedience be the glad answer of a heart that trusts You completely, and let me find my joy in pleasing You. Amen.

# Oneness with Christ

> I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me: and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by the faith of the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself for me. GALATIANS 2:20

The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life builds toward a chapter on oneness with Christ. All God’s dealings with the soul, Hannah Whitall Smith says, aim at this: that we should be one with Him, as our Lord prayed on the night before He died.

> That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us: that the world may believe that thou hast sent me. JOHN 17:21

## From allegiance to union

She traced the usual course of Christian experience through the story of the disciples. First they woke up to their need. Then they came to Christ and pledged their loyalty. They followed Him, worked for Him and believed in Him — and yet how unlike Him they were. They argued about who was greatest, ran from the cross and misunderstood His words. They knew Christ, as she put it, after the flesh: outside them, their Lord and Master, but not yet their life. Then came Pentecost, and they knew Him within. One will now governed them, and it was His.

Many Christians, she said, recognise the earlier stages in themselves but not the last. They have allegiance and confidence, but not yet union. There are still two wills, two interests, two lives. She put the question in a sequence of four short sentences: “Once it was I and not Christ. Then it was I and Christ. Perhaps now it is even Christ and me. But has it come to be Christ only, and not I at all?”

> To whom God would make known what is the riches of the glory of this mystery among the Gentiles; which is Christ in you, the hope of glory: COLOSSIANS 1:27

## Not a feeling but a likeness

Then comes the most important thing she says in this chapter, and one of the most important things she ever said. Many people imagine that oneness with Christ is a feeling — a great emotion of nearness. They look within for it, and if they have delightful religious emotions, they conclude they must be one with Him. Nowhere, she says, is the mistake of trusting to feelings more dangerous. “It is not what we feel, but what we are that settles the question.”

Two people are one when their aims, thoughts and ways are alike, not when they merely feel fond of each other. So oneness with Christ must mean likeness to Christ. She had seen people so ecstatic in their love for Christ that they claimed an intimate union with Him, while showing very few of His traits, and she scarcely knew a sadder sight.

> Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven. MATTHEW 7:21

> He that saith he abideth in him ought himself also so to walk, even as he walked. 1 JOHN 2:6

And then she spells it out with uncomfortable plainness. “If you are one with Christ, you will be sweet to those who are cross with you.” You will bear things without complaint. When reviled, you will not revile again. You will take the lowest place and serve, as He did. You will love your enemies. This, she says, is what oneness means, and no feeling, however ecstatic, can take its place.

> Even so every good tree bringeth forth good fruit; but a corrupt tree bringeth forth evil fruit. MATTHEW 7:17

This is a striking thing to find in the heart of a holiness classic. She was writing among camp meetings where overwhelming emotion was often taken as the sign of the Spirit, and she deliberately set it aside. Emotions, she said, depend largely on temperament and health. Let them come or go as God pleases; the test of union is character.

## The Guest in the house

How does one come into this? She begins with a fact, not a feeling. The indwelling of God is already true of every believer, even the weakest.

> Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwelleth in you? 1 CORINTHIANS 3:16

Paul said that to the Corinthians — carnal, quarrelsome and immature. So it is not a new thing we must ask for, but something we must recognise. She pictured Christ living in a house, shut away in a small back room, while the family came and went about their business without a thought for Him. Then someone announces: the Lord is in the house! Will they welcome Him into every room and consult Him about everything? Or shrink from Him and look for a corner to hide in?

> Behold, I stand at the door, and knock: if any man hear my voice, and open the door, I will come in to him, and will sup with him, and he with me. REVELATION 3:20

Her steps are her usual three. Be sure from Scripture that God dwells in you. Surrender your whole being to be possessed by Him. Believe that He has taken possession, and go on reckoning it, day and night, until it becomes the breath of the soul.

## Christ only — and I live

One qualification. Her phrase Christ only, and not I at all expresses a real truth about self-will and self-life. But it should not be heard as though our personality were meant to disappear. Paul says, nevertheless I live. Union with Christ does not erase the person; it restores the person as God meant him to be. It is my will that is yielded, my mind that is renewed, my hands that serve. That is why she can insist in the same breath that oneness shows itself in character, which is always personal.

> But he that is joined unto the Lord is one spirit. 1 CORINTHIANS 6:17

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Where are you in her sequence: I and not Christ; I and Christ; Christ and I; Christ only?
2. Test your sense of nearness to Christ by her plain question: are you sweet to those who are cross with you?
3. Name one person with whom your behaviour contradicts your devotion, and ask Christ to live His life toward them through you.
4. Walk through the rooms of your life — work, home, money, leisure — and ask whether the Guest has been welcomed in each.
5. Every day this week, say Galatians 2:20 slowly, as a statement of fact and a surrender of will.

### A PRAYER

> Lord Jesus, You are already in the house, and I have kept You in a back room.
> I open every door to You now. Come into my work and my home, my speech and my silences.
> Do not let me be content with warm feelings about You while I remain unlike You.
> Live Your life in me, so that I am gentle when others are cross, patient when I am wronged, and glad to take the lowest place. Amen.

# The Lord Is My Shepherd

> The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want. PSALM 23:1

With The God of All Comfort, published in 1906 when she was in her seventies, Hannah Whitall Smith turned from man’s side of the life of faith to God’s. The early book had asked, how do I trust? The later book asks, what is He like, that I may trust Him? And one of its first answers is the oldest and plainest: He is a shepherd.

## The common text

She tells how the Twenty-third Psalm, familiar to her from the nursery, came alive. At a critical moment of her life she badly needed comfort and could not lay her hands on a Bible. She searched her memory for a text, and the only one that came was The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want. She turned from it almost with scorn: such a common text could hardly do her any good. She tried to think of something more unusual, but nothing else would come. So, reduced to the one verse, she began repeating it to herself — and suddenly it was lit from within, and such floods of comfort poured over her that she felt she could never have a trouble again.

When she could reach a Bible, she built what she called a pyramid of promises about the Lord as Shepherd, until she was convinced beyond doubt that in taking the name He had taken on the duties that go with it.

> I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep. JOHN 10:11

## Whose fault is the thin flock

Her argument then becomes very simple. Imagine two flocks meeting after a hard winter, one healthy and strong, the other starved and sickly. If the healthy sheep could speak, would they boast of their own cleverness? No — they would praise their shepherd, who fed and sheltered and guarded them. And the wretched flock would not blame themselves; they would say their shepherd had failed them.

We see this at once when we speak of sheep. But in our spiritual lives we reverse it. When we are thin and anxious and unfed, we say the fault is ours — we are too weak, too foolish, too unworthy. Yet weakness and foolishness are exactly what sheep are. That is why they need a shepherd. Their safety has never depended on their strength.

> I will seek that which was lost, and bring again that which was driven away, and will bind up that which was broken, and will strengthen that which was sick: EZEKIEL 34:16

The Lord, through Ezekiel, condemned the bad shepherds of Israel for failing to strengthen the weak, heal the sick and seek the lost — and then promised to do all of it Himself. So, Hannah argues, if a sheep of Christ is starving, there are only two explanations. Either the Shepherd is unfaithful, or the sheep has not trusted Him. No Christian would dare to say the first.

## As though I were the shepherd

She quotes a friend who confessed that for years she had said the Lord was her shepherd and lived as though she were the shepherd and He the sheep — anxious that if she did not keep a tight hold on Him, He would wander off. When dark days came, she never expected Him to stand by her. Then everything changed. She was no stronger, she said; she had simply discovered that she had a good Shepherd, and that was enough.

> My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me: And I give unto them eternal life; and they shall never perish, neither shall any man pluck them out of my hand. JOHN 10:27–28

## He is, He is

Her practical counsel is characteristically plain. First face what a good shepherd must be and do. Then face the fact that the Lord is such a shepherd in the highest sense. Then say to yourself with all the determination you can gather: “The Lord is my Shepherd. He is. He is. No matter what I feel, He says He is, and He is.” And then, she suggests, repeat the sentence again and again with the stress on a different word each time — the Lord; is; my; Shepherd — until every word has yielded its meaning.

> He shall feed his flock like a shepherd: he shall gather the lambs with his arm, and carry them in his bosom, and shall gently lead those that are with young. ISAIAH 40:11

She was clear that this does not mean outward troubles vanish. The Shepherd may lead through a desert. But He knows which pastures are best for His sheep, and He can make a desert green. The valley of the shadow is in the same psalm as the still waters.

> Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil: for thou art with me; thy rod and thy staff they comfort me. PSALM 23:4

## A caution about appearances

In this chapter Hannah also argues that our misery dishonours the Shepherd, since the world judges a shepherd by the look of his flock. There is truth in it: a church full of anxious, joyless people does commend its Lord poorly. But the argument can be turned into a burden, as though Christians must look contented at all costs for the sake of the reputation of God. That is not what the psalm teaches. The sheep in Psalm 23 walks through the dark valley with its fear honestly named and conquered, not hidden. The best testimony to the Shepherd is not a performance of happiness but a real trust that holds in the dark.

> Know ye that the LORD he is God: it is he that hath made us, and not we ourselves; we are his people, and the sheep of his pasture. PSALM 100:3

Held that way, her chapter does just what she hoped. It takes the most familiar sentence in the Bible and makes it, as she says, enough.

> Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me all the days of my life: and I will dwell in the house of the LORD for ever. PSALM 23:6

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Say Psalm 23:1 slowly four times, stressing a different word each time. Which word do you most need?
2. Where have you been acting as the shepherd and treating the Lord as the sheep you must keep hold of?
3. When you feel thin and unfed, stop blaming yourself for being a sheep, and ask the Shepherd to feed you.
4. Build your own small pyramid: write out five Scriptures about the Lord as Shepherd and keep them together.
5. If you are in a dark valley, do not pretend otherwise. Tell the Shepherd plainly, and then say, thou art with me.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, You are my Shepherd. You are. No matter what I feel, You say You are, and You are.
> I have tried to be my own shepherd and to keep hold of You as though You might wander off. Forgive me.
> I am weak and foolish, as sheep are. Feed me, lead me, bind up what is broken in me, and carry me when I cannot walk.
> Through the valley and beside still waters, let goodness and mercy follow me, until I dwell in Your house for ever. Amen.

# The God of All Comfort

> Blessed be God, even the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of mercies, and the God of all comfort; Who comforteth us in all our tribulation, that we may be able to comfort them which are in any trouble, by the comfort wherewith we ourselves are comforted of God. 2 CORINTHIANS 1:3–4

The God of All Comfort begins with a rebuke Hannah Whitall Smith never forgot. An agnostic she was trying to win told her politely that if Christians wanted unbelievers to take their religion seriously, they had better look more comfortable in it themselves. The Christians he met seemed to carry their religion as a man carries a headache: he does not want to lose his head, but it is very uncomfortable to have it. She admitted, after some years, that he was largely right, and she wrote the book to find out why.

## Under-believed

Her answer came in a borrowed sentence she loved to repeat. Has the kingdom of God been over-advertised, the writer asked, “or has it simply been under-believed?” The trouble is not that God has promised more comfort than He gives. The trouble is that we do not believe Him, and we do not believe Him because we do not know what He is like. We picture an angry judge watching for faults, a hard taskmaster, or a distant sovereign absorbed in his own affairs — and who could be comfortable with any of those?

So the book sets out to show what God is, not in theological definitions but in real life. And it lays down the same order she had taught for thirty years: “God’s order is simple: believe first, then feel.” Comfort comes through believing what God has said about Himself.

> Comfort ye, comfort ye my people, saith your God. ISAIAH 40:1

## A Father with a mother’s heart

Christ, she says, revealed God above all as Father, and taught His disciples to pray to Him by that name. But she insists that the name must be understood as our best instincts understand it. A harsh or neglectful father is not a good father at all, and God, being good, must be the best of fathers. Even the other titles of God — Judge, King, Lawgiver — are held within the Fatherhood: He is a Father-Judge, a Father-King.

> Like as a father pitieth his children, so the LORD pitieth them that fear him. For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust. PSALM 103:13–14

And she went further. God, she says, is also pictured in Scripture with a mother’s tenderness, and the God who made mothers cannot be less loving than they are. To understand Him, gather together the best of every father and mother you have known, and know that it is only a faint shadow of Him.

> As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you; and ye shall be comforted in Jerusalem. ISAIAH 66:13

> Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee. ISAIAH 49:15

She knew what she was saying. She had been a mother seven times and had buried four children. When she writes that mothers would feel insulted to be suspected of forgetting their children, she writes from inside the experience.

## The Lord is good

Another chapter tells of the hour she first saw that God was really good — not religiously good, in some pious sense she could not understand, but good in the plain sense in which He commands us to be good. “I shall never forget the hour when I first discovered that God was really good.” To be good is to do the best one knows. God knows everything; therefore He always does what is truly best. From then on, whenever appearances were against Him, she was stopped by the words the Lord is good, and saw that the bad things she had imagined of Him were simply impossible.

> If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven give good things to them that ask him? MATTHEW 7:11

She applied it with a housekeeping picture. When a friend known to be a good housekeeper turns her house upside down at spring cleaning, we do not doubt her; we know it will be better when she has finished. So when the world seemed to her all wrong, she would say, “It is not my housekeeping, but it is the Lord’s,” and since He is good, His housekeeping must be good too. And when someone asked a wise Christian whether the world did not look like a wreck, the reply was yes — like the wreck of a bursting seed.

> Verily, verily, I say unto you, Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit. JOHN 12:24

## Looking away from self

One reason Christians are uncomfortable, she believed, is that they spend their lives examining themselves: am I earnest enough, have I repented enough, do I feel what I should? She pointed out that Scripture calls us to look at Christ, and that “we see what we look at, and cannot see what we look away from.” She took as a motto a line from Adelaide Procter: “For every one look at self, take ten looks at Christ.” Later she thought it better to take no looks at self at all.

> Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith; who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is set down at the right hand of the throne of God. HEBREWS 12:2

Here she overstates. She claims only two texts in the Bible speak of self-examination; there are others, and honest self-searching before God is part of repentance.

> Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to the LORD. LAMENTATIONS 3:40

But notice the direction of that verse: search, and turn to the Lord. What Hannah rightly attacked was examination that never turns — the morbid, endless inspection of our own feelings that makes self the centre of religion. The cure for that is not more introspection but a longer look at God.

> Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth: for I am God, and there is none else. ISAIAH 45:22

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Would an unbeliever who watched your life conclude that your faith comforts you or burdens you?
2. Gather the best qualities of the fathers and mothers you have known. Now say: God is better than this.
3. When the world or your own life looks wrecked this week, say: it is not my housekeeping, but the Lord’s.
4. Count your looks: for one day, notice how often you look at yourself and how often at Christ.
5. Comfort someone this week with the comfort God has given you.

### A PRAYER

> God of all comfort, I have carried my religion like a headache, and I am sorry.
> Thank You that You are a Father who pities His children and a Comforter who, like a mother, cannot forget.
> You are good — really good, better than the best I know. Where Your housekeeping looks like ruin to me, let me trust the Housekeeper.
> Turn my eyes from myself to You, and make me a comfort to others in their trouble. Amen.

# The Unselfishness of God

> And we have known and believed the love that God hath to us. God is love; and he that dwelleth in love dwelleth in God, and God in him. 1 JOHN 4:16

On the flyleaf of her Bible, Hannah Whitall Smith kept a sentence whose source she did not know: “This generation has rediscovered the unselfishness of God.” When, past seventy, she wrote the story of her soul, she took it as her title, and said that if she had to sum up her whole religious experience in one sentence, this would be the one.

## The God she grew up with

Many Christians, she wrote, without ever putting it into words, look on God as one of the most selfish beings in the universe — intent on His own honour and glory, watchful of His rights, and too absorbed in His own righteousness to have much pity left for the sinners who offend Him. She had believed exactly this through all her years of searching. God was a stern and distant autocrat who might, with enough effort, be persuaded to take some notice of her. “I grew up believing God was like this. I have discovered that He is exactly the opposite.”

The discovery came, she says, through Christ. We cannot see God as He is; we need an interpreter, an incarnation. She compared it to a person trying to make himself known to a colony of ants: he could speak over the anthill for a lifetime and not one word would reach them, unless he became an ant. So God became a man. “Whatever Christ was, that God is.” All the tenderness, justice, kindness and unselfishness we see in Jesus are simply God shown to us in a form we can grasp.

> Jesus saith unto him, Have I been so long time with you, and yet hast thou not known me, Philip? he that hath seen me hath seen the Father; and how sayest thou then, Shew us the Father? JOHN 14:9

## The duties of ownership

Out of this came one of her most helpful insights. She had always thought of God’s ownership of us in terms of His rights. Now she saw it in terms of His duties. Just as a decent society holds people responsible for the proper care of what they own, so the Creator is bound, by the law of His own loving nature, to care for the creatures He has made. That He knows everything about us, once a terror, became a comfort: it meant He could do His best for us.

And it gave a new meaning to a familiar word. Nothing can be lost unless it belongs to someone. The lost coin still bears the king’s image; the lost sheep is still a sheep; the lost son is still a son. “To me, the word ‘lost’ contains the strongest possible proof of ownership.” If a man is a lost sinner, it means he is owned by the Good Shepherd, who must go after him.

> For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life. JOHN 3:16

> But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. ROMANS 5:8

This is Hannah at her best, and it is simply the gospel. God is not grudging. The cross was not wrung out of Him. It is the natural expression of who He is.

## The restitution of all things

But this chapter must also be honest about where her discovery led her. In The Unselfishness of God she describes a moment, riding on a tramcar in Philadelphia, when the misery of the human race seemed to overwhelm her, and she cried out to God that she could not see how He could bear it. Then, she says, an inward voice answered with the words of Isaiah.

> He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied: by his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many; for he shall bear their iniquities. ISAIAH 53:11

She reasoned that nothing could satisfy Christ except the salvation of every human being. As in Adam all die, so in Christ shall all be made alive; the remedy must be as wide as the disease. She went home and found the restitution of all things on every page of her Bible. She held this hope for the rest of her life. When the Brighton organisers asked her to keep it quiet, she wrote to her husband that she would make no compromises, and that she believed in restitution more firmly than ever.

> Whom the heaven must receive until the times of restitution of all things, which God hath spoken by the mouth of all his holy prophets since the world began. ACTS 3:21

Readers must weigh this carefully, and most will not be able to follow her. The historic churches have not taught universal salvation, and the reasons are in Scripture. The same Lord who reveals the Father’s heart also speaks, more solemnly than anyone in the Bible, of a final division.

> And these shall go away into everlasting punishment: but the righteous into life eternal. MATTHEW 25:46

> He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life: and he that believeth not the Son shall not see life; but the wrath of God abideth on him. JOHN 3:36

Her hope rested largely on her own sense of what love must do — she said that as a mother she could never enjoy heaven while one of her children was shut out. That instinct is not to be despised, and Scripture does say God is not willing that any should perish. But our sense of what love requires cannot overrule what the Lord Himself has said. In places she even speaks as though God owed humanity forgiveness for making us sinners, which reverses the gospel. The wise course is to hold fast to what is revealed and leave the secret things with God.

> The secret things belong unto the LORD our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that we may do all the words of this law. DEUTERONOMY 29:29

## What remains

It is worth noticing what she herself admitted about her zeal for this view. When a visiting preacher at her breakfast table insisted that God’s love had limits and called her view grandmotherly religion, she told him she would not sit at table with such ideas and left the room in tears. Years later she wrote, “I do not claim that this reaction was courteous or even Christ-like.” That honesty is itself instructive.

Set her universalism aside, and what remains is a truth the church always needs: God is not selfish. He is exactly like Jesus. He loves the lost because they are His, and He goes after them.

> The LORD is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works. PSALM 145:9

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Be honest: do you, deep down, think of God as more concerned with His own glory than with your good?
2. Read a Gospel chapter this week and say after each scene: this is what God is like.
3. Think of someone you would call lost. Pray for them as one who belongs to God and is being sought.
4. Weigh her hope of universal restoration against Christ’s own words. Where must you hold your own hopes more humbly?
5. If there is someone whose view of God you find offensive, consider how to disagree in a Christ-like way.

### A PRAYER

> Father, I have imagined You as grudging and self-absorbed, and You are exactly the opposite.
> Thank You for showing me Your heart in Jesus, who came to seek and to save the lost.
> Keep me from making You in my own image, whether harsher or softer than You are. Teach me to hold what You have revealed and trust You with what You have not.
> Give me Your heart for those who are far from You, and let me love them as Yours. Amen.

# God Is Enough

> My soul, wait thou only upon God; for my expectation is from him. He only is my rock and my salvation: he is my defence; I shall not be moved. PSALM 62:5–6

The God of All Comfort ends with a chapter whose title is three words long, and Hannah Whitall Smith called it the last and greatest lesson the soul has to learn. God is enough. All His dealings with us, she believed, are meant to teach us this, and it is the crowning discovery of the Christian life.

## Christ and something else

We sing, she says, that Christ is all we want. But few of us mean it. We want Christ and something else: warm feelings about Him, a sense of His presence, satisfying doctrines, successful Christian work, answers to prayer we can point to. Christ alone, without any of our experiences of Him, has not seemed enough; we cannot even imagine how He could be. So the psalmist’s my soul, wait thou only upon God becomes, in practice, my soul, wait upon my sound doctrine, or my good feelings, or my fervent prayers. Only when everything else fails do we trust God alone.

She quoted George MacDonald to the effect that we treat God as our last and feeblest resource, going to Him only when we have nowhere else to go — and then find that the storms have driven us not onto the rocks but into the harbour.

> Although the fig tree shall not blossom, neither shall fruit be in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail, and the fields shall yield no meat; the flock shall be cut off from the fold, and there shall be no herd in the stalls: Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation. HABAKKUK 3:17–18

Feelings change, she says, and often with our health, or our surroundings, or the weather. Some people, she remarks dryly, “can never believe that God loves them when the wind is in the east.” Doctrines may be shaken, work may come to nothing, prayers may seem to lose their fervour, and even promises may seem to fail. And then only God is left — the bare God, as she dared to put it. And God is enough.

## The Promiser and the promises

She made a distinction that has helped many people in the dark. We often say, if only I could find a promise to fit my case. But promises can be misunderstood or misapplied, and at the very moment we lean our whole weight on one, it may seem to give way. “But the Promiser, who is behind His promises and is infinitely more than His promises, can never fail or change.” A little child does not need promises from its mother; it has its mother, and she is enough.

She told how she learned this. In a time of great perplexity, when she felt no one had ever had difficulties as peculiar as hers, she went to a woman with a reputation for spiritual wisdom and poured out her troubles, expecting deep sympathy. The woman listened, and then said: “Yes, all you say may be very true, but then, in spite of it all, there is God.” Hannah waited for more. Nothing came. She went back again and again, and always got the same answer. At first she thought her friend simply did not understand. Then, slowly, she began to wonder whether God might be enough even for her — and at last she saw that He was, and her troubles vanished.

> Not that we are sufficient of ourselves to think any thing as of ourselves; but our sufficiency is of God; 2 CORINTHIANS 3:5

## You don’t know my mother

The most tender passage in the chapter is a memory of her own childhood. Her mother, she says, was the remedy for all her troubles, and she was sure her mother could have mended the troubles of the whole world if only they were brought to her. When anyone doubted it, the child would silence them with scorn: “Ah! But you don’t know my mother.” Now, when someone could not see that God was enough, she felt like saying the same — not with scorn but with pity: you do not know God.

> Whom have I in heaven but thee? and there is none upon earth that I desire beside thee. My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever. PSALM 73:25–26

She drew the lesson in her usual common-sense way. Suppose a man stands accused before a judge. What matters — his feelings about the judge, or the judge’s feelings about him? Obviously the second. So in our relation to God, the vital question is never how we feel toward Him but how He feels toward us. And the answer to that has been settled at the cross.

> For I am persuaded, that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor things to come, Nor height, nor depth, nor any other creature, shall be able to separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord. ROMANS 8:38–39

## In old age

She lived this. The Unselfishness of God ends with a chapter on old age written when she was seventy and increasingly infirm. Knowing that God is and that God is enough, she wrote, “makes old age a joy in the present and death a delicious prospect for the future.” She was glad to hand the world’s work to younger shoulders. God, she said, is the housekeeper of His own creation, and she could leave it in His care without fear that things would fall apart when she was gone.

## A word of care

Two things should be said so that her phrase is not misheard. First, the bare God she speaks of is not a God stripped of Christ, but the God revealed in Christ — the Father, the Shepherd, the God of all comfort — trusted for Himself rather than for what He gives. Second, she did not despise feelings, doctrines or work. She said so plainly: we may have them all, but as the fruit of salvation, never the ground of it. The Bible, the church and prayer are how we come to know Him. They are not substitutes for Him.

> The LORD is my portion, saith my soul; therefore will I hope in him. LAMENTATIONS 3:24

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Finish the sentence honestly: I could trust God completely if only I had … What is your something else?
2. When your feelings are low this week, notice the wind, and refuse to let it decide whether God loves you.
3. The next time you look for a promise and cannot find one, say: but there is God.
4. Ask yourself the judge’s question: am I more concerned with my feelings about God or His about me?
5. If you are older, or unwell, consider how knowing that God is enough might change the way you face the years ahead.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have wanted You and something else — feelings, answers, success, a promise to fit my case.
> Take away whatever I have leaned on instead of You, and let me find that You are enough.
> When I cannot feel Your love, remind me that it is Your feeling toward me, not mine toward You, that matters, and You have shown it at the cross.
> In life and in death, in youth and in age, be my portion for ever. Amen.

# Conclusion

> Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you. 1 PETER 5:7

Eighteen chapters can make it look as though Hannah Whitall Smith taught a great many things. She taught one thing twice: once from our side and once from God’s. From our side, the Christian life is a handing over. We give God the whole case — ourselves first, then everything else — and we believe He has taken it, and we keep our will on His side whatever our feelings are doing. From God’s side, the handing over is safe because of who He is. He is the Potter who knows His work, the Shepherd who does not lose His sheep, the Father with a mother’s heart, the good Housekeeper of a world that looks like a wreck, the God whose love is unselfish, and in the end simply the God who is enough. Man’s part is to trust; God’s part is to work; and He can be trusted to work because He is good.

She called her discovery a secret only because it had so long been hidden from her. It was not hidden by God. It was an open secret, lying on every page of Scripture, waiting for someone to believe it.

## The danger of this book

A book about rest carries a peculiar risk. The reader may turn Hannah’s teaching into a new technique — the right way to surrender, the right formula to repeat, the right experience to seek — and then anxiously check whether it has worked. That would bring back through the kitchen door the very self-effort she spent her life showing out of the front. She saw the danger herself. She went forward at camp meetings again and again, seeking a blessing that never came in the form she expected, and learned at last that the truth was the blessing. She did not need an experience; she needed to know that God is good.

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. JAMES 1:22

So the plainest counsel is the one given in the other volumes of this series. Go back to the chapter that made you least comfortable, not the one you liked best. Take its application points and live with them for a month. Then take another. Hannah measured teaching by what it did on a wet Monday, and so should we.

## Two things to carry

First, hand it over and leave it there. Whatever you have been carrying in the wagon — a temper, a child, a fear, a failure, your own unmanageable self — give it to God in plain words. When it comes back, and it will, give it again without scolding yourself. That habit, practised for years, is most of what she meant by the life hid with Christ in God.

Second, look at God more than at yourself. Nearly every difficulty she addressed, from doubt to discouragement to morbid self-examination, came back in the end to the direction of our gaze. We see what we look at. Take ten looks at Christ for every look at self, and then take fewer looks at self.

> Looking unto Jesus the author and finisher of our faith. HEBREWS 12:2

## A last word about her

We have been honest about the places where she must be read with care. She sometimes wrote as though feelings could simply be ignored, and as though doubt and discouragement were always the devil’s work; people who grieve or struggle with depression need a gentler word. She held and proclaimed a hope of universal restoration that goes beyond what Scripture teaches, and she admitted that her zeal for it was not always Christ-like. She moved in circles where emotion and impressions were prized, and though she warned against their excesses more wisely than most, the movement she helped to lead was not free of them. And the public ministry she shared with her husband ended in 1875 in a painful collapse, followed over the years by his loss of faith.

None of this has been raised to diminish her. It has been raised because her own teaching requires it. A message whose whole point is that we have nothing in ourselves, and everything in God, cannot be commended by pretending its messenger was an exception. She would have been the first to say so. She called her great discovery not an attainment but a discovery: she did not become a better woman, she said; she found out that Christ was a better Saviour.

> But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellency of the power may be of God, and not of us. 2 CORINTHIANS 4:7

She outlived four of her children, her husband’s faith and her own public reputation, and she ended her life in a wheelchair, in pain, and cheerful. She wrote that she could leave the whole universe in God’s care without fear that anything would go wrong when she was gone. That is the best argument for her teaching: not that it spared her trouble, but that it held her in it.

### FOR REFLECTION AND ACTION

1. Return to the page you wrote at the start of this book about what you think God is like. What would you change now?
2. Choose the one chapter that made you least comfortable, and work its application points for a month.
3. Hand over, in plain words, the burden you have carried longest. When it returns, hand it over again.
4. Practise the ten looks: before you examine yourself, spend time considering what God is like.
5. Read one of her own books through slowly, with the Bible open, weighing what she says.

### A PRAYER

> Lord, I have read about Your goodness, and I am capable of turning even that into one more thing to strive for.
> Keep me from it. I do not want a technique; I want You.
> I hand myself over, and I leave myself with You. Be my Shepherd, my Father, my Comforter and my portion.
> And when my strength fails and my hopes are disappointed, as hers were, hold me still, and let me find that You are enough. Amen.

# A Reader’s Guide to Hannah Whitall Smith

> Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth. 2 TIMOTHY 2:15

Hannah Whitall Smith is one of the easiest devotional writers to read. She wrote for ordinary people, in plain sentences, with illustrations drawn from kitchens, nurseries, railway carriages and sickrooms. Her main books are in the public domain and widely available. The difficulty is not access but balance: her most famous book is her earliest, and it reads best alongside the later ones, which fill out and sometimes correct it.

## Begin here

The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life (1875), in the Ochorus library. Her classic, and the source of most of chapters three to sixteen of this volume. Read the opening chapter on God’s side and man’s side first; it is the key to the rest. The chapters on the will, on doubts, on failures and on guidance are the most practically useful. Remember that it is an early book, written in the first flush of discovery.

The God of All Comfort (1906), in the Ochorus library. Her ripest work, written in old age, and the best counterweight to the Secret. Where the earlier book concentrates on our trusting, this one concentrates on the God who is trusted. The chapters on the Shepherd, on the Father, on the goodness of God and on God being enough are the heart of it.

## Her own story

The Unselfishness of God and How I Discovered It (1903), in the Ochorus library. A spiritual autobiography told in four epochs: the long years of searching, the discovery of the gospel in 1858, the hope of universal restoration, and the life of faith that began at Millville. It is candid, funny and moving, and it shows where her teaching came from. The early chapters on her Quaker childhood are a delight in themselves.

## Other books

Every-Day Religion. A later collection of plain Bible teaching for daily life, in the same vein as the Secret. The Open Secret. Short expositions arranged by subject. Frank: The Record of a Happy Life. Her memoir of her son, who died at eighteen; a mother’s tribute, and a window into her grief and faith in the 1870s.

## Approach with care

The chapters of The Unselfishness of God on what she called the restitution of all things set out her universalist hope. Read them to understand her, not as a guide to Scripture; chapter nineteen of this volume explains why. Her collected notes on religious fanaticism, published after her death in 1928 and edited by her granddaughter Ray Strachey, record the excesses she witnessed in holiness circles. They show how seriously she took the dangers she warned against, but they are written with a sharpness that some readers will find unsettling.

## About her

Ray Strachey’s A Quaker Grandmother (1914) is a brief, affectionate portrait by a granddaughter who knew her well. Logan Pearsall Smith, her son, edited a selection of her letters, A Religious Rebel (1949), which shows her humour and her independence of mind. Barbara Strachey’s Remarkable Relations (1980) tells the story of the whole Pearsall Smith family, including the painful parts, with a historian’s frankness. Marie Henry’s The Secret Life of Hannah Whitall Smith (1984) is a sympathetic Christian biography that draws on her diaries and letters. Read more than one. The family books are frank about the strains her religion brought into the household; the devotional lives admire her and sometimes smooth the edges.

## Three counsels for reading her

Read the early and the late together. The Secret without The God of All Comfort can become a technique; the later book without the earlier can become sentiment. Together they make one argument: trust, because God is good.

Read with the Bible open. She claimed no authority of her own and argued constantly from texts. Check them, and read them in context; she sometimes presses a single verse further than it will go.

And read her slowly, a chapter at a time, with a particular burden in mind. She wrote to be used, not admired. One chapter acted on will do more than the whole shelf enjoyed.

> Trust in the LORD with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths. PROVERBS 3:5–6
