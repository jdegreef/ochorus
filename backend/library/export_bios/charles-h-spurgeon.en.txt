Charles Haddon Spurgeon was born on 19 June 1834 in Kelvedon, Essex, the son and grandson of Congregational preachers, and spent much of his childhood among the Puritan books of his grandfather’s manse at Stambourne. On a snowy Sunday, 6 January 1850, a blizzard drove the fifteen-year-old into a Primitive Methodist chapel in Colchester, where a layman cried, “Young man, look to Jesus Christ.” He looked, and was saved.

Baptized that spring, he was preaching by sixteen, and in 1854, at nineteen, he was called to New Park Street Chapel in London, where the crowds soon outgrew every hall. In 1856 he married Susannah. That October a false cry of fire at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall left seven dead, and he battled depression for the rest of his life. From 1861 he preached at the Metropolitan Tabernacle, whose basement prayer meeting he called its powerhouse.

He founded the Pastors’ College, a Colportage Association and the Stockwell Orphanage, opened in 1867, and published The Sword and the Trowel. His books — Morning and Evening, The Treasury of David, All of Grace — are still read. In the “Down-Grade” controversy of 1887 he withdrew from the Baptist Union rather than compromise on the authority of Scripture.

Worn by illness, he died at Menton on 31 January 1892, aged fifty-seven, leaving more than three thousand five hundred published sermons. He never pretended the power was his own: “May God help me, if you cease to pray for me! Let me know the day, and I must cease to preach.”
