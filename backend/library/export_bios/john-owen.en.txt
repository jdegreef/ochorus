John Owen was born in 1616 at Stadhampton, Oxfordshire, the son of the Puritan minister Henry Owen. He went up to Queen’s College, Oxford, at about twelve, but in 1637 left rather than submit to Archbishop Laud’s statutes. For years he lived under spiritual darkness, until around 1642 an unknown preacher, standing in for Edmund Calamy, took the text “Why are ye fearful, O ye of little faith?” — and the assurance he had begged for came.

His books won him the livings of Fordham and Coggeshall. Oliver Cromwell made him an army chaplain, then in 1651 Dean of Christ Church and, the next year, Vice-Chancellor of Oxford, a post he held until 1657. After The Death of Death in the Death of Christ (1647) came The Mortification of Sin (1656), with its plain charge: “be killing sin, or it will be killing you.” Of Communion with God (1657) showed the sweetness behind the severity.

At the Restoration he lost the deanery, and in 1662 he was ejected with some two thousand ministers. As a Nonconformist he led the Independents, wrote for toleration, and declined a call to Boston. He prized John Bunyan’s preaching and is credibly said to have helped win his release. Of some eleven children, all but one died young, and his wife Mary died in 1675.

He died at Ealing on 24 August 1683 and was buried at Bunhill Fields. Two days before, he wrote to a friend: “I am going to Him whom my soul hath loved, or rather who hath loved me with an everlasting love.”
