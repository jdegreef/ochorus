Bernard of Clairvaux was born in 1090 at Fontaines, near Dijon, the third son of a Burgundian knight. His mother, Aleth, died when he was in his teens. In 1113, aged twenty-two, he entered Cîteaux, a struggling new monastery close to collapse for want of recruits — and he arrived with about thirty men, brothers, an uncle and friends he had spent months persuading.

Two years later he was sent out with twelve monks to found a house in a valley in Champagne. They called it Clairvaux, and he was its abbot for the remaining thirty-eight years of his life. By his death the Cistercian order had grown to some three hundred and forty houses, sixty-eight of them founded from Clairvaux. There he wrote On Loving God, with its four degrees of love, and preached eighty-six sermons on the Song of Songs over eighteen years.

From about 1130 the world would not let him go. His advocacy did more than anything else to make Innocent II pope; he pursued Abelard to condemnation in 1140, and wrote to kings as he wrote to frightened novices. In 1146 he preached the Second Crusade, which achieved nothing at vast human cost, and he faced that failure in writing. In the Rhineland he stopped a monk who was stirring up massacres of Jewish communities.

He died at Clairvaux on 20 August 1153, worn out at sixty-three. Luther, impatient with medieval saints, made an exception for him; Calvin quoted him, and Dante gave him the last word in the Paradiso. His answer to how much God should be loved still stands: “the measure of love due to Him is immeasurable love.”
