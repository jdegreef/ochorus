Isaac Watts was born in Southampton on 17 July 1674, the eldest of a large family. His father, a Dissenting clothier, was imprisoned for nonconformity, and family tradition says his mother nursed the infant on a stone by the prison door. A quick boy who, by the usual account, had Latin, Greek and Hebrew, he declined a university place that would have meant conforming to the Church of England. In 1689, he wrote, he “was taught to trust in Christ, I hope.”

Troubled by the dreary singing of metrical psalms at his Southampton chapel, he was told by his father, the story goes, to write something better — and he did. In 1702 he became pastor of the Independent congregation at Mark Lane in London. His Hymns and Spiritual Songs (1707) gave the church “When I survey the wondrous cross,” and The Psalms of David Imitated (1719) gave it “Our God, our help in ages past” and “Joy to the World.”

In 1712 a violent fever laid him aside for more than four years. Sir Thomas Abney and his wife invited him to their house to recover; he came intending to stay a single week, and stayed thirty-six years. Under their roof he wrote Divine Songs (1715), which generations of children learned by heart, A Guide to Prayer, and a Logick used at Oxford, Cambridge and Yale. Some seven hundred and fifty hymns and psalms are credited to him.

He died on 25 November 1748, aged seventy-four, and was buried in Bunhill Fields. In his last years, going to bed, he would often say: “I bless God. I can lie down with comfort; not being solicitous whether I awake in this world or another.”
