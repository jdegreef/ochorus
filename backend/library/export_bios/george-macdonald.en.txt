George MacDonald was born on 10 December 1824 in Huntly, Aberdeenshire, into a family of farmers and weavers. His mother died when he was eight, and his patient, good father became the pattern for the gentle fathers in his books. He studied at King’s College, Aberdeen, and trained for the ministry at Highbury College in London.

In 1850 he became minister of Trinity Congregational Church in Arundel, and in 1851 married Louisa Powell; they had eleven children. Within two years he was charged with heresy, his salary was cut, and in 1853 he resigned. He never held a pastorate again. Poor and often ill with tuberculosis, he turned to the pen: Phantastes (1858), novels of Scottish life, and for children At the Back of the North Wind and The Princess and the Goblin. “I do not write for children,” he said, “but for the childlike, whether of five, or fifty, or seventy-five.”

His three series of Unspoken Sermons (1867–1889) return again and again to one theme: God is our Father, wholly good, and His love is a fire that will burn away everything in us that is not good. Many readers part company with him on universal salvation and the atonement, yet few writers make the goodness of God feel so near. “Obedience is the opener of eyes.”

Between 1878 and 1891 four of his children died, and from 1880 the family wintered at Casa Coraggio in Bordighera. A stroke took his speech in his last years, and he died at Ashtead, Surrey, on 18 September 1905. C. S. Lewis, who found Phantastes on a station bookstall as a young atheist, came to call him his master.
