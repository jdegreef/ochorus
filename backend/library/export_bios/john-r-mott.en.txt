John Raleigh Mott was born on 25 May 1865 at Livingston Manor, New York, and grew up in Postville, a prairie village in Iowa. A visiting evangelist stirred his faith at thirteen. Meaning to be a lawyer, he moved in 1885 to Cornell. There, in January 1886, he heard J. E. K. Studd give out the text: “Seekest thou great things for thyself? Seek them not. Seek ye first the Kingdom of God.” He went back to his room “not to study but to fight,” and after nights of prayer gave his life to God’s work.

That summer, at Moody’s student conference at Mount Hermon, he was one of a hundred young men who pledged themselves to missions. From them grew the Student Volunteer Movement, which he led for thirty-two years under the watchword “The Evangelization of the World in This Generation.” In 1891 he married Leila Ada White, who shared his work for more than sixty years.

In 1895 he and Karl Fries founded the World’s Student Christian Federation, and he urged students everywhere to keep the “morning watch,” the first half-hour of each day alone with God. He crossed the Atlantic more than a hundred times. In 1910 he presided at the Edinburgh Missionary Conference, and he led the International Missionary Council until 1942. In the First World War he raised vast sums for soldiers and prisoners of war.

In 1946 he shared the Nobel Peace Prize, and two years later the World Council of Churches made him its honorary president. He died in Orlando, Florida, on 31 January 1955, aged eighty-nine. “The missionary church is a praying church,” he wrote. “The history of missions is a history of prayer.”
