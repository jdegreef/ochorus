Amy Beatrice Carmichael was born on 16 December 1867 in Millisle, County Down, in the north of Ireland, the eldest of seven children in a devout Presbyterian home. As a small girl she prayed for her brown eyes to turn blue. In Belfast she gathered the poorest mill girls, the “shawlies,” for Sunday classes, and through the Keswick Convention she learned to lay everything at the feet of Christ.

Her health was judged too frail for China, so in 1893 she sailed for Japan, and in November 1895 she reached South India. She never left. Preaching in the villages around Tinnevelly, she learned that young children were being given to the temples. On 7 March 1901 a girl of seven named Preena, who had fled a temple, found her way to Amy, and her course was set. Around the village of Dohnavur grew a family, not an institution, where rescued children called her Amma, mother.

She would ask no one but God for money, and the Fellowship never lacked one good thing. She was accused of kidnapping and buried children she had fought to save, yet her book Things as They Are told the truth plainly. In 1931 a fall left her largely bedridden for her last twenty years; from that room she wrote most of her thirty-five books, among them Gold Cord and If.

She died at Dohnavur on 18 January 1951, having served fifty-five years without furlough. No stone marks her grave — only a birdbath and the word Amma. Her counsel still stands: “Wait in the stillness, and in that stillness assurance will come to you.”
