John Wesley was born at Epworth rectory, Lincolnshire, on 17 June 1703, the fifteenth child of Samuel and Susanna Wesley. Not yet six, he was pulled from the burning rectory — to his mother, “a brand plucked out of the burning.” Her ordered home shaped him. At Oxford he became a Fellow of Lincoln College and led the Holy Club, mocked as Methodists. A failed mission to Georgia humbled him, and on 24 May 1738, in Aldersgate Street, he felt his “heart strangely warmed.”

In 1739, urged by George Whitefield, he began preaching in the open air at Bristol. “I look upon all the world as my parish,” he wrote. For more than fifty years he rode the roads of England, rising at four and preaching at five — by later estimates some 250,000 miles and 40,000 sermons. His genius was organisation: societies, weekly classes, lay preachers and, from 1744, an annual Conference.

He preached grace at every stage of the Christian life, and taught that love for God and neighbour can grow until it governs a life. He visited prisoners, set up schools, organised care for the sick poor, and gave away nearly all he earned. His marriage in 1751 to Mary Vazeille was unhappy. He loathed slavery, wrote Thoughts upon Slavery in 1774, and six days before his death wrote to urge William Wilberforce on.

He died beside City Road Chapel on 2 March 1791, aged eighty-seven, an Anglican priest to the end, leaving a coat, a few silver spoons and a movement. Near the end he said the thing he had ridden fifty years to tell: “The best of all is, God is with us.”
