Athanasius of Alexandria was born in that city around AD 296, into a Greek-speaking Egyptian world where the church had just come through the worst persecution in its history. He grew up knowing Coptic as well as Greek. As a young deacon, secretary to Bishop Alexander, he travelled to the council at Nicaea in 325, perhaps twenty-seven years old and without a vote.

The question there had been raised by Arius, who taught that the Son was the highest of creatures, yet still a creature. Athanasius saw what it cost: if the Son is a creature, the whole Christian hope collapses. He became bishop of Alexandria in 328 and held that office for forty-five years — seventeen of them in exile, driven out five times by four emperors. In 356 soldiers broke into his church by night; he was carried out, and the desert monks hid him for six years.

Out of those years came The Life of Antony, which did more than any other text to carry the monastic ideal into the West. His festal letter of 367 is the earliest surviving list of exactly the twenty-seven books of the New Testament, and his little book On the Incarnation is still read today. He wrote endlessly to friends from the Egyptian desert to Rome and the Rhine.

He came home for the last time in 366 and died in Alexandria on 2 May 373, having outlived the emperors who exiled him. Eight years later the church confessed that the Son is of one being with the Father. His faith is caught in one line: “He was made man that we might be made God.”
