Samuel Ajayi Crowther was born Àjàyí in the Yoruba town of Osogun around the year 1809. In 1821 raiders burned the town, and the boy, about twelve, was carried off and sold down to the coast — once bartered for a horse. Hours after he was packed into a slave ship, the Royal Navy intercepted it, and he was landed at Freetown, Sierra Leone. There the Church Missionary Society taught him to read, and he came to see “another worse state of slavery.” “It pleased the Lord to open my heart,” he wrote. He was baptized on 11 December 1825.

He was the first student of Fourah Bay College, and in 1829 married Asano, a fellow liberated captive baptized Susan Thompson. After surviving the fever-stricken Niger Expedition of 1841, he was ordained a priest on 11 June 1843 — the first African clergyman of the Anglican Church.

At Abeokuta he found his own mother, Afala, lost to him twenty-five years before; she was later baptized Hannah. He gave himself to translating the Scriptures and the Book of Common Prayer into Yoruba, building its grammar almost from nothing. On 29 June 1864, in Canterbury Cathedral, he was consecrated Bishop of the Niger Territories, the first African bishop of the Anglican communion.

In the 1880s younger European missionaries moved against his mission and pushed out the African clergy he had trained. He did not answer with grievance, and in 1890 resigned with dignity. He died in Lagos on 31 December 1891, about eighty-two years old. Of the day of his capture he had learned to say that he was “now taught, in other respects, to call blessed day.”
