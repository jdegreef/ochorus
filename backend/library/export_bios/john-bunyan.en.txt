John Bunyan was born in 1628 at Harrowden, in Elstow parish near Bedford, the son of a tinker, and a tinker himself. He married, and they were “as poor as poor might be,” but his wife’s dowry was two godly books, and their daughter Mary was born blind. One day, in a game of cat on the green, “a voice did suddenly dart from heaven into my soul.” Hearing poor women of Bedford speak of the new birth, he sought out their pastor, John Gifford, joining his church in 1653.

Years of torment followed, as he feared that, like Esau, he had sold his birthright. Deliverance came in a field, when he saw that his righteousness was Jesus Christ Himself. He began to preach, and in November 1660 he was arrested at Lower Samsell. Offered release if he would stop, he said that if he were out of prison today he would preach again tomorrow. He spent twelve years in Bedford gaol, making laces to feed his family and grieving most for his blind child.

In prison he wrote Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners (1666) and dreamed the dream that became The Pilgrim’s Progress, published in 1678 after his release, with Part Two in 1685. Freed in 1672, he served as pastor of the Bedford church, riding out to preach and settle disputes, and people called him Bishop Bunyan.

In August 1688 he rode to Reading to reconcile a father and his son, then on to London through a storm. He died of fever on 31 August, aged fifty-nine, and was buried in Bunhill Fields. Of the day grace found him, he had written: “Now did my chains fall off my legs indeed.”
