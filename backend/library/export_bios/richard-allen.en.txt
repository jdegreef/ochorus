Richard Allen was born a slave in Philadelphia on 14 February 1760. While he was still a boy his family was sold to Stokeley Sturgis, a farmer near Dover, Delaware. At about seventeen he heard a Methodist preacher, came under deep conviction, and cried to God day and night until the burden broke: “my dungeon shook, my chains flew off.”

He worked all the harder to show that faith had not spoiled him, and Sturgis let Methodist meetings be held in the farmhouse. There Freeborn Garrettson preached against slaveholding; Sturgis was converted and let the brothers buy their freedom. By 1783 the debt was paid, and Allen took to the roads as an itinerant preacher, attending the 1784 Christmas Conference and coming to know Francis Asbury.

In Philadelphia his early-morning preaching filled St. George’s Methodist Church with Black worshippers — until a trustee tried to haul Absalom Jones off his knees in the gallery during prayer, and they all walked out together. When yellow fever struck in 1793, Allen and Jones rallied Black Philadelphians to nurse the sick and bury the dead. In 1794 he made an old blacksmith’s shop into Bethel, “house of God” — Mother Bethel — and in 1799 Asbury ordained him a deacon.

In 1816 Allen gathered Black Methodist congregations into the African Methodist Episcopal Church and was consecrated its first bishop. He championed education, sheltered fugitives from slavery, and died on 26 March 1831, at seventy-one. He had written words that never lost their force: “God himself was the first pleader of the cause of slaves.”
