Jeanne-Marie Bouvier de la Motte Guyon was born on 13 April 1648 in Montargis, south of Paris, into a prosperous and devout family. Taught by nuns, she longed for the cloister as a child, yet felt the pull of vanity too. At fifteen she was married to Jacques Guyon, twenty-two years her senior, in “a house of affliction.” Smallpox scarred her, and several of her children died young. In 1668 a Franciscan friar told her to seek God in her own heart, and she found Him there.

Widowed in 1676 at twenty-eight, she gave herself to the prayer of the heart. With the priest François La Combe she traveled through Savoy and Switzerland, teaching inward prayer to convents and lay people alike. In 1685 she published A Short and Easy Method of Prayer, urging that every believer is called and enabled to pray.

Her teaching stirred alarm. She befriended François Fénelon, later archbishop of Cambrai, and both were drawn into a storm led by Bishop Bossuet, who heard in her words the condemned Quietism. Arrested in 1695, she was held at Vincennes, at Vaugirard and from 1698 in the Bastille, until March 1703. Yet she wrote that the stones of her prison shone like rubies, and she sang in her cell.

Released on condition of silence, she lived out her years at Blois, near her daughter, receiving those who sought her counsel. She died there on 9 June 1717. Poiret, Tersteegen, Wesley, the Quakers and later Watchman Nee carried her books far beyond France. Her final words were as simple as her faith: “I love my God, and my God loves me — that is all.”
