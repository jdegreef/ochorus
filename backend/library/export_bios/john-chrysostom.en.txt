John Chrysostom was born in Antioch around AD 347. His father, an army officer, died while he was an infant, and his mother Anthusa, widowed at twenty, raised him alone. Trained by the great pagan orator Libanius, he went into the law courts, but turned from their corruption, was baptised in his early twenties, and lived as a monk in his mother’s house. After her death he spent six years in the mountains, and ruined his health for good.

Ordained presbyter in 386, he preached through whole books of the Bible verse by verse, hard on wealth and warm to the poor. In 387, when rioters pulled down the emperor’s statues, he held the terrified city together with twenty-one sermons, the Homilies on the Statues, until pardon came. From those years too comes his searching treatise on the priesthood.

In 398 he was made bishop of Constantinople. He sold palace furnishings and church silver to build hospitals, and preached against luxury in a city where the empress Eudoxia’s household embodied it. In 403 Theophilus of Alexandria had him deposed; he was recalled, then exiled for good in 404. From Cucusus he wrote seventeen letters to Olympias, a widow who had given her fortune away.

Ordered on to Pityus, he was marched on foot for three months, and died on 14 September 407 near Comana in Pontus. In 438 his body was brought home, and Eudoxia’s children knelt to ask forgiveness for their parents. Later generations named him golden-mouthed. His last words were his lifelong motto: “Glory be to God for all things.”
