Blaise Pascal was born on 19 June 1623 in Clermont, in central France, and taught by his father in Paris. At sixteen he wrote a treatise on geometry; at nineteen he built a calculating machine; his experiments on the Puy de Dôme proved that the air has weight, and his letters with Fermat founded the mathematics of probability. From the age of eighteen, he said, he never passed a day without pain.

In 1646 his family met the devout followers of Port-Royal and was awakened, but the change did not yet go to the root. Then, on the night of 23 November 1654, alone in his room, he met God. He sewed the record into his coat for the rest of his life: “FIRE. God of Abraham, God of Isaac, God of Jacob, not of the philosophers and the learned. Certainty, certainty, feeling, joy, peace. God of Jesus Christ.”

In 1656–57 he wrote the Provincial Letters, a witty attack on a moral theology that let people keep their sins while feeling at peace with God. He then began a great defence of the Christian faith, starting not from proofs but from man’s greatness and misery. Illness kept him from finishing it; the fragments were published after his death as the Pensées. “The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know.”

In his last years he gave much of what he had to the poor, and moved out of his own house rather than turn out a poor family whose child fell ill. He died in Paris on 19 August 1662, aged thirty-nine. Among his last words: “May God never abandon me.”
