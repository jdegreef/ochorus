Richard Sibbes was born in 1577 at Tostock in Suffolk, the son of Paul Sibbes, a wheelwright, who is said to have wished the boy would take up the family trade. Instead he went from the grammar school at Bury St Edmunds to St John’s College, Cambridge, and became a Fellow in 1601. Around 1603 he was converted under the plain, searching preaching of Paul Baynes, whom he ever after called his father in the gospel.

From about 1610 he lectured at Holy Trinity Church, Cambridge, and the town discovered a preacher. In 1617 he became preacher to the lawyers of Gray’s Inn in London; in 1626 he was made Master of St Catharine’s Hall, and in 1627 Doctor of Divinity. In an age when much preaching pressed hard on the conscience, he sought out the doubting and downcast, and was called “the sweet dropper” and “the Heavenly Doctor.”

His best-known book, The Bruised Reed, argues that Christ does not despise weak, struggling faith but tends it; The Soul’s Conflict with Itself teaches the downcast to reason with their own sinking hearts. Through him John Cotton was converted, and a copy of The Bruised Reed, sold by a pedlar, opened the love of God to the young Richard Baxter.

Sibbes died unmarried at Gray’s Inn on 5 July 1635, having preached a week before on “Let not your heart be troubled.” Asked how his soul stood, he said he should do God much wrong if he did not say, very well. Izaak Walton wrote of him: “Heaven was in him, before he was in heaven.” His own words still comfort: “There is more mercy in Christ than sin in us.”
