Clement of Rome led the church at Rome in the generation after the apostles, yet almost everything about his life is contested. Irenaeus lists him as the third to hold oversight in Rome after the apostles, and says that Clement had seen them and conferred with them. Some identified him with the fellow worker Paul names in Philippians — an attractive idea that cannot be proved.

What is certain is his letter. Around AD 95 or 96 word reached Rome that the church at Corinth had deposed its blameless presbyters. The reply — the earliest Christian document to survive outside the New Testament — names jealousy as the disease, traces it through Scripture, and gives the earliest account outside the New Testament of the deaths of Peter and Paul. Its remedy is order, humility and love.

It closes in a long prayer for the sick, the hungry and the imprisoned, and even for the emperor. That prayer was lost for centuries with a missing leaf of the Codex Alexandrinus, until in 1873 Philotheos Bryennios found a complete copy in Constantinople. Around AD 170 the letter was still being read aloud in Corinthian worship on the Lord’s Day.

Of his death nothing reliable is known; tradition dates his life from about AD 35 to about AD 99, and the stories of exile and an anchor belong to later legend. He left one letter, one prayer, a church at Corinth that stopped fighting — and these words: “Love unites us to God. Love covers a multitude of sins.”
