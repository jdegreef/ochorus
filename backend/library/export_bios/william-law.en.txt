William Law was born in 1686 at King’s Cliffe, the fourth of eight sons of Thomas Law, a grocer, and his wife Margaret. On 7 June 1705 he was admitted a sizar — a poor scholar — of Emmanuel College, Cambridge, and by 1711 he had taken his degree, been elected a fellow and been ordained. But when George I came to the throne in 1714, Law would not swear allegiance against the oath he had already given, and as a Non-Juror he lost his fellowship and any hope of preferment.

Barred from the church’s advancement, he wrote. From 1727 he lived with the Gibbon family at Putney as tutor, and at the end of 1728 published A Serious Call to a Devout and Holy Life. Its argument was simple: if prayer must accord with the will of God, why should the other twenty-three hours be exempt? He urged a rule of fixed hours of prayer, and kept one himself.

The book stirred Samuel Johnson to think in earnest about religion; George Whitefield found God working powerfully upon his soul through it; John Wesley pressed it on his preachers. Later Law turned to the mystic Jacob Boehme, writing The Spirit of Prayer and The Spirit of Love, and parted painfully with Wesley.

From 1744 he lived at King’s Cliffe with Elizabeth Hutcheson and Hester Gibbon, giving almost all their income away in schools, almshouses and soup for the poor. He died on 9 April 1761, aged seventy-five, almost in the act of singing. His book still teaches: “Devotion signifies a life given, or devoted, to God.”
