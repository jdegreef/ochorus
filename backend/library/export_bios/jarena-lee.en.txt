Jarena Lee was born free in Cape May, New Jersey, on February 11, 1783 — a rare mercy for a Black child in revolutionary America. At seven she was sent sixty miles from her parents to work as a servant girl, and grew up lonely and hungry for God. Drawn to Philadelphia, she sat under Richard Allen at Bethel African Methodist Episcopal Church, and there, about 1804, she was converted.

Soon an inward voice told her to preach. Allen replied that the Methodist Discipline made no provision for women preachers. She submitted, but the call only deepened. In 1811 she married the Reverend Joseph Lee of Snow Hill; within about six years she had buried her husband and several of her children. About 1819, when a visiting preacher faltered at Bethel, she sprang to her feet and exhorted on his text. Expecting disgrace, she heard Allen declare that God had called her, and she became the first woman authorized to preach in the AME Church.

For decades she travelled — on foot, by wagon and by rail — preaching to Black and white alike, even in the slave states. In one year she went more than two thousand miles and preached nearly two hundred sermons. In 1836 she paid to print her own life story, the first such book by an African American woman, and in 1849 she enlarged it with her journal.

She is thought to have died about 1864, her last years unrecorded. In 2016 the AME Church posthumously ordained her. Her plea endures: “If the man may preach, because the Saviour died for him, why not the woman? seeing he died for her also. Is he not a whole Saviour, instead of a half one?”
