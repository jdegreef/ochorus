Charles Grandison Finney was born in Warren, Connecticut, in 1792, the youngest of nine, and grew up on the frontier of upstate New York. He never went to college, but read law in the town of Adams. The Scripture cited in his law books pressed on him, and in the autumn of 1821 he went into the woods resolved to settle the matter with God, and gave himself to Christ. That evening he felt “waves and waves of liquid love.”

Telling a deacon the next morning that he had “a retainer from the Lord Jesus Christ to plead his cause,” he left the law, was licensed to preach, and married Lydia Root Andrews. Across the “burned-over district” — Evans’ Mills, Utica, Troy — towns were turned upside down, and the Rochester revival of 1830–31 changed a whole city. He pioneered the “new measures,” including the anxious seat, and credited hidden intercessors such as Daniel Nash and Abel Clary.

His methods made enemies, among them Asahel Nettleton and Lyman Beecher, and critics charged that he made revival a matter of technique. From 1832 he pastored in New York City, barring slaveholders from the Lord’s Table. In 1835 he joined Oberlin, serving as its president from 1851 to 1866, and his Lectures on Revivals of Religion went round the world.

He preached and taught almost to the end, and died at Oberlin on August 16, 1875, at eighty-two. For fifty years he called others to the obedience he had begun in a wood: “A revival is nothing else than a new beginning of obedience to God.”
