Thomas Watson was born around 1620; his birthplace is uncertain, though Yorkshire is often suggested, and of his parents, childhood and conversion almost nothing is known. He emerges at Emmanuel College, Cambridge, the great nursery of Puritanism, where he took his BA in 1639 and his MA in 1642 and left the reputation of a laborious student.

In 1646 he came to St Stephen’s, Walbrook, in the City of London, first as lecturer and then as rector, and for nearly sixteen years the church was constantly full. He married Abigail Beadle, and four of their children died young. Edmund Calamy called him “eminent in the gift of prayer.” A royalist Presbyterian, he was imprisoned in 1651 over Christopher Love’s plot to bring in Charles II, and released in 1652.

Yet in 1662 the Act of Uniformity ejected him with some two thousand others. He preached wherever he safely could, and after the Great Fire of 1666 fitted up rooms for any who would come; from 1672 he preached at Crosby House, later alongside Stephen Charnock. In 1663 he published A Divine Cordial, now known as All Things for Good — the comfort of a newly silenced minister that God’s providences work together for the best to the saints.

He died suddenly at Barnston in Essex in 1686 — said, by tradition, to have been at prayer. His A Body of Practical Divinity, published in 1692, became a people’s theology for generations. Charles Spurgeon judged him “concise, racy, illustrative, and suggestive.” Of prayer Watson wrote: “Prayer keeps the heart open to God, and shut to sin.”
