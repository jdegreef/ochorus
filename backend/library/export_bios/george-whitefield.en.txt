George Whitefield was born in December 1714 at the Bell Inn in Gloucester, where his mother kept the house, and as a boy he put on a blue apron and washed mops. In 1732 he went up to Pembroke College, Oxford, as a servitor, joined the Holy Club, and read Henry Scougal’s The Life of God in the Soul of Man. He tried to earn grace by fasting until his health broke; when it came at last, it filled him with “joy unspeakable.”

Ordained deacon at Gloucester in 1736, at twenty-one, he soon found pulpits closed to his preaching of the new birth. In February 1739 he preached in the open air to about two hundred colliers at Kingswood; within weeks the crowds were thousands, and he saw “the white gutters made by their tears” on their black cheeks. Seven visits to America followed, and scattered stirrings there fused behind him into the Great Awakening.

In Philadelphia Benjamin Franklin, unconverted but affectionate, measured the carrying power of his voice and printed his journals. The burden of his life was the Bethesda orphanage near Savannah, begun on 25 March 1740, which for thirty years he begged for and poured his own money into. He parted from John Wesley over predestination, yet they loved each other to the end, and Wesley preached his funeral sermon.

A friend testified that he preached from forty to sixty hours every week. He died at Newburyport on 30 September 1770 and lies beneath its pulpit. The day before, worn out and asthmatic, he had preached for two hours in the fields, after a prayer that was answered to the letter: “Lord Jesus, I am weary in thy work, but not of thy work.”
