Arthur Tappan Pierson was born on 6 March 1837 in Chatham Street, New York, and named in honour of the merchant and abolitionist Arthur Tappan. At thirteen, at revival meetings in a Methodist church in Tarrytown, he publicly professed faith in Christ. After Hamilton College and Union Theological Seminary, he married Sarah Frances Benedict in 1860 and took his first pastorate at Binghamton.

In 1869 he was called to Fort Street Presbyterian Church in Detroit. In March 1876 the church burned, and in the borrowed opera house revival came: more souls were converted that year than in all his previous ministry, almost all of them outsiders. In 1880 George Müller told him that if he walked in the light, he would have more — words that never left him. In Philadelphia he trained workers among the urban poor.

In 1886 he published The Crisis of Missions and urged students at Mount Hermon to evangelize the world in their own generation; from their response grew the Student Volunteer Movement. He edited The Missionary Review of the World, filled Spurgeon’s pulpit from 1891 to 1893, and in 1896 was baptised as a believer, at the cost of his standing. In 1899 he published George Müller of Bristol.

Having preached more than thirteen thousand sermons and written more than fifty books, he set out past seventy for the missions of East Asia, fell ill, and died in Brooklyn on 3 June 1911. His warning still stands: “We risk the forfeiture of privileges which are not employed for God.”
