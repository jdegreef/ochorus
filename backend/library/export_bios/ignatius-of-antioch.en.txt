Ignatius of Antioch was born around AD 35, probably in Syria. Later writers said he was the second or third to hold the oversight of the church at Antioch after Peter — the church where the disciples were first called Christians — and a tradition that cannot be verified made him a hearer of the apostle John. He called himself Theophorus, God-bearer.

Around AD 107 to 110, most likely in the reign of Trajan, he was arrested and condemned to the beasts at Rome, fifteen hundred miles away. Why, we do not know. Marched overland through Asia Minor, “bound to ten leopards, I mean a band of soldiers,” he received delegations from the churches near Smyrna and wrote seven short letters: to Ephesus, Magnesia, Tralles and Rome, and then from Troas to Philadelphia, to Smyrna and to its young bishop, Polycarp.

The letters are almost entirely about other people. He pleads for unity around the bishop, presbyters and deacons and the one Eucharist; insists that Christ truly suffered in the body; and asks church after church to pray for his flock: “Remember in your prayers the Church in Syria, which now has God for its shepherd, instead of me.” He tells Polycarp to stand firm like an anvil under the hammer, and not to let the widows be neglected.

Of his death we have no eyewitness account; he was executed at Rome, almost certainly in the arena. His letters, readable in an evening, remain among the most urgent in Christian literature. To the Romans he wrote: “I am the wheat of God, and let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, that I may be found the pure bread of Christ.”
