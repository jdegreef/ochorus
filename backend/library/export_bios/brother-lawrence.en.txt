Brother Lawrence was born Nicolas Herman around 1614 in Hériménil, in the Duchy of Lorraine, to poor and devout parents. At about eighteen, seeing a tree stripped of its leaves in winter and knowing they would be renewed, he received “a high view of the Providence and Power of God” that never left him. He went for a soldier, was wounded in the leg in 1635 and lamed for life, and then served as a footman — “a great awkward fellow who broke everything.”

In 1640 he entered the Discalced Carmelites in Paris as a lay brother. For years he was troubled by the fear that he would be damned, until he found himself “changed all at once” into a profound inward peace. For some fifteen years he was the community’s cook, a work he naturally disliked; later he mended its sandals. In every task he found his pleasure in doing little things for the love of God.

His secret he called the practice of the presence of God: “I began to live as if there was none but He and I in the world.” He held that our sanctification depends not on changing our works but on doing for God’s sake what we commonly do for our own. “In the noise and clatter of my kitchen … I possess God in as great tranquility as if I were upon my knees at the blessed sacrament.”

He died in Paris on 12 February 1691. His friend Joseph de Beaufort gathered his conversations and letters, and Catholics and Protestants alike — John Wesley among them — have read them ever since.
