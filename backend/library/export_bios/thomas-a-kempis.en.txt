Thomas à Kempis was born Thomas Hemerken in 1379 or 1380 in the town of Kempen in the Rhineland, the son of John and Gertrude Hemerken, working people. Following his elder brother John, he went at about twelve or thirteen to the famous school at Deventer, heartland of the Modern Devotion. There Florens Radewijns took him in, and the Brethren of the Common Life formed him in a plain, practical faith: less speculation, more obedience.

In 1399 he went to Mount St Agnes, a poor new house of Canons Regular outside Zwolle, where his brother was among the community. He made his profession in 1407 and was ordained priest in 1413, and he stayed there roughly seventy years. He copied manuscripts — the entire Bible by hand among them — kept the house’s Chronicle, and as sub-prior became master of the novices.

For those young men he wrote, between roughly 1418 and 1427, four small booklets of counsel that became The Imitation of Christ — after the Bible, very likely the most widely read Christian book ever written. It went out anonymously, and for centuries its authorship was disputed, but his own signed manuscript of 1441 survives, and modern scholarship has come down decisively on his side. He also wrote sermons, prayers, hymns and the lives of the Modern Devotion’s founders.

He died at Mount St Agnes on 25 July 1471, after Compline, in his ninety-first or ninety-second year, and was never canonised. His book has been read by Ignatius of Loyola, John Wesley and countless ordinary Christians. Its counsel is simple: “Vanity of vanities, all is vanity, save to love God, and Him only to serve.”
