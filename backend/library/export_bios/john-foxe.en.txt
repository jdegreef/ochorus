John Foxe was born at Boston, in Lincolnshire, about 1516. His father died when he was very young, and his mother married Richard Melton, a kind stepfather. At Oxford he became a fellow of Magdalen College, and in those years came over, thoroughly, to the Reformed faith. In 1545 he gave up his fellowship rather than take priest’s orders and enforced celibacy. In 1547 he married Agnes Randall, and he later taught the orphaned Howard children.

When Mary came to the throne the fires began, and in 1554 he fled by ship to Flanders, just ahead of Bishop Gardiner’s officers. In exile at Strasbourg, Frankfurt and Basel, poor and working as a reader of proofs, he gathered news of the martyrs from England — letters from prison, trial records, last words. His Latin folio appeared in 1559.

Home under Elizabeth, he and the printer John Day issued the Actes and Monuments in 1563, 1,827 pages long, known ever since as Foxe’s Book of Martyrs, and an enlarged edition in 1570. It told of Ridley, Latimer and Cranmer, and of weavers and housewives no one else would have remembered. Convocation ordered it set up in every cathedral, and it became perhaps the most widely read book in Protestant England after the Bible. In 1575 he begged the queen to spare two Anabaptists from the fire: “I have no favour for heretics, but I am a man and would spare the life of a man.”

He died in London on 18 April 1587, about seventy, and was buried at St Giles, Cripplegate. He wrote, he said, “that the wonderful works of God in His Church might appear to His glory.”
