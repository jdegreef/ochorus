John Milton was born on 9 December 1608 on Bread Street in the City of London. He learned Latin, Greek and Hebrew as a boy, and went up to Christ’s College, Cambridge, at sixteen. Unable in conscience to take a clergyman’s oaths under the bishops of his day, he gave himself to study and poetry; at twenty-one he wrote “On the Morning of Christ’s Nativity.”

For twenty years he wrote prose for causes that lost: against the bishops, for divorce, and for a free press in Areopagitica (1644) — “Give me the liberty to know, to utter, and to argue freely according to conscience, above all liberties.” He served the Commonwealth as Secretary for Foreign Tongues. By 1652 he was totally blind, and that year lost his wife Mary and their only son. Out of that darkness came his sonnet on his blindness: “They also serve who only stand and wait.”

At the Restoration he went into hiding and was held in custody until December 1660. Blind, he rose at four to have the Hebrew Bible read to him, and dictated Paradise Lost, published in 1667 and in its twelve-book form in 1674. He wrote it to “assert the Eternal Providence, / And justify the ways of God to men.” Paradise Regained and Samson Agonistes followed in 1671. On the Trinity his private treatise departs from the creeds, but readers of every tradition have found the poem a feast of the biblical story.

He died on 8 November 1674 and was buried at St Giles Cripplegate. His epic ends in hope: “The world was all before them, where to choose / Their place of rest, and Providence their guide.”
