Jonathan Edwards was born on 5 October 1703 in East Windsor, Connecticut, the only son among the eleven children of the minister Timothy Edwards. He entered Yale before he was thirteen and delighted in the natural world, even ballooning spiders. Around 1721, reading 1 Timothy 1:17, he found “a sense of the glory of the Divine Being; a new sense,” and God’s sovereignty became sweet to him. He wrote seventy resolutions, among them: “Resolved, to live with all my might, while I do live.”

In 1727 he married Sarah Pierpont and was ordained at Northampton, Massachusetts, beside his grandfather Solomon Stoddard. A revival in 1734–35 brought more than three hundred souls to Christ, told in A Faithful Narrative. In 1741 he preached “Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God” at Enfield, and later weighed the Awakening in Religious Affections (1746).

In 1750 his church dismissed him in a dispute over who should come to the Lord’s Table. At Stockbridge, on the frontier, he served the Mahican and Mohawk peoples, defended their lands, and wrote Freedom of the Will. He also owned enslaved people — a grievous failure. His Humble Attempt (1747), a call to united prayer, later stirred John Sutcliff’s praying circle, from which came the Baptist Missionary Society and William Carey’s voyage to India.

In 1758 he became president of the College of New Jersey. Within five weeks a smallpox inoculation went badly, and he died on 22 March 1758, aged fifty-four. He sent his wife word of their “uncommon union,” and told those grieving by his bed: “Trust in God, and ye need not fear.”
