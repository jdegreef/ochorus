Frederick Brotherton Meyer was born in Clapham, London, on 8 April 1847, and grew up in a comfortable, devout home, sitting as a boy under the preaching of William Brock at Bloomsbury Chapel. Called to the ministry at sixteen, he trained at Regent’s Park College, took his degree at the University of London in 1869, and began his pastoral work in Liverpool and then York. There he befriended the little-known American evangelist D. L. Moody, whose counsel — that a great preacher must be a great worker — shaped the rest of his life.

The decisive hour came in Leicester, where he met C. T. Studd and the young missionaries bound for China. Their joy exposed what he lacked. Unable to hand Christ the last key of his heart, he prayed, “I am not willing, but I am willing to be made willing” — and found the surrender given. From then on his ministry turned from teaching doctrine to leading believers into a Spirit-empowered walk with God.

At Melbourne Hall in Leicester and at Regent’s Park Chapel and Christ Church, Lambeth, in London, his congregations numbered thousands. He addressed twenty-six Keswick Conventions and preached across five continents, campaigning against drink, gambling and vice and founding homes for the destitute. He wrote more than seventy books — Bible biographies and devotional classics such as The Secret of Guidance — which sold in the millions across every denomination.

He died on 28 March 1929, mourned as “the Archbishop of the Free Churches.” Days before, he wrote to a friend: “It may be that before this reaches you, I shall have entered the palace. Don’t trouble to write. We shall meet in the morning.”
