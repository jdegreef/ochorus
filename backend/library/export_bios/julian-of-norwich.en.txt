We do not know her name. She is called Julian because she lived in a cell against the wall of St Julian’s church in Norwich, and she called herself only “a simple creature unlettered.” She was born late in 1342 or early in 1343, and grew up as the Black Death came again and again to her city.

As a young woman she asked God for three gifts, among them three wounds: “the wound of very contrition, the wound of kind compassion, and the wound of steadfast longing toward God.” In May 1373, aged thirty and a half, she lay dying. Her priest set a crucifix before her, her pain was taken away, and over the following hours she received sixteen “shewings” of Christ’s love — among them all creation held in her palm, “the quantity of an hazel-nut,” lasting because “God loveth it.”

She wrote them down, then spent some twenty years praying over what they meant, and the answer came: “Love was His meaning.” Out of those years came the Long Text of Revelations of Divine Love, the first book in English known to have been written by a woman. She faced the hardest question of her century — why sin and sorrow were not prevented — and was told: “all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well.”

As an anchoress she counselled those who came to her window; Margery Kempe sought her out in about 1413. She was still alive in 1416, and the date of her death is unknown. Her book is still read for its promise: “Thou shalt not be overcome.”
