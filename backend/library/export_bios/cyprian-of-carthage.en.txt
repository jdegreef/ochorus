Cyprian of Carthage was born Thascius Caecilius Cyprianus, probably around AD 200 to 210, into wealth in Carthage. A fashionable teacher of rhetoric, he was past forty when an elderly presbyter named Caecilianus spoke to him of Christ, and it undid him. He took the old man’s name as his own, sold his estates for the poor, and told his friend Donatus of the “second birth” that had made him a new man.

Within two years, against his own protest, the Christians of Carthage acclaimed him bishop; five senior presbyters never forgave him. Then, in 250, the emperor Decius ordered every citizen to sacrifice to the gods. Cyprian went into hiding and governed by letter — comforting the imprisoned, holding a shattered church together, and later restoring the lapsed through genuine penance.

When plague struck Carthage in 252 — still called the Plague of Cyprian — he organised relief for Christian and pagan alike, nursing the people who had wanted him dead. He wrote the first Latin commentary on the Lord’s Prayer and a treatise on the unity of the church: “He can no longer have God for his Father, who has not the Church for his mother.” More than eighty of his letters survive.

Exiled to Curubis under Valerian, he was brought back to Carthage, tried, and beheaded on 14 September 258, binding his own eyes with his own hands. He was the first bishop of Carthage to die a martyr. His letter to Donatus still speaks for him: “what had been thought impossible, to be capable of being achieved.”
