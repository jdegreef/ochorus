David Brainerd was born on a Sabbath day, April 20, 1718, in Haddam, Connecticut; both his parents were dead before he was fourteen. A serious, melancholy boy, he sought peace for years without finding it, until on July 12, 1739, walking alone in a grove, “unspeakable glory seemed to open to the view and apprehension of my soul.” At Yale, in the fervor of the Great Awakening, he said of a tutor, “He has no more grace than this chair,” and was expelled in 1742.

Licensed to preach that year, he gave himself to the Native American peoples — first at lonely Kaunaumeek, sleeping on straw, then at the Forks of the Delaware. For long seasons there was almost nothing to show, and he fought gloom all his life. What sustained him was prayer: whole days of fasting alone in the woods.

In June 1745 he came to Crossweeksung, New Jersey, and the drought broke. On August 8, preaching the free grace of Christ to about sixty-five hearers, he saw the power of God descend “like a mighty rushing wind.” Drunkenness was abandoned and debts were paid; within eleven months thirty-eight had made a public profession of faith.

Tuberculosis closed in, and on October 9, 1747, he died in Jonathan Edwards’s home in Northampton, not yet thirty. Edwards published his diary, which became one of the most reprinted devotional books in English; William Carey carried it to India. To the end Brainerd’s desire was unchanged: “My work is done,” he said; “all my desire is to glorify God.”
