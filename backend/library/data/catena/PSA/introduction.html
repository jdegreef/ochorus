The Psalms have been the Church’s prayer book from the beginning. Monks sang all hundred and fifty of them every week; reformers set them to metre so whole congregations could sing them; preachers have opened them to the sick, the dying and the glad for two thousand years. And because so many have prayed them, so many have written on them. This book gathers some of that writing in the old way, as a catena, a chain: under each verse, the words of earlier readers linked one after another, so that a single line of a psalm comes with a whole conversation attached.

How this book works

Each chapter of the book is one psalm, from the first to the hundred and fiftieth, under the heading the Open Word Bible gives it. The psalm comes first, a verse or a few verses at a time, set apart as a quotation. Then come the commentators who wrote on those words, in the order they lived: the early Church first, then the Reformation, then the eighteenth century, then the nineteenth. Then the psalm moves on. Every verse of the Psalms appears once, in order, so the book can be read straight through as the Psalter itself.

You will not find every commentator on every verse. Between them these writers left millions of words on the Psalms, far more than anyone could read in a year. What is here is a selection, chosen so that each psalm is heard from more than one century and more than one tradition. A short psalm gets a few voices; a long one gets more, though never as many as its verses. Where a verse has no comment beneath it, the psalm simply speaks for itself.

The voices

There are seven of them. From the early Church comes Augustine, who preached and wrote on every one of the Psalms. From the Reformation come John Calvin in Geneva and the Jesuit cardinal Robert Bellarmine, a Protestant and a Catholic reading the same psalms within a lifetime of each other. From the 1700s comes Matthew Henry, whose commentary has fed English-speaking Christians for three hundred years. From the 1800s come the German Hebrew scholar Franz Delitzsch, A. R. Fausset of the Church of England, and Charles Haddon Spurgeon, the Baptist preacher of London, whose Treasury of David is the voice heard most often here.

They do not always agree. Augustine hears Christ and his Church in nearly every psalm; Calvin looks first for David’s own meaning; Delitzsch weighs the Hebrew; Spurgeon preaches. That is part of the point. A catena does not hand you one reading and close the book; it lets you overhear the Church praying and thinking aloud, and then sends you back to the psalm. The last chapter of this book says a little more about each of them, and names the edition every excerpt was taken from.

A note on the numbers

This book numbers the psalms and their verses as English Bibles do. Not every commentator did. In the Hebrew Bible a psalm’s title, such as “A psalm of David”, often counts as its first verse, so the verses after it run one number ahead of ours, and Delitzsch, who worked from the Hebrew, numbers them that way: his “ver. 2” is often our verse 1. Augustine and Bellarmine read the Psalms in Latin, in a Bible that counts Psalms 9 and 10 as one, so from there on most of their psalm numbers are one lower than ours: their Psalm 22 is our Psalm 23. None of this changes where an excerpt stands. Each one is placed under the verse it actually discusses, whatever number its author gave it.

A note on the words

The text of the Psalms is the Open Word Bible, a new translation from the Hebrew given freely to the public domain. The commentators wrote long before it, and when they quote a psalm they quote the English of their own editions: usually the King James Version, though Augustine’s and Bellarmine’s translators rendered the Latin they read. So you will read “The Lord is my shepherd” in one place and “The Lord feedeth me” in another. Both reach back to the same psalm.

Every excerpt is copied word for word from a public-domain English edition, as gathered by Logia, an open collection of the Church’s commentary. Old spelling and punctuation stand as printed; only the typesetting has been tidied. Several of these editions survive only as old scans, which misread a word here and there, so excerpts the scan had damaged were passed over.

How to read it

Slowly. A psalm a day takes you through the Psalter in five months; the longest, Psalm 119, can be taken over several days. Read the psalm first, then the voices, then the psalm again, this time as a prayer. Notice where the commentators agree and where they part company, and what each one sees that the others miss. The Psalms end where all of this is meant to end: “Let every creature that breathes praise the LORD!”
