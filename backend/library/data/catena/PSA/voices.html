Seven people speak in this book, across fifteen centuries. Here is a little about each of them, in the order they lived, and then the edition every excerpt was copied from.

The early Church

Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo in North Africa, wrote on every one of the hundred and fifty psalms, some in sermons preached to his people, some dictated for readers. Together these Expositions are the longest of all his works. He hears in the Psalms the voice of Christ, sometimes speaking as the head, sometimes as his body the Church, and he reads them in the Latin of his day.

The Reformation

John Calvin (1509–1564), the reformer of Geneva, published his commentary on the Psalms in 1557. In its preface he called the book “an anatomy of all the parts of the soul”, because there is no feeling a person can have that is not pictured in it. He looks first for what David meant, then turns it to the reader’s use.

Robert Bellarmine (1542–1621), an Italian Jesuit and cardinal, was the leading Catholic theologian of his generation. His explanation of the Psalms, published in 1611, near the end of his life, follows the Latin Vulgate verse by verse. It reads like a meditation.

The 1700s

Matthew Henry (1662–1714) was a Presbyterian minister at Chester in England. His commentary on the whole Bible, written to be read at home, has been loved for three centuries for its warmth and its gift for pointing a verse at the heart.

The 1800s

Franz Delitzsch (1813–1890) was a Lutheran scholar of the Old Testament and of Hebrew, who taught at Erlangen and then, for his last twenty years, at Leipzig. His commentary on the Psalms, written for the series he made with Carl Friedrich Keil, weighs the Hebrew line by line. He numbers the verses as the Hebrew Bible does, so his verse numbers often run one ahead of the ones in this book.

A. R. Fausset (1821–1910), a clergyman of the Church of England, wrote the books from Job to Malachi for the commentary he made with Robert Jamieson and David Brown. His notes on the Psalms are short and exact, often no more than a phrase explained.

C. H. Spurgeon (1834–1892), the Baptist preacher of the Metropolitan Tabernacle in London, spent some twenty years on The Treasury of David, his exposition of the whole Psalter. Each psalm in it has Spurgeon’s own exposition, followed by notes gathered from older writers; only his own words are quoted here. He is the voice heard most often in this book. The scanned volumes it draws on lack a few psalms, among them 111 to 119, so in those the others speak without him.
