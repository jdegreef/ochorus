There is an old way of reading Scripture that is almost lost: slowly, one verse at a time, in company. The Church called it a catena, a chain. Under each verse the reader found the words of earlier readers, linked one after another, so that a single sentence of the Gospel came with a whole conversation attached. Thomas Aquinas made the most famous of them, the Catena Aurea or “Golden Chain” on the four Gospels, from the writings of the Fathers. This book is a chain of the same kind, on the Gospel of Matthew, and its links run on past Aquinas into the centuries after him.

Matthew has stood first in the New Testament for as long as there has been one. It is the Gospel of the Sermon on the Mount and the Lord’s Prayer, of the parables of the kingdom, of the Great Commission, and in the early Church it was the Gospel most often quoted. So it has gathered more commentary than almost any book of the Bible.

How this book works

Each chapter of the book is a chapter of Matthew. The chapters are divided into Matthew’s own passages, the sections the Open Word Bible marks with a heading: the magi, the Beatitudes, the Lord’s Prayer, the two builders, and so on, a hundred and forty-nine in all.

Within a passage the Gospel comes first, a verse or a few verses at a time, set apart as a quotation. Then come the commentators who wrote on those words, in the order they lived: the early Church first, then the Reformation, then the eighteenth century, then the nineteenth. Then the Gospel moves on. Every verse of Matthew appears once, in order, so the book can be read straight through as the Gospel itself.

You will not find every commentator on every verse. Between them these writers left more than a million words on Matthew. What is here is a selection, about one excerpt in eleven, chosen so that each passage is heard from more than one century. Where a verse has no comment beneath it, the Gospel simply speaks for itself.

The voices

There are four of them, one from each age of the Church’s reading. From the early Church comes John Chrysostom, the “golden-mouthed” preacher of Antioch, whose ninety homilies on Matthew were preached to his congregation around the year 390. From the Reformation comes John Calvin in Geneva, who expounded Matthew, Mark and Luke side by side in his Harmony of the Evangelists. From the 1700s comes Matthew Henry, whose commentary has fed English-speaking Christians for three hundred years. From the 1800s comes J. C. Ryle, the first Bishop of Liverpool, writing for families to read aloud at home.

They do not always agree. Chrysostom reads as a bishop of the undivided Church, Calvin as a Reformer, Henry as an English Nonconformist, Ryle as an evangelical Anglican, and on questions like Peter and the keys or the Lord’s Supper you may hear the difference. That is part of the point. A catena does not hand you one reading and close the book; it lets you overhear the Church thinking aloud, and then sends you back to the text. The last chapter of this book says a little more about each of them, and names the edition every excerpt was taken from.

Two of them can be read whole in this library: Chrysostom’s Homilies on Matthew and Ryle’s Expository Thoughts on Matthew. The Gospel itself, in the Open Word Bible with an introduction, is here too, as The Gospel of Matthew.

A note on the words

The Gospel text is the Open Word Bible, a new translation from the Greek given freely to the public domain. The commentators, though, wrote long before it, and when they quote Scripture they quote it in the English of their own editions, usually the King James Version. So you will sometimes read “You are the salt for the earth” above and “Ye are the salt of the earth” below. Both render the same Greek.

Every excerpt is copied word for word from a public-domain English edition, as gathered by Logia, an open collection of the Church’s commentary. Their old spelling and punctuation stand as printed; only the typesetting has been tidied. The translations of Chrysostom and Calvin come from the Victorian scholars who first put them into English, so they read a little formally. Read them aloud and they come alive.

How to read it

Slowly. A passage a day is enough; a hundred and forty-nine passages take you through Matthew in about five months. Read the Gospel first, then the voices, then the Gospel again. Notice where they agree and where they part company. Notice what each one sees that the others miss. Jesus himself described a reader like that: “every scribe who has been trained as a disciple for the kingdom of heaven is like a householder who draws from his store of treasures both new things and old.”
