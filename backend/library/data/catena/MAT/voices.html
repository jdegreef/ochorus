Four people speak in this book, across fifteen centuries, one from each age of the Church’s reading. Here is a little about each of them, in the order they lived, and then the edition every excerpt was copied from.

The early Church

John Chrysostom (c. 347–407) was a preacher at Antioch and later archbishop of Constantinople. His name means “golden-mouthed”. His ninety homilies on Matthew were preached to an ordinary congregation, and he never forgets them: he explains the text, then turns to how they are living.

The Reformation

John Calvin (1509–1564), the reformer of Geneva, published his commentary on the first three Gospels in 1555, setting Matthew, Mark and Luke side by side as one harmony. The excerpts here are his comments on Matthew’s verses. He aimed at what he called “lucid brevity”: plain sense first, then its use for faith.

The 1700s

Matthew Henry (1662–1714) was a Presbyterian minister at Chester in England. His commentary on the whole Bible, written to be read at home, has been loved for three centuries for its warmth and its gift for pointing a verse at the heart.

The 1800s

J. C. Ryle (1816–1900), later the first Bishop of Liverpool, wrote his Expository Thoughts on the Gospels for family reading, beginning with Matthew in 1856. He draws out a few plain lessons from every passage, and presses them home.
