In the early 1260s Pope Urban IV asked Thomas Aquinas, the Dominican friar who would become the Church’s best-known theologian, for a commentary on the Gospels. What Aquinas made was not a commentary in his own words. He went through the four Gospels verse by verse and, under each, set the words of the Fathers of the Church, Greek and Latin, one after another, so that they read almost as a single continuous exposition. He called it a continuous gloss. Later readers, who prized it above most books of its kind, gave it the name it has kept ever since: the Catena Aurea, the Golden Chain.

Aquinas finished the volume on Matthew first and dedicated it to Urban. The Pope died in 1264, and Aquinas dedicated the volumes on Mark, Luke and John to his friend Cardinal Annibaldo degli Annibaldi. For those later volumes he had more of the Greek commentators put into Latin, so that the East is heard beside the West: Chrysostom, Cyril, Theophylact and Basil next to Augustine, Ambrose, Gregory and Bede.

This book is that chain on the Gospel of {gospel}, in the English translation made at Oxford in the 1840s under the editorship of John Henry Newman. It is one of four volumes of The Golden Chain, one for each Gospel.

How to read this book

Each chapter of the book is a chapter of {gospel}, divided into the Gospel’s own passages, which the Open Word Bible marks with a heading. Where Aquinas’s chain on a chapter runs long, it is given in parts, each named by the verses it covers, such as {gospel} 5:1–12, and each ending where one of those passages ends. Within a passage the Gospel comes first, a verse or a few verses at a time, set apart as a quotation. Then comes Aquinas’s chain on those words. Every verse of the Gospel appears once, in order, so the book can also be read straight through as the Gospel itself.

Each link of the chain opens with the name of the one speaking, in bold: Chrysostom, Augustine, Bede. Aquinas set them in the order that best explains the verse, not in the order they lived, and he often lets one Father answer another. When a passage begins “Or”, a second reading of the same words is being offered beside the first; the Fathers do not always agree, and Aquinas does not make them.

A few names need a word of explanation. Gloss is the Glossa Ordinaria, the standard notes that medieval Bibles carried in their margins and between the lines. A name beginning Pseudo-, as in Pseudo-Chrysostom, is a writer whose work came down under a greater Father’s name but is not his; Aquinas knew it under the greater name, and the Oxford translators mark it so. The Greek Expositor is a Greek commentator whom Aquinas quotes without a name.

The Fathers quote Scripture as they knew it, and the Oxford translators rendered their quotations in the English of the old Bibles, so a verse may read one way in the Gospel above and another way in the Father below. Both render the same Gospel.

The Catena Aurea is long, and it is not meant to be hurried. A passage a day, read slowly, with the Gospel first and then the Fathers, and then the Gospel again, is the way it was made to be used.
