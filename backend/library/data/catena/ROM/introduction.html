There is an old way of reading Scripture that is almost lost: slowly, one verse at a time, in company. The Church called it a catena, a chain. Under each verse the reader found the words of earlier readers, linked one after another, so that a single sentence of Scripture came with a whole conversation attached. This book is a chain of that kind, on Paul’s letter to the Romans.

No book of the Bible has been argued over more closely. Romans is where Augustine’s conversion turned, where Luther found the gospel again, where Wesley felt his heart “strangely warmed” while someone read aloud from Luther’s preface to it. It is a letter, written to a church Paul had never visited, and it moves like an argument: sin, then grace, then faith, then the life of the Spirit, then the long question of Israel, then the plain duties of love. A catena suits it well. Each step of the argument is heard here from more than one century, and you can watch the Church follow Paul, and sometimes stumble after him.

How this book works

Each chapter of the book is a chapter of Romans. The chapters are divided into the letter’s own passages, the sections the Open Word Bible marks with a heading, sixty-one in all.

Within a passage the text of Romans comes first, a verse or a few verses at a time, set apart as a quotation. Then come the commentators who wrote on those words, in the order they lived: the early Church first, then the Reformation, then the eighteenth century, then the nineteenth. Then the letter moves on. Every verse of Romans appears once, in order, so the book can be read straight through as the letter itself.

You will not find every commentator on every verse. Between them these writers left nearly three-quarters of a million words on Romans. What is here is a selection, about one excerpt in thirteen, chosen so that each passage is heard from more than one century and more than one tradition. Where a verse has no comment beneath it, Paul simply speaks for himself.

The voices

There are seven of them. From the early Church comes John Chrysostom, the “golden-mouthed” preacher, whose thirty-two homilies on Romans are among the great early expositions of Paul. From the Reformation comes John Calvin, whose commentary on Romans was the first of his commentaries on Scripture. From the 1700s come John Evans, who wrote the exposition of Romans in Matthew Henry’s great commentary after Henry’s death; the German scholar Johann Albrecht Bengel; and John Wesley, who built his own notes on Bengel’s. From the 1800s come George Leo Haydock, an English Catholic priest whose family Bible gathered notes from earlier commentators, and Charles Hodge of Princeton, whose commentary on Romans was read by generations of students.

They do not always agree. On election, on justification, on the place of Israel and on the meaning of Romans 7, a Greek preacher, a Reformer, a Methodist and a Catholic each hear Paul a little differently. That is part of the point. A catena does not hand you one reading and close the book; it lets you overhear the Church thinking aloud, and then sends you back to the text. The last chapter of this book says a little more about each of them, and names the edition every excerpt was taken from.

Two of them can be read whole in this library: Chrysostom’s Homilies on Romans and Calvin’s Commentary on Romans. The letter itself, in the Open Word Bible with an introduction, is here too, as The Letter to the Romans.

A note on the words

The text of Romans is the Open Word Bible, a new translation from the Greek given freely to the public domain. The commentators, though, wrote long before it, and when they quote Scripture they quote it in the English of their own editions, usually the King James Version, and for Haydock the Catholic Douay version. So you will sometimes read “the just shall live by faith” below a verse that says “the one who is righteous will live by faith”. Both render the same Greek.

Every excerpt is copied word for word from a public-domain English edition, as gathered by Logia, an open collection of the Church’s commentary. Their old spelling and punctuation stand as printed; only the typesetting has been tidied. Several of these books reach us through scans of old printings, and where a scan had damaged an excerpt, it was left out.

How to read it

Slowly. A passage a day is enough; sixty-one passages take you through Romans in about two months. Read Paul first, then the voices, then Paul again. Notice where they agree and where they part company. Notice what each one sees that the others miss. And remember why, by Paul’s own account, the old writings were kept at all: “Everything that was written in earlier times was written to teach us. It was written so that we might have hope, as we endure and as the Scriptures encourage us.”
