Seven people speak in this book, across fifteen centuries. Here is a little about each of them, in the order they lived, and then the edition every excerpt was copied from.

The early Church

John Chrysostom (c. 347–407) was a preacher at Antioch and later archbishop of Constantinople. His name means “golden-mouthed”. He loved Paul above all the writers of Scripture, and his thirty-two homilies on Romans follow the letter closely, explaining the argument and then turning to how his hearers were living.

The Reformation

John Calvin (1509–1564), the reformer of Geneva, published his commentary on Romans in 1540, the first of the commentaries he went on to write on most of the Bible. He believed that whoever understood Romans had a way opened into the whole of Scripture.

The 1700s

John Evans (1680–1730) was a Presbyterian minister in London. Matthew Henry died in 1714 with his commentary on the whole Bible finished only as far as the end of Acts, and a group of ministers completed it from his notes; Evans wrote the exposition of Romans. It keeps Henry’s method and warmth, and has been printed as part of Henry’s commentary ever since.

Johann Albrecht Bengel (1687–1752) was a Lutheran scholar in Württemberg. His Gnomon, “pointer”, of 1742 is famously brief: a few words that open a whole verse. His notes often begin with the word of the text they explain.

John Wesley (1703–1791), the founder of Methodism, wrote his Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament for ordinary readers and leaned openly on Bengel. His notes are the shortest in this book.

The 1800s

George Leo Haydock (1774–1849) was an English Catholic priest. His edition of the Douay Bible, with notes gathered from many earlier commentators, became a family Bible for English-speaking Catholics. He signs a borrowed note with its source: “Wi.” for the commentator Robert Witham, “Ch.” for Bishop Challoner.

Charles Hodge (1797–1878) taught for more than fifty years at Princeton Theological Seminary. His commentary on Romans, first published in 1835, set out the letter for students of the English Bible with a Reformed theologian’s care.
