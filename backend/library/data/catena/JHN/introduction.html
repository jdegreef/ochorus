There is an old way of reading Scripture that is almost lost: slowly, one verse at a time, in company. The Church called it a catena, a chain. Under each verse the reader found the words of earlier readers, linked one after another, so that a single sentence of the Gospel came with a whole conversation attached. Thomas Aquinas made the most famous of them, the Catena Aurea or “Golden Chain” on the four Gospels, from the writings of the Fathers. This book is a chain of the same kind, on the Gospel of John.

How this book works

Each chapter of the book is a chapter of John. The chapters are divided into John’s own passages, the sections the Open Word Bible marks with a heading: the Word made flesh, the wedding at Cana, Nicodemus by night, the woman at the well, and so on, eighty-six in all.

Within a passage the Gospel comes first, a verse or a few verses at a time, set apart as a quotation. Then come the commentators who wrote on those words, in the order they lived: the early Church first, then the Reformation, then the eighteenth century, then the nineteenth. Then the Gospel moves on. Every verse of John appears once, in order, so the book can be read straight through as the Gospel itself.

You will not find every commentator on every verse. Between them these writers left millions of words on John, far more than anyone could read in a year. What is here is a selection, about one excerpt in twenty, chosen so that each passage is heard from more than one century and more than one tradition. Where a verse has no comment beneath it, the Gospel simply speaks for itself.

The voices

There are eleven of them. From the early Church come Origen, the first great Christian scholar of the Bible; John Chrysostom, the “golden-mouthed” preacher of Antioch; Augustine, preaching to his congregation in Hippo in North Africa; and Cyril of Alexandria. From the Reformation come John Calvin in Geneva and the Flemish Jesuit Cornelius a Lapide, a Protestant and a Catholic reading the same Gospel in the same century. From the 1700s come Matthew Henry, whose commentary has fed English-speaking Christians for three hundred years, the German scholar Johann Albrecht Bengel, and John Wesley, who built his own notes on Bengel’s. From the 1800s come J. C. Ryle, the first Bishop of Liverpool, and B. F. Westcott, the Cambridge scholar who became Bishop of Durham.

They do not always agree. Calvin argues with the Fathers, Lapide with the Reformers, Westcott with almost everyone about the Greek. That is part of the point. A catena does not hand you one reading and close the book; it lets you overhear the Church thinking aloud, and then sends you back to the text. The last chapter of this book says a little more about each of them, and names the edition every excerpt was taken from.

A note on the words

The Gospel text is the Open Word Bible, a new translation from the Greek given freely to the public domain. The commentators, though, wrote long before it, and when they quote Scripture they quote it in the English of their own editions, usually the King James Version. So you will sometimes read “In the beginning was the Word” above and “the Word was made flesh” below. Both are the same Greek.

Every excerpt is copied word for word from a public-domain English edition, as gathered by Logia, an open collection of the Church’s commentary. Their old spelling and punctuation stand as printed; only the typesetting has been tidied. The translations of the Fathers come from the Victorian scholars who first put them into English, so they read a little formally. Read them aloud and they come alive.

How to read it

Slowly. A passage a day is enough; eighty-six passages take you through John in under three months. Read the Gospel first, then the voices, then the Gospel again. Notice where they agree and where they part company. Notice what each one sees that the others miss. And remember why John said he wrote at all: “These, though, are written down so that you may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and by believing have life in his name.”
