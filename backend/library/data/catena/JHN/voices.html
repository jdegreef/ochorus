Eleven people speak in this book, across sixteen centuries. Here is a little about each of them, in the order they lived, and then the edition every excerpt was copied from.

The early Church

Origen (c. 185–c. 253) taught in Alexandria and later in Caesarea, and was the first Christian to study the Bible as a scholar with everything he had. His commentary on John is one of the earliest Christian commentaries on any book of Scripture. Only part of it survives, and only its opening reaches this book, but it is where the chain begins.

John Chrysostom (c. 347–407) was a preacher at Antioch and later archbishop of Constantinople. His name means “golden-mouthed”. His eighty-eight homilies on John were preached to an ordinary congregation, and he never forgets them: he explains the text, then turns to how they are living.

Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo in North Africa, preached his way through John in 124 sermons, the Tractates. They are the most personal commentary here: he argues, wonders aloud, and keeps coming back to love.

Cyril of Alexandria (c. 376–444), bishop of Alexandria, wrote a long commentary on John with a theologian’s eye, always asking what each verse shows about who Christ is.

The Reformation

John Calvin (1509–1564), the reformer of Geneva, published his commentary on John in 1553. He aimed at what he called “lucid brevity”: plain sense first, then its use for faith.

Cornelius a Lapide (1567–1637) was a Flemish Jesuit who taught at Louvain and Rome and wrote commentaries on almost the whole Bible. He gathers the Fathers generously, so through him many older voices reach the chain.

The 1700s

Matthew Henry (1662–1714) was a Presbyterian minister at Chester in England. His commentary on the whole Bible, written to be read at home, has been loved for three centuries for its warmth and its gift for pointing a verse at the heart.

Johann Albrecht Bengel (1687–1752) was a Lutheran scholar in Württemberg. His Gnomon, “pointer”, of 1742 is famously brief: a few words that open a whole verse.

John Wesley (1703–1791), the founder of Methodism, wrote his Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament for ordinary readers and leaned openly on Bengel. His notes are the shortest in this book.

The 1800s

J. C. Ryle (1816–1900), later the first Bishop of Liverpool, wrote his Expository Thoughts on the Gospels for family reading. He draws out a few plain lessons from every passage, and presses them home.

B. F. Westcott (1825–1901), Regius Professor of Divinity at Cambridge and later Bishop of Durham, wrote one of the most careful commentaries on John in English, first published in 1880. His notes weigh single words.
