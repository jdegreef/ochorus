— Song of Songs 8:14

The Song does not end with a wedding feast or a happy ever after. It ends with the woman calling her beloved to come quickly, like a gazelle over the mountains. After all its delight and union, the last word is still longing. Love, the Song seems to say, is never finished on this side of eternity; it is always calling the beloved nearer.

The question the book leaves with you

The Song asks what love is, and gives its answer in the great words of chapter 8: as strong as death, a flame that floods cannot put out, a treasure no fortune can buy. Then it leaves a question with every reader. Will you honour love as that kind of thing? Will you refuse to stir it up before its time, and keep faith with it when it comes? Will you see in the love of a man and a woman not something cheap or merely private, but one of God’s best gifts, to be guarded and celebrated?

That question matters in every age. Some cultures have made too much of desire, as if it were everything; others too little, as if it were shameful. The Song does neither. It places desire inside faithfulness, “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine”, and calls it good.

Where the Song points

Christians who read the Song have always found that it points beyond itself. Its love is as strong as death; the New Testament tells of a love that went into death and came out the other side. “No one has a love greater than this: that someone lays down their life for their friends” (John 15:13). Paul is sure that not death and not life “will be able to cut us off from God’s love, which is ours in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:39). Here, at last, is a love that many waters cannot put out.

And the Bible’s story ends where the Song ends, with a bride and a longing. Revelation sings that “the Lamb’s wedding has come, and his bride has made herself ready” (Revelation 19:7). Its final pages ring with the same call as the Song’s last verse: “The Spirit and the bride say, ‘Come!’” And the church’s answer is the Bible’s last prayer: “Amen! Come, Lord Jesus!” (Revelation 22:17, 20).

Where to go from here

The Song has drawn out some of the most heartfelt writing in the history of the church, and several of those writers are in this library:

  • On the whole Song. Hudson Taylor’s Union and Communion, six short meditations that walk through the Song from the bride’s first longing (1:2) to the last call (8:14), reading it as the story of the soul’s communion with Christ, broken and restored; and the Ochorus Original The Key Teachings of Hudson Taylor, whose chapters “The Unsatisfied Life”, “Communion Broken and Restored” and “Mine Own Vineyard” (on 1:6) open up that book.
  • On the Song and the love of God. Bernard of Clairvaux’s On Loving God, which hears the bride faint with love (2:5) and the bridegroom’s invitation, “Eat, friends” (5:1); and Hannah Whitall Smith’s chapter “The Song of Songs: The Love of Christ and the Church” in Old Testament Types and Teachings.
  • On praying the Song. Samuel Rutherford’s Letters, whose love for Christ, the beloved “marked out among ten thousand” (5:10), is written in the Song’s own language; and Jeanne Guyon’s A Short and Easy Method of Prayer, which prays the bride’s “Take me with you; let us run!” (1:4).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to the Song of Songs in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

And then read the Song again, slowly and aloud, as it was meant to be read. Its invitation still stands, spoken in a garden in spring:

“Get up, my darling, my beautiful one, and come away.”

— Song of Songs 2:10
