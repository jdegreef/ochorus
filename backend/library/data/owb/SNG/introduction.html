— Song of Songs 2:16

In the middle of the Bible there is a love song. It has no laws, no history of kings, no prophecy of judgment, and it mentions God, if at all, only once. It is a poem of longing and delight between a woman and a man: their first kisses, their searching for each other through the streets at night, their praise of each other’s beauty, their joy in a garden in spring. It is frank and tender and full of the scent of spices. Many readers are surprised to find it in Scripture at all.

Yet it has been treasured by Jews and Christians alike, and some of the greatest saints have loved it above every other book. Rabbi Akiva, in the second century, is remembered for saying that all the Scriptures are holy, but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies. For the Song tells us something the rest of the Bible assumes but seldom sings: that love between a man and a woman is good, God’s own gift, and strong enough to stand beside death itself.

“The Song of Songs”

The Hebrew title, Shir HaShirim, is a superlative. Hebrew joins a word to its own plural to mean the greatest of its kind, as the holy of holies is the most holy place and the king of kings the greatest king. So the title calls this the finest song of all: “The Song of Songs, the greatest of songs, which is Solomon’s.”

The small Hebrew word in front of Solomon’s name can mean by, for, about or belonging to, as in the psalm titles “of David”. Solomon was remembered for composing a thousand and five songs (1 Kings 4:32), and tradition has received the Song as his. He also appears in the poem as a figure, on his wedding day (3:7–11) and as the owner of a great vineyard (8:11–12), and some readers think the poem was written about him or dedicated to him rather than by him. Its date is debated. The title leaves the question open, and so does this translation.

Who is speaking

The Song gives no names and no stage directions. But Hebrew verbs and pronouns show whether a man or a woman is speaking or being spoken to, and whether one person or many, so the voices can usually be told apart. There are three: the woman, the man, and a group of friends, “the women of Jerusalem”, who listen and ask questions and sometimes answer.

The woman’s voice is heard more than any other. She speaks the first words of the poem and the last. She longs, she seeks, she praises, she warns. It is remarkable, in a book from the ancient world, that a woman’s desire is given so large and honoured a place.

One song, or many?

Readers differ over how the Song hangs together. Some find in it a single poem with a story: a courtship, a wedding at its centre (3:6–5:1), a time of loss and searching, and a love that has grown deep and sure by the end. Others find a collection of love songs, gathered and arranged so that they echo each other. Some have even seen three main characters, the woman torn between King Solomon and the shepherd she loves. The poem does not settle the question, and it can be read with delight either way.

What holds it together are its refrains. Three times the same refrain has the woman put her friends under an oath:

{{OWB:SNG.2.7.s1}}

— Song of Songs 2:7

The same words return at 3:5 and 8:4, and each time they close a section. Love has its own time; it is not to be forced or hurried. Another refrain speaks of belonging: “My beloved is mine, and I am his” (2:16), turned around in 6:3, “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine”, and deepened at last in 7:10. A third pictures the embrace: “His left arm is under my head, and his right arm holds me close” (2:6; 8:3).

Twice the woman searches through the city at night for “the one my soul loves” (3:1–4; 5:2–8). The first time she finds him. The second time she hesitates to open the door, and when she does he has gone; the watchmen beat her, and she can only ask her friends to tell him “I am faint with love.” The Song knows that love includes absence and longing as well as joy.

A garden in spring

The language of the Song comes from the countryside: vineyards and orchards, gazelles and doves, lilies and pomegranates, myrrh and frankincense, the mountains of Lebanon and the vineyards of En-gedi. Its lovers praise each other by comparison, and the comparisons are not always the ones a modern reader would choose. To be told that your hair is like a flock of goats pouring down a mountainside, or your neck like the tower of David, was high praise in ancient Israel, where these were pictures of grace and strength.

In one of its loveliest passages the man calls the woman out into the new season:

{{OWB:SNG.2.11.s1}} {{OWB:SNG.2.12.s1}}

— Song of Songs 2:11–12

He calls her “a garden locked up, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed” (4:12), and she answers by inviting him in: “Let my beloved come into his garden and taste its choice fruits” (4:16). Many readers have heard in these gardens an echo of Eden. In Genesis, after the fall, the woman was told, “Your desire will be for your husband, and he will rule over you” (Genesis 3:16). In the Song she says, “I am my beloved’s, and his desire is for me” (7:10). Here, for a moment, love is mutual delight again, as it was in the first garden.

How the Song has been read

Because the Song is in Scripture, and because it speaks so openly of human love, it has been read in more than one way, and Christians still hold different views of how its meanings fit together.

Jewish tradition has long read it as a picture of the love between God and Israel. It is read in the synagogue at Passover, the festival of God’s rescue of his people, and an ancient Aramaic paraphrase retells it as the story of Israel from the exodus onward.

The early church read it as a song of Christ and his church, or of Christ and the soul that loves him. Origen in the third century wrote a long commentary and homilies on it; Gregory of Nyssa preached on it; Bernard of Clairvaux gave eighty-six sermons on it and had reached only the start of the third chapter when he died. That reading ran on through the Puritans to Hudson Taylor, whose meditations on the Song are in this library.

Many readers today begin with what the poem plainly is: a celebration of the love of a man and a woman, which the Bible elsewhere also honours, from the first man’s joy at the first woman (Genesis 2:23–25) to the father’s counsel in Proverbs to “find your joy in the woman you married when you were young” (Proverbs 5:18). On this reading the Song is God’s blessing on desire and faithfulness within the covenant of love.

These readings need not compete. The Bible itself pictures God’s love for his people as a marriage: “your God will be glad over you with a groom’s joy over his bride” (Isaiah 62:5). Jesus calls himself the bridegroom, and Paul, writing of husband and wife becoming one flesh, says, “There is a great mystery here, but I am speaking about Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:32). Many Christians have found that the more truly the Song is read as a song of human love, the more clearly it points beyond itself.

Love as strong as death

Near the end of the poem the woman speaks the words that the whole Song has been building toward:

{{OWB:SNG.8.6.s1}} {{OWB:SNG.8.7.s1}}

— Song of Songs 8:6–7

A seal was a carved stone or ring that marked what was one’s own, worn on a cord over the heart or on the arm and never parted with. She asks to be that close, that much his. Then she says what love is: as strong as death, which takes everyone, and as relentless as Sheol, the world of the dead, which never gives back what it takes. No flood can put it out. No fortune can buy it.

The last word of verse 6 ends in -yah, the short form of God’s name. It may mean “a flame of the LORD”, which would make it the one mention of God in the Song: love is a fire that comes from him. Or it may simply mean “a mighty flame”, as Hebrew sometimes uses God’s name to mean “very great”. Either way, the Song ends its great saying on fire.

How to read the Song of Songs

  • Read it as poetry. Read it aloud, slowly, and let the pictures work on you. Not every image needs to be decoded.
  • Listen for the voices. Ask, as you go, who is speaking and to whom.
  • Follow the refrains. They mark the sections and carry the poem’s meaning.
  • Read it with discretion. The Song is frank about desire. Origen reported that the Jewish teachers of his day kept it back from the young until they were mature, and parents may still wish to choose when to share it.
  • Let it lead you to worship. Whatever else it is, the Song is a gift from God about one of his best gifts.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. The Song is poetry from beginning to end, so this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out. A few of its choices are worth knowing.

The title. The opening line keeps the name “The Song of Songs” and adds what the superlative means, “the greatest of songs”; “which is Solomon’s” keeps the Hebrew’s openness about whether it is by, for or about him.

Names of the lovers. She calls him “my beloved”; he calls her “my darling”, and later “my sister, my bride”, an ancient term of tender closeness. The women who listen are “the women of Jerusalem”, older Bibles’ “daughters of Jerusalem”.

Love’s fire. In 8:6 love’s “passion” stands where older Bibles have “jealousy”, since in English that word suggests suspicion; and the last word is read as “a mighty flame”.

The Hebrew numbers the verses of chapter 7 one ahead of most English Bibles; the references in this edition follow the English numbering. For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
