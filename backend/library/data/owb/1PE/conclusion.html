— 1 Peter 5:10

First Peter never pretends that suffering is small. It speaks of trials by fire, of slander and blows, of a devil who roars. But it measures all of it with one phrase: “for a little while”. Set against an inheritance that never withers and a God of all grace who has called his people to eternal glory, even the fiery trial is short. And the God who allows it is the one who will restore, strengthen and establish them himself.

The question the letter leaves

The question Peter leaves is how to live as an outsider without becoming bitter. His readers could have hardened into a resentful little group, hitting back at those who mocked them, or they could have quietly given in and gone back to their old ways. Peter shows them a third way, the way of Christ, who “did not answer abuse with abuse” but handed himself over to the God who judges justly. They are to bless instead of curse, to do good, to stay ready with a gentle answer for their hope, and to let their lives speak.

That way is not weakness. It takes a settled confidence in God to throw every worry onto him and still stand firm against the devil. Peter had learned that the hard way, on the night he denied his Lord by a fire in a courtyard. The man who writes “Stand up to him and hold firm in your faith” is a man who once did not, and was restored.

Where the letter points

It points forward, to the day “when Jesus Christ is revealed”, a phrase Peter returns to again and again. Hope, in this letter, is not wishful thinking but something solid, an inheritance kept in heaven for people kept by God’s power.

And it points to the church: a spiritual house of living stones, a holy priesthood, a flock with shepherds who serve gladly, and a family of brothers and sisters all over the world who are going through the same sufferings. No one in this letter suffers alone.

Where to go from here

First Peter has comforted the suffering church in every century, and many of its interpreters are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the living hope (1:3–9). Martyn Lloyd-Jones’s A Living Hope of the Hereafter (1:3–4); Andrew Murray’s Kept by the Power of God (1:5), in Absolute Surrender; John Wesley’s Heaviness through Manifold Temptations (1:6), in Sermons on Several Occasions; and Jonathan Edwards’s Religious Affections, which takes 1:8 as its text.
  • On holiness (1:15–16). Murray’s Holy in Christ, a month of readings on the call to be holy as God is holy.
  • On Christ the stone and the shepherd (chapter 2). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Christ Precious to Believers (2:7); his The Sin-Bearer (2:24), in Till He Come; and Hudson Taylor’s Under the Shepherd’s Care (2:25).
  • On Christ suffering for sins (3:18). John Flavel’s The Saints Brought Home to God, in The Method of Grace.
  • On humility under God’s hand (5:5–7). Thomas Boston’s The Crook in the Lot, which includes a sermon on 5:6, and Murray’s Humility, whose chapter Humility and Exaltation takes up the same verse.
  • On Peter and the churches he wrote to. The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp, from a bishop in the province of Asia who quoted this letter often and died for his faith; The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians, sent from Rome a generation later, which remembers Peter’s witness and death; and Eusebius of Caesarea’s The Church History.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 1 Peter in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.

But the best next step may be to read the letter again, the next time life feels like a fiery trial, and to hear it as its first readers did: as a word from someone who had suffered and failed and been restored, and who knew that the grace he wrote about was true.
