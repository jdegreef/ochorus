— 1 Peter 1:3–5

First Peter is a letter of hope written to people who were being made to feel they did not belong. Its readers had turned from the gods of their towns to the God of Israel, and their neighbours had noticed. They were slandered, mocked, treated as wrongdoers, and some of them, slaves and wives in unbelieving households, were suffering for their faith inside their own homes. Peter does not promise them that it will stop. He tells them who they are, what is waiting for them, and how to live in the meantime.

It is a short letter, but a full one. In five chapters it touches almost every great theme of the New Testament: new birth, the cross and the resurrection, baptism, the church as God’s people, holiness, the Christian household, the end of all things. Every one of them is turned toward a single purpose, written in its last lines: to encourage, and to testify that this is the true grace of God.

From Peter, through Silvanus

The letter opens “From Peter, an apostle of Jesus Christ”. Near the end Peter calls himself “an elder with you, a witness of the sufferings of Christ”. He says he has written “through Silvanus”, the Silas who travelled with Paul, and he sends greetings from “my son Mark” and from “She who is in Babylon, chosen together with you”. Most readers take that to be the church at Rome, called by the name of the great city of exile, as Revelation seems to do too. Eusebius, quoting the second-century bishop Papias, says the same.

The church received the letter as Peter’s from the beginning. Polycarp of Smyrna, writing early in the second century, quotes it again and again, and Eusebius counted it among the books no one disputed. Tradition holds that Peter died in Rome under Nero, in the mid-60s, so the letter would date from shortly before. Some modern scholars, noting its polished Greek and wondering whether a Galilean fisherman could have written it, think it came from a circle of Peter’s followers in Rome a decade or two after his death. Others answer that the phrase “through Silvanus” may mean that Silvanus helped write it, as secretaries often did, or simply that he carried it.

To exiles in Asia Minor

The letter is sent to God’s chosen people “who live as foreigners, scattered through Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia”, Roman provinces covering much of what is now northern and western Turkey. The list may trace the route the messenger was to take.

Its readers were mostly Gentiles. Peter speaks of “the useless way of living you inherited from your forefathers”, and reminds them that they once spent their time “doing as the pagans please”. Yet he addresses them with the titles of Israel, and calls them exiles of the dispersion. That is the heart of the letter’s comfort. The people around them treated them as outsiders. Peter tells them that is exactly what they are, “outsiders and temporary residents” in this world, because their home and their inheritance are elsewhere, kept safe in heaven.

The shape of the letter

  • 1:1–2:10: a chosen people with a living hope. New birth, an inheritance that cannot decay, a call to be holy, and a people built as living stones into God’s house.
  • 2:11–4:11: living well among outsiders. Honourable conduct before neighbours and rulers; slaves, wives and husbands; Christ’s example in suffering; and life in the light of the end.
  • 4:12–5:14: the fiery trial. Sharing Christ’s sufferings with joy, shepherds for the flock, humility, watchfulness, and greetings.

A living hope

The letter begins with praise. God, in his great mercy, has given his people a new birth, and the resurrection of Jesus has brought them into “a hope that is alive”. Their trials are real, and they hurt, but they are “for a little while”, and they are testing a faith worth more than gold. Then Peter writes one of the most tender sentences in the New Testament, to people who, unlike him, had never seen Jesus:

Even the prophets searched into this salvation, Peter says, and “Even angels long to bend down and look into these things.”

Holy, ransomed, born anew

Hope is not idle. “So brace your minds for action and stay clear-headed.” God’s people are to be holy, because he is holy, and to live in reverent fear, because of what their freedom cost:

— 1 Peter 1:17–21

They have been born anew through the living word of God. Human glory is like grass that withers. “But the word of the Lord lasts forever.”

Living stones

In chapter 2 Peter draws on a cluster of Old Testament passages about a stone: the stone God lays in Zion, the stone the builders rejected, the stone people trip over. Jesus is that stone, and those who come to him become living stones themselves, built into a house for the Spirit and serving as a holy priesthood. Then Peter gives them, Gentiles though most of them were, the titles God gave Israel at Sinai and in the prophets:

— 1 Peter 2:9–10

Luther drew from these verses his teaching of the priesthood of all believers: every Christian may come to God directly through Christ, and every Christian has something to offer.

Doing good among those who speak against you

The middle of the letter asks how such a people should live among neighbours who distrust them. Peter’s answer is: honourably, so that those who slander them may see their good works and glorify God. They are to respect the emperor and the governors, “Give honor to every person”, and live as free people who are slaves of God.

Then he speaks to the members of a household, as other New Testament letters do. Household slaves are to bear even unjust treatment patiently. Wives are to submit to their husbands, so that unbelieving husbands may be won over without a word, by their conduct and “a gentle and quiet spirit”. Husbands are to live with their wives with understanding and honour them as fellow heirs of “the grace of life”. Christians read these instructions in different ways. Some take them as a lasting pattern for marriage and the home. Others stress how they answer a particular world, in which a wife or slave was expected to share the religion of the head of the house, and in which Peter’s concern was that the gospel not be slandered. Readers of every view notice how much he says to those with the least power, and that he speaks to them directly, as people able to choose their own conduct before God.

And to the slaves, the most vulnerable of all, Peter gives his most beautiful words about Jesus, drawn from Isaiah 53:

Ready to give a reason

If believers suffer for doing what is right, they are blessed. They are not to fear their opponents but to “set Christ apart as the holy Lord” in their hearts, and to be ready to make their defence to anyone who asks the reason for their hope, “with gentleness and with reverence for God.” Christ suffered too, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring them to God.

Here comes the most puzzling passage in the letter. Put to death in the flesh and made alive in the spirit, Christ “went and made a proclamation to the spirits in prison, who had disobeyed long before” in the days of Noah (3:19–20). Who were they, and what did he proclaim? Augustine thought Peter meant that Christ preached through Noah to the people of his day. A long tradition, reflected in the creeds’ “he descended to the dead”, has seen Christ going to the realm of the dead between his death and resurrection. Many scholars today think of the rebellious spirits of Genesis 6, and of Christ, risen, announcing his victory over them. Whatever the answer, Peter’s point is clear: Christ is now at God’s right hand, and “angels, authorities, and powers have been made subject to him.”

The fiery trial

“The end of all things is near.” So Peter calls for clear heads, prayer, hospitality without grumbling, and above all love, “because love covers a great many sins.” Every gift is to be used in service, “as good managers of the richly varied grace of God.”

The trials may come like fire. They should not be surprised by it. To be insulted for the name of Christ is to have the Spirit of glory resting on them, and to suffer “for being a Christian” is no shame. It is one of only three times the New Testament uses that name.

The letter ends with a word to the elders, from one who had once been told by the lake to feed Christ’s sheep: tend the flock willingly, not for money, not as overlords, and the Chief Shepherd will give them a crown of glory. Then a word to all: put on humility, throw every worry onto God, stay awake, and stand firm against the devil, who prowls “like a lion that roars”.

How to read 1 Peter

Read it at a sitting, as a letter from a pastor to frightened people, and listen for three words that keep returning: hope, suffering and grace. Notice how often Peter turns from suffering to glory, and from the believer’s suffering to Christ’s. Notice too how he quotes the Old Testament, the Psalms, Isaiah, Exodus, Proverbs, as though it had been written for these Gentile believers, which, he says, in a sense it was (1:12).

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 1 Peter are worth knowing about before you begin.

Foreigners and outsiders. Peter uses a family of words for people living away from home without the rights of citizens. The translation keeps the picture plain: “foreigners” (1:1), “strangers far from home” (1:17), “outsiders and temporary residents” (2:11).

“The bruises he bore.” In 2:24, where older Bibles say “by whose stripes”, the translation reads “By the bruises he bore you have been healed”, keeping the language of Isaiah 53 in a form a modern reader can picture.

“The weaker vessel.” In 3:7 the translation keeps Peter’s own image, and its notes explain it as weaker in physical strength and in social power, not in mind, faith or worth. The same sentence calls the wife one “who will inherit the grace of life together with you.”

Baptism as a pledge. In 3:21 the translation reads baptism as “the pledge of a good conscience made to God”, a commitment made to God, rather than a request to him, as some Bibles have it.

“Christ.” The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose whether Christos reads “Christ” or “the Messiah”. This edition prints “Christ”.
