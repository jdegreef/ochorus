— 1 Corinthians 16:13–14

After sixteen chapters of argument, rebuke and pleading, Paul sums up in a few short commands. Be steady; be brave; and do all of it in love. It is the letter in miniature. The Corinthians had plenty of courage, plenty of conviction and plenty of gifts. What they lacked was the love that would make all of it build up rather than tear down.

The question the letter leaves

The question 1 Corinthians leaves with its readers is not whether they are gifted, or knowledgeable, or free. The Corinthians were all three. It is what their gifts, knowledge and freedom are for. Again and again Paul answers: for others. Knowledge is for building up the brother or sister for whom Christ died. Freedom is for winning others. Gifts are for the good of all. Even Paul’s rights as an apostle are there to be given up “so as not to block the path of the gospel of Christ in any way.”

That is a hard word for any church that prizes its talent, its eloquence or its spiritual experiences, which is to say for most churches. It is also a freeing one. A church that is a body does not need every member to be an eye. It needs each part to care for the others, so that when one suffers, all suffer, and when one is honoured, all rejoice.

Where the letter points

It points back to the cross. Paul’s answer to a church dazzled by wisdom and status was not a better kind of wisdom but a crucified Messiah, who is “God’s power and God’s wisdom”. Every later section of the letter follows the same pattern of strength found in weakness: an apostle who works with his hands, a body whose weakest parts are most honoured, a love that does not look for its own advantage.

And it points forward to the resurrection. The Corinthians were tempted to think either that the body did not matter, or that the life of heaven was already theirs in full. Paul answers both with the risen Christ, the firstfruits of a harvest still to come. Because the body will be raised, what we do with it now matters. Because the end has not yet come, the church lives in hope, praying “Our Lord, come!” And because Christ has won, “in the Lord your hard work is never wasted.”

Where to go from here

Many of the church’s great preachers have returned to this letter, and a number of them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • The church at Corinth, a generation later. The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians, written from the church in Rome around AD 96 to a congregation still divided, which tells them to take up Paul’s letter again.
  • On the wisdom of the cross (chapter 1). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Christ Crucified (1:23–24), George Whitefield’s Christ the Believer’s Wisdom, Righteousness, Sanctification and Redemption (1:30) and Jonathan Edwards’s God Glorified in Man’s Dependence (1:29–31).
  • On the race and the wilderness (chapters 9–10). Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s A Castaway (9:26–27) and Christmas Evans’s The Smitten Rock (10:4).
  • On the Spirit and his gifts (chapter 12). William J. Seymour’s Gifts of the Spirit (12:1), and in A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, the New Testament volume, the chapters “The Holy Spirit in the First Epistle to the Corinthians” and “The Holy Spirit in the Body of Christ”.
  • On love (chapter 13). Henry Drummond’s The Greatest Thing in the World, Martin Luther’s Paul’s Praise of Christian Love and Corrie ten Boom’s The Greatest of These Is Love (13:13).
  • On the resurrection (chapter 15). Dwight L. Moody’s One Word—“Gospel” (15:1), John Newton’s The Lord Is Risen Indeed (15:20) and Frederick W. Robertson’s Victory over Death (15:56–57).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 1 Corinthians in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.

And then read on, into 2 Corinthians, where the story continues: a painful visit, a letter written in tears, and a reconciliation that Paul could only call comfort. But first it may be worth reading chapter 13 once more, slowly, putting your own name where the word “love” stands, and seeing how far it still has to go. Paul would not mind. He ends, after all, with the three things that last:
