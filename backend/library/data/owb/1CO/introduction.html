— 1 Corinthians 1:22–24

Some time in the mid-50s AD, in the city of Ephesus, Paul had two kinds of news from Corinth on his desk. One was a report brought by “Chloe’s people”, members of a household he trusted, that the church he had founded was splitting into rival camps. The other was a letter from the church itself, full of questions: about marriage, about meat from pagan temples, about spiritual gifts. Three men from Corinth, Stephanas, Fortunatus and Achaicus, had come to see him in person.

His answer is the letter we call 1 Corinthians, though it was not the first he wrote them: he mentions an earlier one (5:9) that has not survived. It is the fullest window we have into a single early church, with all its gifts and all its mess. Paul deals with lawsuits, sex, food, dinner parties, hairstyles, chaotic worship and a collection of money, and brings every one of these ordinary matters, sooner or later, to the cross of Christ and to his resurrection.

A city and a church

Corinth stood on the narrow neck of land joining mainland Greece to the Peloponnese, with a harbour on each side: Lechaeum facing west toward Italy, Cenchreae facing east toward Asia. The Romans had destroyed the old Greek city in 146 BC, and Julius Caesar refounded it in 44 BC as a Roman colony. By Paul’s day it was the capital of the province of Achaia, rich, busy and keenly aware of status. The Isthmian Games were held nearby, which may be why Paul reaches so readily for pictures of runners and boxers.

Paul arrived around AD 50, alone and, he says, “weak and afraid”. Acts tells how he lodged with Aquila and Priscilla, Jewish believers who, like him, made tents, and how he stayed a year and a half (Acts 18:1–18). The church he left was mostly Gentile, with a few people of means and many more who had little, a mixture that lies behind much of the letter.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapters 1–4: divisions and the wisdom of the cross. The church has split into parties around favourite teachers; Paul answers with the foolishness of a crucified Messiah.
  • Chapters 5–6: the body belongs to the Lord. A case of incest the church has tolerated, believers suing one another, and visits to prostitutes.
  • Chapter 7: marriage and singleness. Paul begins answering their letter.
  • Chapters 8–10: food offered to idols. Knowledge, love, freedom and the conscience of others, with Paul’s own life as the example.
  • Chapters 11–14: when the church gathers. Head coverings, the Lord’s Supper, spiritual gifts, the body of Christ, love, tongues and prophecy.
  • Chapter 15: the resurrection of the dead.
  • Chapter 16: the collection, travel plans and greetings.

The repeated “Now about” (7:1, 7:25, 8:1, 12:1, 16:1, 16:12) probably marks each new question from the Corinthians’ letter.

Christ crucified

The trouble Paul tackles first is the one he cares about most. “I am for Paul,” “I am for Apollos,” “I am for Cephas,” “I am for Christ.” The church had turned its teachers into banners. His response is a set of short, stinging questions: “Has Christ been parceled out?” “Was it Paul who was crucified for you?”

Beneath the factions Paul sees a hunger for wisdom as the world measures it, for eloquence and status. So instead of choosing a side, he goes to the cross. “To those on the way to destruction, the message of the cross is foolishness; but to us who are being saved, it is God’s power.” In that weakness God has shown his wisdom and power, and the Corinthians themselves are the proof: when God called them, “not many of you were wise as people measure wisdom, not many held power, not many came from leading families.”

Paul’s own preaching in Corinth, he reminds them, had been deliberately plain:

— 1 Corinthians 2:2

As for the teachers they rally round, they are only servants. “I planted, Apollos watered, but it was God who kept making it grow.” The church is God’s field, God’s building, and “together you are God’s temple.” Boasting in one leader over another makes no sense, because everything they have was a gift: “What do you have that was not given to you?”

The body belongs to the Lord

Chapters 5 and 6 can be uncomfortable reading. A man in the church “is living with his father’s wife”, and the church, far from grieving, is proud of its tolerance. Paul calls them to act, with the hope that the man’s spirit “may be saved” on the day of the Lord. They must clear out the old yeast, for “Christ has been sacrificed as our Passover lamb.” Believers suing one another before pagan courts would do better to accept being wronged.

Some in Corinth had a slogan: “Everything is permitted for me.” This translation, like many, puts it in quotation marks, since Paul seems to be quoting the Corinthians before he answers them: “Yes, but not everything is for the good.” Their view seems to have been that the body did not matter much; what mattered was the spirit. Paul answers that the body matters a great deal. God will raise it. It is part of Christ’s body. It is a temple of the Holy Spirit:

{{OWB:1CO.6.19.s1}} {{OWB:1CO.6.19.s2}}

— 1 Corinthians 6:19–20

Among the hard words of chapter 6 there is a tender one. After listing the ways of life that do not belong in God’s kingdom, Paul adds, “Some of you once lived in these ways. But you have been washed clean; you have been made holy; you have been put right with God.”

Marriage and singleness

With chapter 7 Paul turns to their letter, and the first thing in it seems to be another slogan: “It is good for a man to have no sexual contact with a woman.” Some, it appears, thought spiritual people should give up sex even within marriage. Paul disagrees. Husband and wife belong to each other, and neither should refuse the other. At the same time he values singleness, which he himself lives, as a gift that leaves a person free to give undivided care to the things of the Lord.

Paul is careful about his sources: on divorce, “it is the Lord’s, not mine”; on a believer married to an unbeliever, “it is not a word from the Lord”. Over it all lies a sense of urgency, for “this world’s present shape is passing away.”

Knowledge and love

Much of the meat sold in a city like Corinth had passed through a pagan temple, and many social occasions took place in temple dining rooms. Could a Christian eat it? The confident said yes, with a point: “no idol in the world is anything real.” Paul agrees, and then adds a sentence that sums up the whole letter:

{{OWB:1CO.8.1.s2}}

— 1 Corinthians 8:1

The question is not only what I am free to do, but what my freedom does to “a brother or sister for whom Christ died.” In chapter 9 Paul gives himself as the example. He has every right to be paid for his preaching, and he will not use it. “To all people I have become all things, so that one way or another I may save some.” He trains like an athlete, not for “a wreath that soon withers” but for one that never decays.

Chapter 10 warns from Israel’s wilderness years, when the people drank from a rock (“that rock was Christ”) and still fell into idolatry. Then comes a promise many readers have leaned on in hard times: “God is faithful, and he will not allow the testing to go past your strength.” And a rule for all of life: “whatever you do, do everything for God’s glory.”

When you come together

Chapters 11 to 14 concern the church at worship. They open with instructions about men and women praying and prophesying with their heads covered or uncovered. This is one of the hardest passages in Paul to understand, and Christians read it in different ways: some as a lasting principle about men and women, some as guidance about the customs of a particular culture, with much discussion over whether “head” speaks of authority or of source. Paul plainly expects women to pray and prophesy aloud, and he insists, “Yet in the Lord there is no woman without man, and no man without woman.”

Then he turns to the Lord’s Supper, which in Corinth was eaten as part of a full meal. The richer members, free to arrive early, ate and drank their fill before the poorer ones came: “one is left hungry, and another is drunk.” That, says Paul, is not really the Lord’s Supper at all. And he reminds them of what he had handed on to them, in the earliest written account of the Supper we have:

{{OWB:1CO.11.23.s1}} {{OWB:1CO.11.25.s1}}

— 1 Corinthians 11:23–25

Christians have understood the bread and the cup in different ways, but Paul’s practical instruction is simple: “wait for each other.”

One body, many parts

The Corinthians prized spiritual gifts, especially speaking in tongues, and some seem to have treated them as a ladder of spiritual rank. Paul begins with what all believers share: “nobody can say, ‘Jesus is Lord,’ except through the Holy Spirit.” There are many gifts but one Spirit, and each gift “is given for the good of all.”

Then comes his most memorable picture: the church is a body, and a body needs every part. The foot cannot leave because it is not a hand. The eye cannot tell the hand, “I can do without you.” The parts that seem weakest are the most necessary. “If one part is in pain, all the parts share its pain.”

{{OWB:1CO.12.27.s1}}

— 1 Corinthians 12:27

The greatest is love

Between the gifts of chapter 12 and the order of chapter 14 Paul sets the passage on love, which is read at weddings the world over but was first written to a quarrelling church. Without love, tongues are noise, knowledge and faith are nothing, and even giving away everything does no good. Then he describes love, and every line is a portrait of what the Corinthians were not:

{{OWB:1CO.13.4.s1}} {{OWB:1CO.13.7.s1}}

— 1 Corinthians 13:4–7

“Love never comes to an end.” The gifts belong to the present age and will be done away with. “For now we see by means of a mirror, as if in a riddle, but then we will see face to face.” Most readers take “when what is complete arrives” to mean the return of Christ; some read it as the completed New Testament, and Christians who differ over whether gifts such as tongues continue today often part ways here.

Chapter 14 applies love to worship. Prophecy, which speaks to people in words they can understand, builds up the church; tongues without interpretation build up only the speaker. “I would sooner speak five words with my mind, so as to teach others too, than thousands upon thousands of words in a tongue.” Speakers should take turns, since “God is the God of peace, not of disorder”. The chapter also contains the instruction that women keep silent in the churches (14:34–35), which Christians have read in very different ways: as a limit on a particular disruptive kind of speaking, such as questions called out during the meeting, as a wider rule for church order, or as a later note copied into the text. The chapter ends: “But let everything be done properly and in good order.”

Raised from the dead

The letter’s last great argument begins with the gospel itself, a summary Paul had received and passed on, and which most scholars think was already a fixed formula within a few years of the first Easter:

{{OWB:1CO.15.3.s1}}

— 1 Corinthians 15:3–5

The witnesses follow: more than five hundred at once, James, all the apostles, and “Last of all, as though to a child born before its time”, Paul himself. Some in Corinth denied any resurrection of the dead, and Paul will not let them keep a faith without it: “if Christ has not been raised, your faith is worthless, and you are still in your sins.” But Christ has been raised, “the firstfruits of those who have died”, the first sheaf of a harvest still to come.

What will that resurrection be like? Paul’s answer is a seed. “A seed you sow does not come alive unless it dies.” What rises is continuous with what was sown, and gloriously different: “It is sown in decay; it is raised imperishable.” It is a “spiritual body”, by which Paul seems to mean not a body made of spirit, but a body given life by God’s Spirit. The chapter rises to a taunt flung at death itself: “Death, where is your victory? Death, where is your sting?” And it ends not in speculation but in work: “in the Lord your hard work is never wasted.”

A real church

The last chapter returns to earth. There is a collection for the poor believers in Jerusalem, to be put aside “on the first day of each week”. There are plans: Paul will stay in Ephesus until Pentecost, where “a great door stands open”; Timothy may come; Apollos will not, just yet. And there is a short command that gathers up the whole letter: “Whatever you do, do it in love.”

Then Paul takes the pen from his secretary: “I, Paul, am adding this greeting with my own hand.” Among his last words is a prayer that Christians were still saying in Aramaic, the language of Jesus, in a Greek-speaking church: Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!”

1 Corinthians in the life of the church

The church in Corinth did not stop quarrelling. Around AD 96 the church in Rome wrote to it again, in the letter known as First Clement, because the Corinthians had removed their elders. “Take up the epistle of the blessed Apostle Paul,” Clement urged them, reminding them that Paul had once had to write about Cephas and Apollos and the parties formed around them. It is among the earliest references to a New Testament letter by name.

John Chrysostom preached through the letter at Antioch in forty-four homilies. Chapter 15 is read at funerals in every Christian tradition, and Handel set its words about the last trumpet in Messiah. And the words of 11:23–26 are said, in one form or another, wherever Christians break bread.

How to read 1 Corinthians

Read it as a conversation of which we hear only one side. Notice when he seems to be quoting them, and how he answers.

Notice too how often he takes a practical question back to the centre. A dispute about teachers leads to the cross. A dispute about sex leads to the body as the Spirit’s temple. A dispute about food leads to the brother or sister for whom Christ died. A dispute about gifts leads to love. And a doubt about the future leads to the risen Christ. Read it straight through at least once, to hear how chapter 13 answers chapter 1 and chapter 15 answers them all.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 1 Corinthians are worth knowing about before you begin.

The Corinthians’ own words. Ancient Greek had no quotation marks, and scholars generally agree that Paul sometimes quotes sayings from Corinth in order to answer them. This translation marks the likeliest of these as quotations: “Everything is permitted for me” (6:12, and in a shorter form in 10:23), the saying about food and the stomach (6:13), “It is good for a man to have no sexual contact with a woman” (7:1), and “all of us have knowledge” (8:1).

“Brothers and sisters.” Paul addresses the whole church, men and women, with a word that is masculine in form. This edition prints “brothers and sisters”; the Open Word Bible lets a reader choose “brothers” instead. In the same way, those older Bibles call “saints” are here “God’s holy people”.

Love. The King James Version followed the Latin in calling the subject of chapter 13 “charity”. This translation, like most today, says “love”, and keeps the same word throughout, so that “love builds others up” in chapter 8 can be heard preparing for chapter 13.

Places where manuscripts differ. In 13:3 the manuscripts are divided between “hand over my body to the flames” and “hand over my body so that I may boast”; the earliest manuscripts have the second, but this translation follows the first. In 11:24 it reads “This is my body; it is for you”, without the words added in later copies. And 14:34–35 stands where most Bibles print it, after verse 33, though some manuscripts place those verses after verse 40.

“Christ.” The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose whether Christos reads “Christ” or “the Messiah”. This edition prints “Christ”, but it is worth remembering, when Paul preaches “Christ crucified” to Jews and Greeks, that the word means Israel’s anointed King.
