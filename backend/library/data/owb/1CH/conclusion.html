1 Chronicles ends with a funeral and a coronation, but its real ending is a prayer. Before he dies, David stands before the whole assembly, surrounded by the gold and silver given for a house he will never see, and confesses that none of it was ever his. The king who had everything calls himself and his people outsiders and temporary settlers, whose days pass like a shadow.

The question the book leaves with you

Chronicles was written for people who had been through the shadow. They had lost their king, their independence and Solomon’s temple, and they lived as a small province of a foreign empire. The book’s answer to them was not to look back in nostalgia, but to look up. The kingdom had always been the LORD’s: “The kingdom is yours, LORD.” David had only ever sat on “the throne of the LORD”. So the kingdom could not be lost when the throne was empty.

That leaves every reader with a question: what are you building for a God whose kingdom outlasts you? David spent his last years gathering stone and timber, training singers and gatekeepers and arranging the work of people not yet born, for a temple his son would finish. He did it gladly. And he asked his people to give “freely”, not because God needed their gold, but because “Everything comes from you; what we have given you came from your own hand.”

The New Testament gives David’s life the same shape. Paul, preaching in Pisidian Antioch, said that David, “after serving God’s purpose in his own generation, fell asleep in death” (Acts 13:36). To serve God’s purpose in one generation, and leave the rest to him, is what 1 Chronicles asks of its readers.

Where the story goes next

2 Chronicles begins where this book ends, with Solomon building the house his father prepared, on the threshing floor where David saw the angel stop. The cloud of glory fills it while the singers David appointed are singing the very words he gave them: “For he is good; his loyal love lasts forever.”

But the promise of chapter 17 reached further than Solomon. The prophets after the exile kept looking for a son of David whose throne would “stand firm for all time”. The New Testament says he came. The angel told Mary that God would give her son “the throne of David, his forefather”; Matthew begins his Gospel with Jesus, “the son of David”; and James, at the council in Jerusalem, heard in the prophets God’s promise, “I will build again the fallen tent of David” (Acts 15:16), being fulfilled as the nations came to seek the Lord.

And the temple David longed to build pointed beyond itself. Solomon would say at its dedication that the highest heaven cannot contain God. Jesus spoke of his own body as the temple, and the apostles of the church as God’s house built of living stones.

Where to go from here

1 Chronicles has been less preached than Samuel and Kings, but its prayers and its promise to David have fed the church, and the library holds some good companions to it:

  • On David’s kingdom. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “2 Samuel and 1 Chronicles: The Kingdom Under David” reads David’s reign as a picture of the soul that has taken Christ for its King; and Book XVII of Augustine’s The City of God, which argues that the promises God made to David about his son (1 Chronicles 17; 2 Samuel 7) are not fulfilled in Solomon but in Christ.
  • On the prayers of Chronicles (4:10, 5:20, 29:19). R. A. Torrey’s What the Bible Teaches, whose chapter on prayer gathers the prayers of Jabez, of the tribes east of the Jordan, and of David for his son among its Bible proofs.
  • On the lists themselves (4:23, 5:22). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Morning by Morning, whose readings for June 3 and June 8 find the gospel in two verses of the genealogies: the potters who lived with the king to do his work, and the battle that was God’s doing.
  • On the census (1 Chronicles 21). John Wesley’s sermon “National Sins and Miseries”, in his Sermons on Several Occasions, preached on David’s plea in 2 Samuel 24:17, “But what have these sheep done?”, and drawing on the account in 1 Chronicles 21.
  • On the worship David ordered (1 Chronicles 15–16). The Psalms in this library, in the same Open Word Bible translation, where the psalm of chapter 16 can be read in its full form in Psalms 96, 105 and 106; and Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s sermon Thanksgiving Obtains the Spirit (2 Chronicles 5:13–14), on the day the singers David had appointed praised the LORD and the cloud filled the temple.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 1 Chronicles in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
