No book of the Bible begins less promisingly. 1 Chronicles opens with three names, “Adam, Seth, Enosh,” and goes on listing names for nine chapters. Many readers stop there. That is a pity, because the lists are a door, and behind them is one of the most joyful books in the Old Testament: a book about worship, about prayer, and about a people learning who they are after they had lost almost everything.

Its Hebrew name is Divrei HaYamim, “the events of the days”, the ordinary phrase for a court’s annals. The ancient Greek translators called it Paraleipomena, “things left out”, because they took it to supply what Samuel and Kings had passed over; the name Chronicles comes from Jerome, who suggested it be called a chronicle of the whole of sacred history. Like Samuel and Kings it was one book in Hebrew, divided in two by the Greek translation. 1 Chronicles runs from Adam to the death of David; 2 Chronicles from Solomon to the decree that sent the exiles home. In most Hebrew Bibles it stands last of all, so that the Hebrew Scriptures end with a king’s invitation to go up to Jerusalem.

A book for people after the exile

To understand Chronicles, start with its first readers. It was written after the return from Babylon: David’s family tree in chapter 3 runs several generations past Zerubbabel, who led the exiles home around 520 BC, and chapter 9 lists “the first to settle again on their own property in their towns”. Its readers were a small community in a corner of the Persian Empire. They had a rebuilt temple, smaller than Solomon’s, and no king. They had every reason to wonder whether the promises still applied to them.

Chronicles answers by retelling their story, mostly from Samuel and Kings, but with its own emphases and many additions. Its message is that they are still “all Israel”, still the people of the God who chose David and Jerusalem; that the worship of the temple is the heart of their life; and that the God who answered prayer in David’s day still answers those who seek him. It is not a second, inferior copy of Samuel and Kings. It is a sermon on that history for a new generation.

A family tree from Adam

The genealogies of chapters 1–9 are a map of who belongs. They begin with the whole human race, “Adam, Seth, Enosh”, narrow to Abraham and then to “the sons of Israel”, and then go tribe by tribe through all twelve. Judah, David’s tribe, comes first and gets the most space; Levi, the tribe of priests and temple singers, stands at the centre; and the northern tribes, long since scattered by Assyria, are included too. Chronicles will not write anyone out of Israel.

Christian readers will recognise the method. The New Testament opens the same way, with “The record of the family line of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham” (Matthew 1:1), and Luke traces that line back, as Chronicles does, to Adam. A genealogy is a way of saying that God has been keeping his promises through every generation, including the ones nobody remembers.

Some tribes east of the Jordan win a battle “because they called out to God during the battle, and he answered their prayer, since they trusted in him”. A family of potters “made their home there with the king, working for him.” The men of Issachar were “men with insight into the times, who knew what Israel ought to do” (12:32). Even the lists keep saying the book’s great themes: God hears those who call on him, and the humblest work done for the King matters.

Saul, and the word “seek”

The story proper begins with Saul’s last battle, told in a single chapter, and a verdict:

{{OWB:1CH.10.14.s1}}

— 1 Chronicles 10:14

“Seek” is one of the key words of Chronicles. Saul did not seek the LORD, and asked a medium for guidance instead. David brings the ark back because, as he says, “we did not seek it in the days of Saul.” David tells Solomon, “If you seek him, he will let you find him” (28:9), and the theme runs on through 2 Chronicles. To seek the LORD, in this book, means to turn to him in worship and prayer, to ask his will, and to rely on him rather than anything else.

David, king of all Israel

In chapter 11 “all Israel” gathers to David at Hebron, “we are your own bone and flesh,” and anoints him king. He takes Jerusalem, and Chronicles lists the mighty warriors who stood with him. One story among them shows the kind of king he was. Hiding from the Philistines, David longs aloud for water from the well by the gate of Bethlehem, his home town; three of his men break through the enemy camp to bring it. He will not drink it. He pours it out as an offering to the LORD: “Shall I drink the blood of these men who went at the risk of their lives?”

Men come over to David from every tribe, and the Spirit comes on one of their leaders, Amasai, who cries out, “We are yours, David! We are with you, son of Jesse!” Chapter 12 ends with a feast at Hebron and the people’s neighbours bringing food on donkeys and camels, “For there was joy in Israel.” Joy is another of the book’s words.

The ark comes home

David’s first act as king of all Israel is to bring the ark of the covenant, the sign of God’s presence, to Jerusalem. The first attempt ends in disaster. The ark is carried on a cart, the oxen stumble, and Uzzah puts out his hand to steady it: “Then the anger of the LORD blazed against Uzzah, and he struck him down.” David is angry and afraid. The ark stays three months in the house of Obed-Edom, and that house is blessed.

The story is hard, and Chronicles does not soften it. But it draws a lesson: the holy God is not to be handled as a possession. When David tries again, the ark is carried by Levites, as the law of Moses required, and David admits, “we did not seek him as the rule required.” This time it comes up to Jerusalem with shouts, trumpets, harps and lyres, and with the king dancing before it.

Then David appoints Levites “to invoke, to thank and to praise the LORD”, and gives them a psalm. It is woven from parts of Psalms 105, 96 and 106, and it is the first song of the new order of worship in Jerusalem:

{{OWB:1CH.16.34.s1}}

— 1 Chronicles 16:34

That refrain will be heard again and again in Chronicles, at the dedication of the temple and on the battlefield. For people who had lost their king, it was a reminder of what they had not lost.

“The LORD will build you a house”

Chapter 17 is the theological heart of the book. Settled in his palace, David is troubled that the ark of the LORD “stays under tent curtains”, and he proposes to build it a temple. That night God sends Nathan the prophet with a reversal. David will not build a house for God; God will build a house, a dynasty, for David: “I declare to you that the LORD will build you a house.” One of David’s sons will build the temple, and of him God says:

{{OWB:1CH.17.14.s1}}

— 1 Chronicles 17:14

Set it beside the same promise in 2 Samuel 7:16, where God says to David, “Your house and your kingdom will be secure forever before you.” Chronicles has “my house and my kingdom”. The kingdom of Israel belongs to God; its king sits, as Chronicles later says, “on the throne of the LORD” (29:23). For readers who had no king, that was the point: the kingdom had never really been David’s, and so it could not die with David’s dynasty. Christians have heard in it the promise that the angel would repeat to Mary, that her son would be given “the throne of David” and that “There will be no end to his kingdom” (Luke 1:32–33).

David’s answer is wonder: “LORD God, who am I, and what is my house, that you have brought me as far as this?”

What Chronicles leaves out

Readers who know 2 Samuel notice what is missing. Bathsheba appears only once, as a name in a list. There is no murder of Uriah, no Nathan saying “You are the man!”, no story of Amnon or Absalom, no long years of family tragedy. Some have accused the Chronicler of whitewashing his hero. But his first readers had the books of Samuel and knew the story well. He was not hiding it; he was telling a different part of it, the David who gathered Israel, brought the ark home and prepared for the temple. Like the four Gospels, the Old Testament gives more than one witness to its history, each with its own purpose.

And Chronicles does not pretend David was sinless. It tells, in full, the sin that brought the most suffering on the people.

The census and the threshing floor

“Satan rose up against Israel and stirred David up to count Israel.” The parallel in 2 Samuel 24:1 says that the anger of the LORD “stirred David up”. Readers have long discussed the difference. Many see two sides of one event: God permitting what the adversary intended, as in the book of Job. Some note that the Hebrew word here has no article and may mean simply “an adversary”. Either way, David is held responsible, and he says so: “I have sinned greatly by doing this.”

Given a choice of punishments, he answers, “Let me fall into the hand of the LORD, for his mercy is very great.” When the plague falls, he pleads for the people: “It is I who have sinned and done great wrong. But these sheep, what have they done?” He is told to build an altar on the threshing floor of Ornan the Jebusite, and he insists on paying full price: “I will not take what is yours for the LORD, or offer a burnt offering at no cost to myself.” Fire falls from heaven on the altar. And David declares, “Here is the house of the LORD God.” The place of his sin and God’s mercy becomes the site of the temple, on Mount Moriah (2 Chronicles 3:1), the name of the land where Abraham once offered Isaac (Genesis 22:2).

Preparing for a temple he would not build

The last eight chapters show David spending the end of his life on a building he will never see. He is not allowed to build it, because he has “poured out blood”; the builder will be Solomon, “a man of rest”, whose name sounds like the Hebrew word for peace. So David gathers materials, organises the priests and Levites into divisions, appoints gatekeepers, treasurers and officials, and sets apart singers “who prophesied with lyres, harps and cymbals”. Their task is to stand “each morning to give thanks and praise to the LORD, and to do the same in the evening”. Long lists again, but lists of people whose work was worship.

Then David hands Solomon the plans, “in writing from the hand of the LORD upon me”, and a charge: “Be strong and courageous, and do the work.” He gives his own treasure, and asks the people, “who is willing to give freely and consecrate themselves today to the LORD?” They give gladly, “for with a whole heart they had given freely to the LORD.”

The book ends with David’s last prayer, which begins with the verse at the head of this introduction and turns, at its centre, on a question every giver should ask: “But who am I, and who are my people, that we should have the means to give as freely as this? Everything comes from you; what we have given you came from your own hand.” Then David dies “at a good old age, having had his fill of days, riches and honor”, and Solomon sits on the throne of the LORD.

How to read 1 Chronicles

Do not be afraid to read chapters 1–9 quickly, watching for their shape and for the small stories in them; then slow down for David. It helps to read the narrative beside 2 Samuel and notice what Chronicles adds, especially its long attention to the ark, the Levites and the music of worship. Psalms 96, 105 and 106 are worth reading with chapter 16. If you have time for only two chapters, read 17 and 29: the promise to David, and David’s prayer at the end of his life.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition, recorded in the Talmud, credited it to Ezra, and the end of 2 Chronicles repeats the opening of the book of Ezra. Many scholars speak simply of “the Chronicler”, writing in the Persian period, perhaps around 400 BC; some date it earlier, and whether the same hand wrote Ezra and Nehemiah is debated. The writer names his sources, among them “the records of Samuel the seer, the records of Nathan the prophet and the records of Gad the seer”. Some of his numbers differ from those in Samuel and Kings, through copying or different ways of counting, and Christians hold a range of views on the details.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 1 Chronicles are worth knowing.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation follows long custom and writes “the LORD”. The title older translations give as “the LORD of hosts” appears as “the LORD of Armies”.

“Loyal love.” The Hebrew word hesed, God’s steadfast covenant love, is “loyal love” in the great refrain “his loyal love lasts forever”, and “faithful love” in the promise to David’s son.

Seeking. The Hebrew verbs for seeking God are usually rendered “seek”, but in the psalm of chapter 16 the line older translations give as “seek his face continually” becomes “come into his presence again and again”, which is what it means. That psalm, like the other poetry in the book, is printed line by line.
