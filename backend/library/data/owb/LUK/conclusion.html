Luke’s Gospel ends where it began, in the temple, with people praising God. In the first chapter an old priest came out of the sanctuary unable to speak, and could not give the blessing the people were waiting for. In the last chapter Jesus lifts his hands and blesses his disciples, and they go back to the temple full of joy. The blessing Zechariah could not give has been given.

The question the book leaves with you

Luke is full of people who are found: shepherds, a widow, a sinful woman, a Samaritan leper, a chief tax collector up a tree, a criminal on a cross. And it is full of people who must decide whether to come in. The clearest of them is the elder brother in the parable of the lost son. His father comes out to plead with him, and says:

— Luke 15:31–32

Jesus does not tell us whether he went in. The story was told to people who grumbled that Jesus welcomed sinners, and it leaves them, and us, standing at the door of the feast. Luke’s Gospel is good news for the lost. But it asks those who think they were never lost whether they will rejoice when others are found.

The two on the road to Emmaus leave another question. They walked beside the risen Jesus for seven miles and did not know him. Their hearts burned as he opened the Scriptures, and their eyes were opened when he broke the bread. Luke wrote, perhaps, for readers who would never see Jesus as the apostles did. He tells them where he is to be found: in the Scriptures opened, at the table, among those who gather in his name.

Where the book points

It points, first, to Jerusalem and beyond. The risen Jesus tells his disciples that repentance and the forgiveness of sins are to be announced in his name “to all the nations, starting from Jerusalem.” They are to wait in the city until power from on high clothes them. Luke’s Gospel is the first of two volumes, and the second, the Acts of the Apostles, begins with that promise kept and follows the message from Jerusalem to Rome.

It points, second, to a way of life. The Jesus of Luke’s Gospel eats with outcasts, notices widows, warns the rich and prays all night. He tells his followers to pick up their cross “every day”, to show compassion as their Father shows compassion, and to go and do as the Samaritan did. To read Luke and not be changed in how we treat the poor and the outsider is not really to have read it.

And it points to joy. The book that begins with an angel announcing “a great joy that is for all the people” ends with disciples going home “with great joy”. Between them stand a shepherd carrying a lost sheep home on his shoulders, a woman calling her neighbours to celebrate a coin, and a father running down the road.

Where to go from here

Luke’s stories have been preached more than almost any others in the Bible, and many of the best sermons on them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the birth of Jesus (chapter 2). Martin Luther’s The Story of the Birth of Jesus (2:1–14) and John Newton’s The Angel’s Message and Song (2:8–14).
  • On Martha and Mary (10:38–42). Augustine of Hippo’s sermon Martha and Mary.
  • On prayer. Andrew Murray’s With Christ in the School of Prayer, which begins with the disciples’ request, “Lord, teach us how to pray” (11:1), and J. C. Ryle’s Do You Pray? (18:1).
  • On the great invitation (chapter 14). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Compel Them to Come In (14:23).
  • On the lost son (chapter 15). Alexander Maclaren’s The Prodigal and His Father (15:11–24), and Frederick W. Robertson’s The Prodigal and His Brother (15:11–32), which gives the elder brother his due.
  • On money (chapter 16). John Wesley’s The Use of Money (16:9).
  • On the tears of Jesus (19:41–42). Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s The Saviour’s Tears over the Lost.
  • On the cross. D. L. Moody’s The Dying Thief (23:39–43).
  • On the risen Lord (chapter 24). Andrew Murray’s Jesus Himself, on the two disciples whose eyes were opened (24:31), and John Bunyan’s The Jerusalem Sinner Saved, on the command to preach “starting from Jerusalem” (24:47).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Luke in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this Gospel.

But the best next step is the one Luke himself intended. Turn the page to his second book, the Acts of the Apostles, and watch what happened when the promise of the Father came. And then come back to the first, and read it again, listening for the word that runs all through it, today: the day of the Saviour’s birth, the day salvation came to Zacchaeus’s house, and the day Jesus promised to the man dying beside him.
