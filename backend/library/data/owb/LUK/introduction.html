{{OWB:LUK.19.10.s1}}

— Luke 19:10

Luke is the only Gospel writer who opens by telling us, in his own voice, how he went about his work. He begins with a single long sentence, in the polished style of a Greek historian, addressed to a man named Theophilus:

{{OWB:LUK.1.1.s1}}

— Luke 1:1–4

He was not an eyewitness himself, he says. Others had already written accounts. He had gone back over the whole course of things from the start, drawing on what had been handed down by those who saw it, and set it out in order, so that his reader could be sure. It is a modest and serious beginning, and it tells us what kind of book this is: a careful account, by a man who cared about getting things right, of the events that changed the world.

And then, a few verses later, the polished Greek gives way to the language of the Old Testament, and we are in the temple at Jerusalem, where an old priest is burning incense and an angel is standing beside the altar. Luke can write like a historian. But the story he has to tell is full of angels and songs, of the poor lifted up and the lost found, and of a joy that keeps breaking out.

The beloved physician

The Gospel does not name its author, but its second volume, the Acts of the Apostles, is addressed to the same Theophilus and begins by recalling “the first book”. From the second century the church has said that both were written by Luke, a companion of Paul. Paul mentions him three times: as “Our dear friend Luke, the doctor”, as a fellow worker, and, near the end of his life, in a single plain sentence, “Only Luke is with me.” In Colossians Paul seems to list him apart from his Jewish fellow workers, and many readers think Luke was a Gentile, the only non-Jewish author in the New Testament.

Several passages in Acts slip from “they” into “we”, as if the writer was there, travelling with Paul. Most readers have taken them that way; some scholars think they come from a travel diary the author used, or are a literary device. Irenaeus, around 180, calls Luke “the companion of Paul” and says he recorded in a book the gospel Paul preached. Together, Luke and Acts make up more than a quarter of the New Testament, more than any other single writer.

Dates suggested for the Gospel range from the early 60s to the 80s. Like Matthew, Luke seems to have used Mark, and a collection of Jesus’s sayings that the two share; but a great deal of his Gospel, perhaps a third or more, is found nowhere else.

Luke is also the Gospel writer who most often ties his story to the wider world. Jesus is born when “an order went out from Caesar Augustus” for a registration of the empire, “while Quirinius was governor of Syria”. John the Baptist’s work begins in “the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar”, which is about AD 28, and Luke names the governor, the local rulers and the high priests of that year. Historians have long debated how the census fits what is known of Quirinius, and the question is not settled. But Luke’s point is plain. Nothing in this story happened out of sight. It happened in the real world of emperors and taxes, to people with names.

A Gospel that sings

The first two chapters of Luke are among the most loved in the Bible, and almost all of their contents are his alone: the angel Gabriel’s visit to Zechariah, and then to Mary at Nazareth; Mary’s visit to her relative Elizabeth; the birth of John; the census, the journey to Bethlehem, the shepherds and the angels; the old man Simeon and the prophet Anna in the temple; and the boy Jesus, twelve years old, sitting among the teachers.

These chapters are full of songs, and the church has sung them ever since. Mary’s song, the Magnificat, begins:

{{OWB:LUK.1.46.s1}}

— Luke 1:46–47

Zechariah sings when his tongue is loosed; the angels praise God, “Glory to God in the heights of heaven”; and Simeon, holding the child in his arms, sings that he can now die in peace, because his eyes have seen the salvation God has prepared, “a light of revelation for the nations, and the glory of your people Israel.” In these songs the whole Old Testament seems to rise up to greet the child. They are full of Abraham and David, of promises remembered and mercy shown. And they announce, before Jesus has said a word, what kind of king he will be. In Mary’s words, God “has pulled the mighty down from their thrones and raised up the lowly.”

Luke’s story begins and ends in the temple: with Zechariah praying there in the first chapter, and with the disciples there in the last, “blessing God”.

Good news to the poor

Luke sets the first scene of Jesus’s public work in the synagogue of his home town. He is handed the scroll of Isaiah, finds the place, and reads:

{{OWB:LUK.4.18.s1}}

— Luke 4:18–19

Then he rolls up the scroll, sits down, and with every eye fixed on him says, “Today, as you have been listening, these words of Scripture have come true.” The rest of the Gospel unfolds that reading. Again and again Luke shows Jesus with people whom others pass by: the poor, the sick, the sinful, the grieving, the foreigner. In Luke’s beatitudes it is simply “you poor” who are blessed, and he adds woes for the rich. The first to hear of the Saviour’s birth are shepherds in the fields. Jesus raises a widow’s only son at Nain because “pity for her moved him deeply”. He lets a woman known in the town as a sinner wash his feet with her tears, and tells her that her sins stand forgiven. Of ten lepers healed, the only one who comes back to give thanks is a Samaritan. And on the road through Jericho, Jesus invites himself to the house of Zacchaeus, the chief tax collector, and says: “Today salvation has come to this household.”

Luke also has more to say about women than any other Gospel writer. Elizabeth, Mary and Anna open the story. Women travel with Jesus and the Twelve, “using their own resources to provide for them”, among them Mary Magdalene, Joanna and Susanna. Martha’s sister Mary sits at the Lord’s feet as a disciple would. And it is women who are the first to hear that he is risen.

Money, too, is never far from Luke’s mind. Jesus warns that life “does not come from the things a person owns”, and tells of a rich farmer who built bigger barns and died that night, of a rich man who feasted every day while a beggar named Lazarus lay at his gate, and of a dishonest manager who used money to make friends. Zacchaeus gives half of what he owns to the poor; the rich ruler is filled with sorrow.

The journey to Jerusalem

Midway through the Gospel there is a turning:

{{OWB:LUK.9.51.s1}}

— Luke 9:51–52

From here until Jesus enters Jerusalem in chapter 19, nearly ten chapters, Luke presents his story as one long journey. It is not a travel itinerary, and the places are rarely named. It is the road to the cross, and along it Luke has gathered much of the teaching that only he records. Here are the parables that have entered the language of the world: the good Samaritan; the friend at midnight; the rich fool; the lost sheep, the lost coin and the lost son; the rich man and Lazarus; the persistent widow; the Pharisee and the tax collector.

Lost and found

The heart of the journey, and for many readers the heart of the whole Bible, is chapter 15. It begins with a complaint. Tax collectors and sinners are gathering to listen to Jesus, and the Pharisees and scribes complain. “This man welcomes sinners,” they say, “and even shares meals with them.” Jesus answers with three stories: a shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to search for one, a woman who sweeps the house for a lost coin, and a father with two sons.

The younger son takes his inheritance, wastes it in a far country, and comes home rehearsing a speech. He never finishes it:

{{OWB:LUK.15.20.s2}}

— Luke 15:20

Each of the three stories ends in a party, because something lost has been found, and each says the same thing: there is joy in heaven over one sinner who repents. But the third story has a second son, the elder brother, who has never left home and will not come in to the feast. Jesus leaves him standing outside, with his father pleading with him. It is an open ending, addressed to the people who were muttering at the start of the chapter, and perhaps to us.

Prayer, the Spirit, and joy

Luke is the Gospel of prayer. Jesus is praying when the Spirit comes down on him at his baptism. He slips away to the wilderness to pray; he spends a whole night in prayer before choosing the Twelve; he is praying when he is transfigured; and it is after watching him pray that his disciples ask, “Lord, teach us how to pray.” Only Luke tells the parables of the friend who knocks at midnight and the widow who will not give up, both about praying without losing heart. And only Luke tells us that Jesus prayed for Peter, that his faith would not give out.

It is also the Gospel of the Holy Spirit. John the Baptist is filled with the Spirit before he is born; the Spirit comes upon Mary; Elizabeth, Zechariah and Simeon all speak by the Spirit. Jesus goes into the wilderness full of the Spirit and returns in the Spirit’s power, and he promises that the Father will give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him. Luke’s second volume begins with that promise kept.

And it is the Gospel of joy. The angel brings “a great joy that is for all the people”; there is joy in heaven over the lost; the seventy-two return with joy; Jesus himself rejoices in the Holy Spirit; and the Gospel ends with the disciples going back to Jerusalem “with great joy”. One small word keeps sounding through it: today. Today a Saviour has been born; today this Scripture is fulfilled; today salvation has come to this house; today you will be with me in paradise.

The cross and the road to Emmaus

Luke’s account of the passion is his own in many details. Only Luke tells us that Jesus was sent to Herod, who mocked him; that an angel strengthened him in Gethsemane; that he healed the ear of the high priest’s servant; and that on the way to the cross he spoke to the women of Jerusalem who were weeping for him. Three of the sayings from the cross are found only here: the prayer for those who crucified him, “Father, forgive them. They do not know what they are doing”; the promise to the criminal beside him, “this very day you will be with me in paradise”; and his last words, a prayer from Psalm 31, “Father, I place my spirit in your hands.”

The resurrection chapter holds the loveliest story Luke tells. Two disciples are walking home to Emmaus on the first Easter Day, sad and confused, when a stranger falls into step with them and asks what they are discussing. As they walk, he goes through the Scriptures with them, “starting with Moses and going on through all the prophets”. At the village they press him to stay, and at the table he takes the bread, says the blessing, breaks it, and hands it to them:

{{OWB:LUK.24.31.s1}}

— Luke 24:31

Then he appears to the eleven, eats a piece of fish in front of them to show he is no ghost, opens their minds to understand the Scriptures, and tells them to wait in the city until they are clothed with power from on high. And he leads them out as far as Bethany, blesses them, and is carried up into heaven.

How to read Luke

Notice its shape. The prologue (1:1–4) leads into the birth and childhood of John and Jesus (1:5–2:52); then the preparation for Jesus’s work (3:1–4:13), and the work in Galilee (4:14–9:50); the long journey to Jerusalem (9:51–19:27); and the last days in Jerusalem, the cross and the resurrection (19:28–24:53).

Read it with Acts beside it. Luke wrote the two as one work, and they echo each other everywhere: the Spirit coming on Jesus and then on the church, Jesus’s prayer for his killers and Stephen’s, a Gospel that ends in Jerusalem and a second volume that begins there and goes out to Rome.

And read it, as Luke would want, with your eyes on the people Jesus meets. Ask who is welcomed and who is offended, who is found and who stays outside. Luke wrote so that Theophilus could “be sure of the things you were taught”. He also wrote so that every reader could find himself or herself somewhere on the road, and be found.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Luke are worth knowing about before you begin.

The guest room and the feeding trough. In 2:7 the familiar “no room at the inn” reads “the guest room had no space for them”. Luke uses the same word for the guest room of the last supper (22:11), and a different word for an inn; Joseph and Mary were probably staying in a house whose guest room was full. The baby is laid in “a feeding trough”, which is what a manger is.

“Put right with God.” At the end of the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector (18:14), where older Bibles say the tax collector went home “justified”, this translation says what the word means: he “went home put right with God”.

Places where manuscripts differ. In 10:1 and 10:17 Jesus sends out seventy-two, where many manuscripts read seventy; the early copies are divided. The angel who strengthens Jesus in Gethsemane and his sweat like drops of blood (22:43–44), and the prayer “Father, forgive them” (23:34), are missing from some important early manuscripts but found in others; this translation includes them. Luke 17:36 and 23:17, which older Bibles print, are not in the earliest manuscripts and were probably added from Matthew and Mark; they are not in the text, and the verse numbers are skipped.

“Christ.” The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose whether Christos reads “Christ” or “the Messiah”. This edition prints “Christ”, as in the angel’s words to the shepherds, “a Savior has been born for you: Christ, the Lord.”
