— Galatians 6:14

Galatians ends where it began, at the cross. In the first chapter Christ “gave himself for our sins to rescue us from this present evil age”; in the last, Paul will boast of nothing else. Everything between, his story, his quarrel with Peter, Abraham, the law, Hagar and Sarah, the flesh and the Spirit, is an argument that the cross is enough.

The question the letter leaves

The Galatians were not tempted to give up Christ. They were tempted to add something to him: a mark in the flesh, a calendar of holy days, a way of proving that they really belonged. Paul’s question to them is the one the letter leaves with every reader: is Christ enough, or is he only the start? “You made your start by the Spirit; are you now going to reach the goal by the flesh?”

The temptation takes new forms in every age, and not all of them are religious. Whatever we lean on to make ourselves acceptable, to God or to others, Galatians names it a yoke of slavery. And it does not answer with a new set of rules, but with a person: “Christ lives in me.”

Where the letter points

It points to freedom, and to a freedom that serves. The same chapter that cries “stand firm” also says “through love, be slaves to one another.” Paul does not trade law for licence. He trades life under the law for life in the Spirit, whose fruit is love, joy, peace and the rest: “No law stands against things like these.”

And it points to one family. At Antioch the gospel was at stake around a dinner table, and in Galatia around who could be counted Abraham’s children. Paul’s answer was that all who belong to Christ are heirs together, “one in Christ Jesus”, Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female. A church that lives by faith eats at one table.

Where to go from here

Galatians has set more than one heart free, and a number of the people it reached are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On freedom. Martin Luther’s Concerning Christian Liberty, in First Principles of the Reformation, and The Key Teachings of Martin Luther, whose chapters on law and gospel and on the freedom of a Christian follow the man who called this letter his own.
  • On grace for a wounded conscience. John Bunyan’s Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners, with his account of finding Luther on Galatians.
  • On being crucified with Christ (2:20). Elisabeth Elliot’s Christ Lives in Me, A. W. Tozer’s I Am Crucified with Christ and A. B. Simpson’s The Death of Self.
  • On faith at work through love (5:6). Catherine Booth’s A True and a False Faith.
  • On the Spirit. In Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, the New Testament volume, the chapter “The Holy Spirit in Galatians”.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Galatians in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.

Then read Romans, where Paul, calmer and at greater length, takes up the same questions of faith, law, Abraham and the Spirit. Galatians is the battle; Romans is the account written afterwards. But it may be best first to read this letter again, at a sitting, and to hear, under the anger, a man who cannot bear to see anyone lose the freedom Christ has won.
