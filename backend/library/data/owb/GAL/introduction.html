— Galatians 5:1

Almost every letter Paul wrote opens, after the greeting, with thanks or praise to God. Galatians does not. Where the thanksgiving should be, Paul writes: “I am astonished that you are so quickly turning away from God, who called you by the grace of Christ, and going over to a different gospel.”

It is the angriest of his letters, and one of the most loving. Paul is frightened for these churches, which he founded himself, and he writes with the urgency of a parent whose child has wandered toward a cliff. In six short chapters he tells his own story, argues from Scripture, pleads, scolds, jokes bitterly, and at the end takes the pen in his own hand to write in large letters. The question at stake seemed to some a small one: whether Gentile believers needed to be circumcised. Paul saw that the whole gospel hung on the answer.

The churches of Galatia and the crisis

Galatia was a region in the middle of what is now Turkey, named after Celtic tribes who settled there in the third century BC, and also the name of a larger Roman province reaching further south. Christians have long debated which Paul means. If he means the southern part of the province, the churches are those of Pisidian Antioch, Iconium, Lystra and Derbe, which Acts says he founded with Barnabas (Acts 13–14), and the letter may be one of his earliest, written around AD 48–49. If he means the Celtic north, he founded the churches on a later journey (Acts 16:6; 18:23), and wrote perhaps in the mid-50s, not long before Romans. Good scholars hold both views.

What happened is clearer. After Paul left, other teachers came, Jewish believers in Jesus, who taught that Gentile converts must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to belong fully to God’s people, the children of Abraham. Paul calls them “some people who are unsettling you”. They seem also to have said that Paul was a second-hand apostle, dependent on Jerusalem, who had watered the gospel down to win Gentiles. Paul answers both charges.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapters 1–2: Paul’s gospel and Paul’s story. His call came straight from God, not from any human source; the leaders in Jerusalem recognised it; and at Antioch he defended it even against Peter.
  • Chapters 3–4: the argument from Scripture. The Spirit received by faith; Abraham; the curse of the law and the cross; the law as a guardian until Christ; children and heirs; and Hagar and Sarah.
  • Chapters 5–6: the life of freedom. Freedom not to be lost, not to be abused; the flesh and the Spirit; bearing one another’s loads; the cross and the new creation.

An apostle from God

Paul begins by insisting that he was “sent not from any human source, nor through any human go-between”. He had been a zealous persecutor of the church. Then God, who had set him apart from birth, “chose to reveal his Son in me”. He did not go to Jerusalem to be taught. He went away to Arabia, then back to Damascus, and only three years later went to Jerusalem to get to know Cephas, that is Peter, staying fifteen days. Fourteen years later he went up again, with Barnabas and with Titus, an uncircumcised Greek believer. The leaders there, “James, Cephas and John, who were regarded as pillars”, added nothing to his message. They gave him the right hand of fellowship, and asked only that he “keep remembering the poor”.

Scholars differ on how this visit fits the story in Acts, whether it is the council of Acts 15 or the earlier famine visit of Acts 11:30. Either way, Paul’s point is that his gospel did not come from Jerusalem, and that Jerusalem recognised it.

Then comes the most dramatic scene in the letter. At Antioch, Peter had been eating with Gentile believers, until people came from James, and Peter, afraid, pulled back. Even Barnabas followed. Paul confronted him in front of everyone: “How then can you force the Gentiles to live like Jews?” For Paul, a church that ate at separate tables was denying the gospel. Whether his words to Peter end in 2:14 or run on to the end of the chapter, the chapter ends in the gospel itself:

Faith, the Spirit and Abraham

“You foolish Galatians! Who has put you under a spell?” Paul appeals first to their own experience. They had received the Spirit, and seen miracles, not by keeping the law but by believing the message. “You made your start by the Spirit; are you now going to reach the goal by the flesh?”

Then he turns to Abraham, the very figure the other teachers relied on. Abraham “believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness”, and God promised that in him all the nations would be blessed. So “the people of faith are the ones who are Abraham’s children”. Those who rely on the law, Paul argues, fall under its curse, because no one does everything it requires. But “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse on our behalf”, hanging on a tree, so that the blessing of Abraham might reach the Gentiles.

What, then, was the law for? It came 430 years after the promise and cannot cancel it. It served as a guardian, the household slave who looked after a child until he came of age. And now that faith has come, a new family has been formed:

{{OWB:GAL.3.26.s1}} {{OWB:GAL.3.27.s1}} {{OWB:GAL.3.28.s1}}

— Galatians 3:26–28

The old dividing lines of the ancient world, between Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female, do not decide who belongs to God’s family. Christians apply this verse in different ways, but no reading can make it say less than that all are equally heirs.

Children and heirs

Chapter 4 tells the same story as a family drama. An heir who is still a child is no better off than a slave, until the date his father has set. “But when the time God had set was complete, God sent out his Son”, born of a woman and under the law, so that we might be adopted. The proof of adoption is the Spirit, who cries out in believers’ hearts, “Abba, Father!”, the word Jesus himself used. Why would they go back to slavery?

Then Paul speaks more personally. When he first came to them he was ill, and they welcomed him “like an angel of God, like Christ Jesus himself”; they would have given him their own eyes. Now he is suffering birth pains over them again, “until Christ is formed in you.” The chapter ends with an allegory of Abraham’s two sons, one born of Hagar the slave woman and one of Sarah the free woman, which Paul reads as two covenants: “we are children not of a slave woman but of the free woman.”

Freedom, flesh and Spirit

Chapter 5 begins with the letter’s battle cry and its warning. If they accept circumcision as the way to be justified, they have cut themselves off from Christ. “What counts is faith at work through love.” In his anger at the teachers, Paul uses words as coarse as any he wrote, wishing they would go the whole way and castrate themselves.

But freedom is not licence. “Only do not let your freedom become an opening for the flesh. Instead, through love, be slaves to one another.” The whole law is summed up in one sentence: “You must love your neighbor as you love yourself.” Where the law could not make people good, the Spirit can. Paul sets the works of the flesh, a grim list from sexual immorality to drunken bouts, beside a single harvest:

{{OWB:GAL.5.22.s1}}

— Galatians 5:22–23

Notice that it is fruit, not fruits: one life of the Spirit, showing itself in nine ways. Chapter 6 turns it into practice. Restore the one who falls, gently. “Take up one another’s heavy loads, and so you will fulfill the law of Christ.” Do not tire of doing good.

In Paul’s own hand

At the end Paul takes the pen from his secretary. “Notice what large letters I make as I write to you in my own hand.” Perhaps it was for emphasis; perhaps his eyesight was poor. And in those large letters he writes the letter’s summary: the other teachers want to boast in the Galatians’ flesh, “but far be it from me to boast of anything except the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ.” What matters is not circumcision or uncircumcision but “a new creation.”

Galatians in the life of the church

Martin Luther lectured on Galatians twice, and his great commentary on it, from lectures of 1531, became one of the most influential books of the Reformation. He said that Galatians was his own epistle, to which he was betrothed: “my Katie von Bora”, after his wife.

More than a century later, John Bunyan, in despair over his sins, found an old copy of that commentary, “so old that it was ready to fall piece from piece”. He wrote in Grace Abounding that he preferred it, after the Bible, to all the books he had ever seen, “as most fit for a wounded conscience.” And in May 1738 Charles Wesley was reading Luther on Galatians in the days before his own assurance of faith, which came on Pentecost Sunday, three days before his brother John’s at Aldersgate.

How to read Galatians

Galatians can be read in half an hour, and should be, aloud and at a sitting, so that the heat of it comes through. Then go back and follow the argument: Paul’s story in chapters 1 and 2, the Scriptures in 3 and 4, the life of freedom in 5 and 6. Notice how each part answers the same question, whether faith in Christ is enough. And notice that the most fiercely argued letter in the New Testament ends with love, the fruit of the Spirit, and bearing each other’s burdens.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Galatians are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Faith in Jesus Christ.” In 2:16 and 3:22 the Greek says literally “faith of Jesus Christ”, and in 2:20 “faith of the Son of God”. It can mean faith that has Christ as its object, or Christ’s own faithfulness to God. This translation follows the first reading, and its notes give the second, which many scholars hold.

“Justified.” In Galatians the translation keeps the familiar word “justified” for God’s verdict that a person is in the right with him, and “righteousness” for the related noun, so that a reader can follow the argument from 2:16 to 5:5.

“Children of God.” In 3:26 and 4:5–7 Paul’s word is “sons”, the standing of a son and heir in the ancient world, which he gives to women and men alike. This edition prints “children”; the Open Word Bible lets a reader choose “sons” instead, which keeps the link to the Son whom God sent. In the same way it prints “brothers and sisters” where the Greek word is masculine in form but Paul addresses the whole church.

“The elemental forces of the world.” In 4:3 and 4:9 Paul uses a word that can mean the basic elements of the world, the first principles of religion, or spiritual powers thought to rule the world. This translation chooses a phrase broad enough to hold more than one of these.

“The Israel of God.” In the closing blessing (6:16) it is uncertain whether Paul means the whole church, Jewish and Gentile believers together, Jewish believers in particular, or the people of Israel. The translation leaves the phrase as it stands, and Christians read it in all three ways.
