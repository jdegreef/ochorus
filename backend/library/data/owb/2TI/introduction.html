— 2 Timothy 1:12

This is a letter from a prison cell, written by a man who expects to die. Paul is in chains in Rome. Most of his friends have gone. He has already faced one hearing with no one to stand beside him, and he knows how the next one is likely to end. Winter is coming, and he wants his cloak. Above all, he wants to see Timothy once more.

If, as the church has long believed, it is the last letter Paul wrote, then these are the final words we have from the apostle who carried the gospel across the Roman world. They are not the words of a defeated man. They are full of thanksgiving, courage and charge, and of the plain confidence that the one he has trusted will keep what he has entrusted to him.

A letter from prison

Paul had been a prisoner in Rome before, under house arrest, free to receive visitors and to preach, as Acts ends (Acts 28:30–31). This imprisonment is harsher. He is “chained like a criminal”. When Onesiphorus came to Rome, he had to search hard to find him. The church historian Eusebius, writing in the fourth century, records that Paul was released after his first imprisonment, preached again, and was arrested a second time, and that in this imprisonment he wrote his second letter to Timothy. Tradition holds that he was beheaded in Rome under Nero, in the mid 60s.

As with the other Pastoral Letters, many modern scholars think this one was written after Paul’s death by a follower who honoured his memory; some who hold that view think it preserves genuine notes from Paul’s last days. Others find its personal detail, the names, the cloak, the parchments, the grief, very hard to explain as anything but Paul’s own. The introduction to 1 Timothy in this edition says more about the question.

Timothy seems to have been in Ephesus, or nearby in the province of Asia. Paul asks him to come before winter, when ships stopped sailing, and to bring Mark with him.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapter 1: do not be ashamed. Thanks for Timothy’s faith, a call to stir up his gift and share in suffering, and news of who has deserted Paul and who has stood by him.
  • Chapter 2: be strong. The soldier, the athlete and the farmer; a trustworthy saying; how to handle the truth and how to treat those who oppose it.
  • Chapter 3: continue in what you have learned. Terrible times, Paul’s own example, and the Scriptures that make a person wise for salvation.
  • Chapter 4: preach the word. A last charge, Paul’s farewell, and personal requests and greetings.

Stir up the gift

Paul begins with memories. He remembers Timothy’s tears at their last parting, and the faith that “first lived in your grandmother Lois and in your mother Eunice”. The gospel came to Timothy through a family, and Paul wants him to know it is truly his.

Then the first great charge:

{{OWB:2TI.1.6.s1}} {{OWB:2TI.1.7.s1}}

— 2 Timothy 1:6–7

Timothy may have been tempted to keep his head down. To be linked with a prisoner, and a prisoner of Rome, was dangerous and shameful. Paul tells him not to be ashamed of the Lord or of him, but to share in suffering for the gospel, “relying on the power of God”. The God who calls us to suffer is the one who saved us, “not because of anything we had done but because of his own purpose and grace”, and who through Christ “has destroyed death and has brought life and immortality to light.”

The chapter ends with two lists of names. “Everyone in the province of Asia has deserted me,” Paul writes. But Onesiphorus “often refreshed me and was not ashamed that I was in chains.” It is a small, human moment, and a reminder that faithfulness is often a matter of simply turning up.

Soldier, athlete, farmer

Chapter 2 opens with a chain of four generations: what Timothy heard from Paul, he is to pass on to trustworthy people, who will teach others as well. That is how the gospel has always travelled. Then Paul gives three pictures of the life it calls for: the soldier who does not get tangled in civilian affairs, the athlete who competes by the rules, the hardworking farmer who eats first from the harvest. Each speaks of single-mindedness, discipline and patience.

“Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead and descended from David,” Paul writes. He is in chains for this gospel. “But God’s word is not chained.” And he quotes what seems to be a saying the churches already knew:

{{OWB:2TI.2.11.s1}} {{OWB:2TI.2.12.s1}} {{OWB:2TI.2.13.s1}}

— 2 Timothy 2:11–13

The last line breaks the pattern. Each line so far has matched what we do with what he does. But if we are unfaithful, he does not become unfaithful in return. Most readers hear in it the deepest assurance of the letter; some hear also a warning that he stays true to every word, including the one before it. Either way, his faithfulness does not hang on ours.

A worker with nothing to be ashamed of

Timothy is to avoid quarrels about words and “godless, empty chatter”. Two teachers, Hymenaeus and Philetus, were saying that the resurrection had already taken place, perhaps meaning that it was only spiritual, and they were destroying the faith of some. Against all this, Paul gives Timothy a single aim: “Make every effort to stand before God tested and approved, a worker with nothing to be ashamed of, who handles the message of truth correctly.”

Then he describes how such a worker treats opponents. “The Lord’s servant must not be quarrelsome but kind to everyone, able to teach and patient with difficult people, gently correcting those who oppose him.” The goal is not to win but to see them come to their senses and escape the devil’s trap. It is one of the most searching passages in the New Testament for anyone who has to defend the truth.

All Scripture

Paul warns that “in the last days there will be terrible times”, and describes people who “love themselves and love money”, who keep up an appearance of godliness but deny its power. Some read the last days as the whole time between Christ’s first coming and his return; others as a time still ahead. Paul plainly expects Timothy to meet such people now.

Against them he sets two things: Paul’s own example, his teaching, way of life and sufferings, which Timothy has seen; and the Scriptures Timothy has known since infancy, “which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.” Then comes the verse that has shaped the church’s understanding of the Bible more than any other:

{{OWB:2TI.3.16.s1}}

— 2 Timothy 3:16–17

Paul was speaking of the Jewish Scriptures, what Christians call the Old Testament. The church has applied his words to the New Testament too, since it came to be recognized as Scripture alongside them.

Preach the word

The last chapter opens with Paul’s most solemn charge, made “in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus, who will judge the living and the dead”: “Preach the word. Be ready whether the time seems right or not.” A time is coming when people will want teachers who tell them what their ears itch for. Timothy must keep a clear head, endure hardship and “do the work of an evangelist”.

Then Paul speaks of himself:

{{OWB:2TI.4.6.s1}} {{OWB:2TI.4.7.s1}}

— 2 Timothy 4:6–7

A drink offering was wine poured out on the altar to complete a sacrifice. Paul sees his coming death as the last part of an offering his whole life has been.

The cloak and the scrolls

And then, astonishingly, the letter turns practical. Demas, “in love with this present world”, has deserted. “Only Luke is with me.” Bring Mark, “because he is helpful to me in my work”: the young man Paul once refused to take with him (Acts 15:37–39) has become a friend he wants at the end. Bring the cloak I left at Troas, “and my scrolls, especially the parchments.” Beware of Alexander the metalworker. “Do your best to get here before winter.”

Readers have been moved by these lines for centuries. In 1535, imprisoned in the castle of Vilvoorde near Brussels, William Tyndale wrote asking for a warmer cap, a warmer coat, and above all his Hebrew Bible, grammar and dictionary, so that he could go on translating. He was executed the following year. Like Paul, he wanted the Scriptures to the end.

How to read 2 Timothy

Read it in one sitting, as a last letter. Hear the loneliness in it and the courage. Notice how often Paul speaks of shame and of not being ashamed, of suffering and of guarding what has been entrusted. Notice how many people are named, for good and ill. And read it alongside 1 Timothy, to see the same friendship at two different moments.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 2 Timothy are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Breathed out by God” (3:16). The Greek word is literally “God-breathed”. Where older Bibles say “given by inspiration of God”, this translation keeps the picture of Scripture coming from God’s breath, as his word goes out of his mouth.

“What I have entrusted to him” (1:12). The Greek says “my deposit”. It may mean what Paul has entrusted to God, his life and work, or the gospel God has entrusted to Paul, as in 1:14. Because God is the one who guards it here, the translation follows the first reading.

“Sound judgment” (1:7). The word in older Bibles is “a sound mind”. It means clear thinking and self-control, the opposite of panic.

“The one who belongs to God” (3:17). The Greek has “the man of God”, a title of prophets and leaders. Here it probably includes any believer, and the translation says so.
