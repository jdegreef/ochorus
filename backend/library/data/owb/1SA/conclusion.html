1 Samuel ends on a battlefield. The king the people asked for, the one who stood head and shoulders above them, lies dead on Mount Gilboa beside his sons, and the Philistines hang his body on the wall of Beth-shan. The only kindness left for him comes from the men of Jabesh-gilead, who remember that he once saved their town. And the man God has chosen is far away in Ziklag, in exile.

The question the book leaves with you

Saul is one of the saddest figures in the Bible, and his story is not told to make us despise him. He was brave, and modest at first. But he could not wait for God, and he could not obey him all the way, and when he was found out, he was more concerned for his honour before the people than for what he had done. Little by little he became a man who threw spears at the people who loved him, and at the end he went looking for God’s word in the one place God had forbidden.

David, by contrast, is no saint in 1 Samuel. He lies, he schemes, he nearly takes bloody revenge. But again and again, when the moment comes, he waits for God. He will not kill the LORD’s anointed. He lets Abigail turn him back. At Ziklag, when his own men talk of stoning him, “David drew strength from the LORD his God.”

That is the question 1 Samuel leaves with its readers: not whether we are impressive, but what is in our hearts. “A person looks at what is there for the eyes, but the LORD looks at the heart.” Saul looked like a king. David had a heart that turned toward God, even after it had turned away.

Where the story goes next

2 Samuel opens with David hearing the news of Saul’s death and weeping for him and for Jonathan. It tells how he became king, how God promised him a house for ever, and, with the same honesty, how badly David himself would fall.

The New Testament takes up the thread. Preaching in Antioch, Paul retells this very history: God gave Israel judges “until the time of the prophet Samuel”, then Saul, and then raised up David, “a man in tune with my heart, who will carry out all that I want”, and “From this man’s line God has brought Israel a Savior, Jesus.” Hannah had sung of God’s “anointed” before Israel had a king. Mary sang a song like hers. And Jesus himself pointed to David in this book, eating the holy bread when he and his men were hungry, to show that the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath.

Where to go from here

1 Samuel has given the church some of its best-loved preaching on prayer, calling and obedience, and much of it is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “1 Samuel: The Kingdom of Man’s Traditions” reads the kingdom the people demanded as a picture of rule by human tradition rather than by God; and Book XVII of Augustine’s The City of God, which reads Hannah’s song, the fall of Eli’s house and the choice of David as prophecies of Christ and his church.
  • On Hannah (1 Samuel 1–2). “Pouring Out the Soul”, on 1 Samuel 1:15, in The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original; and E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, with its chapter “Samuel, the Child of Prayer”.
  • On the call of Samuel (1 Samuel 3). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon The Child Prophet (1 Samuel 3:1–14) and John Henry Newman’s Divine Calls (1 Samuel 3:10).
  • On Samuel’s intercession (1 Samuel 12). “The Sin of Not Praying”, on 1 Samuel 12:23, in The Prayers Jesus Learned.
  • On Saul and obedience (1 Samuel 13–15). Andrew Murray’s The School of Obedience, whose chapter “Obedience: Its Place in Holy Scripture” dwells on Saul’s failures and Samuel’s verdict; and A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, whose chapter “The Holy Spirit in the Lives of Saul and David” sets the two kings side by side.
  • On David and Goliath (1 Samuel 17). Phillis Wheatley’s poem “Goliath of Gath”, in Poems on Various Subjects.
  • For reading with children. “Hannah’s Prayer”, “David and the Giant”, “David and Jonathan” and “David Spares the King” in Tell Me the Story – Book 3: Kings and Prophets, and “A King Like the Nations” in The Big Story, both Ochorus Originals.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 1 Samuel in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
