1 Samuel is a book about change. It begins in the world of Judges, with a priest growing old and blind at a sanctuary in Shiloh, his sons abusing their office, and “the word of the LORD was rare in those days”. It ends with Israel a kingdom, its first king dead on a battlefield, and a young man from Bethlehem waiting in exile for the throne God has promised him.

Three lives hold the book together. Samuel, the child of a mother’s prayer, who becomes prophet, priest and the last of the judges. Saul, the tall, handsome king the people asked for, who began well and ended at a medium’s house the night before his death. And David, the shepherd boy whom no one thought to send for, the man after God’s own heart. Their stories overlap and pass on from one to the next, and between them they tell how God gave his people a king, and what kind of king he wanted.

One book in two

1 and 2 Samuel were originally a single book, named after the prophet who dominates its first half: in Hebrew, Shemuel. The ancient Greek translators divided it in two, along with Kings, and called the four “Books of Kingdoms”; Christian Bibles have followed the division ever since, and Hebrew Bibles since the late Middle Ages. Samuel himself dies before the end of 1 Samuel, but the name is fitting. It was Samuel who anointed both of Israel’s first kings, and his words over them shape all that follows.

The book falls into three overlapping parts:

  • Samuel (chapters 1–7). Hannah’s prayer, Samuel’s call, the fall of Eli’s house, the capture and return of the ark, and Samuel’s leadership at Mizpah.
  • Saul (chapters 8–15). The people’s demand for a king, Saul’s anointing and early victories, and his rejection for disobedience.
  • Saul and David (chapters 16–31). David anointed in secret, the victory over Goliath, Jonathan’s friendship, Saul’s jealousy, David’s years as a fugitive, and Saul’s death on Mount Gilboa.

Hannah’s prayer

The book does not open with a king or a prophet. It opens with a woman who cannot have children, mocked every year by her husband’s other wife as they go up to the sanctuary. Hannah’s husband loves her, but asks, “Do I not mean more to you than ten sons?” She goes to the tent of the LORD and prays so intensely that only her lips move, and the priest Eli thinks she is drunk. Her answer is one of the great descriptions of prayer: “I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD.”

She vows that if God gives her a son, she will give him back to the LORD for his whole life. A son is born, and she names him Samuel, “I asked the LORD for him.” When he is weaned, she brings him to Shiloh and leaves him with Eli. And then she sings:

{{OWB:1SA.2.7.s1}} {{OWB:1SA.2.8.s1}}

— 1 Samuel 2:7–8

Hannah’s song is the overture to the whole book. Its theme is reversal: the proud brought low, the humble lifted up, “for no one wins by strength.” That is the story of Eli’s house and Samuel, of Saul and David, of Goliath and the shepherd boy. And it ends on a word no one in Israel has yet used of a ruler: God “will grant strength to his king and raise high the horn of his anointed.” There is no king yet. The word “anointed”, mashiach, is the word that will become “Messiah”. More than a thousand years later, another woman, pregnant with a promised son, would sing a song so like Hannah’s that readers have heard the one in the other ever since: “He has pulled the mighty down from their thrones and raised up the lowly.”

“Speak, for your servant is listening”

Eli’s sons, Hophni and Phinehas, are priests at Shiloh, and they treat the offerings of God’s people with contempt. “Now Eli’s sons were worthless men; they did not know the LORD.” Eli rebukes them feebly and does nothing. Against that background the boy Samuel grows, “and both the LORD and the people were pleased with him”, words Luke would echo of the boy Jesus.

One night, as Samuel lies in the sanctuary near the ark, a voice calls his name. Three times he runs to Eli, and three times Eli sends him back, until the old priest realises who is calling, and tells him how to answer:

{{OWB:1SA.3.10.s1}}

— 1 Samuel 3:10

The message Samuel receives is hard: judgement on Eli’s house. But the chapter ends with the word of the LORD no longer rare. Samuel grows up, “and the LORD was with him; he did not let any of his words fall to the ground.” All Israel, “from Dan to Beersheba”, knows that there is a prophet in the land again.

The ark and the glory

Chapters 4–6 can seem a detour, since Samuel himself is not mentioned in most of them. But they teach something Israel needed to learn. Beaten by the Philistines, the elders send for the ark of the covenant, thinking that if they carry it into battle, it “will save us from the power of our enemies.” They treat God as a charm. The ark is captured, Eli’s sons are killed, Eli falls and dies at the news, and his daughter-in-law, dying in childbirth, names her son Ichabod: “The glory has gone into exile from Israel.”

But the God of Israel does not need Israel to defend him. In the Philistine temple at Ashdod, the statue of their god Dagon is found the next morning “fallen face down on the ground before the ark of the LORD”, and the following morning broken. Plague follows the ark from city to city until the Philistines send it home on a cart. Twenty years later, when Israel at last turns back to the LORD under Samuel, they win a victory at Mizpah, and Samuel sets up a stone and names it Ebenezer, saying, “To this point the LORD has helped us.”

“Give us a king”

Samuel grows old, and his sons are no better than Eli’s. So the elders come to him with a demand: “appoint a king to judge us, as all the nations have.” Samuel is hurt, but God tells him something more painful:

{{OWB:1SA.8.7.s1}}

— 1 Samuel 8:7

The request is not wrong in itself. The law of Moses had foreseen that Israel would one day have a king, and Judges ended by lamenting that there was none. The problem is the reason: they want to be “like all the nations”, with a king who will “go out before us and fight our battles”. That was exactly what the LORD had done for them. Samuel warns them what a king will cost: he will take their sons, their daughters, their fields and a tenth of their flocks. They refuse to listen: “we will have a king over us.” And God gives them one.

Saul

Saul is everything the people could have wanted. He is from Gibeah in Benjamin, “a fine young man”, and “from the shoulders up he stood taller than all the people.” Samuel anoints him privately; when he is chosen publicly, he is found hiding among the baggage. His first act as king is a good one: the Spirit of God rushes on him, and he rescues the town of Jabesh-gilead from the Ammonites. For a moment it looks as if the kingdom will be well served.

Then come two failures, and both have the same root. In chapter 13, with the Philistines gathering and his troops deserting, Saul grows tired of waiting for Samuel and offers the sacrifice himself. Samuel arrives as he finishes, and tells him that his kingdom will not last: “The LORD has looked for a man after his own heart.” In chapter 15, ordered to devote the Amalekites to destruction, Saul spares their king and the best of the livestock, then greets Samuel with “I have carried out the command of the LORD.” Samuel’s answer is famous for its dry wit, “Then what is this bleating of sheep that fills my ears?”, and for its verdict:

{{OWB:1SA.15.22.s1}}

— 1 Samuel 15:22

The same chapter raises a puzzle readers have long discussed. Twice God is said to regret that he made Saul king (15:11, 15:35), and in between Samuel says that “the Glory of Israel will not lie or change his mind, for he is not a mortal, that he should change his mind.” Most interpreters have taken the two together: God is not fickle or surprised, like a human being who changes course; and yet he is not unmoved by what his people do, and his grief over Saul is real. The chapter holds both, and leaves Samuel grieving too.

The LORD looks at the heart

God sends Samuel to Bethlehem, to the house of Jesse, to anoint a new king. Jesse’s eldest son Eliab is tall and impressive, and Samuel is sure this is the one. The verse at the head of this introduction is God’s reply. Seven sons pass before the prophet, and none is chosen. The youngest has not even been called in. He is out with the sheep.

When David is brought in, God says, “Get up and anoint him. He is the one.” And the book records two movements of the Spirit side by side: “the Spirit of the LORD rushed on David from that day on”, and “the Spirit of the LORD had left Saul.” The rest of 1 Samuel is the long, painful working out of those two sentences.

David and Goliath

The story of David and Goliath has become a proverb for any underdog. In the book itself it is something more specific. The Philistine champion, “about 9 feet 9 inches tall”, has defied the armies of Israel for forty days, and Saul, the tallest man in Israel, the one whose job it was to fight their battles, is afraid. David, sent by his father with food for his brothers, is indignant: “who is he, that he dares to defy the ranks of the living God?”

He refuses Saul’s armour, takes five smooth stones and his sling, and tells Goliath what the battle is really about:

{{OWB:1SA.17.45.s1}}

— 1 Samuel 17:45

“The battle belongs to the LORD,” he says, so that everyone will know “that the LORD does not save by sword or spear.” It is Hannah’s song in action: no one wins by strength.

Jonathan

The most moving figure in the second half of the book may be Saul’s son Jonathan. He is a brave warrior, who once attacked a Philistine outpost with only his armour-bearer, saying, “Nothing can keep the LORD from saving, whether by many or by few.” He is the heir to the throne. And from the day he meets David, “Jonathan’s life became bound up with David’s life, and Jonathan loved him as he loved himself.” He makes a covenant with David, gives him his own robe and sword, warns him of his father’s plans, and at last, when David is hunted in the wilderness, goes to find him and “gave him fresh strength in God”. He tells David, “You will reign over Israel, and I will be at your side as your second.” Jonathan gives up a crown for a friend, because he sees that God has chosen him.

Saul’s jealousy and David’s restraint

After Goliath, the women of Israel sing, “Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his tens of thousands”, and from that day Saul watches David with suspicion. More than once he hurls a spear at him. Soon David is a fugitive, hiding in caves and deserts with a band of men who were “in trouble”, in debt or “bitter about their lot”. The priests of Nob who helped him are massacred by Saul’s order.

Twice in these years David has Saul at his mercy, once in a cave near En-gedi and once in Saul’s own camp at night. Twice his men urge him to strike. Twice he refuses:

{{OWB:1SA.24.7.s1}}

— 1 Samuel 24:6

David will not take by force the kingdom God has promised him. He will wait. Between the two episodes, he is about to take bloody revenge on the churlish Nabal when Nabal’s wife Abigail, “a woman of good sense”, meets him on the road and talks him out of it, and David blesses God for keeping him “from taking revenge with my own hand.” The man after God’s heart is not sinless; he deceives a priest, feigns madness before a Philistine king, and lives for a time in the service of Israel’s enemies. But at the decisive moments he trusts God to give what God has promised.

The witch of Endor and Mount Gilboa

Saul’s story ends in darkness. Samuel has died. The Philistines gather for war. Saul inquires of the LORD, “but the LORD did not answer him”. And so the king who had driven the mediums out of the land goes in disguise by night to a medium at Endor and asks her to bring up Samuel. Readers have long debated what happened next: whether Samuel himself appeared, by God’s permission, or something else. The text says that the woman saw Samuel and screamed, and the message is one only Samuel would give: “Tomorrow you and your sons will be with me.”

The next day, on Mount Gilboa, Saul’s sons, Jonathan among them, are killed, and Saul, badly wounded, falls on his own sword. The book ends with one act of honour. The men of Jabesh-gilead, whom Saul had rescued at the start of his reign, travel all night to take his body down from the Philistine wall and bury it.

How to read 1 Samuel

1 Samuel is one of the finest pieces of storytelling in the Bible, and it reads best in long stretches. Read it with 2 Samuel, since the two were once one book. Watch the three main figures, and watch how often the book contrasts them: Samuel’s sons and Eli’s, Saul’s disobedience and David’s restraint, Saul’s spear and Jonathan’s robe. Notice who inquires of the LORD, and who does not wait for an answer. Listen for Hannah’s song as the story unfolds.

The book does not name its author. Chronicles mentions records of the prophets Samuel, Nathan and Gad, and Jewish tradition held that Samuel wrote the part up to his death. Many scholars think the book draws on several older sources and was completed as part of a longer history in the time of the kings or later. Its Hebrew text is also one of the most difficult in the Old Testament, and the ancient Greek translation and the Dead Sea Scrolls preserve readings that differ from it in places. Christians hold a range of views on how the book was composed, while receiving it as Scripture and as the story of how God chose David.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 1 Samuel are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD of Armies.” “The LORD” in capitals stands for God’s personal name. The title that older Bibles render “the LORD of hosts”, first heard on Hannah’s lips, is translated “the LORD of Armies”.

“The LORD’s anointed.” David’s phrase for Saul is kept word for word: “the LORD’s anointed.” It is the language of Hannah’s song, and the root of the word Messiah.

A gap in the text. The Hebrew of 13:1 seems to have lost a number, and the translation does not guess: “Saul was ... years old when he became king.”

Measures. Ancient measures are given in modern terms, so that Goliath is “about 9 feet 9 inches tall”, the height given in the standard Hebrew text.

Poetry. Hannah’s song in chapter 2 is printed one line per line, as the poetry it is.

Verse numbers. In chapters 21 and 24 the Hebrew verse numbers run one ahead of those in English Bibles; the references in this introduction follow the English numbering.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
