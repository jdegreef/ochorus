2 Kings begins with a king who has fallen through the lattice of his upper room and sends to ask a foreign god whether he will recover. Elijah meets his messengers on the road with a question: “Are you going to consult Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron, because there is no God in Israel?” It ends, twenty-five chapters later, with the last king of David’s line in Babylon, released from prison and given a seat at the conqueror’s table. Between those two scenes, both kingdoms of God’s people are lost.

It is the second half of the book the Hebrew Bible calls Melakhim, “Kings”, divided in two by the ancient Greek translation; it picks up the story in the middle of a reign. It runs from the middle of the ninth century BC to about 561 BC: through the fall of Samaria to Assyria in 722 BC and the burning of Jerusalem by Babylon in 586 BC. It is the story of how the people who were promised a land came to be carried out of it.

But it is not only a story of loss. It holds some of the most beloved scenes in the Old Testament: a chariot of fire, a widow’s jar of oil that will not run dry, a foreign general washing seven times in the Jordan, a servant who sees the hills full of fire. And it never stops saying why the end came, and that it came only after long patience.

Three movements

Chapters 1–8 belong mostly to the prophet Elisha, Elijah’s successor, and are full of wonders done among ordinary people. Chapters 9–17 follow both kingdoms from Jehu’s bloody revolution to the fall of the northern kingdom, and pause to explain it. Chapters 18–25 follow Judah alone, through two great reforming kings, Hezekiah and Josiah, and one terrible one, Manasseh, to the fall of Jerusalem and a brief, quiet coda in Babylon.

“Where is the LORD, the God of Elijah?”

Elijah’s last day is a farewell walk. From Gilgal to Bethel to Jericho to the Jordan, he tells Elisha to stay behind, and three times Elisha refuses: “As surely as the LORD lives and as surely as you live, I will not leave you.” Across the river, Elijah asks what he can do for him, and Elisha asks for “a double portion of your spirit”, the share an eldest son inherited from his father. Then:

{{OWB:2KI.2.11.s1}}

— 2 Kings 2:11

Elisha cries out, “My father, my father! The chariots of Israel and its horsemen!” He picks up the cloak that fell from Elijah, goes back to the Jordan, strikes the water and asks, “Where is the LORD, the God of Elijah?” The water divides. The prophet has gone, but the prophet’s God has not.

Not every Elisha story is comfortable. On the road up to Bethel, the centre of Jeroboam’s calf worship, a crowd of “young lads” mock him, and when he curses them in the LORD’s name two bears come out of the forest and maul forty-two of them. Readers have long struggled with the scene. Some note that the Hebrew words can describe young men rather than small children, and that the mockery was aimed at the LORD’s prophet at the heart of rebellion against him; even so, it remains a hard story, told without comment.

The man of God among ordinary people

Most of Elisha’s wonders are done for people who would never appear in a royal chronicle. A prophet’s widow is about to lose her two sons to a creditor, and has nothing in the house but “a small jar of oil”. Elisha tells her to borrow empty containers from all her neighbours, “and not just a few”, and to start pouring. The oil flows until she asks for another, and her son tells her, “There are no more.” “Then the oil stopped flowing.” God’s supply was limited only by the room she made for it.

A wealthy woman in Shunem builds a little room on her roof for the prophet, with “a bed, a table, a chair and a lamp”. She is given a son, and when the boy dies in her lap she rides straight to Elisha and will not leave him. He lies on the child, mouth to mouth and hands to hands, and “The boy sneezed seven times and opened his eyes.” Twenty loaves feed a hundred men, “and there was some left over, as the LORD had said.” An axe head, borrowed and lost in the Jordan, floats. These are small mercies, and they foreshadow greater ones: the New Testament’s readers would recognise a man of God who feeds a crowd with a few loaves, raises a mother’s only son and cleanses a leper.

Naaman

The most famous of these stories is about an enemy. Naaman, commander of the army of Aram, is a great man and a mighty warrior, but he has a serious skin disease. A captive Israelite girl who serves his wife tells her mistress about the prophet in Samaria. Naaman arrives at Elisha’s door with horses, chariots and a fortune in gifts, and Elisha does not even come out. He sends a messenger: “Go and wash seven times in the Jordan.” Naaman storms off in a rage. Are not the rivers of Damascus better? Then his servants reason with him:

{{OWB:2KI.5.13.s1}}

— 2 Kings 5:13

He goes down to the Jordan, and his flesh becomes “like the flesh of a young boy”. He returns, a different man, to confess, “Now I know that in all the earth there is no God except in Israel.” Jesus told the synagogue at Nazareth that of all the people with leprosy in Israel in Elisha’s time, only this Syrian was made clean (Luke 4:27), and they tried to throw him off a cliff for it. Grace, then as now, is not earned and not limited to insiders. Elisha’s servant Gehazi, who runs after Naaman to collect the gifts his master refused, goes away with the disease himself.

The same generosity shows in war. When an Aramean army surrounds Elisha’s town, his servant panics: “Oh no, my master! What shall we do?” Elisha prays:

{{OWB:2KI.6.17.s1}}

— 2 Kings 6:17

The raiders are led, blinded, into Samaria, and the king of Israel asks whether he should kill them. “Do not kill them,” Elisha answers. “Set food and water before them, so that they can eat and drink and then go back to their master.” The king gives them a feast and sends them home, “And the Aramean raiding bands did not come into the land of Israel again.” It is the Old Testament’s own picture of loving one’s enemies.

Jehu and the lamp in Jerusalem

Elisha sends a young prophet to anoint Jehu, an army commander, as king of Israel, with orders to destroy the house of Ahab. Jehu, who “drives like a madman”, does it with a thoroughness that leaves Jezebel dead beneath her window, Ahab’s seventy sons beheaded and the worshippers of Baal slaughtered in their own temple. On the way he meets Jehonadab and asks, “Is your heart right with mine, as my heart is with yours?”, the question John Wesley would take for one of his best-loved sermons.

The verdict on Jehu is mixed. “So Jehu wiped out Baal worship from Israel,” and the LORD commends him; but “Jehu took no care to walk in the law of the LORD, the God of Israel, with his whole heart”, and he kept Jeroboam’s golden calves. Nearly a century later the prophet Hosea would announce judgement on Jehu’s house “for the blood shed at Jezreel” (Hosea 1:4). Zeal for God is not the same as a whole heart.

In Judah, meanwhile, David’s lamp nearly goes out. When her son the king is killed, Athaliah, a daughter of Ahab’s house, sets out to destroy the whole royal family. One baby, Joash, is rescued by his aunt and hidden in the temple for six years. Then the priest Jehoiada brings him out, crowns him, and makes a covenant “that they would be the people of the LORD.” The whole promise to David had hung on one hidden child.

The fall of Samaria

The northern kingdom’s last decades are a blur of assassinations. Even then God was patient: when Israel was suffering bitterly, he “was gracious to them and had compassion on them”, and through the prophet Jonah son of Amittai he promised to restore its borders. But in 722 BC the king of Assyria captured Samaria and carried its people into exile.

Here the narrator stops and preaches. Chapter 17 is the theological heart of Kings. “This happened because the Israelites had sinned against the LORD their God.” They had worshipped other gods; they had ignored the prophets the LORD sent “through every prophet and every seer”; “They went after worthless idols and became worthless themselves.” Then the king of Assyria resettled the land with peoples from far away, who learned to fear the LORD while keeping their own gods, often seen as the beginning of the long division between Jews and Samaritans that still stands in the background of John 4. The sermon is addressed to Judah too: “Even Judah failed to keep the commandments of the LORD their God.”

Hezekiah

Then comes a king like David. Hezekiah tears down the high places and even breaks up the bronze snake Moses had made, because the people had made it an idol. “He trusted in the LORD, the God of Israel.” In 701 BC, Sennacherib of Assyria sweeps through Judah and sends his chief officer to stand outside Jerusalem’s walls and mock: “What sort of confidence is this that you are trusting in?” When a threatening letter arrives, Hezekiah does something unforgettable:

{{OWB:2KI.19.14.s1}}

— 2 Kings 19:14

He prays that “every kingdom on earth will know that you, LORD, alone are God.” Through Isaiah the answer comes that the king of Assyria will not enter the city, and that night the angel of the LORD strikes the Assyrian camp. Sennacherib’s own inscriptions, which survive, boast that he shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage”, and say nothing of taking the city.

When Hezekiah falls ill, he weeps and prays, and is told, “I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears,” and given fifteen more years. But he shows envoys from Babylon his whole treasury, and Isaiah warns that everything in it will one day be hauled off to Babylon. Hezekiah’s answer is chilling: “Is it not good, if there will be peace and stability as long as I live?” Even the best kings are not the King the promise needs. These chapters appear again, almost word for word, in Isaiah 36–39.

Manasseh and Josiah

Hezekiah’s son Manasseh reigns fifty-five years, longer than any other king of Judah, and undoes everything. He rebuilds the high places, worships the stars, sacrifices his own son and fills Jerusalem with innocent blood. The LORD says that he will bring such disaster on Jerusalem “that it will make both ears of all who hear of it tingle”, and wipe it clean “as someone wipes a dish”.

Then his grandson Josiah becomes king at eight years old, and during repairs to the temple the high priest makes a discovery: “In the house of the LORD I have found the book of the law.” When Josiah hears it read he tears his clothes. Many scholars think the book was Deuteronomy, or its core. Josiah sends to the prophet Huldah, then gathers the whole people, reads them the book of the covenant, and leads the greatest reform and the greatest Passover in Judah’s history. At Bethel he burns bones on Jeroboam’s altar, as the man of God had foretold three centuries before.

— 2 Kings 23:25

And yet the next verse begins, “Even so.” The reform came too late to turn away the judgement Manasseh’s reign had brought. Josiah was killed at Megiddo in 609 BC, and within a generation Babylon had come.

The fall of Jerusalem

The end is told with terrible economy. Nebuchadnezzar takes King Jehoiachin and ten thousand captives to Babylon in 597 BC. Eleven years later, after Zedekiah rebels, the city falls; Zedekiah is made to watch his sons killed before his eyes are put out; and the captain of the guard “burned the house of the LORD, the king’s palace and all the houses of Jerusalem”. “So Judah went into exile, away from its own land.”

And then, in the last four verses, a small door opens. In the thirty-seventh year of his exile, Jehoiachin is released from prison and given a seat of honour above the other captive kings, and “for the rest of his life he ate regularly at the king’s table.” Clay tablets from Babylon, found in the twentieth century, list the rations given to “Jehoiachin, king of Judah” and his sons. David’s line is in chains, but it is alive.

How to read 2 Kings

Read it as the second half of one book with 1 Kings. Take Elisha’s stories slowly, one at a time; then read chapters 17–25 straight through, because their cumulative weight is the point. Many of the prophets lived in these years, and their books open up the history from the inside: Jonah and Amos in the days of Jeroboam son of Joash, Hosea in Israel’s last decades, Isaiah and Micah in Hezekiah’s, Jeremiah from Josiah to the fall. 2 Chronicles retells the Judean story from another angle and adds things Kings leaves out, including the repentance of Manasseh.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition credited Kings to Jeremiah, and the last chapter of Jeremiah repeats the end of 2 Kings almost exactly. Many scholars think the book reached its final form during the exile, since its last event can be dated to about 561 BC, and some think there was an earlier edition in Josiah’s day. Christians hold a range of views on its composition; all agree that it was written for people who had lost everything, to tell them why, and to show them that God had not finished with David’s house.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 2 Kings are worth knowing.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation follows long custom and writes “the LORD”.

“Serious skin disease.” The Hebrew word traditionally translated “leprosy” covered a range of skin conditions, not only what is now called leprosy. This translation calls Naaman’s illness, and Gehazi’s, “a serious skin disease”.

“The company of prophets.” The groups older translations call “the sons of the prophets”, the bands of prophets gathered around Elijah and Elisha, appear as “the company of prophets”.
