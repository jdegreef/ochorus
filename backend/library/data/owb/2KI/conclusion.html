— 2 Kings 25:30

2 Kings ends with a meal. The temple is ash, the walls of Jerusalem are rubble, the people are scattered from Egypt to Babylon, and the last word is about a captive king eating his daily bread at a foreign table. It is a very small hope, but the book chose to end on it.

The question the book leaves with you

The first readers of 2 Kings had lost everything the earlier books had promised: the land, the city, the temple, the throne. They would have asked whether God had failed. Kings answers no. It says, over and over, that the end came because the people would not listen, and that it came only after centuries of warnings: “Yet the LORD warned Israel and Judah through every prophet and every seer.” Judgement was not God breaking his word but keeping it.

But that is not the only thing Kings says. The same God who sent the prophets also kept a lamp burning for David when every king of Judah seemed bent on putting it out. He hid one child in the temple for six years. He was “gracious to them and had compassion on them” when they deserved neither. And he kept Jehoiachin alive in Babylon. So the question the book leaves is not only “Why did this happen?” but “What will God do now with the promise he made to David?”

The book does not say. It simply shows a son of David out of his prison clothes, eating at a king’s table. Readers who know the rest of the Bible see more in that scene than its first readers could.

Where the story goes next

The story of the exile is told from the inside by the prophets who lived it, Jeremiah in Jerusalem and Ezekiel among the captives in Babylon, and its end is told in 2 Chronicles and Ezra. Jeremiah had said the exile would last seventy years: “When Babylon’s seventy years have been completed, I will come to your aid.” Chronicles ends with Cyrus of Persia telling the exiles to go home and rebuild, and Ezra tells how they did.

And the line of Jehoiachin did not end in Babylon. Matthew’s Gospel opens with the family tree of Jesus, and when it reaches the end of Kings it names this very king, under another form of his name:

— Matthew 1:12–16

Matthew counts the generations in three sets of fourteen, “fourteen from David to the deportation to Babylon, and fourteen from the deportation to Babylon to Christ.” The exile is not the end of David’s story but its turning point.

Elijah and Elisha, too, walk into the Gospels. Jesus began his ministry at Nazareth by recalling the widow of Zarephath and Naaman the Syrian, foreigners to whom the prophets were sent (Luke 4:25–27). He fed crowds with a few loaves, raised a widow’s son and touched lepers, and his disciples saw the God of Elijah at work in him.

Where to go from here

2 Kings has given preachers some of their favourite texts. A few places to begin:

  • On Elijah’s cloak and Elisha’s double portion (2 Kings 2). D. L. Moody’s Secret Power, whose chapter “Power: In and Upon” follows Elisha across the Jordan.
  • On the widow’s oil (2 Kings 4:1–7). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon When the Oil Flows (2 Kings 4:6), on God giving as long as we bring something he can fill; and “The Borrower’s Chains”, a chapter of Faithful with Little, an Ochorus Original, which reads the same story as a lesson about debt.
  • On the Shunammite’s son (2 Kings 4:8–37). Charles H. Spurgeon’s The Soul-Winner, whose chapter “How to Raise the Dead” draws from Elisha’s prayer over the child lessons for everyone who longs to bring others to life in Christ.
  • On Naaman (2 Kings 5). D. L. Moody’s sermon Naaman the Syrian, on pride, plain obedience and the cleansing no rank can buy.
  • On Jehu and Jehonadab (2 Kings 10:15). John Wesley’s sermon Catholic Spirit, which builds a doctrine of Christian love across differences of opinion on Jehu’s question, “Is your heart right with mine, as my heart is with yours?”
  • On Hezekiah (2 Kings 18–20). E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, with its chapter “Hezekiah, the Praying King”; and “Opening the Letter Before the Lord”, a chapter of The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original, on 2 Kings 19:14–19.
  • On the whole story of the divided kingdom. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “2 Kings and 2 Chronicles: The Divided Kingdom” reads it as a warning against carelessness after seasons of blessing.
  • For younger readers. Tell Me the Story – Book 3: Kings and Prophets, an Ochorus Original, with its stories of the chariots of fire, Naaman washing seven times, and Josiah, the boy king.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 2 Kings in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
