2 Chronicles ends in mid-movement. The temple has been burned, the land has lain empty for seventy years, and now a Persian emperor announces that the God of heaven has charged him to rebuild the house in Jerusalem. The last words are not a summary or a farewell but an invitation: “let them go up.” In most Hebrew Bibles, they are the last words of the whole Old Testament.

The question the book leaves with you

Chronicles was written for those who had gone up, or for their children: a small people with a modest temple, no king and a great deal of history behind them. The book’s question to them was not “What went wrong?”, which Kings had answered, but “What now?” And its answer runs through every reign it tells: seek the LORD. Humble yourselves, pray, turn back, and he will hear. Rely on him, and not on the king of Aram or the physicians or your own strength. Give him a whole heart.

The book is honest that this is not easy. Asa began by praying and ended by trusting silver. Joash forgot the man who had saved his life. Uzziah was “wonderfully helped until he became strong.” But Chronicles is just as honest about mercy: Rehoboam humbled himself and was spared; the unclean worshippers at Hezekiah’s Passover were pardoned; and Manasseh, the worst of them all, prayed in chains in Babylon and was brought home. Every reader is left with the same choice those kings faced, and with the same promise: “If you seek him, he will let you find him.”

Where the story goes next

The book of Ezra begins by repeating the decree of Cyrus, and tells how the exiles went up, rebuilt the altar and laid the foundations of a new temple, weeping and shouting for joy at once. Nehemiah tells of the rebuilding of Jerusalem’s walls. The prophets Haggai and Zechariah urged the builders on.

But the hope of Chronicles was larger than a building. Its readers still waited for the son of David whose throne would stand forever, and for the glory that had once filled the temple. The New Testament announces both in Jesus. Challenged by the Pharisees on a Sabbath, he said:

{{OWB:MAT.12.6.s1}}

— Matthew 12:6

He spoke of his own body as the temple that would be torn down and raised in three days. And he summed up the Hebrew Scriptures, from their first book to their last, when he spoke of the blood of the prophets “from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah, who died between the altar and the house of God” (Luke 11:51): from Cain’s brother in Genesis to the priest stoned in the temple court in 2 Chronicles 24. The last book of the Hebrew Bible was already, for Jesus, part of the story he had come to finish.

And Cyrus’s invitation, “let them go up”, has never been withdrawn. The letter to the Hebrews tells Christians, “you have approached Mount Zion, the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem” (Hebrews 12:22).

Where to go from here

2 Chronicles has given the church some of its most-preached texts on prayer and revival, and many of them are treated in this library:

  • On the glory filling the temple (2 Chronicles 5–7). Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s sermon Thanksgiving Obtains the Spirit (2 Chronicles 5:13–14), on the cloud that came when the singers praised the LORD as one; and “If My People Will Pray”, a chapter of The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original, on Solomon’s prayer and the promise of 7:14, and what it does and does not promise.
  • On Solomon and the queen of Sheba (2 Chronicles 9). Hudson Taylor’s sermon Coming to the King (1 Kings 10:13, told again in 2 Chronicles 9:12).
  • On Asa (2 Chronicles 14–16). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon The Search that Always Finds (2 Chronicles 15:15), on seeking, finding and resting; and Catherine Booth’s Godliness, whose chapter “The Perfect Heart” opens with 2 Chronicles 16:9 and the history of Asa.
  • On Jehoshaphat (2 Chronicles 20). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon Holding Fast and Held Fast (2 Chronicles 20:20), on the Hebrew wordplay in Jehoshaphat’s charge; and “‘Our Eyes Are Turned Toward You’”, a chapter of The Prayers Jesus Learned, on his prayer in 20:12.
  • On Hezekiah. E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, with its chapter “Hezekiah, the Praying King”.
  • On the whole history. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapters “1 Kings and 2 Chronicles: The Kingdom of Peace” and “2 Kings and 2 Chronicles: The Divided Kingdom” read Solomon’s reign and the divided kingdom as lessons for the Christian life.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 2 Chronicles in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
