— 2 Chronicles 7:14

2 Chronicles begins with a young king asking God for wisdom and ends with a pagan emperor telling God’s people to go home. In between, it tells the story of Judah’s kings over four centuries, from Solomon’s temple filled with glory to the same temple in ashes, and it tells it as a long lesson in what happens when people seek God, and when they do not.

It is the second half of the book called in Hebrew Divrei HaYamim, “the events of the days”, divided in two by the ancient Greek translation. It runs from about 970 BC to 538 BC, the first year of Cyrus king of Persia. In most Hebrew Bibles it is the last book of all, and its last words are a king’s proclamation to the scattered people of God: “let them go up.” The Hebrew Scriptures end not with a full stop but with an open door.

The shape of the book

Chapters 1–9 tell of Solomon, and above all of the temple he built. Chapters 10–36 follow the kings of Judah, the descendants of David, from the division of the kingdom to the exile in Babylon. The northern kingdom of Israel, which fills so many pages of Kings, appears only where it touches Judah; for Chronicles, the line of God’s promise runs through David’s house and Jerusalem’s temple. Yet the book never forgets the northern tribes, and more than once it shows Judah’s kings inviting them home.

The story is told with great freedom. Some reigns that take pages in Kings are summarised in a few lines, and others are greatly enlarged, especially those of four reforming kings: Asa, Jehoshaphat, Hezekiah and Josiah. Much of what Chronicles adds is preaching and prayer. Prophets appear again and again, many of them unknown from any other book, and kings pray long prayers before battles and festivals. The writer is not only reporting what happened. He is showing his readers how to live.

Solomon and the glory

Solomon begins his reign by asking, as in 1 Kings, for wisdom rather than riches: “Now give me wisdom and knowledge, so that I may go out and come in before this people as their leader.” He builds the temple “on Mount Moriah”, on the threshing floor David bought from Ornan, and Chronicles lingers over its gold, its bronze pillars and its furnishings.

The high point comes at the dedication. The priests have brought the ark into the inner room; the Levite singers in fine linen stand by the altar with cymbals, harps and lyres, and a hundred and twenty priests with trumpets:

{{OWB:2CH.5.13.s1}} {{OWB:2CH.5.14.s1}}

— 2 Chronicles 5:13–14

In Chronicles the glory comes while the people are singing the refrain David gave them. Solomon prays the great prayer of dedication, asking, “But will God really live on earth with human beings?”, and when he finishes, “fire came down from heaven and devoured the burnt offering”, and the people kneel with their faces to the pavement and give thanks, “for he is good; his loyal love lasts forever.”

Chronicles does not tell of the many wives who turned Solomon’s heart away; it leaves him at the height of his glory, greater “in riches and in wisdom than all the kings of the earth.” That is not ignorance of Kings, which its readers knew well, but a choice to remember Solomon as the builder of the house of prayer.

“If my people…”

After the dedication the LORD appears to Solomon by night, and answers his prayer with the promise that heads this introduction. Its setting matters: it is God’s reply to Solomon’s request that he would hear prayers made toward the temple in times of drought, locusts or plague. “When I shut up the sky so that no rain falls,” God says, and then, if his people humble themselves, pray, seek his face and turn from their evil ways, he will hear, forgive and heal.

Many Christians have prayed this verse for their own countries. Others point out that it was spoken to Israel, the people who bore God’s name, and was tied to “the prayer made in this place”. Both are worth hearing: the promise was first Israel’s, and yet the pattern it describes is God’s way with his people in every age, and the church, which also bears his name, has rightly taken it to heart.

In any case, the rest of 2 Chronicles is a kind of commentary on this verse. Its four verbs, humble, pray, seek and turn, come back again and again, and so does God’s response. When king and officials humble themselves under Rehoboam, the word comes, “They have humbled themselves, so I will not destroy them.” When Hezekiah prays for people who are not ritually clean, “The LORD heard Hezekiah and healed the people.”

Seeking and finding

Asa’s reign is the first full illustration. Faced with a vast army from Cush, he prays one of the great short prayers of the Bible: “LORD, there is no one but you to help when the weak stand against the strong. Help us, LORD our God, because we are depending on you” (14:11). After the victory, a prophet named Azariah meets him with a word that could stand over the whole book:

— 2 Chronicles 15:2

Asa and the people enter a covenant to seek the LORD “with all their heart and with all their soul”, and “he let them find him. So the LORD gave them rest on every side.” But late in his reign, threatened by the northern kingdom, Asa pays the king of Aram to rescue him instead of trusting God, and the seer Hanani rebukes him:

{{OWB:2CH.16.9.s1}}

— 2 Chronicles 16:9

Asa jails the prophet. In his last illness, the book says, “he did not seek the LORD; he sought only the physicians.” The point is not that doctors are wrong, but that Asa, who once prayed so well, had come to rely on anything but God.

Jehoshaphat: “the battle is not yours”

Asa’s son Jehoshaphat sends officials and Levites through the towns of Judah, “having the book of the law of the LORD with them”, to teach the people. He appoints judges and tells them, “the LORD our God does no wrong, plays no favorites and takes no bribe.” He is rebuked for his alliance with Ahab, but his great moment comes when a coalition of Moab, Ammon and others invades. Jehoshaphat “resolved to seek the LORD”, proclaims a fast, and stands before the people in the temple to pray. His prayer ends with one of the most honest sentences in Scripture:

{{OWB:2CH.20.12.s1}}

— 2 Chronicles 20:12

The Spirit comes on a Levite named Jahaziel, who tells them, “the battle is not yours; it belongs to God.” The next morning Jehoshaphat sends the singers out ahead of the army, praising the LORD, and tells the people, “Hold firm in your trust in the LORD your God, and you will be held firm.” The Hebrew is a play on a single word, to believe and to be made firm, which Alexander Maclaren made the subject of a sermon. As the singing begins, the invaders turn on one another.

Good beginnings, bad endings

Again and again Chronicles shows kings who start well and finish badly. Joash, the boy king saved from Athaliah, “did what was right in the sight of the LORD all the days of Jehoiada the priest.” After Jehoiada dies, he lets his officials lead him back to idols. When Jehoiada’s son Zechariah warns them, “Because you have forsaken the LORD, he has forsaken you,” they stone him in the temple courtyard at the king’s command. His last words are, “May the LORD see this and call you to account!” Jesus remembered him. He spoke of the blood of the prophets “from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah, who died between the altar and the house of God” (Luke 11:51); since Chronicles stands last in the Hebrew Bible, many readers take him to mean the first murder and the last in the Scriptures.

Amaziah “did what was right in the sight of the LORD, yet not with a whole heart.” Uzziah, who sought God in the days of his teacher Zechariah, prospered, “for he was wonderfully helped until he became strong.” Then “his heart grew proud, to his own ruin”, and he went into the temple to burn incense, a duty only the priests could do, and left it with a serious skin disease he carried to his death. The danger in Chronicles is rarely weakness. It is strength that forgets where it came from.

There are surprises of mercy, too. When the army of the northern kingdom carries off thousands of captives from Judah, a prophet named Oded meets them at Samaria and tells them to send their own relatives home. They clothe the captives from the plunder, give them food and drink, anoint them with oil, put the weak on donkeys and bring them to Jericho (28:15). Many readers have heard in it an echo of the parable of the Good Samaritan.

Hezekiah opens the doors

Ahaz, Hezekiah’s father, had shut the doors of the temple. In the first month of his reign, Hezekiah “opened the doors of the house of the LORD and made them strong again.” Chronicles gives three chapters to his reforms, and most of all to a Passover, to which he invites not only Judah but the remnant of the northern tribes. His couriers carry letters as far as Zebulun: “Israelites, return to the LORD, the God of Abraham, Isaac and Israel, and he will return to those of you who are left.” Most of the north laughs at them; some humble themselves and come.

Many of those who come have not been purified as the law required, and they eat the Passover anyway. Hezekiah does not turn them away. He prays:

{{OWB:2CH.30.18.s1}} {{OWB:2CH.30.19.s1}}

— 2 Chronicles 30:18–19

“The LORD heard Hezekiah and healed the people.” It is the promise of 7:14 kept. When Sennacherib invades, Hezekiah tells the people, “With him is only an arm of flesh, but we have the LORD our God with us.” And when his heart grows proud after his recovery from illness, he humbles himself too.

Manasseh

The greatest surprise in Chronicles is the story of Manasseh. In Kings he is the worst of Judah’s kings, whose sins sealed the city’s fate. Chronicles tells the same evil, but then something Kings does not: the Assyrians take him to Babylon with hooks and bronze chains, and there:

{{OWB:2CH.33.12.s1}} {{OWB:2CH.33.13.s1}}

— 2 Chronicles 33:12–13

He comes home, removes the foreign gods from the temple and restores the altar of the LORD. Readers have asked how this fits with Kings. The two accounts need not conflict: Kings is concerned with the harm Manasseh did, which outlived him, and Chronicles with what God did for Manasseh. For the exiles who first read it, his story meant that no one was beyond repentance, not even a king who had led a whole nation into evil. A short prayer in his name, the Prayer of Manasseh, was later composed and is printed among the Apocrypha in some Bibles; it is not part of the Hebrew text.

Josiah, and the end

Of Josiah, Manasseh’s grandson, the book says that “while he was still young, he began to seek the God of his ancestor David.” He purges the land, repairs the temple, hears the book of the law, renews the covenant and keeps a Passover unequalled “since the days of Samuel the prophet”. But he dies at Megiddo, and Chronicles adds a sobering detail: he went out against Pharaoh Neco after “He did not listen to Neco’s words, which came from the mouth of God.” Even the best king could fail to listen.

Then the end comes quickly, and Chronicles explains it in some of the most moving sentences in the Old Testament:

{{OWB:2CH.36.15.s1}} {{OWB:2CH.36.16.s1}}

— 2 Chronicles 36:15–16

The temple is burned and the people taken to Babylon. Yet even the exile is given a meaning: the land keeps its Sabbaths, “until seventy years were complete”, as Jeremiah had said. The years of loss are years of rest. And then the LORD stirs the spirit of Cyrus, and the book ends with an invitation.

How to read 2 Chronicles

Read it beside 1 and 2 Kings, which tell much of the same story, and notice what Chronicles adds: the prophets’ words, the kings’ prayers, the singers and the festivals. Follow its key words, seek, humble, rely and whole heart. Chronicles often shows faithfulness rewarded and unfaithfulness punished within a king’s own lifetime. Some readers find this too tidy; others note that the writer is teaching a pattern, not claiming that all suffering is deserved, and that he is just as insistent on mercy for those who humble themselves.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition credited Chronicles to Ezra, and its last two verses are repeated at the start of the book of Ezra; many scholars speak simply of “the Chronicler”, writing in the Persian period, perhaps around 400 BC. Some numbers differ from those in Kings: Jehoiachin is eighteen when he becomes king in 2 Kings 24:8 and eight in 2 Chronicles 36:9, a difference most explain as an error in copying. Christians hold a range of views on such details.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 2 Chronicles are worth knowing.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation follows long custom and writes “the LORD”.

“He will let you find him.” The Hebrew of Azariah’s promise says literally that God “will be found by you”. This translation renders it “he will let you find him”, making clear that finding God is his gift.

“Held firm.” Jehoshaphat’s words in 20:20 play on one Hebrew root, to trust and to be made firm, and the translation keeps the play: “Hold firm in your trust in the LORD your God, and you will be held firm.”

“Loyal love.” The refrain sung at the temple and on the march, “his loyal love lasts forever”, renders the Hebrew hesed, God’s steadfast covenant love.
