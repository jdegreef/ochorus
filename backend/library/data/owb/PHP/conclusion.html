— Philippians 4:19–20

Philippians ends with a promise and a doxology. The church had met Paul’s need, and now Paul, who has nothing to give them in return, hands them over to a God who has everything. It is how the whole letter works. A prisoner with no certain future writes to a small church with troubles of its own, and almost every sentence turns toward Christ, until the letter closes in glory to God the Father, for ever and ever.

The question the letter leaves

“For me, living is Christ.” Paul says it as plainly as anyone could, and the sentence waits for each reader to finish it in their own way. For me, living is my work, my family, my comfort, my reputation? Paul had his list too, and he counted it all as loss. He was not despising the good things of life; he was saying that none of them could carry the weight of being his life.

The letter does not leave that as an idea. It turns it into a way of treating other people. If Christ, who was in the form of God, emptied himself and took on the form of a servant, then the people who belong to him will regard each other as standing above themselves, and care about the concerns of others. Euodia and Syntyche are asked to agree in the Lord, and the whole church is asked to help them. Joy, in Philippians, is not a mood. It is what grows in people who have stopped grasping.

Where the letter points

It points forward. Paul is waiting for a Saviour from heaven, for the day of Christ, for the resurrection from the dead. The God who started a good work in the Philippians will keep working at it until it is finished. Nothing in the letter is meant to be the last word: not Paul’s chains, not the rivals preaching Christ to spite him, not the quarrel in the church, not even death.

We do not know for certain how Paul’s imprisonment ended. Christian tradition says that he was released for a time and later put to death in Rome under Nero. But the church at Philippi went on. Some fifty years later, the Philippians wrote to Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, and his reply survives. He reminds them that Paul “wrote you a letter, which, if you carefully study, you will find to be the means of building you up” in the faith.

Where to go from here

Polycarp’s advice still holds, and many of the church’s best teachers have helped readers study this letter. A few places to begin, all in this library:

  • The church at Philippi. The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp, his letter to the Philippians, with the story of his death; and Devoted to Prayer, whose chapters “The Place of Prayer by the River” and “Songs at Midnight” tell how the church began (Acts 16).
  • On the mind of Christ (chapter 2). Andrew Murray’s Humility, especially “The Secret of Redemption” (2:5–8) and “Humility and Death to Self” (2:8); Athanasius’s Against the Arians, whose first Discourse explains 2:9–10; and John Chrysostom’s sermon Concerning Lowliness of Mind, on 1:18.
  • On working out your salvation (2:12–13). Martyn Lloyd-Jones’s Working Out Our Own Salvation, and John Wesley’s On Working out our own Salvation, in his Sermons on Several Occasions.
  • On knowing Christ (chapter 3). George Whitefield’s The Power of Christ’s Resurrection (3:10), in Selected Sermons of George Whitefield.
  • On joy, prayer and contentment (chapter 4). Alexander Maclaren’s Rejoice Evermore (4:4) and How to Say ‘Thank You’ (4:10–14); Charles H. Spurgeon’s “Prayer, the Cure for Care” (4:6), in Mighty Power in Prayer; and Thomas Watson’s The Art of Divine Contentment, a whole book on 4:11.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Philippians in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.
