— Philippians 1:21

Philippians is a thank-you letter written from a prison. Paul is in chains, waiting to learn whether he will live or die. Some of the preachers around him are trying to make his time in chains more painful. And yet the words “joy” and “rejoice” run through the letter more than a dozen times, and its best-loved verses are about peace, contentment and strength for every situation.

It is also the warmest of Paul’s letters. He writes to the Philippians not as a teacher correcting a wayward church but as a friend writing to friends: “I hold you in my heart.” They had supported him from the very first, and they have just sent him another gift. Philippians is his answer, and it is full of Christ.

The church at Philippi

Philippi stood in the north of Greece, on the great Roman road across Macedonia. It was named after Philip of Macedon, the father of Alexander the Great, and after the civil war battle fought outside it in 42 BC it was resettled with Roman veterans. Acts calls it “a leading city of its district of Macedonia” and “a Roman colony” (Acts 16:12). Its people were proud of their Roman citizenship, a pride Paul draws on when he tells them to “live as citizens in a way worthy of the gospel of Christ” and reminds them that “we hold our citizenship in heaven.”

Paul came there around AD 49, on his second missionary journey, after a vision in the night of a man of Macedonia pleading, “Cross over to Macedonia and help us!” (Acts 16:9). It was the first church he founded in Europe, and Acts tells its beginnings in three unforgettable scenes. A businesswoman named Lydia, a dealer in purple cloth, meets with other women for prayer by a river: “The Lord opened her heart to heed what Paul was saying” (16:14). A slave girl who told fortunes is set free from the spirit that possessed her, and her owners, seeing their profits gone, have Paul and Silas beaten and thrown into prison. And at midnight in the prison, “as Paul and Silas prayed, they sang praises to God, and the prisoners listened” (16:25), until an earthquake shakes the doors open and the jailer and his household believe.

The church that began in Lydia’s house and a jailer’s home never forgot Paul. When he left Macedonia, theirs was the only church that went into partnership with him in giving and receiving, and they sent help to him in Thessalonica more than once (4:15–16).

Where Paul was writing from

Paul is in chains (1:7, 13), and he speaks of “the whole imperial guard” and sends greetings from “those in the emperor’s household” (4:22). The traditional view is that he wrote from Rome, during the two years Acts describes him under guard there, around AD 60–62. Some scholars prefer an earlier imprisonment in Ephesus, around AD 54–55, which would put him closer to Philippi for all the coming and going the letter describes; others suggest Caesarea, where he was held for two years before he was sent to Rome. Imperial guards and imperial servants could be found in all three places. Rome remains the most widely held view.

Some scholars also think the letter as we have it joins together two or three shorter letters, because of the sudden change of tone at 3:2 and because Paul’s thanks for the gift come so late, in 4:10–20. Many others see one letter written by a man whose thoughts and feelings moved quickly, and the book can be read with profit either way.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapter 1: Paul’s chains and the gospel. Thanksgiving and prayer for the Philippians; how his imprisonment has moved the gospel forward; living and dying; and a call to stand firm together.
  • Chapter 2: the mind of Christ. A plea for humility and unity, rooted in the great passage on Christ who emptied himself; and news of Timothy and Epaphroditus.
  • Chapter 3: knowing Christ. A warning against those who insist on circumcision; Paul counts all his advantages as loss; he presses on toward the prize.
  • Chapter 4: joy and peace. Two women urged to agree; rejoicing, prayer and peace; contentment; thanks for the gift; and greetings.

To live is Christ

Paul begins with thanks for their partnership “from the very first day until now”, and with a confidence that rests not on them but on God: “the one who started a good work in you will keep working at it until it is finished on the day of Christ Jesus” (1:6). He prays that their love will overflow, with knowledge and insight to recognize what really matters.

Then he tells them his news, and it is surprisingly good. His chains have moved the gospel forward. Everyone in the guard knows why he is there, and other believers have grown bolder. Some preach Christ out of rivalry, hoping to hurt him. “What does it matter?” If Christ is proclaimed, Paul will rejoice.

He does not know whether he will be released or executed, and he finds that either would be good. “I am pulled in two directions.” To leave this life and be with Christ is far, far better; to stay means fruitful work and their progress in the faith. The sentence that opens this introduction says it all. If life is Christ, then death can only be gain.

The mind of Christ

The church at Philippi was not without its troubles. Two of its leading women, Euodia and Syntyche, were at odds (4:2), and Paul’s plea for unity in chapter 2 may have them in mind. He does not simply tell the Philippians to get along. He shows them Christ:

— Philippians 2:5–11

Many readers think these lines were an early Christian hymn, which Paul either quotes or wrote himself. They move like a great V: down from equality with God to the form of a servant, to human likeness, to death, death on a cross; and then up to the highest place, and the name above every name. The last lines take words that God speaks of himself in Isaiah 45:23, every knee bending and every tongue declaring, and apply them to Jesus.

Christians have thought hard about what it means that “he emptied himself.” The church has always held that the Son did not stop being God; he emptied himself by taking on the form of a servant, not by giving up what he was. Paul’s point, though, is practical. This is how the Philippians are to think about one another: not grasping, but giving. “With fear and trembling, keep working out your own salvation,” he goes on, “For it is God who is at work in you.”

Then two examples of that mind at work. Timothy, of whom Paul says, “I have no one else of the same heart.” And Epaphroditus, the Philippians’ own messenger, who brought their gift and fell so sick he almost died, risking his life for the work of Christ.

Everything as loss

Chapter 3 opens with “rejoice in the Lord”, and turns at once into a sharp warning: “Be on guard against the dogs.” Teachers were insisting that Gentile believers be circumcised and keep the law to belong to God’s people. Paul answers with his own life. If anyone could boast in human credentials, he could: circumcised on the eighth day, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Pharisee, so zealous that he persecuted the church. All of it he now counts as loss, as dung, so that he may gain Christ and be found in him, with the righteousness that comes through faith.

— Philippians 3:13–14

He has not arrived. He is a runner, straining forward, and he invites them to run with him, waiting for a Saviour from heaven who will change our humble bodies to be like his glorious body.

The peace of God

The last chapter gathers up the whole letter in a few short commands. Stand firm. Agree in the Lord. “Rejoice in the Lord always. I will say it again: rejoice!” Be gentle, because “The Lord is near.” And then one of the most loved passages in the Bible:

— Philippians 4:6–7

Only at the end does Paul thank them for their gift, and he does it carefully, almost shyly. He has learned to be content in every situation, with plenty or with little, and he is not after their money. What delights him is what the gift means: a sweet-smelling offering to God, and a sign of a partnership in the gospel. “And my God will fill every need you have.”

How to read Philippians

It is short enough to read in a quarter of an hour. Read it first as what it is, a letter between friends, and imagine it read aloud in Lydia’s house. Then read it slowly.

Notice how often Paul speaks of joy, and where: in chains, in the face of rivals, in the shadow of death. Notice how often he speaks of the mind, of thinking and setting one’s mind. And notice that every part of the letter, its news, its warnings, its thanks, comes back to Christ: living is Christ, thinking as Christ thought, knowing Christ, strength in Christ.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Philippians are worth knowing about before you begin.

Citizens. In 1:27 the verb Paul uses means to live as a citizen, and the translation keeps that: “live as citizens in a way worthy of the gospel of Christ.” It belongs with 3:20, “we hold our citizenship in heaven”, words that would have rung clearly in a Roman colony.

Christ’s equality with God. In 2:6 the translation reads that he “did not treat being equal with God as something to exploit for himself.” The point is not that he lacked equality with God, but that he did not use it for his own advantage.

“Dung.” In 3:8 Paul uses a deliberately coarse word for what he now thinks of his former advantages, and the translation does not tidy it into “rubbish”.

Philippians 4:13. The familiar “I can do all things through Christ” reads here “I have strength for every situation in the one who makes me strong.” The verse comes straight after Paul’s words about plenty and hunger, and the translation lets it mean what it means there. The oldest manuscripts say “the one who makes me strong”; the name “Christ” was added later, though it is plainly Christ whom Paul means.
