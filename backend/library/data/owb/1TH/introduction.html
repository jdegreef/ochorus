{{OWB:1TH.1.9.s1}}

— 1 Thessalonians 1:9–10

Some time around AD 50, in the city of Corinth, Paul wrote to a church he had been forced to leave only a few months before. It is very likely the oldest of his letters that we have, and so perhaps the oldest Christian writing of any kind to survive. Before any of the Gospels had been written down, a small congregation in a Greek port city was hearing this letter read aloud.

Here, within twenty years of the cross, is a church that already prays to God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ together and waits for Jesus to come from heaven. And here is a missionary writing not as a theologian at a desk but as a parent separated from his children too soon. He cannot stop thanking God for them, and he cannot stop worrying about them.

A church founded in a hurry

Thessalonica, today Thessaloniki, was the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia. Named after a half-sister of Alexander the Great, it stood on the Via Egnatia, the great road that crossed the Balkans from the Adriatic to the Aegean, and it had a busy harbour. News travelled fast from such a place, as Paul says it did (1:8).

Acts 17 tells how the church began. Paul, Silas and Timothy came on from Philippi, where Paul and Silas had been beaten and jailed. For three Sabbaths Paul reasoned in the synagogue that the Messiah had to suffer and rise from the dead, and that Jesus was the Messiah. Some Jews believed, together with a large number of God-fearing Greeks and a good many leading women. Then a mob attacked the house of a believer named Jason, the missionaries were accused of proclaiming another king, Jesus, and they were sent away by night.

The stay may have been longer than three weeks (compare 2:9 and Philippians 4:16), but it was short, and it ended in danger. Paul went on to Berea and then to Athens. Unable to bear not knowing how the new believers were standing up to the pressure, he sent Timothy back to them. When Timothy returned with good news, Paul, now in Corinth, wrote this letter. An inscription found at Delphi dates Gallio, the governor before whom Paul was brought in Corinth (Acts 18:12), to about AD 51, so the letter can be dated more securely than almost anything else in the New Testament.

Most of the church were Gentiles. They had “turned to God from idols”, which no Jewish believer would need to do, and their own people had turned against them for it (2:14). They had no New Testament, no buildings and very little teaching. What they had was the word they had welcomed, the Spirit, one another, and this letter.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapters 1–3: thanksgiving and a story. Paul gives thanks for the church’s faith, recalls how the gospel came to them and how he lived among them, and tells how he sent Timothy and what joy his news has brought.
  • Chapters 4–5: how to live while waiting. Holiness in the body, love in the family of believers, quiet work, the hope for those who have died, the day of the Lord, and a string of short commands for the life of the church.

Each half ends with a prayer for the church to be kept holy at the coming of the Lord Jesus (3:11–13; 5:23–24), the horizon of the whole letter.

Faith, love and hope

In its third verse the letter names three things Paul remembers about the Thessalonians: the work their faith does, the labour their love takes on, and the endurance their hope gives them. The same three return near the end, as armour: “faith and love for our breastplate and the hope of salvation for our helmet” (5:8). It is probably the earliest place in Christian writing where faith, love and hope appear together, a few years before Paul wrote of them again in 1 Corinthians 13.

Notice how active they are. Faith does work; love labours; hope endures.

Like a nursing mother

Chapter 2 is Paul’s account of how he lived among them, and it reads like a reply to slander. Travelling speakers in the Greek world were often in it for money and applause, and someone seems to have said Paul was one more of them. He answers by reminding them of what they saw. He never used flattery and never put on a show to cover up greed. He worked night and day so as not to be a burden. And he reaches for the tenderest pictures he can find:

{{OWB:1TH.2.8.s1}}

— 1 Thessalonians 2:8

He was gentle among them, like a nursing mother with her children; he urged and comforted them, as a father treats his own children; and when he was torn away, he felt orphaned himself.

The same chapter holds one of the hardest passages in Paul’s letters (2:14–16), where he speaks of the Jews of Judea “who killed the Lord Jesus and the prophets and drove us out”. A few scholars have thought it was added later; most accept it as Paul’s, written in the heat of persecution. It needs to be read with care. Paul is comparing the Thessalonians’ sufferings at the hands of their own people with those of the Judean churches, themselves Jewish, at the hands of theirs. He was a Jew himself, and in Romans 9–11 he writes of his own people with anguish and hope. Whatever these lines mean, they cannot mean a verdict on the Jewish people as a whole.

Now we can truly live again

Chapter 3 is the story of an anxious wait. Paul stayed alone in Athens and sent Timothy, his “brother and God’s fellow worker in the gospel of Christ”, to strengthen the church. He feared that the tempter had got to them and that all his work had come to nothing. Then Timothy came back:

{{OWB:1TH.3.6.s1}}

— 1 Thessalonians 3:6–8

Paul’s own life, he tells them, hung on whether they were standing firm. The chapter ends in prayer, that the Lord would make their love overflow and their hearts stand firm until the day he comes “with all his holy ones”.

This is God’s will for you

The second half begins with instruction, though Paul insists the Thessalonians already know it and are already doing it; they need only do so “more and more”. First comes holiness: “This is God’s will for you: that you be holy.” New believers from a pagan world had to learn to keep away from sexual immorality. Then love for one another, which God himself has taught them. Then, more surprisingly, a quiet life: “Be eager to live calmly, look after your own affairs and earn your living with your hands.”

Paul does not say why. Perhaps some believers, sure that the Lord was coming soon, had stopped working. The theme returns at 5:14 and much more sharply in 2 Thessalonians 3. Hope in the Lord’s coming, Paul thinks, should make a person more faithful in ordinary work, not less.

Those who have fallen asleep

Then Paul turns to a question that had clearly been troubling the church. Since he left, some believers had died. Would they miss the Lord’s coming? Had they lost their share in the hope? Paul answers:

{{OWB:1TH.4.13.s1}} {{OWB:1TH.4.14.s1}}

— 1 Thessalonians 4:13–14

He does not forbid grief, only grieving “as others do who have no hope”. Then he describes the Lord’s coming: a shout of command, an archangel’s call, the sound of God’s trumpet; the dead in Christ rising first, and the living swept up with them “into the clouds to meet the Lord in the air”.

Christians have read these verses in different ways. The Latin Bible’s word for “swept up” gave English the word rapture, and many evangelical Christians, especially since the nineteenth century, have taught that believers will be taken from the earth before a time of great tribulation, with the Lord’s visible return following later. Many others read it as the single return of Christ in glory: the word for “meet” was used when a city’s people went out to welcome an arriving ruler and escort him in. Paul is not answering our questions about timing. He ends with the point that matters most to him and to his grieving readers: “From then on we will be with the Lord always.” And then, “So comfort one another with these words.”

Like a thief at night

About times and dates, Paul says, they know all they need: the day of the Lord comes like a thief at night. It will surprise those who say “All is peaceful and safe”. But believers are “children of light and children of the day”, so the right response is not calculation but readiness: to stay awake, keep a clear head, and put on faith, love and hope. And they need not fear:

{{OWB:1TH.5.9.s1}}

— 1 Thessalonians 5:9–10

“Awake or asleep”: living or dead, believers belong to the one who died for them.

Be joyful at all times

The letter ends with a burst of short commands for the life of a young church. Respect those who lead and work hard among you. Keep peace. Correct the idle, encourage the fainthearted, stand by the weak, be patient with everyone. Never pay back harm with harm. And then three lines that have been learned by heart ever since:

{{OWB:1TH.5.16.s1}}

— 1 Thessalonians 5:16–18

Paul is not commanding a mood but describing a life turned toward God at every moment. “Never stop praying” has been taken up by Christians in every century, from the desert monks who repeated a short prayer through the day to the lay brother in a Paris kitchen who learned to practise the presence of God among his pots and pans.

Then come instructions for gathered worship: do not smother the Spirit, do not look down on prophecy, test everything, keep hold of what is good. Then a solemn charge: “have this letter read to all the brothers and sisters.” This is the first time we hear of a Christian letter being read aloud to a church as a matter of course.

How to read 1 Thessalonians

It takes about fifteen minutes to read aloud, and it is worth reading that way, in a single sitting, as its first hearers did. Then read it again with Acts 17:1–10 open beside it.

Notice how often Paul says “you know”: he is reminding, not informing. Notice the coming of the Lord at the end of every chapter, and how practical the hope is: it makes people holy, hardworking, patient and grateful. If you are grieving, go straight to 4:13–18, and read it as Paul meant it, as words to comfort one another with.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 1 Thessalonians are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Brothers and sisters.” Paul addresses the church as adelphoi, literally “brothers”, nearly twenty times in this short letter, more often for its length than anywhere else in his writing. The word took in the whole family of believers, women as well as men, and this edition says “brothers and sisters”. The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose the traditional “brothers” instead.

“Gentle” or “infants” (2:7). Many early manuscripts read “we were infants among you” instead of “we were gentle among you”; the two Greek words differ by a single letter. This translation reads “gentle”, which fits the picture of the nursing mother that follows.

“Your own body” (4:4). The Greek speaks of a “vessel”. It may mean one’s own body, as here, or one’s wife, as in “learn to take a wife for yourself”. The translation follows the first reading.

“Swept up” (4:17). The verb means to seize or snatch suddenly. This translation keeps the suddenness with “swept up”, rather than the more familiar “caught up”.

“The Jews of Judea” (2:14). Paul means those in Judea who persecuted the churches there, and the translation makes that plain, rather than letting the words sound like a statement about the Jewish people as a whole.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Greek beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
