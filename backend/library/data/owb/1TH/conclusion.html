The most famous passage in the letter ends with an instruction, not a timetable. Paul has just described the most astonishing event he can imagine, and what he wants the Thessalonians to do with it is to sit with one another in their grief and say these words aloud. The hope of the Lord’s coming, in this letter, is never a puzzle to be solved. It is comfort for the bereaved, courage for the persecuted, and a reason to go on living well.

The question the letter leaves

Paul’s worry in chapter 3 is a simple one: are they standing firm? It is the question the letter leaves with every reader. The Thessalonians had heard the gospel for a few weeks, from men who were then driven out of town. They had lost friends and family to persecution and death. They might easily have drifted back to the gods of the city. Instead, Timothy found them still believing, still loving, still waiting.

What kept them was not their own strength. Paul is clear that the word they received was “God’s own word, which is now at work in you who believe”, and that the God who called them “can be trusted, and he will do it.” But they had to keep hold of it, and they had to keep hold of each other. That is why the letter ends with so many commands about life together: encourage, correct, stand by, be patient, pray. A faith that stands firm is rarely a solitary one.

Where the letter points

It points forward, to the day when the living and the dead in Christ will be together with the Lord. It points inward, to holiness of body and heart, to a quiet and honest life, to joy and prayer and thanks in every circumstance. And it points onward, to a second letter. Some in Thessalonica came to think the day of the Lord had already arrived, and a second letter was sent to steady them. The Second Letter to the Thessalonians, in this same edition, is the natural next thing to read.

Where to go from here

Many of the church’s great teachers have written on the themes of this letter, and several of them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On holiness (4:3 and 5:23). J. C. Ryle’s Holiness, whose chapter on sanctification takes 4:3 as its text; John Wesley’s A Plain Account of Christian Perfection, which leans on Paul’s prayer that God would make them holy in every part; and A. B. Simpson’s sermon Wholly Sanctified, on 5:23–24.
  • On the hope of the Lord’s coming (4:13–18). Richard Baxter’s The Saints’ Everlasting Rest, written when he thought himself dying, which draws on this passage in describing the resurrection and the life with the Lord that follows it.
  • On prayer without ceasing (5:17). Brother Lawrence’s The Practice of the Presence of God, from a monastery kitchen in Paris, and E. M. Bounds’s The Necessity of Prayer.
  • On praying for one another (5:25). George Whitefield’s sermon Intercession Every Christian’s Duty, on Paul’s request, “Brothers and sisters, pray for us.”
  • On the Spirit in the church (5:19). A. B. Simpson’s Hindering the Holy Spirit, which takes up “do not smother the Spirit” beside two other texts.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 1 Thessalonians in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.

But the best next step may be to read the letter again, aloud, as Paul charged that it be read. It is short enough for one sitting and warm enough to be heard as what it is: a father’s letter to children he could not stop thinking about. And his prayer for them may become a prayer for you:
