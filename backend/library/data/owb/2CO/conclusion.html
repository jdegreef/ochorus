— 2 Corinthians 13:14

A letter that began with grief, misunderstanding and tears ends with a blessing on “every one of you”. Paul has rebuked the Corinthians, pleaded with them, mocked their rivals and laid his own heart bare. But his last word is a prayer that they will know the grace of Christ, the love of God and the fellowship of the Spirit, the one God in whose life a broken church can be made whole. It is no accident that churches still close their worship with it. Every congregation, sooner or later, needs what Corinth needed.

The question the letter leaves

The Corinthians wanted a leader who looked strong: impressive in person, eloquent, confident, successful. 2 Corinthians asks what strength really is. Paul’s answer is a man lowered from a wall in a basket, a body carrying “the dying of Jesus”, a prayer three times refused. And yet through that man the light of the knowledge of God’s glory reached Corinth.

The question is not only about leaders. It reaches every believer who has prayed for a thorn to be taken away and found it still there. Paul does not say the thorn is good, or that the prayer was wrong. He says that the answer he received was better than the one he asked for: “My grace is enough for you.” The letter does not promise that weakness will be removed. It promises that it will not have the last word.

Where the letter points

It points, first, to the cross and the resurrection, not as doctrines only but as the shape of Christian life. Christ “was indeed crucified in weakness, but he lives by the power of God”, and those who belong to him share both: dying and yet alive, sorrowful and yet always full of joy, poor and yet making many rich.

It points, second, to reconciliation. The God who reconciled the world to himself in Christ gave his people a ministry of reconciliation, and Paul practised it on the church that had hurt him. He forgave, urged them to forgive the man who had wronged him, and opened his heart wide. The gospel he preached to the world, he lived in the church.

And it points to what cannot yet be seen. “What can be seen lasts only a while; what cannot be seen is eternal.” The tent will come down, and a house not made with hands is waiting. That is why Paul did not give up, and why his readers need not either.

Where to go from here

Many preachers have found in this letter words for the suffering and the weary, and a number of them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On comfort in affliction (chapter 1). Hannah Whitall Smith’s The God of All Comfort, whose title chapter opens with 1:3–4, and Charles H. Spurgeon’s Consolation Proportionate to Spiritual Sufferings (1:5).
  • On being changed into his image (chapter 3). Henry Drummond’s The Changed Life (3:18), and in A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, the New Testament volume, the chapter “The Holy Spirit in Second Corinthians”.
  • On the things that are not seen (chapter 4). Alexander Maclaren’s Looking at the Unseen and Thomas Chalmers’s The Transitory Nature of Visible Things, both on 4:18.
  • On the love of Christ and the call to be reconciled (chapter 5). Maclaren’s The Love that Constrains (5:14) and Samuel Davies’s Sinners Entreated to Be Reconciled to God (5:20).
  • On godly sorrow (chapter 7). Frederick W. Robertson’s The Power of Sorrow (7:10).
  • On spiritual warfare and strength in weakness (chapters 10–12). Simpson’s The Weapons of Our Warfare (10:4) and Maclaren’s Strength in Weakness (12:8–9).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 2 Corinthians in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.

It is worth reading 1 Corinthians and 2 Corinthians together, as a story of one church and its apostle over several hard years, and then reading Romans, which Paul wrote from Corinth not long afterwards, when the reconciliation was complete and he was finally staying with them. The church that had caused him so much grief became the place from which he sent out his fullest account of the gospel.

But the best next step may be to take this letter into whatever is hardest in your own life, and to read it there. It was written by a man under pressure, at a loss and thrown down, and it was written for people like him:
