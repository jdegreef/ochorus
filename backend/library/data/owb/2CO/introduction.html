{{OWB:2CO.4.6.s1}} {{OWB:2CO.4.7.s1}}

— 2 Corinthians 4:6–7

Of all Paul’s letters, this is the one in which we come closest to the man himself. In 1 Corinthians he is a teacher answering questions. In 2 Corinthians he is a wounded friend, by turns grieving, relieved, tender, sarcastic and fierce. He tells of a hardship in Asia so heavy that he gave up hope of staying alive, of a letter written “with many tears”, of sleepless nights and shipwrecks, of being lowered from a city wall in a basket, of a vision of paradise he would rather not talk about, and of a “thorn in the flesh” that God would not take away.

And through all of it runs one discovery, which may be the deepest thing Paul ever wrote: that God’s power is not seen in spite of human weakness, but in it. The treasure is in pots made of clay, so that everyone can see the power is God’s.

What happened after 1 Corinthians

The letter itself lets us piece together much of what happened between Paul’s first surviving letter to Corinth and this one, though not every detail is certain.

Things did not go well. Paul made a short visit to Corinth that he calls a visit “bringing grief” (2:1), apparently because someone in the church opposed or insulted him and the church did not stand with him. He went away and, instead of returning as he had planned, wrote them a severe letter, out of “great affliction and anguish of heart” (2:4). Like the earlier letter of 1 Corinthians 5:9, this “tearful letter” has not survived, unless, as some think, part of it is preserved in 2 Corinthians 10–13. He sent it with Titus.

Then he waited. At Troas, where a door stood open for preaching, he could not settle because Titus had not come back, and he went on to Macedonia to look for him. There, at last, Titus arrived with good news: the church had grieved, repented, disciplined the man who had wronged Paul, and longed to see him again. Paul writes from Macedonia, probably around AD 55–56, with Timothy, in relief and gladness, to complete the reconciliation, to prepare them for the collection for Jerusalem, and to confront a new danger: visiting teachers who were winning the church away from him.

One letter or several?

Anyone who reads the letter straight through notices how much its tone changes. Chapters 1 to 9 are warm and full of comfort; chapters 10 to 13 are sharp, ironic and defensive. Chapters 8 and 9 both take up the collection, as if from the beginning. And 6:14–7:1 seems to interrupt an appeal that flows smoothly from 6:13 to 7:2.

Many scholars think that 2 Corinthians gathers more than one letter, or parts of letters, into a single book: perhaps chapters 10 to 13 are the tearful letter, or a later letter sent when fresh trouble broke out. Others hold that it is one letter, dictated over some time, perhaps with new news arriving before the last part was written, and that a pastor can move from relief over the many to alarm about the few. There are no manuscripts of the letter in any other form. Whatever its history, the church has always read it as one book, and its parts speak to one another: the weakness Paul describes in chapter 4 is the weakness he boasts of in chapter 12.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapters 1–7: reconciliation and the ministry of the new covenant. The God of all comfort; Paul’s changed plans and the tearful letter; a ministry of the Spirit, carried in clay pots; the hope of the resurrection body; the message of reconciliation; and joy at Titus’s news.
  • Chapters 8–9: the collection. The generosity of the poor churches of Macedonia, the grace of Christ who became poor, and the God who loves a giver who gives gladly.
  • Chapters 10–13: Paul and the “super-apostles”. A defence of his ministry against rivals, in which he is forced to boast, and boasts of his weaknesses.

The God of all comfort

Paul usually opens a letter by thanking God for its readers. Here he blesses God for comfort:

{{OWB:2CO.1.3.s1}}

— 2 Corinthians 1:3–4

The word for comfort, with its verb, comes ten times in five verses. It is not the comfort of an easy life. Paul has just been through a crisis in the province of Asia, perhaps in Ephesus, so severe that “we had given up hope even of staying alive.” He sees God’s purpose in it: “so that our confidence would rest, not on ourselves, but on God, who raises the dead.” The comfort he has received is meant to flow on to others, and that is the pattern of the whole letter: suffering and comfort, death and life, passed from Christ to the apostle and from the apostle to the church.

Some in Corinth had accused Paul of being unreliable, because he had changed his travel plans. His answer is to point to God’s faithfulness: “However many promises God has made, in him each one is ‘Yes.’” He stayed away, he explains, to spare them another painful visit. And now he urges them to forgive the man who caused the trouble, “or he may be swallowed up by grief that is more than he can bear.”

A ministry of the Spirit

The rivals in Corinth came with letters of recommendation. Paul needs none: “Our letter is you yourselves.” The Corinthians are a letter written “not in ink but by the Spirit of the living God, and not on stone tablets but on tablets of flesh, on human hearts.” That phrase recalls the promises of Jeremiah and Ezekiel, of a new covenant written on the heart, and Paul draws the contrast out: “For the written letter kills, but the Spirit gives life.”

Chapter 3 then turns to Moses, who came down from Sinai with his face shining and covered it with a veil. Paul reads the story as a picture of the old covenant’s glory, real but passing, and of the far greater glory of the new. “But the veil is lifted away whenever a person turns to the Lord.” The chapter ends with one of the great sentences of the New Testament:

{{OWB:2CO.3.18.s1}}

— 2 Corinthians 3:18

Treasure in clay pots

If that is the glory of the ministry, the minister himself hardly looks the part. Paul does not hide it. The treasure is held in common earthenware, the cheap pots of any household, so that no one will mistake whose power it is:

{{OWB:2CO.4.8.s1}}

— 2 Corinthians 4:8–10

So Paul does not give up. “Our outer self may be decaying, but our inner self is being made new day after day.” He calls his sufferings, which he will list in chapter 11, troubles that “are light and last only a moment”, outweighed by an eternal glory. And he looks beyond death. The body is a tent, to be taken down; God has prepared “a house that no human hands have made, eternal, in heaven.” Meanwhile he groans, longing to be clothed with it, and holds the Spirit “as a down payment”, the first instalment of what is to come.

Readers differ on what Paul expects between death and resurrection in chapter 5, whether to “make our home with the Lord” at death, as many take 5:8 to mean, or to wait for the resurrection body, or both in turn. All agree that his hope is not to be free of a body, but to put on a new one, “so that life may swallow up all that is mortal.”

The message of reconciliation

Why does Paul go on, through all this? “It is the love of Christ that drives us on.” One died for all, so that those who live would live for him. And the result is a whole new order of things:

{{OWB:2CO.5.17.s1}}

— 2 Corinthians 5:17

Paul calls his work “the ministry of reconciliation”. God, in Christ, was reconciling the world to himself, not holding people’s trespasses against them, and he has made Paul and his companions his ambassadors: “we plead with you: be reconciled to God.” The reason they can say so is given in one compressed sentence that has been pondered by every generation of the church:

{{OWB:2CO.5.21.s1}}

— 2 Corinthians 5:21

Christians have explained this exchange in different ways, whether “made him to be sin” means that Christ bore sin’s judgment, or that he became a sin offering, as some translate it. But its heart is plain: the sinless one was identified with sinners, so that sinners might be united with him and share God’s righteousness. And the time to receive it is not some later day: “Look: the day of salvation is here now.”

Then Paul turns the word of reconciliation on the Corinthians themselves. “Our heart has opened wide to take you in,” he says; “let your hearts open wide to us as well.” Chapter 7 tells, with obvious joy, how Titus came, and how their grief “in God’s way” had led to repentance. “Grief that follows God’s way produces repentance that leads to salvation, a repentance no one will ever regret.”

The grace of giving

Chapters 8 and 9 are the longest passage on money in the New Testament, and they are about grace from start to finish. Paul is gathering a gift from the Gentile churches for the poor believers in Jerusalem, and he wants Corinth to finish what it began a year before. The churches of Macedonia, in deep poverty, had begged to give. The model for all such giving is Christ:

{{OWB:2CO.8.9.s1}}

— 2 Corinthians 8:9

The gift should be freely given, according to what one has, not what one lacks; it aims at a kind of equality, as when Israel gathered manna in the wilderness. “God loves a giver who gives gladly.” And Paul shows his care for honesty: the money will be carried by trusted delegates chosen by the churches, so that no one can accuse him of mishandling it. He ends not with a target but with praise: “Thanks be to God for his gift, which no words can describe!”

Boasting of weakness

With chapter 10 the tone changes. Some in Corinth say of Paul, “His letters are weighty and strong, but when he is here in person he is weak, and the way he speaks is contemptible.” Teachers have arrived who are polished speakers, proud of their Jewish credentials, ready to accept the church’s money and to lord it over them. Paul calls them, with heavy irony, the “super-apostles”, and in 11:13 he calls such people false apostles. Who exactly they were, and what they taught, scholars still debate.

Paul hates to compare himself with them, but the church has forced him to it, so he plays the fool and boasts. Only his boasting is upside down. Are they Hebrews? “So am I.” Are they servants of Christ? “I am a better one”, and the proof is a list not of successes but of sufferings: five floggings of the forty lashes less one, three beatings with rods, a stoning, three shipwrecks, a night and a day adrift, dangers everywhere, hunger, cold, “my anxious care for all the churches.” He crowns the list with an escape from Damascus “in a rope basket”, which some readers see as a deliberate reversal of the Roman honour given to the first soldier over an enemy wall.

Then he tells, in the third person, of “a man in Christ” caught up fourteen years before to the third heaven, to paradise, who heard things beyond speaking. He will not boast of that. Instead he tells of what was given to keep him humble: “a thorn in the flesh”. What it was, no one knows. Suggestions have ranged from an illness or disability, perhaps of the eyes, to persecution, to opponents, to temptation. Paul asked three times for it to be taken away, and received an answer instead:

{{OWB:2CO.12.9.s1}} {{OWB:2CO.12.9.s2}}

— 2 Corinthians 12:9

“For it is when I am weak that I am strong.” That is the key to the whole letter. The power of Christ, crucified in weakness and living by the power of God, rests on those who are weak with him.

The letter ends with a warning that Paul is coming a third time, a call for the Corinthians to test themselves, and a prayer that they will be mended and made whole. Its last verse is the blessing that churches across the world still use to close their worship, naming Father, Son and Spirit together.

How to read 2 Corinthians

Read it as a letter between people who love each other and have hurt each other. Paul’s emotions run high, and his sentences sometimes run on, break off or double back, as people’s do when they are moved. Do not try to tidy them too quickly.

Watch for the paired words that keep returning: affliction and comfort, death and life, weakness and power, poverty and riches, sorrow and joy. In almost every chapter Paul holds the two together, never pretending that the first has disappeared, always insisting that the second is stronger. And notice the questions behind the letter that are still ours: what makes a leader worth following, what real spiritual power looks like, and how a broken relationship in the church can be mended.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 2 Corinthians are worth knowing about before you begin.

“A down payment.” Where older Bibles call the Spirit a “guarantee” or “earnest” (1:22, 5:5), this translation uses a plain commercial phrase, “a down payment”: the first instalment of what God will give in full, and the promise that the rest will come.

“Done away with.” In chapter 3 many Bibles say that the glory on Moses’ face was “fading”. This translation says it was “being done away with”, a glory brought to an end and not meant to last, which is how it reads the Greek verb Paul uses there and elsewhere.

“Pots made of clay” and “spread its tent”. In 4:7 the treasure is in “pots made of clay”, the everyday earthenware of the original. In 12:9, where older Bibles say the power of Christ may “rest upon” Paul, this translation keeps the picture in Paul’s verb, which is related to the word for a tent: the power of Christ may “spread its tent over me”, as God’s glory once dwelt in the tent in the wilderness.

“God’s holy people.” Those older Bibles call “saints” are here “God’s holy people”, so it is plain that Paul means every believer, as in the collection “for God’s holy people” in Jerusalem.

The last verses. Bibles number the end of the letter differently. In most English Bibles the greetings are 13:12–13 and the closing blessing is 13:14; in the Greek text, and some modern Bibles, the blessing is 13:13. This edition cites the English numbering.
