— Nehemiah 6:3

Nehemiah is the most personal book in the history of Israel. Most of it is told in the first person by a man who held one of the most trusted posts at the Persian court, the king’s cupbearer, and gave it up to go and rebuild the walls of a ruined city on the edge of the empire. We hear him pray, lose his temper, lie awake planning, and talk back to his enemies. We even hear what he says to God under his breath. No other book of the Old Testament lets us so close to the mind of the man who wrote it.

His name, Nechemyah in Hebrew, means “The LORD comforts”. The book is the story of how that comfort came to a people in disgrace: not in a vision or a miracle, but through prayer, hard work, good organisation, courage under threat and the public reading of God’s word.

The sequel to Ezra

In the Hebrew Bible, Ezra and Nehemiah were a single book, and Nehemiah carries on where Ezra leaves off. The temple had been rebuilt seventy years earlier, and Ezra the scribe, on the usual dating, had come to teach the law some thirteen years before. But Jerusalem itself was still a ruin, its walls broken and its gates burned, and a city without walls in the ancient world was a city without safety, without dignity and, in practice, without many inhabitants.

The book opens in the month of Kislev, in the twentieth year of King Artaxerxes I, around 445 BC. Nehemiah served as governor of Judah for twelve years (5:14), went back to the king, and later returned for a second term (13:6–7). One of his enemies is known from outside the Bible too: a letter found among papyri from the Jewish community at Elephantine in Egypt, written in 407 BC, mentions the sons of Sanballat, governor of Samaria.

The book falls into four parts:

  • The wall rebuilt (chapters 1–7): Nehemiah’s prayer, his request to the king, the building of the wall in fifty-two days in the face of mockery, threats and plots, and the list of the first returning families.
  • The people renewed (chapters 8–10): Ezra reads the law aloud, the people keep the Festival of Booths, confess their sins in a great prayer, and bind themselves by written agreement to keep God’s law.
  • The city filled and dedicated (chapters 11–12): one family in ten moves into Jerusalem, and two great choirs process around the top of the wall in thanksgiving.
  • The reforms of a second term (chapter 13): Nehemiah returns from the king to find the people’s promises already broken, and sets about mending them.

A man who prayed

The book begins with bad news. Nehemiah’s brother Hanani arrives in Susa from Judah, and Nehemiah asks how things are. The answer is grim: “The wall of Jerusalem is broken down, and its gates have been burned.” Nehemiah’s response is the first thing we learn about him: “When I heard these words, I sat down and wept.” For some days he mourns and fasts and prays, and the prayer he records is a model of how to pray in a crisis. It begins with who God is:

— Nehemiah 1:5

Then it confesses sin, including Nehemiah’s own: “I and my father’s family have sinned.” Then it reminds God of his promise through Moses, that if his scattered people returned to him he would gather them again. Only at the end does it ask for something specific: “Give your servant success today, and grant him mercy in the sight of this man.” And then the book adds one dry line, which explains what “this man” is: “Now I was cupbearer to the king.”

Four months later the moment comes. Nehemiah is serving wine, and the king notices his face: “This can only be sadness of heart.” Nehemiah tells us, “Then I was terribly afraid.” A servant who brought gloom into the king’s presence was taking a risk. But when the king asks, “What is it you want?”, there follows a prayer so brief that the book records only that it happened:

{{OWB:NEH.2.4.s1}} {{OWB:NEH.2.5.s1}}

— Nehemiah 2:4–5

Between the king’s question and his own answer, Nehemiah prays. Months of fasting have prepared him for a moment that lasts a few seconds, and he has his answer ready: letters for safe passage, timber from the king’s forest, and a date for his return. “And the king granted me these things, because the good hand of my God was on me.” Prayer and planning are never rivals in this book.

A ride by night

When Nehemiah reaches Jerusalem, he tells no one why he has come. After three days he rides out by night with a few men to inspect the walls in the dark, until the rubble is so thick that there is “no room for the animal I was riding to get through.” Only when he knows exactly what the task is does he call the people together:

{{OWB:NEH.2.17.s1}} {{OWB:NEH.2.18.s1}}

— Nehemiah 2:17–18

Chapter 3 lists who built which section, gate by gate around the city. Like other lists in Scripture it rewards a slow reading. The high priest and his fellow priests begin at the Sheep Gate. Goldsmiths and perfume makers take their turn with the stones. Shallum, an official over half of Jerusalem, builds “he and his daughters.” Priests repair the wall “each one opposite his own house.” And one group is remembered for the wrong reason: the men of Tekoa worked, “but their nobles would not bow their necks to the work of their masters.” Every name in the list is a person who turned up.

Mockery, threats and a sword at the side

Opposition comes from Sanballat the Horonite, Tobiah the Ammonite official and Geshem the Arab, the powers in the neighbouring provinces, who did not want a strong Jerusalem. It begins with mockery. “What are these feeble Jews doing?” Sanballat asks, and Tobiah adds that if a fox climbed on their wall, it would fall down. Here he does not answer them. He prays:

{{OWB:NEH.3.36.s1}} {{OWB:NEH.3.37.s1}}

— Nehemiah 4:4–5

It is a hard prayer, in the manner of some of the Psalms, but notice what Nehemiah does with his anger: he hands it to God and goes back to the work. “So we built the wall, and the whole wall was joined together up to half its height, for the people had a mind to work.”

Then come threats of an armed attack, and with them exhaustion and fear among the builders themselves. Nehemiah’s answer joins faith and good sense in a single sentence: “But we prayed to our God and posted a guard day and night.” He arms the people by families, and tells them, “Do not be afraid of them. Remember the Lord, who is great and awesome”. From then on, half the men stand guard while half build, and the carriers, the book says, “did the work with one hand and held a weapon in the other.” The man with the ram’s horn stays at Nehemiah’s side, because wherever it sounds, “Our God will fight for us.”

The enemy within

Chapter 5 is a surprise. In the middle of the building, the threat comes not from outside but from among the Jews themselves. In a time of famine and heavy taxes, poor families have mortgaged their fields and sold their children into slavery to wealthier neighbours: “Our bodies are the same as those of our fellow Jews, and our children are just like theirs.”

Nehemiah is furious, and says so. He calls a great assembly and confronts the nobles: “What you are doing is not right.” They agree to return the land and stop charging interest. Then he tells us how he himself lived as governor: for twelve years he refused the food allowance that was his by right, “because I feared God”, and fed a hundred and fifty people at his own table. A wall built by a people who devour one another would not be worth building. Nehemiah closes the chapter with the first of his short, characteristic prayers: “Remember me, my God, for good, for all that I have done for this people.”

Plots, and a wall finished

When the wall is almost done, the enemies change tactics. They invite Nehemiah to a meeting in the plain of Ono, far from the city, and he answers with the sentence that opens this introduction. They send an open letter accusing him of plotting rebellion, and he replies, “You are making it all up in your own mind,” and prays, “But now, O God, strengthen my hands.” A hired prophet urges him to hide in the temple, and he refuses: “Should a man like me run away?” He was not a priest, and to go into the sanctuary would have been both cowardice and sin.

The book of the law

The wall is not the climax of the book. In chapter 8, the people gather in the square by the Water Gate and ask Ezra to bring out the book of the law of Moses. He reads it from early morning until midday, standing on a wooden platform, while the Levites move among the people helping them understand:

{{OWB:NEH.8.8.s1}}

— Nehemiah 8:8

The people weep as they hear it, because they see how far they have fallen short. But this is a festival day, and Nehemiah, Ezra and the Levites tell them to stop mourning:

{{OWB:NEH.8.10.s1}}

— Nehemiah 8:10

The next day the leaders study further and find the command to live in booths at the festival of the seventh month. The whole city goes out to cut branches, and booths go up on roofs and in courtyards and squares. The book notes that nothing like it had been done since the days of Joshua. “And there was very great joy.”

A prayer that tells the whole story

Later that month, the people gather again in sackcloth to confess their sins, and the Levites lead a long prayer (9:5–37) that retells the history of Israel from creation to the present: the call of Abraham, the exodus, Sinai, the wilderness, the land, and generation after generation of rebellion and rescue. Its refrain is God’s patience, in words that echo his own description of himself to Moses:

{{OWB:NEH.9.17.s1}}

— Nehemiah 9:17

The prayer ends not with a happy resolution but with the truth of their present: “See, we are slaves today, slaves in the very land you gave our ancestors.” Then the people put their promises in writing and seal them. They will not intermarry with the peoples of the land, will keep the Sabbath, and will support the temple with tithes and offerings: “We will not neglect the house of our God.”

Choirs on the wall

Jerusalem is still nearly empty, so the people cast lots for one family in ten to move into the city, and “The people blessed all the men who volunteered to live in Jerusalem.” Then comes the dedication of the wall. Nehemiah sets two great choirs on top of it and sends them in procession in opposite directions, one led by Ezra, until they meet at the house of God. The sound recalls the day the temple foundation was laid in Ezra 3, but this time there is no weeping: “the joy of Jerusalem could be heard far away.”

The last chapter

If the book ended there, it would be a triumph. It does not. In chapter 13 Nehemiah returns from a visit to the king to find that a priest named Eliashib has given Tobiah, the old enemy, a room in the temple courts. The Levites have not been paid and have gone back to their farms. Traders are selling in Jerusalem on the Sabbath. Jews have married women of Ashdod, Ammon and Moab, and half their children cannot speak the language of Judah. The chief promises the people made in chapter 10 have all been broken.

Nehemiah does not take it calmly. He throws Tobiah’s furniture out of the room, shuts the city gates on the Sabbath and threatens the traders who camp outside, and confronts the men who had married foreign wives. In his own words, “I contended with them and cursed them, and I struck some of their men and pulled out their hair.” He reminds them of Solomon: “yet even he was made to sin by foreign wives.” Readers have often been uneasy with this side of Nehemiah, and the book does not ask us to copy his temper. It does ask us to see his grief that the people God had brought home were already drifting away. And its last words are a prayer: “Remember me, my God, for good.”

How to read Nehemiah

Nehemiah is thirteen chapters, and it reads quickly, because so much of it is a man telling his own story. The long lists of names (chapters 3, 7, 10, 11 and 12) can be read more lightly, but do not skip them altogether: they are full of ordinary people who built, signed, moved and sang.

Watch for Nehemiah’s short prayers, scattered through the narrative, often with no warning (2:4; 4:4–5; 5:19; 6:9, 14; 13:14, 22, 29, 31). They show a life lived in constant conversation with God. Watch, too, for the pairing of prayer and action: he prays and posts a guard, prays and asks the king for timber, prays and finishes the wall.

Read chapters 8–10 slowly. They are the heart of the book, and among the most important chapters in the Old Testament for understanding how God’s people after the exile became a people of the book. The pattern they show, God’s word read aloud and explained, followed by tears, joy, confession and commitment, has shaped the worship of synagogue and church ever since.

Nehemiah’s own memoir is generally accepted as the core of the book; it is not certain who joined it to the account of Ezra and the lists, or when, though many think it was done not long after Nehemiah’s time.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices are worth knowing before you begin.

“The joy of the LORD is your stronghold.” Older Bibles render Nehemiah 8:10 “the joy of the LORD is your strength”. The Hebrew word means a place of refuge, a fortress, and this translation renders it so. The picture is of a people sheltered by joy in God’s goodness on the very day the law has made them grieve.

“Faithful love.” The Hebrew hesed, God’s love that keeps faith with his covenant, appears in the opening prayer, where God “keeps the covenant and faithful love with those who love him”, again in the great prayer of chapter 9, and in Nehemiah’s last request to be spared “according to the greatness of your faithful love.”

“The LORD” and “the God of heaven.” God’s personal name is written “the LORD”, following long custom. Nehemiah, who lived at the Persian court, often prays to “the God of heaven”, the title used of Israel’s God in the Persian decrees.

Weights and measures are given in modern equivalents, so the governors before Nehemiah took “about 1 pound of silver” from the people.
