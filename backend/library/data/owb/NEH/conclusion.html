— Nehemiah 13:30–31

Nehemiah ends as it began, with a man praying. But the mood has changed. The book that opened with tears for a broken wall closes with a governor tidying up after his people’s broken promises, and asking God, one last time, to remember him. In time, if not in the order of our Bibles, these are the last events in the Old Testament’s history. Its history does not end in triumph. It ends with a prayer.

The question the book leaves with you

Nehemiah did everything a leader could do. He prayed and planned, built the wall in fifty-two days, defended it, made the rich give back what they had taken from the poor, gathered the people to hear God’s word, led them in confession, and had them sign a covenant to keep it. The city was dedicated with such joy that it “could be heard far away.” And within a few years the temple storeroom held Tobiah’s furniture, the Levites had gone back to their fields, the Sabbath was a market day, and the old marriages were being made again.

That is the question Nehemiah leaves with every reader: what does it take to change a people? Walls can keep enemies out. They cannot keep hearts in. Good laws, clear teaching and solemn promises are gifts of God, and the book honours all of them. But the last chapter shows, with painful honesty, that they are not enough by themselves. The people needed something done in them that no governor could do.

Nehemiah’s repeated prayer, “Remember me, my God”, points the same way. He does not ask to be remembered for his success, which he knows is fragile. He asks God to keep faith with him, and, in one of those prayers, to spare him “according to the greatness of your faithful love.” At the end, even Nehemiah rests on mercy.

Where the story goes next

The prophet Malachi, who spoke to much the same community, rebukes many of the same sins: neglected tithes, careless priests, broken marriages. And he looks forward to the day when the Lord himself will come to his temple. The prophets before him had already said what that coming would bring. Through Jeremiah the LORD promised a new covenant:

“I will set my instruction deep inside them and write it on their hearts. I will be their God, and they will be my people.”

— Jeremiah 31:33

Ezra read the law to the people from a wooden platform. The new covenant writes it within them. The New Testament says that this is what Christ came to do, by his death and by the gift of his Spirit.

And the city Nehemiah rebuilt pointed beyond itself. The letter to the Hebrews says that Abraham “was waiting for the city with firm foundations, a city planned and built by God,” and tells its readers: “There is no city here that remains for us; the city we are looking for is the one still to come.” At the end of the Bible, John sees that city, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven. Nehemiah ordered his gates shut at nightfall to keep the city safe. Of the new Jerusalem it is said, “Its gates will never be shut in the daytime, and there will be no night there.”

Where to go from here

Nehemiah has long been a favourite of preachers on prayer, leadership and the joy of God’s word. Some of the best of that work is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On Nehemiah’s prayers (Nehemiah 1–2). The chapter “Arrow Prayers and Long Fasts” in The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original, on the months of fasting in Susa and the moment of prayer before the king; and E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, whose chapter “Nehemiah, the Praying Builder” dwells on Nehemiah weeping, fasting and praying at the news from Jerusalem.
  • On the joy of the LORD (Nehemiah 8:10). Two sermons on the same verse: Alexander Maclaren’s The Joy of the Lord and A. B. Simpson’s The Joy of the Lord.
  • On faithful service. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Nehemiah: Faithful Service” reads the rebuilding of the walls as a picture of the believer’s work for God; and her chapter on Ezra, “Ezra: Restoration from Backsliding”, which treats the two books together.
  • On Ezra, who stands beside Nehemiah. Bounds’s chapter “Ezra, the Praying Reformer”, in the same book, on Ezra’s prayer of confession (Ezra 9–10); and Maclaren’s sermon Heroic Faith (Ezra 8:22–23).
  • On the whole story of the return. The Big Story, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “Coming Home” tells the story of Ezra 1, Nehemiah 8 and Esther 4 together.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Nehemiah in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
