— Habakkuk 2:4

Most prophets speak to the people for God. Habakkuk spends most of his book speaking to God, and much of the time he is arguing. His three chapters are a conversation: two complaints, two answers, and a prayer. They begin with a cry that God is not listening and end with a song of joy in a ruined field. Between the two lies one of the most quoted sentences in the Old Testament.

The time

Habakkuk gives no king’s name and no date, but he tells us enough. God says, “For I am raising up the Chaldeans”, the people of southern Mesopotamia whose kings now ruled Babylon. Babylon threw off Assyrian rule in 626 BC, helped destroy Nineveh in 612, and defeated Egypt at Carchemish in 605, becoming master of the region. Within twenty years its armies had destroyed Jerusalem. Habakkuk most likely prophesied in the last decades of Judah’s independence, perhaps in the reign of Jehoiakim (609–598 BC), when Babylon was rising and Judah’s own life was full of violence and injustice. Of the prophet himself we know nothing but his name, which has no certain meaning, and his calling: “Habakkuk the prophet”.

The shape of the book

  • 1:2–4: the first complaint. Why does God let violence and injustice go on in Judah?
  • 1:5–11: the first answer. He is raising up the Babylonians to judge it.
  • 1:12–17: the second complaint. How can a holy God use a nation more wicked than the one it punishes?
  • 2:1–20: the second answer. The vision will come in its time; the righteous will live by their faithfulness; and five woes fall on the proud.
  • Chapter 3: the prayer. A psalm of the God who comes to save, ending in trust.

“How long?”

The book opens with a question many people of faith have asked, and Habakkuk asks it without softening it:

{{OWB:HAB.1.2.s1}}

— Habakkuk 1:2

He sees the instruction of God losing its force, the wicked hemming in the righteous, and “justice comes out twisted”. God’s answer is not what he expects. Something is coming that “you would not believe even if you were told”: the Chaldeans, swifter than leopards and fiercer than wolves at dusk, men “whose own strength is their god”. Centuries later Paul quoted this warning in the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, so that his hearers would not be the scoffers it describes (Acts 13:41).

The answer only sharpens the problem. If Judah is bad, Babylon is worse. Habakkuk appeals to what he knows of God:

{{OWB:HAB.1.13.s1}}

— Habakkuk 1:13

He pictures the Babylonians as fishermen hauling nations up in nets and then burning incense to the net. Will it go on for ever? Then he does something worth noticing. He does not walk away. He says, “I will stand at my watchpost”, and waits for an answer.

The vision waits

God’s reply begins with an instruction to write:

{{OWB:HAB.2.2.s1}} {{OWB:HAB.2.3.s1}}

— Habakkuk 2:2–3

Then comes the heart of the book, the verse printed at the head of this introduction. The proud one, swollen with his own desires, stands on one side; on the other, “the righteous will live by their faithfulness”. The Hebrew word means firmness and steadiness, a loyal trust in God that holds on and shows itself in a faithful life. It is what Habakkuk himself is doing on the rampart.

The New Testament quotes the line three times, from its Greek form, as “The one who is righteous will live by faith” (Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11; compare Hebrews 10:38). For Paul it shows that people are put right with God by trusting him, not by keeping the law. Reading Romans 1:17 in a monastery in Wittenberg, Martin Luther came to see the righteousness of God as a gift received by faith, a discovery that lay at the heart of the Reformation. The Hebrew and the Greek put the weight in slightly different places, one on faithfulness, one on faith, but they describe one life: the person who trusts God and keeps trusting him while the vision waits.

Five woes

The rest of chapter 2 is a taunt song that the nations will one day sing over Babylon. Five times it says “Woe”: to the one who piles up what is not his own, who builds his house high on wicked profit, who builds a city with bloodshed, who makes his neighbors drunk to shame them, and who prays to an idol of wood and stone. Even the building cries out against him: “The very stones will cry out from the wall”.

Twice the song lifts its eyes beyond Babylon. In the middle it promises:

{{OWB:HAB.2.14.s1}}

— Habakkuk 2:14

And at the end, after the idol that has “no breath in it at all”, it turns to the living God: “But the LORD is in his holy temple; all the earth, be hushed before him.”

The prayer

Chapter 3 is a psalm, with the marks of one: a heading, the word Selah three times, and a note at the end for the music director. Habakkuk prays, “in wrath, remember mercy”, and then sees God coming from the south, from Teman and Mount Paran, as he came at Sinai, shaking the earth, splitting it with rivers, riding out “to save your people”. The vision leaves him trembling. But it also settles him: “Yet I will wait quietly for the day of trouble”. The prayer ends with the most famous lines in the book, a joy that does not depend on the harvest.

How to read Habakkuk

Read it as a conversation, and notice who is speaking in each part. It is short enough for one sitting. Mark the movement from “How long?” at the start to “yet I will rejoice” at the end, and ask what has changed. The Babylonians are still coming; the fields may still fail. What has changed is the prophet, who has brought his complaint to God, waited, and been given not an explanation but a vision of God himself.

Habakkuk gives every reader permission to ask hard questions, and shows where to take them: not away from God, but to him.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Habakkuk is poetry almost throughout, and it is printed here one line per line, as the poetry it is. A few of the translation’s choices are worth knowing.

“Faithfulness.” In 2:4 the translation says “by their faithfulness”, not “by faith”, because the Hebrew word means steady loyalty. Where the New Testament quotes the verse, it is translated from the Greek, whose word means faith or trust.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation writes “the LORD”; where the Hebrew joins it with the word for master, “the Lord GOD”, as in the last verse: “The Lord GOD is my strength”.

Words of worship. The musical terms of chapter 3, “shigionoth” and “Selah”, are left untranslated, as their exact meaning is no longer known.
