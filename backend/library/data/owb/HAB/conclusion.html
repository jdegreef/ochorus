— Habakkuk 3:17–19

Habakkuk ends where farmers in Judah feared most to be: a fig tree with no blossom, empty vines, failed olives, bare fields, an empty sheep pen. It is a picture of what the Babylonian invasion would leave behind. And in the middle of it the prophet sings.

The question the book leaves with you

Habakkuk never gets the explanation he asked for. He is not told why the wicked prosper for a time, or why God chose such a crooked instrument to judge his people. He is given a promise that the vision will come at its appointed time, a sentence about how the righteous live while they wait, and a sight of God himself. That turns out to be enough.

So the question the book leaves is not whether we can understand what God is doing, but whether we will trust him when we cannot. “Though it lingers, wait for it”. The book’s answer is the word “yet”: though everything fails, “yet I will rejoice in the LORD”. That is not a mood. It is a decision, made on the rampart, by a man whose legs were still shaking.

Where it points

The New Testament takes Habakkuk’s central line and builds on it. Paul writes that in the gospel the righteousness of God is revealed, “As it is written, ‘The one who is righteous will live by faith.’” (Romans 1:17), and Hebrews urges believers who are tempted to give up not to draw back, because “the one who is coming will arrive and will not be slow in coming” (Hebrews 10:37). The vision Habakkuk waited for has a face. And the hope of 2:14, an earth full of the knowledge of the LORD’s glory, is the hope of the whole Bible.

Where to go from here

A few places in this library to take Habakkuk further:

  • On joy when the fields fail (3:17–19). The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “Though the Fig Tree Does Not Flower” walks through the whole book; and Hannah Whitall Smith’s The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life, whose chapter “A Lesson in the Interior Life” is built around Habakkuk’s song (3:17–19).
  • On asking God why (1:2–4, 1:12–17). The Key Teachings of Elisabeth Elliot, an Ochorus Original, and its chapter “On Asking God Why”.
  • On “in wrath, remember mercy” (3:2). Charles G. Finney’s sermon What a Revival of Religion Is, on Habakkuk 3:2, and Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s The Cry for Revival (Psalm 85:6).
  • On living by faith (2:4). The Key Teachings of Martin Luther, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “The Righteousness of God” tells how Romans 1:17 changed Luther’s life; John Calvin’s Commentary on Romans, on Romans 1:17; and John Wesley’s sermon Salvation by Faith (Ephesians 2:8).
  • Two evening and morning readings. Charles H. Spurgeon’s Evening by Evening, September 10, on “Evening wolves” (Habakkuk 1:8), and his Morning by Morning, December 12, on “His ways are everlasting” (Habakkuk 3:6).
  • On Habakkuk and Christ. Augustine’s The City of God, Book XVIII, chapters 31 and 32, on Habakkuk’s vision and on his prayer and song.

And read Romans and Galatians in this same edition, where Habakkuk’s sentence becomes the hinge of Paul’s gospel.
