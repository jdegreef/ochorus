Judges is the darkest book in the history of Israel, and one of the most honest books in the Bible. It begins where Joshua left off, with the land given and much of it still to be taken, and a people who have just promised, three times over, to serve the LORD. It ends, twenty-one chapters later, with a civil war, a tribe almost wiped out, and a sentence that sums up everything in between: “In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what they thought was right.”

Between those two ends are some of the most vivid stories ever written: a left-handed assassin, a woman with a tent peg, a farmer threshing wheat in a winepress, three hundred men with torches and jars, a strongman with seven braids. Children love these stories, and adults are often unsettled by them. Both reactions are right. Judges is a book about the mercy of God and the downward pull of the human heart, and it does not soften either.

Who were the judges?

The Hebrew name of the book, Shofetim, means “judges”, but the word means more than the English suggests. A few of these leaders settled disputes. Deborah held court under a palm tree, and the Israelites “came up to her to have their disputes decided.” But most of them are judges in an older sense: leaders raised up by God to set things right, to rescue his people from their oppressors and to govern them afterward. The book’s own summary makes that clear: “the LORD raised up judges, who rescued them from the power of those who plundered them.” The word it uses again and again for what they do is not “judge” but “save”.

There are twelve of them. Six are given fuller accounts (the “major” judges): Othniel, Ehud, Deborah with Barak, Gideon, Jephthah and Samson. Six more are named only briefly: Shamgar, Tola, Jair, Ibzan, Elon and Abdon. None of them is a king. They come from different tribes, at different times, often ruling only part of the land, and when each one dies, there is no one to take their place.

The shape of the book

  • Two openings (1:1–3:6). The first tells, tribe by tribe, how Israel failed to drive out the Canaanites. The second steps back to explain the pattern that will run through the whole book.
  • The stories of the judges (3:7–16:31). Twelve deliverers, from Othniel, the model judge, to Samson, the most gifted and the most wayward.
  • Two closing stories (chapters 17–21). A household shrine stolen by a whole tribe, and a crime in Gibeah that leads to civil war. Neither has a judge in it. Both are framed by the refrain “In those days there was no king in Israel.”

The two openings and the two endings mirror each other. At the start the tribes fight Canaanites together; at the end they fight one another. At the start a judge from Judah, Othniel, wins a wife by courage; at the end the tribes of Israel get wives for Benjamin by kidnapping. The book is not just a list of episodes. It is the story of a slide.

The cycle

The second opening, in chapter 2, explains it all in advance. It begins with one of the saddest sentences in the Bible:

— Judges 2:10

Then it describes a pattern that the stories will repeat. The Israelites do what is evil in the sight of the LORD and serve the Baals. The LORD hands them over to their enemies. They cry out to him. He raises up a judge who rescues them, and the land has peace. Then the judge dies, and the people turn back to other gods, and it begins again.

Readers often call it a cycle, but the book itself says it is worse than that. When each judge died, the people “would turn back and behave worse than their ancestors.” It is a spiral, and it winds downward. The early judges are men and women of faith; the later ones are increasingly compromised; and by the last chapters the people of God are doing things the Canaanites were judged for.

What does not change, through all of it, is God. The verse at the head of this introduction gives the reason he keeps raising up deliverers: “the LORD was moved to pity when they groaned.” In chapter 10, after Israel has abandoned him yet again, he tells them to cry to the gods they chose for themselves. They put away those gods, and then comes one of the most tender lines in the Old Testament: “he could bear Israel’s misery no longer.” The mercy of God in Judges is not earned. It is the only reason the book does not end sooner.

Othniel and Ehud

The first judge is Othniel, Caleb’s younger brother (or, as some read it, his nephew), and his story is told in five verses, as a pattern for the rest: Israel sins, cries out, and “the LORD raised up a deliverer”. “The Spirit of the LORD came on him, and he judged Israel.” He wins, and “the land had peace for forty years.” There is nothing in his story to regret.

Then comes Ehud, and the book changes tone. He is from Benjamin, a name that means “son of the right hand”, and he is left-handed, which lets him carry a sword on his right thigh past the guards of Eglon, the very fat king of Moab. The story is told with dark humour, down to the servants waiting outside the locked door, thinking the king is relieving himself. God uses an unlikely man and an unexpected means, and the land has rest for eighty years.

Deborah, Barak and Jael

Chapters 4 and 5 tell the same victory twice, first in prose and then in one of the oldest poems in the Bible. Israel is oppressed by Jabin, king of Canaan, whose general Sisera has nine hundred iron chariots. Deborah, “a prophet” who “was judging Israel at that time”, summons Barak to lead the army. He will go only if she goes with him. She agrees, but tells him that “the LORD will hand Sisera over to a woman.”

The battle is won when the LORD throws Sisera’s army into panic, and the song tells how even the weather fought for Israel: “the stars, along their courses, made war on Sisera.” The woman who ends the war is not Deborah but Jael, the wife of a Kenite, who welcomes the fleeing Sisera into her tent and kills him as he sleeps. The song calls her “blessed above all women”, and then, with startling sympathy, imagines Sisera’s mother at her window, waiting for a son who will never come home.

Deborah is the only judge in the book described as a prophet, and the only one shown deciding cases. Christians read her leadership in different ways: some see in her a precedent for women leading God’s people, others an exception the book itself marks as unusual in a time of weak men. All can agree on how the song remembers her: “a mother in Israel.”

Gideon

Gideon is introduced hiding. He is threshing wheat in a winepress, down out of sight, so that the Midianite raiders will not take it. And the angel of the LORD greets him with words that sound almost like a joke: “The LORD is with you, mighty warrior.” Gideon’s answer is the honest question of every generation that has heard of God’s wonders but not seen them:

— Judges 6:13

God does not answer the question. He gives a commission: “Go in this strength of yours… Am I not the one sending you?” And when Gideon protests that his clan is the weakest and he is the least in his family, God gives the only answer that matters: “I will be with you.” Gideon builds an altar and names it “The LORD Is Peace.”

The rest of the story is about God making sure the victory will be his. Gideon asks for signs, twice, with a fleece, and God patiently gives them. Then God reduces the army from thirty-two thousand to three hundred, so that Israel could never say, “Our own strength has saved us.” At night the three hundred surround the Midianite camp, break their jars, raise their torches, blow their horns and shout, “A sword for the LORD and for Gideon!” The enemy flees in confusion.

Afterwards the people ask Gideon to found a dynasty. His answer is the right one: “I will not rule over you, and my son will not rule over you. The LORD will rule over you.” But then he makes a golden ephod from the plunder, and “all Israel prostituted themselves by worshiping it, and it became a trap for Gideon and his family.” And he names one of his sons Abimelech, “my father is king”.

Abimelech and Jotham’s fable

Abimelech takes the name at its word. He murders seventy of his brothers on a single stone and has himself made king at Shechem. Only the youngest, Jotham, escapes, and from a mountaintop he tells the first fable in the Bible: the trees went looking for a king, and the olive, the fig and the vine all declined, too busy bearing fruit. Only the bramble accepted, offering the cedars its shade, or fire. Abimelech’s three-year reign ends with a woman dropping a millstone on his head. Israel’s first attempt at kingship is a bramble.

Jephthah

Jephthah is the son of a prostitute, driven out by his half-brothers, who becomes the leader of a band of outlaws. When the Ammonites attack, the elders of Gilead come begging. He is a good negotiator and a brave fighter, and “the Spirit of the LORD came on Jephthah.” But before the battle he makes a vow no one asked him to make: whatever comes out of his house to meet him when he returns in victory, he will offer as a burnt offering. The one who comes out, dancing with tambourines, is his only child, his daughter.

Readers have argued for centuries about what happened next. The text says that “he did to her what he had vowed”, and many, ancient and modern, take this to mean that he sacrificed her, in a terrible imitation of the Canaanite religion all around him. Others, noting that the passage dwells on her never marrying, have read it as a lifelong dedication to the LORD’s service. Either way, the story is a tragedy, and the book offers no word of approval. The daughter is remembered: “Every year the young women of Israel would go out for four days to commemorate the daughter of Jephthah the Gileadite.”

Samson

The last judge has the most remarkable beginning. An angel announces his birth to a childless woman, in a pattern that runs from Sarah to Elizabeth, the mother of John the Baptist. He is to be a Nazirite from the womb, set apart to God, with no razor ever touching his head, and he “will begin to save Israel from the power of the Philistines.” When his father asks the angel’s name, the answer is, “Why do you ask about my name? It is wonderful.”

And yet Samson spends his life breaking almost everything he was set apart for. He goes after Philistine women, eats honey from a dead lion, makes riddles and loses his temper. The Spirit of the LORD rushes on him again and again, and he tears a lion apart with his bare hands and kills a thousand men with the jawbone of a donkey. But he never leads Israel into battle, and he never seems to pray except for himself. Finally Delilah coaxes the secret of his strength from him, his hair is cut, and the saddest words in his story follow: “But he did not know that the LORD had left him.”

Blind, shackled, grinding grain in a Gaza prison, Samson prays once more:

— Judges 16:28

He pulls down the temple of Dagon, “he killed more people by his death than he had killed during his whole life.” It is a strange, mixed ending, part revenge and part faith. Samson only began to save Israel, as the angel said. The rescue would have to be finished by someone else.

The Spirit of the LORD

Judges speaks often of the Spirit of the LORD. The Spirit comes on Othniel and Jephthah; clothes Gideon like a garment; rushes on Samson. It is clear that God’s Spirit empowered these deliverers to do what they could not have done themselves. It is equally clear that the gift was not a reward for godliness, and that it did not make them godly. Gideon still made an idol, Jephthah still made his vow, Samson still went to Gaza. The book is a lasting warning not to mistake power for holiness.

When there was no king

The last five chapters have no judges at all. A man named Micah sets up a private shrine with an idol and hires a wandering Levite as his priest, saying, “Now I know that the LORD will treat me well, because I have a Levite as my priest.” Then the tribe of Dan, looking for land, steals the idol and the priest, massacres a peaceful town, and sets up their own sanctuary.

The last story is one of the most terrible in the Old Testament. A Levite’s concubine is abused to death by men of Gibeah, a town of Benjamin, in a scene that deliberately echoes Sodom. The Levite cuts up her body and sends it to the tribes. Israel goes to war against Benjamin and nearly destroys it, then, to save the tribe, kills the men of another town and seizes women for wives. The narrator offers no comment except the refrain. The horror is the comment.

The refrain points forward. Israel needs a king, a good one. The closing stories are full of names that will matter in the next book: a Levite from Bethlehem, a woman from Bethlehem, the town of Gibeah. Bethlehem will be David’s town; Gibeah will be the home of Saul.

How to read Judges

Read the book straight through, if you can, in two or three sittings, so that the slide is felt. Watch for the repeated phrases: “did what was evil in the sight of the LORD”, “cried out to the LORD”, “the Spirit of the LORD”, “the land had peace”. Notice that the refrain about peace disappears after Gideon. Notice who prays, and who does not.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition assigned it to Samuel; many scholars see it as part of a longer history from Deuteronomy to Kings, completed later, perhaps in the time of the exile. Its stories cover perhaps three centuries between Joshua and Saul, though the dates of the judges, some of whom may have overlapped in different regions, are much debated.

Above all, do not read Judges as a gallery of heroes to copy. The letter to the Hebrews does name Gideon, Barak, Samson and Jephthah among those who acted through faith, and it is right to: each, at some moment, trusted God. But the book is not about their virtue. It is about a God who kept rescuing a people who kept forgetting him, and who was preparing, through all their failure, to give them a better deliverer.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Judges are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”. Where it uses the ordinary word for God, it writes “God”.

“A deliverer.” The Hebrew word for one who saves is translated “deliverer”, as when “the LORD raised up a deliverer for them” in Othniel’s and Ehud’s day.

The Spirit’s coming. The translation keeps the different verbs the Hebrew uses: the Spirit “came on” Othniel, “clothed” Gideon, and “rushed on” Samson.

“What they thought was right.” The book’s refrain, familiar from older Bibles as “every man did that which was right in his own eyes”, is rendered “everyone did what they thought was right.”

Poetry. The Song of Deborah in chapter 5 is printed one line per line, as the poetry it is.
