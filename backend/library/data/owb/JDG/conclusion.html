Judges ends with the same words it has been repeating for five chapters. There is no final victory, no last judge, no word of comfort from the narrator. The tribes go home, “each man to his own tribe and clan”, from a war in which they nearly destroyed one of their own. And the book closes on a diagnosis.

The question the book leaves with you

The diagnosis has two halves, and both matter. “There was no king in Israel”: the people needed someone to lead them, to hold them together, to keep them faithful to the covenant. And “everyone did what they thought was right”: the deeper trouble was not the lack of a government but the human heart left to itself.

That is the question Judges leaves with every reader: what will it take to rescue people who keep needing to be rescued? Again and again God raised up a deliverer, and again and again the rescue lasted only as long as the deliverer lived. Gideon refused a crown and then made an idol. Samson only began to save Israel. Every judge died, and every death began the slide again. The book has shown, with painful honesty, that a better leader is necessary. It has also shown that a better leader would not be enough, unless he could change the heart.

Where the story goes next

The next book in the English Bible, Ruth, opens “In the days when the judges judged”, and shows a different side of the same dark time: an ordinary family in Bethlehem, a Moabite widow who chose Israel’s God, and a man who kept the law with kindness. It ends with the name of David. Then 1 Samuel begins with a woman praying in the sanctuary at Shiloh, and her son becomes the last of the judges and anoints the first two kings. The refrain of Judges is answered: there will be a king in Israel. And the book of Samuel will show that kings, too, can do what they think is right.

The New Testament gathers the judges into its long list of faith. “Time would run out on me,” says the letter to the Hebrews, “if I told about Gideon, Barak, Samson and Jephthah”, people who, for all their failures, “found strength when they were weak”. And the Christian church has always read the pattern of Judges as a promise. The deliverers came with the Spirit of the LORD on them, and saved, and died. The gospel announces a Deliverer on whom the Spirit rested without measure, who saved by his death, and who did not stay dead. He is the King Judges was waiting for, and he does more than rule: he gives a new heart.

Where to go from here

Judges has been read in the church as a mirror of the Christian life, its failures and its rescues, and some of the best of that reading is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Judges: The Christian’s Failures” reads the book as a warning of the failures that beset believers; and A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, whose chapter “The Holy Spirit in the Book of Judges” follows the Spirit from Othniel to Samson.
  • On the cycle (Judges 2). Two sermons by A. B. Simpson: Bochim, or the Cause of Spiritual Failure (Judges 2:5) and Sinning and Repenting (Judges 2:14–19).
  • On Gideon (Judges 6–7). Simpson’s Gideon, or the Strength of Weakness, on the call of Gideon and the three hundred, and his The Weapons of Our Warfare, on the torches, jars and horns of Judges 7.
  • On Jephthah (Judges 11). Simpson’s Jephthah, or the Faith That Leads to Faithfulness (Judges 11:30–36).
  • On Samson (Judges 13–16). E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, whose chapter “Praying Saints of the Old Testaments” dwells on Samson’s last prayer.
  • On the judges and faith. The closing chapter, “What More Shall I Say?”, of Gareth Evans’s He Holds My Tomorrows, on Hebrews 11:32–40 and the judges it names.
  • For reading with children. “Deborah and the Rainstorm”, “Gideon’s Small Army” and “Samson the Strong” in Tell Me the Story – Book 2: Out of Egypt, and “Everyone Did What Was Right in Their Own Eyes”, on Judges 2 and Ruth, in The Big Story, both Ochorus Originals.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Judges in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

And then read on, into Ruth and Samuel, where the darkness of Judges begins to give way to a promise. But first let the book’s one tender sentence stay with you.
