— 2 Samuel 7:16

2 Samuel is the story of David as king. It begins with the news of Saul’s death reaching David in exile, and it ends with David, old and chastened, buying a threshing floor outside Jerusalem and building an altar there to stop a plague. Between those two moments David reaches the height of his power, receives the greatest promise God made to any king of Israel, and then, at the very height, falls into the sins that will shadow the rest of his life.

It is a book of extraordinary honesty. Ancient kings had their victories carved in stone and their failures left out. This book tells of David’s triumphs, his generosity and his faith, and then tells, in unsparing detail, of adultery, murder, a cover-up, a prophet’s rebuke, a family torn apart by rape and revenge, a son’s rebellion and a father’s grief. It does not do this to diminish David. It does it to show what God’s grace and God’s promise look like in the life of a real man.

The shape of the book

1 and 2 Samuel were once one book, and 2 Samuel picks up exactly where the first part ends. It falls into three parts.

  • David’s rise (chapters 1–10). The lament for Saul and Jonathan, David king over Judah at Hebron, the long war with Saul’s house, David king over all Israel, the capture of Jerusalem, the ark brought into the city, God’s covenant with David, his victories, and his kindness to Jonathan’s son.
  • David’s sin and its aftermath (chapters 11–20). Bathsheba and Uriah, Nathan’s rebuke, the death of the child, the violence among David’s sons, Absalom’s rebellion and death, and David’s return to Jerusalem.
  • A closing collection (chapters 21–24). Two stories of national judgement, two lists of David’s warriors, and, at the centre, two poems: David’s song of deliverance and his last words.

The turning point comes almost exactly halfway, in chapter 11, with a sentence that is easy to read past: “But David himself stayed behind in Jerusalem.”

“How the warriors have fallen”

The book opens with a man from Saul’s camp bringing David the news of the king’s death, and Saul’s crown and armband, expecting a reward. He claims to have killed Saul himself. David has him executed for raising his hand against “the LORD’s anointed”. Then he sings a lament:

{{OWB:2SA.1.23.s1}}

— 2 Samuel 1:23

It is a remarkable song from a man Saul had spent years trying to kill. There is no bitterness in it, no word of Saul’s faults, only grief, and a refrain that has passed into proverb: “How the warriors have fallen!” For Jonathan, David’s grief is personal: “I am in anguish over you, my brother Jonathan.” The new king’s reign begins with mourning for the old one.

King over Judah, then over Israel

David does not seize the throne. He asks the LORD where to go, and is told, “To Hebron.” There the men of Judah anoint him king over their tribe, while Saul’s commander Abner makes Saul’s son Ish-bosheth king over the rest. “The war between Saul’s house and David’s house dragged on for a long time.” It is a sordid, bloody story of shifting loyalties and murders, many of them committed by David’s nephew and commander Joab, which David publicly mourns and cannot control: “these men, the sons of Zeruiah, are too harsh for me.”

At last the elders of all Israel come to Hebron and make a covenant with David, and he is anointed king over the whole nation. He takes Jerusalem, the Jebusite fortress no tribe had been able to capture, and makes it his capital, the City of David. The book sums up these years in one sentence: “David became greater and greater, for the LORD, the God of Armies, was with him.”

The ark comes to Jerusalem

David’s next act is to bring the ark of the covenant, the sign of God’s presence, into his new city. The first attempt ends in disaster. The ark is carried on a new cart, as the Philistines had once sent it, and when the oxen stumble, a man named Uzzah reaches out to steady it, and God strikes him dead. David is angry, and afraid, and leaves the ark in a nearby house for three months. It is a hard story, and readers have found it so. It teaches that the holiness of God is not a thing to be handled carelessly, even with good intentions; the law had said how the ark was to be carried, and it was not on a cart.

The second attempt is a festival. The ark is carried properly, sacrifices are offered, and the king himself leads the procession, “dancing before the LORD with all his might”. His wife Michal, Saul’s daughter, watches from a window and despises him for making a fool of himself in front of servant girls. David’s answer is the answer of a worshipper who has forgotten himself: “It was before the LORD that I danced.”

The house God builds

Chapter 7 is the heart of the book, and one of the most important chapters in the Old Testament. David, settled in his palace of cedar, is troubled that the ark of God still lives in a tent, and resolves to build God a house, a temple. The prophet Nathan approves. But that night the word of the LORD comes to Nathan with a different plan. God has never asked for a house. Instead, “the LORD will make a house for you”:

{{OWB:2SA.7.12.s1}} {{OWB:2SA.7.13.s1}}

— 2 Samuel 7:12–13

The word “house” turns in God’s hand. David wanted to build God a house of cedar; God will build David a house of descendants, a dynasty. The son who builds the temple will have God as his father: “I will be a father to him, and he will be a son to me.” When that son does wrong, he will be disciplined, but God’s faithful love will not be taken from him as it was from Saul. And the promise reaches beyond any one son: “your throne will stand firm forever.”

David goes in and sits before the LORD, overwhelmed:

{{OWB:2SA.7.18.s1}}

— 2 Samuel 7:18

This promise became the hope of Israel through all the centuries that followed. When the kings failed and the kingdom fell and the throne stood empty, the prophets went on looking for a son of David who would reign for ever. The New Testament begins with that name. Matthew’s first line calls Jesus “the son of David”; the angel tells Mary that God will give her son “the throne of David, his forefather”; and the letter to the Hebrews applies to Jesus the very words of verse 14, “I will be a Father to him, and he will be a Son to me.”

For Jonathan’s sake

Chapter 9 is a short, lovely story that shows what kind of king David meant to be. Kings of that time made sure no one from the previous dynasty survived. David asks the opposite question: “Is there anyone still left from the house of Saul, so that I can show him faithful love for Jonathan’s sake?” There is: Jonathan’s son Mephibosheth, lame in both feet since childhood, living in obscurity. David restores Saul’s land to him and gives him a place at the royal table “like one of the king’s sons.” Mephibosheth calls himself “a dead dog”; David treats him as family. Many readers have seen in the scene a picture of grace.

David and Bathsheba

Then comes chapter 11. In the spring, “at the season when kings set out on campaign”, David sends his army out and stays home. One evening, walking on the palace roof, he sees a woman bathing. She is Bathsheba, the wife of Uriah, one of his own best soldiers. David sends for her and sleeps with her, and she becomes pregnant.

What follows is a cover-up that grows worse with every step. David calls Uriah home from the front, hoping he will sleep with his wife. Uriah, more loyal than his king, refuses to go home while the ark and the army are in tents. So David sends him back to the battle carrying his own death warrant, a letter telling Joab to put Uriah where the fighting is fiercest and then fall back. Uriah dies. David marries Bathsheba. The chapter ends with one quiet line: “But the thing David had done was evil in the eyes of the LORD.”

“You are the man!”

God sends Nathan the prophet to David, and Nathan tells a story about a rich man who, to feed a guest, took the one little ewe lamb a poor man loved like a daughter. David is furious: the man deserves to die. And Nathan says four words that have rung through history: “You are the man!”

He tells David what God had given him, and what he had taken. And he announces the consequence: “the sword will never depart from your house.” David’s answer is short, and it is the difference between him and Saul:

{{OWB:2SA.12.13.s1}}

— 2 Samuel 12:13

There are no excuses, no blaming of others, no talk of what the people wanted. David is forgiven, and he still suffers what his sin set in motion. The child dies. The title of Psalm 51, the great psalm of repentance, ties it to this moment, “when the prophet Nathan came to him after he had slept with Bathsheba”. And then, quietly, the book tells of another son, Solomon, whom “The LORD loved”, and whom Nathan names Jedidiah, “loved by the LORD”.

The sword in David’s house

The next chapters show Nathan’s word coming true, and they are painful to read. David’s eldest son Amnon rapes his half-sister Tamar. David is “very angry”, but does nothing. Tamar’s brother Absalom waits two years and has Amnon murdered, then flees. After three years in exile he is brought home, through the plea of a wise woman of Tekoa who reminds the king that “God does not take away life; instead he devises ways for the banished one not to remain an outcast from him.”

But reconciliation is half-hearted, and Absalom, handsome and ambitious, sits at the city gate winning over everyone with a grievance: “In this way Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel.” Then he proclaims himself king at Hebron, and David has to flee his own city. The picture of the king leaving Jerusalem is one of the most moving in the Old Testament: “David went up the ascent of the Mount of Olives, weeping as he went.” He sends the ark back to the city rather than use it as a charm, saying, “let him deal with me as he sees fit”. When Shimei, a man of Saul’s clan, curses him and throws stones, David will not let his men kill him: “Perhaps the LORD will look on my distress and repay me with good in place of his cursing today.”

Absalom’s rebellion ends in a forest, where his head is caught in the branches of a great tree and Joab kills him, against David’s order to “deal gently with the young man Absalom.” When the news reaches David, the victory means nothing to him:

{{OWB:2SA.19.1.s1}}

— 2 Samuel 18:33

It is one of the great cries of grief in all literature. Joab has to shake the king out of it, before he loses the army that has just saved him. David returns to Jerusalem, a king again, but not the king he was.

The closing chapters

The last four chapters step outside the story, and are carefully arranged. At their centre are two poems. The first, in chapter 22, is David’s song of thanksgiving “on the day the LORD rescued him from the power of all his enemies and from the power of Saul”, almost word for word the same as Psalm 18: “The LORD is my cliff, my stronghold, the one who sets me free.” The second, in chapter 23, is introduced as “the final words of David”, a short oracle about the righteous ruler, who “is like the morning light when the sun rises, a morning without clouds”, and about God’s everlasting covenant with David’s house.

Around the poems are lists of David’s mighty warriors, including the story of three who broke through enemy lines to bring him water from the well at Bethlehem, which he would not drink but poured out to the LORD, because “Is it not the blood of men who went at the risk of their lives?”

And the book ends with David taking a census of his fighting men, which the book calls a sin and David himself confesses as one. Readers have noticed that this book says the LORD “stirred David up” to do it, while Chronicles, telling the same story, says Satan did; many have understood the two together as God allowing, for his own purposes, a temptation that David was still responsible for yielding to. A plague follows. David prays that it fall on him and not on the people, “But what have these sheep done?” The plague stops at the threshing floor of Araunah the Jebusite, and David buys it, refusing to accept it as a gift: “I will not offer the LORD my God burnt offerings at no cost to myself.” According to Chronicles, that threshing floor became the site of the temple his son would build.

How to read 2 Samuel

Read 2 Samuel with 1 Samuel, as one story, and with the Psalms open beside it, since so many of them carry David’s name and a few tie themselves to events in this book. Notice how the book is built around chapter 7, the promise, and chapter 11, the fall, and how the second half keeps echoing David’s own sin back at him: a son who takes what is not his, a household where lust leads to murder, a man who must leave the city weeping.

The book does not name its author. Chronicles mentions records of the prophets Nathan and Gad, and Jewish tradition held that they completed what Samuel began. Many scholars believe that chapters 9–20, with the opening chapters of 1 Kings, once formed a separate history of the succession to David’s throne, written close to the events by someone who knew the court well. Christians hold a range of views on how the book was composed, while receiving it as Scripture and the story of the covenant with David.

Above all, read it for its two great truths held together: that David was a sinner, whose sin had real and lasting consequences, and that God’s promise to him did not fail. The Bible calls him a man after God’s own heart not because he never fell, but because when he was confronted, he turned back to God.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 2 Samuel are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD” and “Lord GOD.” “The LORD” in capitals stands for God’s personal name. Where the Hebrew joins the word for “master” to the name, as it does again and again in David’s prayer in chapter 7, the translation writes “Lord GOD”. The title older Bibles render “the LORD of hosts” is “the LORD of Armies”.

“Faithful love” and “loyal love.” The Hebrew hesed is “faithful love” in the story of David and Mephibosheth, and “loyal love” in David’s song in chapter 22, as in the Psalms.

“How the warriors have fallen.” The refrain of David’s lament, familiar from older Bibles as “How are the mighty fallen”, is translated “How the warriors have fallen”.

Poetry. David’s lament in chapter 1, his song in chapter 22 and his last words in chapter 23 are printed one line per line, as the poetry they are.

Verse numbers. In chapter 19 the Hebrew verse numbers run one ahead of those in English Bibles, since the Hebrew begins the chapter with David’s cry for Absalom; the references in this introduction follow the English numbering.
