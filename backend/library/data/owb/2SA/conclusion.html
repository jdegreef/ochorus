2 Samuel ends at an altar. The king who had danced before the ark, sinned with Bathsheba, wept on the Mount of Olives and cried out for Absalom, stands at last on a threshing floor outside Jerusalem, having confessed one more sin, and offers sacrifices he has paid for himself. The angel’s hand is held back. The plague stops. It is a quiet ending for Israel’s greatest king: an old man praying for his people, and the mercy of God answering.

The question the book leaves with you

The book has told us two things about David, and it insists that both are true. God made him an everlasting promise: his house, his kingdom and his throne would stand firm forever. And David failed, badly, in ways that wounded his family and his nation for the rest of his life. The sword never did depart from his house.

That is the question 2 Samuel leaves with every reader: can the promises of God survive the sins of the people he makes them to? The book’s answer is yes, but not cheaply. David was forgiven the moment he said “I have sinned against the LORD”, and he still lost the child, and Amnon, and Absalom. Grace did not cancel the consequences. But neither did the consequences cancel the grace. God had said that he would discipline David’s son “with a rod such as people use”, and that his faithful love would not be taken away, and he did not take back his word.

David’s own last words hold the two together. He sings of the righteous ruler who is like “a morning without clouds”, and then asks a question about his own house, and answers it with the covenant:

— 2 Samuel 23:5

His hope at the end of his life is not in himself, or his sons, or his kingdom. It is in a covenant God made and God will keep.

Where the story goes next

The story continues without a break into 1 Kings, which opens with David old and cold in his bed, and a struggle over who will succeed him. Solomon builds the temple. But the kingdom divides after Solomon’s death, and Kings ends with Jerusalem destroyed and David’s heir an exile in Babylon. The prophets go on looking, through all of it, for a son of David who will reign in righteousness, on whom the Spirit of the LORD will rest.

The New Testament opens with that hope fulfilled. Its first verse calls Jesus “the son of David, the son of Abraham”. He is born in Bethlehem, “David’s town”. And at its very end, the risen Christ gives himself the title that gathers up the whole of 2 Samuel, the promise to David’s house and the morning without clouds:

“I am the Root and the Offspring of David, the bright morning star.”

— Revelation 22:16

Where to go from here

David’s story has been preached, prayed and sung in the church for as long as there has been a church, and some of the best of that work is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “2 Samuel and 1 Chronicles: The Kingdom Under David” reads David’s reign as a picture of the kingdom of God under the king of his own choosing.
  • On the promise to David (2 Samuel 7). Book XVII of Augustine’s The City of God, which argues that the promises Nathan brought to David are not fulfilled in Solomon but in Christ.
  • On David’s sin and repentance (2 Samuel 11–12). “A Pure Heart”, on Psalm 51 and Nathan’s rebuke, in The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original; E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, whose chapter “Praying Saints of the Old Testaments (Continued)” follows David’s prayer for the dying child and his confession to Nathan; and the eighteenth chapter of Clement of Rome’s The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians, “David as an example of humility”, on Psalm 51.
  • On David’s last words (2 Samuel 23). J. C. Ryle’s sermon Without Clouds (2 Samuel 23:4–5), on the old king’s sorrow over his house and his comfort in the everlasting covenant.
  • On David and the Spirit. A. B. Simpson’s chapter “The Holy Spirit in the Lives of Saul and David”, in The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High.
  • For reading with children. “A Seat at the King’s Table”, on David and Mephibosheth, and “The Story of the Little Lamb”, on Nathan’s parable, in Tell Me the Story – Book 3: Kings and Prophets; and “A House Forever”, on 2 Samuel 7 and 11–12, in The Big Story, both Ochorus Originals.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 2 Samuel in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

And then read the Psalms that bear David’s name, beginning with the song this book itself records, almost word for word, as Psalm 18. They are the inside of the story 2 Samuel tells from outside: the prayers of a king who knew where his help came from.
