The letter ends before the story does. Paul is confident, he hopes to visit, he asks for a room. But we are never told what Philemon did when the letter had been read aloud in his house and every eye turned to him and to the slave standing beside the reader.

The question the letter leaves

That silence is the letter’s question, and it is put to every reader. What will you do with the person the gospel has made your brother or sister? Philemon was asked to welcome as an equal a man he legally owned, who had wronged him, and to do it freely. The church has always had its Philemons and its Onesimuses, people divided by money, class, race or old grievances, and this letter will not let them stay apart once both belong to Christ.

The fact that the letter was kept at all suggests that Philemon said yes. Some fifty years later, Ignatius of Antioch wrote to the church at Ephesus and praised its bishop, a man named Onesimus, “whose love passeth utterance”. It was a common name, and no one can know whether he was the same man. But some readers have wondered whether the useless slave became a bishop, and whether that is why this little letter was treasured.

Where the letter points

It points, uncomfortably, into later history. In the American debates over slavery, some defenders of slavery cited Philemon to argue that a Christian slave should be returned to his master. Those who opposed slavery answered from the same page: “no longer as a slave but as more than a slave, a dear brother.” Paul did not write a law against slavery. He wrote a letter that made slavery between brothers impossible to defend.

Where to go from here

Philemon is short, but it has not been overlooked. A few places to go next, all in this library:

  • Charles H. Spurgeon’s Morning by Morning, whose reading for 1 November is on “the church in thy house” (verse 2).
  • John Chrysostom’s sermon Concerning Lowliness of Mind, where he recalls the letter Paul sent on behalf of Onesimus and insists that a runaway slave is a human being for whom Christ died.
  • E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, whose chapter “Paul and His Requests for Prayer (Continued)” takes up Paul’s hope of being given back through Philemon’s prayers (verse 22).
  • The Epistles of Ignatius and His Life’s Story, for the letter to the Ephesians and its bishop Onesimus.
  • Tertullian’s Against Marcion, whose fifth book ends with Philemon, the one letter Marcion could not shorten.
  • Devoted to Prayer, whose chapter “Always Wrestling in Prayer” is about Epaphras, Paul’s fellow prisoner in verse 23.
  • Richard Allen’s The Life, Experience, and Gospel Labours, the story of a man born a slave whose master, convicted by a sermon, let him buy his freedom, and who went on to found the African Methodist Episcopal Church.

But the best next step may be to read Philemon again, beside Colossians, and to ask who in your own life Paul would be writing to you about. And then to pray for that person in the words Paul used for his friend:
