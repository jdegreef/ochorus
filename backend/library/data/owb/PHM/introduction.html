— Philemon 1:15–16

Philemon is the shortest of Paul’s letters, about three hundred and thirty-five words in Greek, and the most personal. It contains no teaching on justification, no warnings against false teachers, no hymn to Christ. It is a request from one friend to another about a third man, and it can be read aloud in under five minutes. Yet the church has kept it in the Bible for nineteen centuries, because in this small letter the gospel walks into a household and changes the way three people must look at one another.

The people in the letter

Philemon was a believer, a householder, and a friend of Paul’s, who calls him “our dear friend and fellow worker.” A church met in his home. With him are greeted Apphia, “our sister”, perhaps his wife, and Archippus, “our fellow soldier”, perhaps his son. Paul seems to have brought Philemon to faith himself, since he can say, half teasing, that Philemon owes him his own self.

Philemon most likely lived at Colossae. The letter to the Colossians names Onesimus as “one of your own” and sends a message to Archippus (Colossians 4:9, 17), and the same friends of Paul send greetings in both letters: Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas and Luke. The two letters were very likely carried together, by Tychicus and Onesimus, from Paul’s imprisonment, probably in Rome around AD 60–62, though some place it earlier, at Ephesus.

Onesimus was Philemon’s slave. Something had gone wrong between them; Paul speaks of Onesimus having been “useless” to Philemon, of his being “parted from you”, and of any wrong he has done or anything he owes. Then Onesimus met Paul, in prison, and became a Christian: Paul calls him “my child Onesimus, who became my son while I was in chains.”

What had happened?

The traditional answer is that Onesimus ran away, perhaps taking money with him, made his way to the city where Paul was held, and there, by God’s providence, met the apostle and believed. Many readers still find that the most natural reading.

Others point out that Roman law did not treat a slave as a fugitive if he went to a friend of his master to plead for help after a quarrel, and suggest that Onesimus went to Paul on purpose, as someone Philemon would listen to. A letter of Pliny the Younger, written some forty years later, pleads with a friend to forgive a freedman who had offended him, and it is a close parallel. But the contrast is just as telling. Pliny asks for mercy for a penitent dependant. Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus as a brother.

The appeal

The letter is a small masterpiece of tact. Paul could give orders, he says, but “I would rather appeal to you on the basis of love.” He does not begin with Onesimus at all, but with thanks for Philemon’s love and faith, and with the joy it has brought him, because “through you God’s holy people have been refreshed at heart.” Only then does he name his request, and he names himself first: “an old man and now also a prisoner for Christ Jesus.”

He plays on Onesimus’s name, which means “useful”: “Once he was useless to you, but now he is useful to you and to me.” He is sending him back, and it costs him: “he is my very heart.” He would have liked to keep him, but he wants Philemon’s goodness to come “from your own free choice and not be forced.” And then the heart of the letter, in the words that open this introduction. Onesimus is to come back “no longer as a slave but as more than a slave, a dear brother”, both in everyday life and in the Lord.

Paul goes further. “So if I am your partner, take him in as though he were me.” If Onesimus owes anything, “put it on my account.” And at this point Paul takes the pen himself:

— Philemon 1:19

Many readers have heard in these verses a picture of what Christ does for sinners, receiving them as his own and taking their debts on himself. Luther put it memorably: we are all his Onesimus. That is not the letter’s first purpose, but it is not far from its heart.

What did Paul ask for?

Paul never quite says what he wants Philemon to do, beyond receiving Onesimus as a brother. Some think he is hinting that Philemon should send Onesimus back to help him, since he would have liked to keep him “so that he could serve me in your place.” Some think he is asking for Onesimus’s freedom, especially when he adds, “I know you will do even more than I say.” Others think he deliberately leaves it to Philemon. What is plain is that once a master has to call his slave “a dear brother”, and welcome him as he would the apostle himself, the old relationship cannot go on as it was.

How to read Philemon

Read it aloud, as it was first read, in the church that met in Philemon’s house, with Onesimus perhaps standing there while it was read. Imagine Philemon listening, with everyone watching him. Then read Colossians 3:11 and 4:7–9, which were heard on the same day.

Notice the words that keep returning: love, heart, partner, brother, refresh. Notice how Paul speaks of Onesimus: my child, my son, my very heart. And notice that the letter ends, as it began, among friends, with a request to have a room ready, because Paul hopes to come and see how it has all turned out.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Philemon are worth knowing about.

“An old man.” In verse 9 the Greek word for an old man differs by a single letter from the word for an ambassador, and some translations read “ambassador”. This translation reads “an old man”.

“Useless” and “useful”. The translation keeps Paul’s play on Onesimus’s name in verse 11, so that an English reader can hear the pun, even without the Greek.

“My very heart.” Paul’s word in verses 7, 12 and 20 is literally the inward parts, the seat of deep feeling. The translation renders it with the English word that does the same work: “heart”.
