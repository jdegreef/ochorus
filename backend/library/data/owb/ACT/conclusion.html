— Acts 28:28

The last scene of Acts is a conversation in a rented room in Rome. Paul has called the leaders of the city’s Jewish community, and from morning to evening he lays out for them the kingdom of God and the things about Jesus, from the law of Moses and the Prophets. Some are persuaded; others refuse. Paul quotes Isaiah, as Jesus did, on a people who listen and never understand, and then announces that God’s salvation has been sent to the Gentiles. It is not a door shut on Israel. Paul goes on welcoming “everyone who visited him”, and the story ends with the door open.

The question the book leaves with you

On the day of Pentecost, when Peter had finished preaching, his hearers asked a question that echoes through the whole book:

{{OWB:ACT.2.37.s1}}

— Acts 2:37

A jailer in Philippi asks it at midnight, shaking, after an earthquake has thrown open the prison doors: “Sirs, what must I do to be saved?” Paul gives an answer that echoes Peter’s call to repent and be baptized, and that the whole book gives: “Believe in the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved, you and your household.” Acts is full of people hearing the message and deciding what to do with it: three thousand at Pentecost, a eunuch in his chariot, a centurion and his household, a businesswoman named Lydia whose heart the Lord opened, philosophers in Athens who scoff or ask to hear more another time. King Agrippa, after listening to Paul’s defence, says half in mockery, “In so short a time you are persuading me to become a Christian!” Paul answers that he prays that everyone who hears him would become as he is, “though without these chains.”

Acts asks its readers the same question. Its message has reached us across nineteen centuries. What are we to do?

Where the book points

It points, first, to the Spirit. The disciples could do nothing until they had received power from on high, and nothing in Acts is done without him: the Spirit fills, sends, guides, forbids, and speaks to the church at prayer. Christians have differed on how the Spirit’s gifts in Acts are to be sought and expected today. But no reader of the book can doubt that the church lives by the Spirit or not at all.

It points, second, to witness. The last thing Jesus said before he was taken up was that his followers would be his witnesses to the farthest end of the earth. Acts tells how that began; it does not tell how it ends, because it has not ended. The book leaves Paul in Rome, and the rest of the journey to the ends of the earth to those who come after.

And it points to the church: a community devoted to the apostles’ teaching, the shared life, the breaking of bread and the prayers; Jews and Gentiles at one table; a people who quarrel and forgive, suffer and rejoice, and keep telling what they have seen and heard.

Where to go from here

Many writings in this library preach from Acts or continue its story. A few places to begin:

  • On the Holy Spirit. The second volume of A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, with its chapters on waiting for the Spirit and the outpouring at Pentecost; R. A. Torrey’s Baptism with the Holy Spirit, a classic statement of one view of the gift; and A. W. Tozer’s All with One Accord (2:1).
  • On the first preaching. Tozer’s The Triumph of the Resurrection (2:22–39), J. C. Ryle’s Only One Way (4:12), and Catherine Booth’s Witnessing for Christ (1:8; 5:32).
  • On the life of the first church. Devoted to Prayer, on how the first Christians prayed, from the upper room to Paul and Silas singing at midnight; and Andrew Murray’s The Holy Spirit and Money, on Ananias and Sapphira (5:3).
  • On Paul’s conversion (chapter 9). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Paul’s First Prayer (9:11).
  • On the jailer’s question (chapter 16). D. L. Moody’s The Way of Salvation (16:23–40) and R. A. Torrey’s The Way of Salvation Made as Plain as Day (16:30–31).
  • On Agrippa (chapter 26). John Wesley’s The Almost Christian (26:28).
  • On what happened next. Clement of Rome’s The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians, written from the church in Rome a generation later, which remembers Peter and Paul as witnesses who suffered to the end; and Eusebius of Caesarea’s The Church History, the first history of the church, which picks up the story where Acts leaves off.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Acts in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

But the best next step may be to read Acts again with Paul’s letters open beside it, and then to read the Gospel of Luke again, knowing how its story continues. And remember, as you close the book, the words two men in white spoke to the apostles as they stood staring into the sky. They were not to stay gazing, but to wait for the promise and then be witnesses. The one they had watched go would come again:
