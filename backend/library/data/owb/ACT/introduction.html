{{OWB:ACT.1.7.s1}}

— Acts 1:7–8

Acts begins with a backward glance. “Theophilus,” Luke writes, “the first book I composed told of all that Jesus began both to do and to teach.” The word to notice is began. The Gospel told what Jesus began; this second book tells what he went on doing, through his Spirit, in the lives of his followers. It is the only book of its kind in the New Testament, the story of how about a hundred and twenty people gathered in an upper room in Jerusalem became, within a single lifetime, a movement that reached the capital of the world.

Without it we would know almost nothing of how the church began. We would not know of Pentecost, of Stephen, of the Ethiopian on the desert road or the centurion at Caesarea. We would have Paul’s letters, but not the story of his conversion or his journeys, and we would have to guess at the order in which he wrote them. Acts is the bridge between the Gospels and the letters, and it makes sense of both.

The second volume

Acts is the second half of a two-part work by the author of the Gospel of Luke. Both books are addressed to Theophilus; the end of one and the beginning of the other overlap, telling of the risen Jesus’s last words and his being taken up into heaven. Their language, style and concerns are the same: prayer, the Holy Spirit, the poor, the welcome of outsiders, and the steady movement of the story outward from Jerusalem.

From the second century the church has said the author was Luke, a doctor and a companion of Paul. In several passages the narrative slips from “they” into “we”: from Troas to Philippi (16:10–17), on the voyage from Philippi to Jerusalem (20:5–21:18), and on the long, dangerous voyage to Rome (27:1–28:16). Most readers have taken these as the places where the author himself was present. Some scholars think the “we” comes from a source the author used, or is a way of telling a sea story; many others find the simplest explanation the best.

The book ends with Paul in Rome, living for two years under guard in rented lodgings, around AD 60–62. Some think Acts was written soon after, which would explain why it does not tell of Paul’s trial or his death. Many date it later, in the 80s or after. Either way, its author knew the world he wrote about closely. He gets the titles of local officials right, city by city, and his account of the voyage and shipwreck is one of the most detailed descriptions of ancient seafaring that has come down to us. An inscription found at Delphi dates Gallio’s time as proconsul of Achaia, before whom Paul was tried in Corinth, to about AD 51, and it has become one of the fixed points from which the dates of Paul’s life are worked out.

Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, and the ends of the earth

The risen Jesus gives Acts its table of contents in his last words before he is taken up: they will be his witnesses “in Jerusalem, through all Judea and Samaria, and out to the farthest end of the earth.” The book follows that plan:

  • Jerusalem (1–7). The waiting, Pentecost, the first church, the apostles before the council, and the death of Stephen.
  • Judea and Samaria (8–12). The scattering after Stephen’s death, Philip in Samaria and on the desert road, the conversion of Saul, Peter and Cornelius, and the new church at Antioch.
  • To the ends of the earth (13–28). Paul’s journeys through Cyprus, Asia Minor and Greece, the council at Jerusalem, his arrest, his trials before governors and a king, and the voyage to Rome.

Luke marks the stages with short summaries, like milestones: “So God’s word went on spreading”; “the word of God went on growing and spreading”; “with great power the word of the Lord kept growing and gaining strength.” The real hero of Acts is not Peter or Paul. It is the word of God, which cannot be stopped.

Pentecost

For forty days the risen Jesus appears to his apostles and speaks to them about the kingdom of God. Then he is taken up, and they go back to Jerusalem to wait, as he told them, praying together with the women, Mary his mother and his brothers. About ten days later comes the Jewish harvest festival of Pentecost, fifty days after Passover, when Jerusalem was crowded with pilgrims from every nation:

{{OWB:ACT.2.1.s1}}

— Acts 2:1–4

The crowd hears these Galileans speaking in their own languages, and Peter stands up to explain. This is what the prophet Joel foretold: God will pour out his Spirit “on all humanity”, sons and daughters, young and old. Jesus, whom they crucified, God has raised from the dead and made “both Lord and Christ”. Pierced to the heart, the crowd asks what they should do. Peter tells them to repent and be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of their sins, and they will receive the Holy Spirit as a gift. About three thousand are baptized that day.

Many readers have seen Pentecost as Babel undone. At Babel, in Genesis, human pride was scattered into many languages that could not understand one another. At Pentecost God gathers people of many languages, and each of them hears the mighty deeds of God in his own tongue.

Christians have understood the gift of the Spirit in Acts in different ways. Pentecostal and charismatic believers see in Pentecost and in later scenes in Acts (in Samaria, at Cornelius’s house, at Ephesus) a pattern of a baptism in the Spirit, often with speaking in tongues, that may follow conversion and is meant for every age. Others see Pentecost as a once-for-all beginning, like the giving of the law at Sinai, and the later scenes as marking the gospel’s crossing of new frontiers. All agree that in Acts the Spirit is the one who drives the story.

The first church

Luke pauses to show what the new community was like:

{{OWB:ACT.2.42.s1}}

— Acts 2:42

They met in the temple courts and in one another’s homes. They shared what they had, selling land and goods so that no one among them was in need. They ate together “with joyful and simple hearts”. It is an idealised picture, and Luke knows it. A few chapters later he tells of a couple who lied about their gift, and of Greek-speaking widows overlooked in the daily distribution of food, and of the seven men, Stephen among them, appointed to see that it was done fairly. The church in Acts is not perfect. But it is real, and it keeps finding the grace to deal with its failures.

It is also bold. Peter and John, “men without formal schooling”, are arrested after healing a lame man, for teaching that in Jesus there is resurrection from the dead, and ordered to stop speaking of him. Their answer has steadied persecuted believers ever since: “we cannot stay silent about the things we have seen and heard.” When the apostles are arrested again and flogged, they go away glad to have been found worthy to bear shame for the sake of the Name.

Stephen

Stephen is the first martyr. Charged with speaking against the temple and the law, he answers with a long retelling of Israel’s history, showing that God was never confined to one land or one building, and that Israel had often resisted those God sent. His hearers rush at him, drag him out of the city and stone him. As he dies he sees the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing at God’s right hand, and he prays as Jesus prayed on the cross:

{{OWB:ACT.7.60.s1}}

— Acts 7:60

Luke adds that the witnesses laid their cloaks at the feet of a young man named Saul.

The Way opens outward

Stephen’s death sets off a persecution that scatters the believers, and wherever they go they carry the message. Philip preaches in Samaria, to the people Jews had long avoided. Then an angel sends him to a desert road, where an Ethiopian official, a eunuch who has come to Jerusalem to worship, is sitting in his chariot reading Isaiah. “Do you know what it is you are reading?” Philip asks. “How could I,” he answers, “if no one guides me?” Starting from that very passage, Philip tells him the good news about Jesus, and he is baptized by the roadside and travels on with a glad heart.

Then the persecutor himself is stopped. On the road to Damascus, carrying letters to arrest anyone who belongs to “the Way”, Saul is thrown to the ground by a light from heaven and hears a voice: “Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?” Luke thinks this moment so important that he tells it three times: once in the story itself (chapter 9), and twice in Paul’s own words, before a hostile crowd in Jerusalem (22) and before King Agrippa (26). The enemy of the church becomes its greatest missionary, chosen, the Lord says, “to bear my name before Gentiles and kings and the people of Israel.”

The hardest frontier is crossed by Peter. In Caesarea a Roman centurion named Cornelius, a God-fearing Gentile, is told in a vision to send for him; in Joppa, Peter sees a sheet let down from heaven full of animals the law called unclean, and hears a voice telling him not to call unholy what God has made clean. When he arrives at Cornelius’s house and preaches, the Holy Spirit comes down on the Gentile listeners before he has finished speaking. Peter’s words there are a turning point of the book: “In truth I now see that God does not favor one person over another.” Soon afterwards, in Antioch, believers begin to tell the good news to Gentiles as well, and it is there that the disciples are first called Christians.

Paul and the council

From chapter 13 the story belongs mostly to Paul. The church at Antioch, while worshipping and fasting, hears the Spirit say, “Set Barnabas and Saul apart for me”, and sends them out. Acts traces three great journeys, through Cyprus and the cities of southern Asia Minor, then across into Macedonia and Greece, and then a long stay at Ephesus. Paul’s pattern is to go first to the synagogue, where Jews and God-fearing Gentiles gather to hear the Scriptures, and then, when he is driven out, to the Gentiles. Everywhere some believe and others oppose him; he is stoned at Lystra, beaten and jailed at Philippi, and run out of Thessalonica, and everywhere he leaves a church behind.

The success among the Gentiles raised the most important question the early church faced. Must Gentile believers be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be saved? Some teachers from Judea said they must. Chapter 15 tells how the apostles and elders met in Jerusalem to decide. Peter recalls Cornelius: “we believe that we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, and they are saved in just the same way.” James, the brother of Jesus, agrees, and the council writes to the Gentile churches laying on them no burden beyond a few essentials, so that, as many read it, Jewish and Gentile believers could share a table. It is the hinge of Acts and of the whole history of the church. The gospel would not be the property of one nation.

Acts does not hide the church’s disagreements. Paul and Barnabas, partners since the first journey, quarrel so sharply over taking John Mark with them again that they part ways. And later readers have puzzled over how Luke’s account of these years fits Paul’s own in Galatians; scholars still debate which visit to Jerusalem Paul means there.

Athens

One speech in Acts shows how Paul spoke to people who had never read the Scriptures. In Athens, distressed by a city full of idols, he stands before the council of the Areopagus and begins with an altar he has seen, inscribed “To an unknown god.” He tells them of the God who made the world and does not live in temples made by hands, who is not far from any of us. He quotes their own poets: “It is in him that we live and move and exist”, and, “We too are his offspring.” Then he calls them to repent, because God has fixed a day of judgment and has given proof of it by raising a man from the dead. At the mention of the raising of the dead, some scoff. A few believe, among them a member of the council and a woman named Damaris.

The prisoner

The last quarter of Acts tells of Paul’s arrest and trials. He goes up to Jerusalem knowing that chains await him; when friends beg him not to go, he answers that he is ready not only to be bound but to die there “for the name of the Lord Jesus.” In the temple a mob seizes him, and Roman soldiers rescue him. For two years he is held at Caesarea and heard by the governors Felix and Festus and by King Agrippa II. Finally, as a Roman citizen, he appeals to Caesar, and is sent to Rome.

Luke tells the voyage in vivid detail: the late season, the storm, day after day with no sun or stars to be seen, Paul standing on the deck to tell the crew that an angel has promised that not one life will be lost, and the shipwreck on Malta. It is told as an adventure, and it is one. But it is also a picture of what the whole book has shown: no storm, no plot and no prison can stop the word of God from reaching the place God means it to go.

The open ending

Acts does not end with Paul’s trial before Caesar, or with his death, which tradition places in Rome under Nero a few years later. It ends with him in his rented lodgings, welcoming everyone who comes, and the last word in Greek is “unhindered”:

{{OWB:ACT.28.30.s1}}

— Acts 28:30–31

Many readers have felt that the ending is open on purpose. The story is not finished, because the witness to the ends of the earth is not finished. Every generation of the church since has been living in the twenty-ninth chapter of Acts.

How to read Acts

Read it first for the story. It is the most exciting book in the New Testament, full of riots, escapes, trials and voyages, and it deserves to be read straight through, perhaps in two or three sittings.

Then read it for its speeches, which make up about a quarter of the book: Peter at Pentecost and in the temple, Stephen before the council, Peter at Cornelius’s house, Paul in the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, at Athens, to the elders of Ephesus at Miletus, and in his own defence before the crowd, the governor and the king. Taken together they show how the apostles preached Jesus from the Scriptures to Jews and to Gentiles.

And read it with Paul’s letters beside it. When Acts tells of Philippi or Thessalonica or Corinth or Ephesus, turn to the letters he wrote to those churches, and you will hear the other side of the conversation.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Acts are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Languages.” At Pentecost (2:4) the Greek word for “tongues” is used both for the flames and for what the disciples spoke. This translation says that they spoke in “other languages”, since the crowd heard its own languages. At Caesarea (10:46) and Ephesus (19:6) it keeps the familiar “speaking in tongues”.

“The shared life.” In 2:42 the word often translated “fellowship” is rendered “the shared life”, which says what it was: a life held in common, down to meals and money.

“Brothers and sisters.” Where the Greek word for brothers plainly means the whole community of believers, the translation says “brothers and sisters”.

Verses not found in the oldest manuscripts. Some manuscripts of Acts, chiefly the one called Codex Bezae, have a longer form of the text, with many small additions. This translation follows the shorter text of the earliest manuscripts. So Acts 8:37, in which the Ethiopian confesses his faith before his baptism, is not in the text, nor are 15:34, 24:7 and 28:29, which older Bibles print; the verse numbers are skipped. One more numbering note: in this translation 19:40 includes what most English Bibles number 19:41.

“Christ.” The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose whether Christos reads “Christ” or “the Messiah”. This edition prints “Christ”, but it is worth remembering, in speeches like Peter’s at Pentecost, that the claim being made is that Jesus is Israel’s promised King.
