Hebrews ends with a prayer, and the prayer gathers the whole letter into a single sentence. God has led Jesus up from the dead; Jesus is the great shepherd, as he was the great high priest; the covenant is eternal, and it was made with blood. And after twelve chapters of urging the hearers to hold on, the author asks God to do in them what he has been asking them to do. The God who calls them to endure is the God who will make them ready.

Then, with a touch of humour, he asks them to bear with his “short letter”, sends news of Timothy, and is gone.

The question the letter leaves

Hebrews leaves its readers with one question, asked in many forms: will you keep going? Its first hearers were not wondering whether Jesus was real. They had believed, suffered for it, and seen their goods taken. What they faced was the slow pull of weariness, the habit of missing the gatherings, the thought that the old, familiar ways had been safer. The author never answers that weariness by scolding alone. He answers it by showing them Jesus, again and again, until the question becomes: having seen him, where else would you go?

That is why the letter keeps returning to the word “Today”. The rest is still open, the throne of grace is still approachable, the voice is still speaking. But it speaks now, and the answer is given now. “Be careful not to refuse the one who is speaking to you.”

Where the letter points

It points forward. Hebrews is full of people who died without receiving what was promised and went on looking for a country of their own. The author counts his readers among them. They have come to Mount Zion already, and yet they are still seeking the city to come. The Christian life, in Hebrews, is a pilgrimage with the destination already in view:

— Hebrews 12:28–29

And it points to one another. The letter that soars into the heavenly sanctuary also tells its readers to keep each other in mind, to rouse each other to love, to open their homes, to visit prisoners, and to go on meeting. Endurance, in Hebrews, is never a solitary virtue. It is something a congregation does together, encouraging one another day by day.

Where to go from here

Hebrews has fed the church’s preaching and prayer for as long as there has been a church, and many of its best interpreters are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • Through the whole letter. F. B. Meyer’s The Way into the Holiest, devotional expositions that follow Hebrews from its first verse to its closing prayer.
  • On Christ the Son (chapter 1). Athanasius of Alexandria’s Against the Arians, which works through Hebrews 1:4 and 3:2 in defending the full divinity of the Son.
  • On the rest that remains (chapters 3–4). Richard Baxter’s The Saints’ Everlasting Rest, his great treatise on 4:9; Charles H. Spurgeon’s sermon Heavenly Rest, on the same verse; and R. A. Torrey’s The Day of Golden Opportunity, on “Today” (3:7).
  • On the high priest who intercedes (chapters 4–7). Spurgeon’s Salvation to the Uttermost and J. C. Ryle’s Christ’s Power to Save in Old Paths, both on 7:25; Andrew Murray’s The School of Obedience, whose chapter The Secret of True Obedience is on 5:8; and Martyn Lloyd-Jones’s So Great Salvation, on the first warning (2:1–4).
  • On the new covenant and the open way (chapters 8–10). Andrew Murray’s The Two Covenants; Horatius Bonar’s The Rent Veil, on the curtain torn open; and A. W. Tozer’s The Pursuit of God, whose chapter Removing the Veil is on 10:19.
  • On faith (chapter 11). Spurgeon’s Faith (11:6); John Wesley’s sermons On Faith and On the Discoveries of Faith (11:1), in Sermons on Several Occasions; and Jonathan Edwards’s The Christian Pilgrim (11:13–14).
  • On the race and the mountain (chapter 12). A. B. Simpson’s The Cloud of Witnesses (12:1), The School of Faith (12:11) and The Goal of Faith (12:18–24); Spurgeon’s The Shameful Sufferer (12:2); J. C. Ryle’s Holiness, whose title chapter is on 12:14; and George MacDonald’s The Consuming Fire (12:29), in Unspoken Sermons.
  • Outside the camp (chapter 13). John Calvin’s Enduring Persecution for Christ (13:13) and Spurgeon’s The Blood of the Everlasting Covenant, on the closing prayer (13:20).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Hebrews in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.
