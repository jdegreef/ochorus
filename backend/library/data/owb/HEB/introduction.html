{{OWB:HEB.1.1.s1}}

— Hebrews 1:1–2

Hebrews begins like no other letter in the New Testament. There is no greeting and no sender’s name, no “grace and peace”. It opens instead with one long, carefully balanced sentence, some of the finest Greek in the New Testament, about a God who speaks. Long ago he spoke through the prophets, in pieces and in many ways. Now, at the last, he has spoken through a Son.

Everything that follows unfolds that sentence. The Son is greater than the angels, greater than Moses, greater than the priests of Aaron’s line. His one sacrifice does what the endless sacrifices of the old covenant could only point toward. And because God has spoken so fully and so finally, those who have heard him dare not drift away.

That last point is why the book was written. Hebrews is not a treatise for scholars. Its author calls it “this message of encouragement” (13:22), and it reads like a sermon sent by post to a congregation that was tired. They had stood firm once, under real persecution. Now some had stopped meeting, and some were thinking of turning back. Into that weariness the author pours the fullest portrait of Jesus as priest anywhere in the Bible, and a call to keep running.

Who wrote it, and to whom?

No one knows who wrote Hebrews, and the early church knew that it did not know. The letter names no author. He was not one of the first eyewitnesses: the message of salvation, he says, was “made certain to us by those who had listened to him” (2:3). He knew Timothy (13:23).

In the East the letter was counted among Paul’s from early on. The oldest surviving collection of Paul’s letters, a papyrus copied around AD 200, places it straight after Romans. Clement of Alexandria thought Paul had written it in Hebrew and Luke had put it into Greek. Origen thought the ideas were Paul’s and the wording someone else’s, and concluded, in a line Eusebius preserved, that who wrote the letter, in truth, God knows. In the West, Tertullian named Barnabas, and the church at Rome was slow to count the letter as Paul’s at all. Luther later guessed Apollos, the learned Alexandrian Jew of Acts 18:24; others have suggested Luke, Silas, or Priscilla. Most scholars today agree that the style and the shape of the argument are not Paul’s. The church, East and West, received the letter as Scripture all the same.

The title “To the Hebrews” was attached early, but not by the author. Still, it fits. The whole argument moves through Israel’s Scriptures, its priesthood, its tabernacle and its Day of Atonement, and it assumes readers who know them well, very likely Jewish believers in Jesus who were tempted to fall back on the familiar ways they had left. “Those from Italy send you their greetings” (13:24) may mean the author was writing from Italy, or that Italians away from home were greeting their friends there.

As for the date, Clement of Rome echoes Hebrews in a letter written around AD 96, so it is older than that. Many think it was written before the temple fell in AD 70. The author speaks of the priests’ service as something still going on (8:4, 10:1–2), and he never mentions the temple’s destruction, which would have made his argument for him. Others place it a decade or two later. Either way, its readers had once “endured a hard contest of suffering” (10:32). Now they were drifting.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapters 1–4: the Son who speaks. Higher than the angels, faithful over God’s house as Moses was faithful in it, and the one through whom God’s promised rest is still open, “Today”.
  • Chapters 5–10:18: the great high priest. A priest of the order of Melchizedek, mediator of a new covenant, who has entered the true sanctuary with his own blood, once for all.
  • Chapters 10:19–13:25: so let us draw near, and hold on. Faith in the long roll call of chapter 11, endurance in the race of chapter 12, and love in the ordinary life of chapter 13.

Woven through the whole are five warnings, each searching (2:1–4; 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:12; 10:26–39; 12:14–29). Hebrews never lets teaching stand on its own. Every unveiling of who Jesus is ends in a “so”: so pay attention, so do not harden your hearts, so let us approach.

God has spoken in a Son

The opening sentence does not stop with the Son as God’s last word. It goes on to say who he is:

{{OWB:HEB.1.3.s1}}

— Hebrews 1:3–4

Radiance and stamp: the light that streams from the sun, and the image pressed by a seal into wax. The Son is not a reflection of God at a distance; he shows exactly what God is. Then, in seven quotations from Israel’s Scriptures, most of them from the Psalms, the author sets the Son above the angels. To which angel did God ever say, “You are my Son”? Of which angel did he say, “Your throne, O God, lasts forever and ever”?

The first warning follows at once. If the law given through angels carried its penalties, “how will we escape if we pay no attention to a salvation as great as this?” The danger Hebrews fears is not open rebellion so much as drift.

Made like his brothers and sisters

Psalm 8 marvels that God should crown human beings with glory and put all things under their feet. “But as it is,” the author admits, “we do not yet see all things put under him.” Then: “But Jesus we do see.” It is the first time the letter uses his name, and it comes at the place where the human story has fallen short.

The Son who is the radiance of God’s glory shared “a common life of flesh and blood”, so that by his death he might break the devil’s power and set free those whom the fear of death had kept enslaved all their lives. He is “not ashamed to call them brothers and sisters.” And this is the point where the letter’s great theme first appears:

{{OWB:HEB.2.17.s1}} {{OWB:HEB.2.18.s1}}

— Hebrews 2:17–18

Today, if you hear his voice

Moses was faithful in God’s house as a servant; Christ is faithful over it as the Son. But the house is a people, “and we are that house, if we keep hold of our confidence”. To show what is at stake, the author turns to Psalm 95 and the generation that came out of Egypt and never reached the promised land. What kept them out was not a lack of evidence but a lack of trust: “it was unbelief that kept them from entering.”

The psalm says “Today”, and the author seizes on the word. David wrote it long after Joshua had led Israel into Canaan, so the true rest cannot have been Canaan. “So a Sabbath rest is still waiting for God’s people.” The door stands open, but only today, and so believers are to “encourage one another day by day, while there is still a ‘Today,’” before sin can harden them.

Then comes one of the letter’s most famous sentences, about the word that has been speaking all along, which “has a keener edge than any two-edged sword” and weighs “the thoughts and purposes of the heart.” No one can hide from it. But the passage turns straight from exposure to welcome:

{{OWB:HEB.4.15.s1}} {{OWB:HEB.4.16.s1}}

— Hebrews 4:15–16

A priest like Melchizedek

No high priest takes the honour for himself; God calls him, and so with Christ. Here the author offers a glimpse of Jesus at prayer, most likely in Gethsemane, that no other letter in the New Testament gives: in the days of his flesh Jesus offered up prayers and pleas, “crying aloud and weeping”, and though he was a Son, “he learned obedience by the things he suffered.”

But Jesus was from the tribe of Judah, not Levi. How can he be a priest? The answer lies in Psalm 110, the psalm the New Testament quotes more than any other, where God says to David’s Lord, “You are a priest forever, of the order of Melchizedek.” Melchizedek appears in Genesis 14 for three verses, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, and then vanishes. The author reads the silence of Genesis about his parents, his birth and his death as a picture of a priesthood that has no beginning and no end. Abraham, Levi’s ancestor, gave him a tenth and received his blessing, so a priest of his order is greater than any of Aaron’s. Jesus has become one “by the power of a life that cannot be destroyed.”

The Levitical priests died one after another. This one does not:

{{OWB:HEB.7.25.s1}}

— Hebrews 7:25

The hardest warning

Before that argument, the author stops himself. His readers have become “sluggish listeners”; they should be teachers by now, and still need milk. He urges them on to maturity, and then writes the passage that has troubled more readers than any other in the letter: for those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the gift from heaven and shared in the Holy Spirit, and then have fallen away, “It is impossible for them to be brought back again to repentance” (6:4–6).

Christians have read this in more than one way. In the Reformed tradition, many hold that those described had tasted God’s gifts without true saving faith, or that the warning is one of the very means by which God keeps his people from falling. In the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions, it shows that real believers can turn away for good. Every reading should notice what the author does next: “Yet about you, dear friends, we are confident of better things” (6:9). The warning is meant to keep them, and the chapter ends with an anchor:

{{OWB:HEB.6.19.s1}}

— Hebrews 6:19–20

A better covenant, a single sacrifice

“Here is the heart of what we are saying” (8:1). Our high priest serves in the true tabernacle, of which the earthly one was only “a copy and shadow”. He is mediator of a better covenant, the new covenant promised in Jeremiah 31. The author quotes the whole promise, the longest quotation of the Old Testament anywhere in the New, and its last line is the one he cares most about: “For I will show mercy to their wrongdoing, and their sins I will never again call to mind.”

Chapter 9 walks through the tabernacle and the Day of Atonement, when once a year the high priest went alone behind the curtain with blood. That yearly return was itself the proof that the work was never finished. Christ has entered not a sanctuary made by hands but “heaven itself”, not with the blood of goats and calves but with his own, and not over and over but once:

{{OWB:HEB.9.11.s1}}

— Hebrews 9:11–12

“Every priest stands at his duties day after day,” the author writes. But “This priest, though, offered a single sacrifice for sins, good for all time, and then sat down at the right hand of God.” The work is done.

Draw near, hold on

At 10:19 the letter turns from what Christ has done to what his people may now do. Since the way into God’s presence is open, “let us approach”, “Let us cling to the confession of our hope”, and let us not walk away from our own gatherings, “as some have made it their practice”. Then comes the fourth warning, as severe as the third, “Falling into the hands of the living God is a terrifying thing”, and again the turn to encouragement. Remember the early days, he says, when you lost your goods and took it with joy. “What you need is endurance.”

By faith

Then the great chapter. It opens with a definition:

{{OWB:HEB.11.1.s1}}

— Hebrews 11:1

And it goes on with a procession. By faith Abel, Enoch and Noah; by faith Abraham, who set out “without knowing where he was headed”, and Sarah, and Isaac, Jacob and Joseph; by faith Moses, and Israel at the Red Sea, and the walls of Jericho, and Rahab. Then the pace quickens, through judges, kings and prophets, to those who shut the mouths of lions and those who were sawn in two. “The world did not deserve such people.”

What they share is not success. Many of them “did not receive what was promised, but they saw it from far off and welcomed it”, and they called themselves “foreigners and passing visitors on the earth.” Faith, in Hebrews, is trusting God about what has not happened yet, and living now as if his promises were already solid ground. And the chapter ends with a surprise: none of them has yet been made perfect. They are waiting for us.

The race and the mountain

So chapter 12 pictures them filling the stands of a stadium:

{{OWB:HEB.12.1.s1}}

— Hebrews 12:1–2

The hardships of the race, the author says, are a Father’s discipline, the training every true child receives. Then he sets two mountains side by side. Israel came to Sinai, to fire and darkness and a voice so terrible that they begged it to stop. Believers have come to Mount Zion, “the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem”, to myriads of angels in festival gathering, to Jesus, and to his sprinkled blood, “which says something better than Abel said.” Abel’s blood cried out for justice; Jesus’ blood speaks pardon. The privilege is greater, and so is the responsibility: “For our God really is a consuming fire.”

Outside the camp

The last chapter is plain and practical. Keep loving one another. Open your homes to strangers, for “some people have had angels as their guests and never knew it.” Remember prisoners. Honour marriage. Be content, for God has said, “I will not ever let you go, and I will never, never abandon you.”

And then one last image from the Day of Atonement. The bodies of the sacrificed animals were burned outside the camp, and Jesus too “suffered outside the gate”. So, says the author, “let us go out to him, outside the camp, and share the insults heaped on him.” For readers tempted to retreat into safety, it is the letter’s final appeal. The security they long for is not behind them. “There is no city here that remains for us; the city we are looking for is the one still to come.”

How to read Hebrews

Read it aloud, at one sitting if you can, as its first hearers did. You will hear its rhythm: each stretch of teaching about Christ ends with an appeal, and each warning gives way to hope.

Keep an Old Testament close by. Almost every argument in Hebrews rests on a passage of Scripture: Psalm 2, Psalm 8, Psalm 95, Psalm 110, Jeremiah 31, Leviticus 16, Genesis 14. Look them up and the letter opens. Notice the word “better”: a better hope, a better covenant, better promises, better sacrifices, a better country, a better resurrection. Notice how often the author says “let us”, joining his hearers rather than standing over them. And notice the word “Today”, which he means for whoever is reading.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Hebrews are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Apart from God” in 2:9. Almost all manuscripts, including the oldest, say that Jesus tasted death for everyone “by the grace of God”. A very few, known to Origen and other early writers, read “apart from God”, two Greek words that look much alike. The Greek text this translation follows prints the rarer reading, and the translation does the same, with a note giving the other.

“For a little while.” Psalm 8, quoted in 2:7, says God made human beings “a little lower than the angels”. When the author applies it to Jesus in 2:9, the translation reads the same Greek words as time rather than degree: “For a little while he was placed lower than the angels”, in his earthly life and death, and is now crowned.

“Pioneer.” In 2:10 and 12:2 Jesus is “the pioneer”, the one who goes first and leads others after him, where older Bibles have “captain” or “author”.

Covenant and will. The Greek word for covenant can also mean a last will and testament, and in 9:16–17 the author plays on both senses. The translation says “a will” there, and “covenant” everywhere else, so the change of sense can be seen.

“Brothers and sisters.” Where the author addresses his hearers, or speaks of those Jesus is not ashamed to call his own, the Greek word for brothers is rendered “brothers and sisters”, since the whole congregation is meant.

“Christ.” The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose whether Christos reads “Christ” or “the Messiah”. This edition prints “Christ”, but in a letter so full of Israel’s hopes it is worth remembering that the word means God’s anointed King.
