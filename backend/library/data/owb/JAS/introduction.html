— James 1:22

James is the most practical book in the New Testament. It has no long arguments and no stories, only short, sharp paragraphs about the things that fill an ordinary week: hardship, temptation, money, speech, quarrels, business plans, sickness and prayer. Its hundred and eight verses hold more than fifty commands. And it asks one question over and over, in a dozen forms: does what you believe make any difference to how you live?

It has not always been loved. Martin Luther, in the preface to his German New Testament of 1522, called it “an epistle of straw” beside the great letters of Paul, John and Peter, because he could not find in it the gospel of free grace. Yet the church has read it for nineteen centuries as the conscience of the New Testament, the voice that will not let faith become a matter of words alone.

Who was James?

The letter opens simply: “From James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ.” There were several men named James among Jesus’ first followers. James the son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve, was put to death by Herod Agrippa around AD 44 (Acts 12:2). From early times the church has identified the writer as another James, the brother of the Lord (Mark 6:3; Galatians 1:19).

That James did not believe in his brother during his ministry (John 7:5). But the risen Jesus appeared to him (1 Corinthians 15:7), and he became the leading figure in the church at Jerusalem, who gave the decisive word at the council of Acts 15 and whom Paul counted among its pillars (Galatians 2:9). The Jewish historian Josephus records that he was stoned on the orders of the high priest Ananus around AD 62. Later tradition remembered him as James the Just, a man so often on his knees in prayer that they grew hard like a camel’s.

It is a striking thing that the Lord’s own brother calls himself only “a servant” of him. Many readers, ancient and modern, accept that this James wrote the letter, perhaps in the 40s, which would make it one of the earliest writings in the New Testament, or else in the years before his death. Others think it was written later, by a teacher who wrote in his name and carried on his teaching, noting its polished Greek and its slow acceptance by the wider church. Eusebius, in the fourth century, still listed it among the books that some disputed, though most of the churches used it.

To the twelve tribes

The letter is addressed “To the twelve tribes scattered among the nations”. James writes as a Jewish believer to Jewish believers, most likely congregations of Jesus’ followers living outside the land of Israel. Their meeting place is called, in the Greek, a synagogue (2:2). Their Scripture is the law and the prophets; their examples are Abraham, Rahab, Job and Elijah. Many of them were poor, and they were exploited by rich landowners who dragged them into court and held back the pay of the labourers who mowed their fields.

The letter reads less like Paul’s carefully argued letters than like the wisdom books of the Old Testament, Proverbs above all: short sayings, vivid pictures, blunt commands, and a single theme turned over from many sides. But it is wisdom filled with the teaching of Jesus. Readers have long noticed how close James stands to the Sermon on the Mount. Ask God, and it will be given. The poor are blessed, and heirs of the kingdom. Do not judge. Riches rot and are eaten by moths. Let your yes be yes and your no be no. James names Jesus only twice, but his words are everywhere.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapter 1: trials, wisdom and the word. Joy in testing; asking God for wisdom; temptation and the good gifts of the Father; and the call to do the word, not only to hear it.
  • Chapter 2: faith that works. No favouritism toward the rich; the royal law of love; and faith without works, which is dead.
  • Chapter 3: the tongue and true wisdom. The small fire that sets a forest ablaze, and the wisdom that comes from above.
  • Chapter 4: friendship with God. Quarrels and their source; humility; and the arrogance of planning without God.
  • Chapter 5: patience and prayer. Warning to the rich; waiting for the Lord’s coming; and the prayer of faith.

Trials and the God who gives

James begins where his readers were living, in hardship. His first command is startling:

Joy, not because trials are pleasant, but because God uses them to make people whole. For anyone who cannot see how, there is a simple remedy: ask. God “gives to everyone with an open hand and without scolding”. But temptation, the pull toward evil that trials can bring, never comes from God. It grows from our own desire, which conceives and bears sin, and sin full grown gives birth to death. What comes from God is only good:

— James 1:17

Hearing and doing

The best of those gifts is the word, by which God has given his people new birth. And the word is meant to be done. Anyone who only hears it, James says, is like a man who glances at his face in a mirror, walks away, and forgets what he looked like. The one who looks closely into “the perfect law of freedom” and stays with it will be blessed in what they do.

Faith without works

Chapter 2 begins in the meeting room. A man walks in with gold rings and fine clothes, and is shown to a good seat; a poor man in dirty clothes is told to stand, or sit on the floor. James is appalled. Has not God chosen the poor to be rich in faith? To show favouritism is to break “the royal law”, which is to love your neighbour as yourself.

Then comes the passage that made Luther uneasy, and that every reader of the New Testament must reckon with. A brother or sister has nothing to wear and nothing to eat, and someone says, “Go in peace; keep warm and eat well,” and gives them nothing. What use is that? “It is the same with faith: if it has no works, it is dead in itself.” Even the demons believe that God is one, and they tremble. Abraham proved his faith when he offered Isaac; Rahab proved hers when she welcomed the messengers. And James concludes:

{{OWB:JAS.2.24.s1}}

— James 2:24

Paul, in Romans, uses the same example of Abraham to make what sounds like the opposite point: “a person is declared righteous by faith, apart from works of the law” (Romans 3:28). Do the two apostles contradict each other? Most Christians, through most of the church’s history, have answered no, though they have explained it in different ways. Paul is fighting the idea that anyone can earn God’s favour; James is fighting the idea that a faith which does nothing is faith at all. Paul’s “works” are the works of the law offered as grounds for acceptance; James’s are the deeds of love that living faith cannot help doing. Paul looks at the root, James at the fruit. The Reformers summed it up in a phrase: faith alone justifies, but the faith that justifies is never alone. Catholic and Orthodox readers hear James as a reminder that faith and love work together in the whole of salvation. All agree with James’s last word on it: “Just as a body with no spirit in it is dead, so faith with no works is dead too.”

The tongue, and wisdom from above

Chapter 3 turns to speech, and James’s pictures come thick and fast. A bit in a horse’s mouth, a rudder on a great ship, a spark in a forest: small things that steer or destroy large ones. People have tamed every kind of animal, “But no human being can tame the tongue.” With the same mouth we bless God and curse those made in his likeness. “My brothers and sisters, it must not be this way.”

Friendship with God

Where do quarrels come from? From desires at war inside us. “You lack, because you do not ask.” James calls his readers “Adulterous people!”, using the prophets’ picture of Israel as God’s unfaithful bride, because “friendship with the world is hostility toward God.” But the grace God gives is greater, and the way back is open:

{{OWB:JAS.4.7.s1}} {{OWB:JAS.4.8.s1}}

— James 4:7–8

Then James rounds on the merchants making their plans for a year of profit in another city. “You are a puff of vapor, seen for a short time and then gone.” The wiser way is to say, “If the Lord wills, we will live, and we will do this or that.”

Patience and prayer

The last chapter opens with words as fierce as any in the prophets, against the rich who hoarded wealth and cheated their labourers: the cries of the harvesters “have reached the ears of the Lord of Armies.” To the oppressed James says, be patient, like a farmer waiting for the early and late rains, “for the Lord’s coming has drawn near.” Look at the prophets; look at Job.

And the letter ends in prayer. Whoever is suffering should pray; whoever is cheerful should sing. The sick should call the elders of the church to pray over them and anoint them with oil in the name of the Lord. Christians have drawn different practices from these verses: the Catholic and Orthodox churches see in them the sacrament of anointing the sick, and many Protestants, especially in the healing movements of the nineteenth century, took them as a direct promise for today. All can learn from James’s final example, Elijah, “a human being just like us”, whose prayers shut and opened the sky.

How to read James

James can be read in a quarter of an hour, and it is worth reading straight through first, to feel how its themes return: trials and patience at the beginning and end, the rich and the poor, speech, wisdom, prayer. Then read it slowly, a paragraph at a time, as you might read Proverbs, and let each saying ask its question of you. James is not a book to be agreed with. He wants to be obeyed.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in James are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Justified.” In 2:21–25 the translation keeps the familiar word “justified”. It is the same Greek verb that this translation renders “declare righteous” in Romans, so that readers comparing the two can see that Paul and James are using one word, and asking two different questions with it.

“Brothers and sisters.” James calls his readers brothers fifteen times. The translation says “brothers and sisters”, since the whole congregation is meant.

“The Lord of Armies.” In 5:4 James uses the ancient Hebrew title for God, the Lord of Sabaoth, “hosts” in older Bibles. The translation gives its meaning, “the Lord of Armies”, the God who commands the powers of heaven and hears the cry of the unpaid.

A hard verse. James 4:5 is one of the most difficult sentences in the New Testament, and Bibles translate it in very different ways. This translation reads it as speaking of God’s jealous love: “He longs with jealous love for the spirit he caused to live in us.”
