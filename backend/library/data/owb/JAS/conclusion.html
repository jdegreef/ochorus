— James 5:19–20

James ends abruptly. There is no farewell, no greeting to friends, no prayer of blessing. The last word is a charge: if someone wanders, go after them. It is a fitting end for a letter that has spent five chapters refusing to let faith stay in the head. Even its closing sentence is something to do.

The question the letter leaves

The question James leaves is the one he asked at the start: are you a hearer or a doer? It is not a question about how much a person knows. James’s readers knew that God is one; so do the demons. It is a question about the mirror. Having looked into the word and seen yourself, do you walk away and forget, or do you stay with it and let it change what you do with your money, your tongue, your plans and your neighbour in dirty clothes?

That can sound like a heavy demand, and James does not soften it. But it is not a cold one. The God of this letter gives to everyone with an open hand, and from him comes every good and perfect gift. His grace is greater than our divided hearts. “Draw close to God, and he will draw close to you.” The works James asks for are the shape a grateful faith takes, as naturally as a fig tree bears figs.

Where the letter points

It points, first, to the poor. Few books of the Bible speak so plainly about the gap between rich and poor, or side so clearly with the labourer whose pay is held back. True religion, for James, is caring for orphans and widows in their trouble. A church that seats the rich man in the best place has misunderstood its Lord.

It points, second, to patience. Its readers were waiting, as the farmer waits for the rain, for the coming of the Lord. James does not tell them when. He tells them how: steadily, without grumbling, with hearts made firm.

And it points to prayer. The letter that begins with “ask God” ends with the elders at a sickbed, believers confessing to one another, and Elijah on Carmel. For all its talk of works, James knows that the greatest work is often done on one’s knees.

Where to go from here

James has been preached on more for its single sentences than as a whole, and many of those sermons are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On trials and patience (chapter 1). A. B. Simpson’s The Practical Discipline of Life (1:2), John Wesley’s On Patience (1:4) in Sermons on Several Occasions, and Hannah Whitall Smith’s A Word to the Wavering Ones (1:6) in The God of All Comfort.
  • On hearing and doing (1:22–25). Charles G. Finney’s Self Deceivers, the first of his Lectures to Professing Christians, and Andrew Murray’s A Doer That Worketh Shall Be Blessed in Doing, in Working for God!
  • On grace and humility (chapter 4). Simpson’s God’s Jealous Love, on 4:5, in The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, Part II; and Murray’s Humility, whose chapter Humility and Exaltation takes up 4:10.
  • On waiting for the Lord (5:7–8). Simpson’s The Practical Hope of the Lord’s Coming.
  • On the prayer of faith (5:13–18). Three sermons on 5:16: Finney’s Prevailing Prayer, A. W. Tozer’s The Praying Church and Simpson’s Practical Prayer; and, on praying for the sick, Andrew Murray’s Divine Healing.
  • On bringing the wanderer back (5:19–20). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Encouragement to Soul-Winners, in The Soul-Winner, and Murray’s The Work of Soul-Saving, in Working for God!
  • On James himself. Eusebius of Caesarea’s The Church History, which tells of James the Just and his death in Jerusalem.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to James in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.
