{{OWB:PRO.1.7.s1}}

— Proverbs 1:7

Some books of the Bible take you up a mountain or into the throne room of heaven. Proverbs takes you into the kitchen, the marketplace, the field at harvest time and the gate where the elders sit. It has things to say about how you talk, how you work, how you spend your money and how long you stay in bed. It is the most down-to-earth book in Scripture, and that is exactly the point. The God who made the heavens with wisdom wants that same wisdom to shape an ordinary Tuesday.

The book is made of short sayings and longer poems gathered over several centuries, most of them in two lines that balance each other like the pans of a scale. They are easy to remember and hard to forget. And because the book has thirty-one chapters, readers have long taken it a chapter a day through the month, as this library invites you to do with its Proverbs in a Month plan.

“Proverbs”

The Hebrew name of the book, Mishlei, is its first word: “the proverbs of”. A mashal is a saying that teaches by comparison, setting one thing beside another so that each throws light on the other. The word stretches wide: a two-line saying, a long teaching poem, a riddle, even a taunt.

The book opens by naming Solomon, “David’s son, king of Israel”. The account of his reign remembers that God gave him wisdom “as vast as the sand on the seashore”, and that “He spoke 3,000 proverbs, and his songs numbered 1,005” (1 Kings 4:29, 32). Proverbs does not claim that all its sayings are his, as the next section shows. But it places the whole collection under the name of the king who became, for Israel, what wisdom looked like.

A book of collections

Read the headings and you can see the seams. Proverbs is an anthology, put together in stages, and it tells you so:

  • A father’s instruction and Wisdom’s call (chapters 1–9): long poems in which a parent speaks to a son, and Wisdom herself calls out in the streets.
  • “The proverbs of Solomon” (10:1–22:16): several hundred two-line sayings, most of them standing on their own.
  • The sayings of the wise (22:17–24:22): introduced with “Bend your ear and hear what the wise have said”, and described as “thirty sayings”.
  • More sayings of the wise (24:23–34), ending with the field of the lazy man.
  • Proverbs of Solomon copied in Hezekiah’s day (chapters 25–29), “collected and copied by the officials of Hezekiah, king of Judah”, some two centuries after Solomon.
  • The words of Agur (chapter 30), full of riddles and numbered lists.
  • The words of King Lemuel (31:1–9), “an oracle that his mother taught him”.
  • The capable wife (31:10–31), an alphabetic poem that closes the book.

Two things stand out. First, the book was still growing in the time of Hezekiah, around 700 BC, and it gathered wisdom from beyond Solomon’s court: Agur and Lemuel are otherwise unknown and may not have been Israelites at all. The “thirty sayings” of 22:17–24:22 have long been compared with an Egyptian book of instruction, the Teaching of Amenemope, which is also arranged in thirty chapters and shares some of the same advice. Israel’s sages listened to the nations, and set what they heard under the fear of the LORD.

Second, the long poems of chapters 1–9 come first for a reason. They are the door into the book, and they tell you how to read everything after them.

The fear of the LORD

The opening verses say what the book is for: “for learning wisdom and discipline, for grasping words of insight”, for “making the naive shrewd”, and even for the wise, who will “gain still more learning”. Then comes the sentence at the head of this introduction, the motto of the whole book. Knowledge begins with the fear of the LORD.

The same words return at the end of the opening poems, “Wisdom starts with the fear of the LORD” (9:10), and again in the book’s last poem, where the woman who fears the LORD “is the one to be praised” (31:30). Proverbs is framed by them.

This fear is not terror that runs from God. It is the awe that comes from knowing who he is, and it shows itself in trust and in turning from evil. Proverbs defines it plainly: “To fear the LORD is to hate evil” (8:13). And its best-known lines describe it as a whole-hearted trust:

— Proverbs 3:5–6

That is why the wisdom of Proverbs is never merely cleverness. It is skill in living, the ability “to steer their way”, as 1:5 puts it, through a world that has a grain to it because God made it.

“My son”

Chapters 1–9 are spoken by a parent. “My son, listen when your father corrects you, and do not let go of what your mother teaches you” (1:8). Again and again the voice returns with the same words, “My son”, and once it remembers being taught in turn: “When I was a boy in my father’s house”, he says, his own father told him, “Hold on to my words with all your heart; keep my commands and you will live” (4:3–4). Wisdom is handed down, from one generation to the next, like a family inheritance.

The son addressed in these poems is a young man about to make his way in the world, and the dangers set before him are a young man’s dangers: a gang that promises easy money (1:10–19), and a woman who is not his wife (chapters 5–7). But the book has always been read by daughters as well as sons, and its last chapter puts a woman at the centre.

His great picture is of two roads. One is the road of the wicked, which looks inviting at the start and ends in the dark. The other is described in one of the loveliest sayings in the book:

{{OWB:PRO.4.18.s1}}

— Proverbs 4:18

A few verses later comes the advice on which the whole road depends: “More than anything you protect, protect your heart, because the whole course of your life flows out of it” (4:23). In the Bible the heart is the centre of a person, where thinking, choosing and loving begin.

Wisdom and Folly

Into the father’s teaching steps another voice. “Wisdom shouts out in the street; in the open squares she makes her voice heard” (1:20). In Hebrew the word for wisdom is feminine, and Proverbs pictures her as a woman, a prophet in the public square, calling to anyone who will listen. She is not hidden away for the few.

In chapter 8 she speaks of where she came from. Before the mountains were shaped, before there were oceans or springs, she was there. And when the LORD set the heavens in place and gave the sea its boundary, she was beside him:

{{OWB:PRO.8.30.s1}} {{OWB:PRO.8.31.s1}}

— Proverbs 8:30–31

This is poetry, not a lecture on how the world began. Its point is that wisdom is woven into creation itself. The world was made with wisdom, so to live wisely is to live with the grain of the world, and to live foolishly is to cut against it. Wisdom’s joy in creation spills over into delight in “the human race”.

Chapter 9 brings the opening poems to a close with two invitations. Wisdom has built her house on seven pillars, prepared a feast and sent out her servants. Across the way sits the woman Folly, loud and knowing nothing, at the door of her own house. Both call out in exactly the same words: “Whoever is naive, come in here!” (9:4, 16). Wisdom offers bread and wine and life. Folly offers stolen water, which “tastes sweet”, but her guests “lie deep in Sheol”. Every reader has to decide which house to enter.

How a proverb works

From chapter 10 the poems give way to sayings, most of them two lines long. Often the second line turns the first around with a “but”: “A wise son is his father’s joy, but a foolish son is a sorrow to his mother” (10:1). Sometimes it sharpens it, sometimes it adds a picture. “Like golden apples set in silver is a word spoken at just the right moment” (25:11). The sayings are compressed on purpose, to be turned over in the mind.

It matters that they are proverbs and not promises. A proverb says how things usually go in God’s world, not what must happen in every case. “Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not leave it” (22:6) is wise and true, but it is not a guarantee that every child of faithful parents will walk with God. Proverbs itself shows this by setting side by side two sayings that seem to contradict each other:

{{OWB:PRO.26.4.s1}} {{OWB:PRO.26.5.s1}}

— Proverbs 26:4–5

Both are right, and the wise person is the one who can tell which fits the moment. Proverbs does not give you a rule book; it trains your judgement.

The book can also be funny. “As a door swings back and forth on its hinge, so the lazy one turns over in bed” (26:14). The lazy man will not go out because “There is a lion on the road!” (26:13). Agur marvels at things too wonderful to understand: an eagle in the sky, a snake on a rock, a ship at sea, “and the way a man and a young woman come together” (30:19).

Words, work, wealth and friends

Read through the sayings and certain subjects keep coming back.

  • Words. No subject is more common. “A gentle reply calms anger, but a hurtful word makes tempers flare” (15:1).
  • Work. “You lazy one, go and look at the ant; watch how it lives, and learn wisdom” (6:6). Diligence is honoured; idleness ends in a field overgrown with thorns (24:30–34).
  • Wealth and the poor. Proverbs commends honest gain and warns that riches cannot buy what matters: “Better to have little and fear the LORD than great riches with trouble” (15:16). Above all it insists that how you treat the poor is how you treat God.
  • Friendship. “A friend is loving through every season, and a brother is born to stand with you in trouble” (17:17). “As iron is sharpened against iron, so one person sharpens another” (27:17).
  • Pride and humility. “Pride comes first, then the crash; a haughty spirit, then the fall” (16:18). “The fear of the LORD teaches wisdom, and humility comes before honor” (15:33).

Our plans and God’s purpose

Proverbs is a practical book, full of advice about planning ahead, taking counsel and working hard. Yet it never lets the reader think that a well-made plan puts life under human control. Running through the sayings is a quiet confidence that God is at work in every outcome:

{{OWB:PRO.16.9.s1}}

— Proverbs 16:9

“People have many plans in their minds, but it is the purpose of the LORD that will prevail” (19:21). Even the toss of the lot is his: “The lot is tossed into the lap, but how it falls is decided by the LORD” (16:33). This is not a reason to stop planning, but to plan humbly, to “Hand over what you do to the LORD” (16:3), and to hold tomorrow loosely: “Do not brag about tomorrow; you have no idea what a single day will bring” (27:1).

Proverbs also knows that the wicked sometimes prosper for a while, and that the honest may stay poor. It says so in its “better” sayings: “Better a little gained honestly than a fortune made by injustice” (16:8). But it mostly leaves the hardest questions, why the righteous suffer and why everything seems to come to nothing, to the books that stand beside it, Job and Ecclesiastes. Together the three make up the wisdom books of the Old Testament, and each needs the others.

The capable wife

The book ends with a poem of twenty-two verses, each beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, a praise of wisdom from the first letter to the last. It opens with a question: “A capable wife, who can find her? She is worth far more than red coral” (31:10). She rises before dawn, buys a field, trades, provides for her household and opens her hand to the poor. “Strength and splendor clothe her, and she laughs at the time to come” (31:25).

Readers have heard this poem in more than one way. Many read it as the portrait of a real and admirable woman, and in Jewish homes it is sung at the table on the eve of the Sabbath in honour of the wife and mother. Others notice how closely she resembles Wisdom in chapters 1–9: she is worth more than red coral, as Wisdom is (3:15; 8:11), she speaks wisdom, and she is praised in the city gates where Wisdom called out. The book that began with Wisdom calling ends with Wisdom lived out in a home. The two readings belong together, and neither makes the poem a weight to lay on any one woman. Its last word is the book’s first:

Proverbs and the New Testament

The apostles quote Proverbs as living Scripture. James and Peter both cite 3:34, which James gives as “God sets himself against the proud, but to the humble he gives grace” (James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5). Hebrews 12:5–6 quotes the father’s words about the LORD’s discipline (3:11–12). Paul tells the Romans to feed a hungry enemy, in the words of 25:21–22 (Romans 12:20), and 2 Peter 2:22 calls 26:11 “The true proverb”.

More than that, the New Testament finds the wisdom of Proverbs fulfilled in a person. Jesus said of himself that “here is something greater than Solomon” (Matthew 12:42). Paul calls Christ “God’s power and God’s wisdom” (1 Corinthians 1:24), and says that in him “all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge lie hidden” (Colossians 2:3). When John writes that the Word was with God in the beginning and that all things came into being through him, and when Colossians says that “all things are held together in him”, many readers hear an echo of Wisdom beside the Creator in Proverbs 8.

In the fourth century that chapter became the centre of a great argument. Arius pointed to 8:22, where the ancient Greek translation has Wisdom say that the Lord created her, as proof that the Son was a creature. Athanasius answered that the verse spoke of the Son taking flesh for our sake, not of his beginning, and that the Word through whom all things were made could not himself be made. Since then, Christians have generally read Wisdom in Proverbs 8 as pointing to Christ while denying that 8:22 makes him a creature; many modern readers take the chapter first as a poem about God’s wisdom, which the New Testament shows to have a human face.

How to read Proverbs

Proverbs is best read slowly and often. A few ways in:

  • A chapter a day. With thirty-one chapters, the book fits the library’s Proverbs in a Month plan exactly. On the fourteenth, read chapter 14.
  • Read chapters 1–9 first. They are the frame for everything else. Once you have heard the father, Wisdom and Folly, the short sayings make more sense.
  • Take one saying with you. From each day’s chapter, choose one proverb and carry it through the day.
  • Remember what kind of book it is. A proverb is a true word about how life usually goes under God, not a promise for every case. Let Job and Ecclesiastes keep it honest.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Proverbs is almost entirely poetry, so this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out, and you will see the two halves of each saying stand one above the other. A few of its choices in Proverbs are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” When you see LORD in capital letters, it stands for God’s personal name, the four Hebrew letters YHWH, which this translation, like most English Bibles, follows long custom in printing as “the LORD”.

“The fear of the LORD.” The book’s motto keeps its familiar wording, “the fear of the LORD”. It means reverent awe that trusts and obeys, as 3:5–7 and 8:13 show, not dread.

“The naive.” The young person who is open to every influence, older Bibles’ “the simple”, is called “the naive”: “You naive people, how much longer will you love being naive?” (1:22). It is not an insult but a warning. The naive can still become wise, if they will listen.

“The woman who is not yours.” The woman of chapters 2, 5 and 7, older Bibles’ “strange woman”, is rendered by what she is: “the woman who is not yours to have, the stranger whose words are smooth and flattering” (2:16).
