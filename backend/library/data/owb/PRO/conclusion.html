— Proverbs 30:4

Near the end of a book full of confident advice comes a man who admits he does not know. Agur says he is “the most ignorant of all people” and has not learned “knowledge of the Holy One”. Then he asks the questions above, the oldest questions there are: who holds the wind, who set the bounds of the earth, what is his name? The book that has taught so much about how to live stops to confess that the source of all wisdom is beyond us, and must be given if it is to be known.

Agur’s answer is not more cleverness, but trust in what God has said: “Every word God speaks is proven true; he is a shield to all who take refuge in him” (30:5). Wisdom begins, and ends, with the fear of the LORD.

The question the book leaves with you

Proverbs is a book of choices. On every page two ways open up, the wise and the foolish, the righteous and the wicked, the diligent and the lazy, and the sayings describe where each one leads. At the end of the opening poems stand two houses, Wisdom’s and Folly’s, and from both doors the same call goes out to the same people: “Whoever is naive, come in here!” The question Proverbs leaves is simply which house you will enter, and whether you will keep coming back to Wisdom’s door.

That is not a choice made once. The book was meant to be read again and again, a little at a time, so that its sayings wear into the mind the way water wears a path in stone. A chapter a day is a good way to do it: in a month you will have read the whole book.

Where Proverbs points

Agur asked for the name of the one who holds the wind, “and the name of his son”. Christians have never been able to read that question without thinking of the answer the New Testament gives. Jesus taught as the wise teach, in sayings and parables, and the ancient Greek translation often used for mashal the word from which “parable” comes. He spoke of himself as “something greater than Solomon”. He invited the weary to come to him and learn, as Wisdom invites the naive to her table: “Come to me, all of you who are worn out with work and weighed down with burdens, and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28).

Paul goes further. Christ, he says, “has become God’s wisdom for us, which means our righteousness, our holiness and our redemption” (1 Corinthians 1:30). The wisdom that delighted beside the Creator and called out in the streets has come close enough to touch. And James adds a promise for anyone who reads Proverbs and feels how far short they fall: “If any of you is short of wisdom, ask God, who gives to everyone with an open hand and without scolding, and it will be given to you” (James 1:5).

Where to go from here

Preachers and teachers have mined Proverbs for practical wisdom for as long as there has been a church, and some of the best of that work is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the book’s purpose and its opening call (Proverbs 1). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon A Young Man’s Best Counsellor, on 1:1–19; and the chapter “The Holy Spirit in the Book of Proverbs” in A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, Part I, which begins with Wisdom calling in the streets.
  • On guarding the heart (Proverbs 4:23). Maclaren’s Keeping and Kept, which sets the command to keep the heart beside the promise that God keeps us.
  • On Wisdom in Proverbs 8. Athanasius’s Against the Arians, whose second discourse works through 8:22 and its context at length.
  • On friendship, courage and drifting. Charles H. Spurgeon’s A Faithful Friend (18:24), R. A. Torrey’s Heroes and Cowards (29:25), and A. W. Tozer’s Causes of Backsliding (14:14).
  • On humility under God’s hand (Proverbs 16:19). Thomas Boston’s The Crook in the Lot, whose fourth part takes the saying that it is better to be humble among the lowly.
  • On wisdom for every day. Hannah Whitall Smith’s chapter “Proverbs: Guidance” in Old Testament Types and Teachings; and Spurgeon’s John Ploughman’s Talk, proverb-wisdom in plain country speech on idleness, debt, gossip and home.
  • Ochorus Originals. Growing in Wisdom, a guide for students built on the sayings of Proverbs; Clothed with Strength and Dignity, for women, which takes its title from 31:25 and returns to the capable wife throughout; and, for reading aloud with children, Tell Me the Story – Book 9: Songs and Wise Words, with chapters on the ant, a gentle answer and a friend who sticks close.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Proverbs in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
