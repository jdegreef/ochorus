Exodus does not end in the promised land. It ends in the wilderness, at the foot of a mountain, with a tent and a cloud. The slaves are free, the covenant is made, and God has come to live among them. But the journey has hardly begun, and the last chapter says so: the cloud will rise, and they will move on, “at every stage of their journeys.”

The question the book leaves with you

The deepest question in Exodus is not whether God can defeat Pharaoh. It is whether a holy God can live among the people he has rescued. The book’s answer is yes, but at a cost, and not yet completely. At the burning bush Moses had to take off his sandals. At Sinai the people stood back at a distance and begged not to hear God’s voice. After the golden calf God said he would not go up among them, “since you are a stiff-necked people”, and only Moses’ prayer changed that. And in the very last scene, when the glory fills the tabernacle, even Moses cannot go in.

So the book leaves its readers with Moses’ own question: will God go with us? “Unless your presence goes with us, do not make us leave this place.” And with a second question hidden inside the first: how can sinful people come near to him and live? The next book, Leviticus, opens at the door of that tent, “From the tent of meeting the LORD called to Moses”, and spends itself on the answer.

Where the story goes next

The exodus became the pattern for every later rescue in the Bible. The psalmists sing it, the prophets promise a new one, and when the first Christians wanted to say what Jesus had done, they reached for its words. John writes that “The Word became flesh and made his home among us. We have seen his glory”, using a word that recalls the tent where the glory dwelt. Luke says that on the mountain of the transfiguration Moses and Elijah spoke with Jesus of “the exodus he was about to complete in Jerusalem.” Paul calls Christ our Passover lamb, and says of the rock in the wilderness, “and that rock was Christ.”

And the story ends where Exodus was always heading. In the last book of the Bible, those who have overcome stand beside a sea of glass and “sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb”, and a voice from the throne announces what the tabernacle only foreshadowed:

“See, God’s dwelling is with human beings! He will make his home with them, and they will be his peoples; God himself will be with them.”

— Revelation 21:3

Where to go from here

Exodus has fed Christian preaching, prayer and teaching for as long as there has been a church. A few places to begin in this library:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Exodus: God’s Redemption” reads the book as the story of God’s way of redeeming his people, set beside Romans 3.
  • On the God who hears the oppressed (Exodus 3). Absalom Jones’s A Thanksgiving Sermon on the Abolition of the African Slave Trade, preached in Philadelphia on 1 January 1808 on Exodus 3:7–8; and Gareth Evans’s Four Reasons We Need Revival in North America, which begins with Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:1–6).
  • On the rock in the wilderness (Exodus 17). Christmas Evans’s The Smitten Rock, on 1 Corinthians 10:4, which follows the rock struck at Horeb to Calvary.
  • On the covenant and the Ten Commandments (Exodus 19–20). Andrew Murray’s The Two Covenants, whose chapter “The First Covenant” begins from Exodus 19:5; Thomas Watson’s The Ten Commandments; Martin Luther’s Large Catechism, which begins with the commandments one by one; and Book 2, Chapter 8 of John Calvin’s Institutes of the Christian Religion, “Exposition of the Moral Law”.
  • On the tabernacle (Exodus 25–40). Gareth Evans’s Burdens and Gifts (Exodus 25:40); and Horatius Bonar’s The Rent Veil, which moves from the veil of the tabernacle to the veil torn at the cross.
  • On Moses the intercessor (Exodus 32–34). E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, with its chapter “Moses, the Mighty Intercessor”; Andrew Murray’s The Inner Chamber, with “Moses, the Man of Prayer”; Gareth Evans’s Broken Before the Throne (Exodus 33:18–23); and A. W. Tozer’s The Hidden Life of Faith, on the cleft of the rock (Exodus 33:22).
  • On the faith of Moses. J. C. Ryle’s Holiness, with its chapter “Moses — An Example”, on Hebrews 11:24–26.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Exodus in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
