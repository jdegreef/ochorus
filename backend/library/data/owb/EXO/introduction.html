— Exodus 3:14

Exodus is the Bible’s great story of rescue. In Hebrew it is called Shemot, “Names”, from its opening words, “These are the names of the sons of Israel who came to Egypt with Jacob.” The name we use comes from the ancient Greek translation and means “going out”, or “the way out”. Both names are fitting. The book begins with a list of names, a family of seventy people, and it ends with a nation camped around the dwelling place of God. In between is the way out.

It picks up exactly where Genesis left off. Joseph has died in Egypt, asking to be carried home when God comes to help his people. Exodus tells how God came. But it tells much more than an escape. The people who are led out of slavery are led to a mountain, where God binds himself to them by covenant, and then he does something stranger still: he tells them to build him a tent, so that he can live among them on the road.

That is the shape of the whole book. God hears, God rescues, God speaks, and God comes to stay.

Three places

Exodus is easiest to follow by where it happens. Chapters 1–15 are set in Egypt and at its border: the slavery, the call of Moses, the plagues, the Passover night and the crossing of the sea. Chapters 15–18 are a journey through the wilderness, with hunger, thirst, battle and a father-in-law’s good advice. And chapters 19–40 take place at the foot of Mount Sinai, where the people stay for the rest of the book.

The proportions are surprising. More than half the book is spent at Sinai, on laws and on the plans for the tabernacle, the portable sanctuary, given in chapters 25–31 and carried out almost word for word in chapters 35–40. Between the plan and the building comes the worst moment in the book, the golden calf. Exodus thinks that what God does with a rescued people matters as much as the rescue itself.

A king who had never heard of Joseph

The book opens with a blessing coming true. The Israelites “were fruitful; they teemed and multiplied”, echoing God’s blessing at creation. Then a new king comes to the throne, “one who had never heard of Joseph”, and sees in that growth only a threat. He enslaves them, and when that does not work, he orders every Hebrew boy thrown into the Nile.

The first people in Exodus to resist him are two midwives, Shiphrah and Puah. They are named; the Pharaoh is not. “But the midwives feared God”, and they kept the boys alive. Then comes a chain of women who outwit the most powerful man in the world: a mother who hides her son, a sister who watches from the reeds, and Pharaoh’s own daughter, who takes pity on a crying Hebrew baby and raises him in the palace. She names him Moses, “because,” she says, “I pulled him out of the water.”

Moses grows up, kills an Egyptian he sees beating a Hebrew, and has to flee. He spends years as a shepherd in Midian. And then the narrator turns back to Egypt with a few short sentences that change everything:

The bush and the name

At Horeb, the mountain of God, Moses sees a bush on fire that does not burn away, and turns aside to look. God calls him by name, tells him to take off his sandals because “the place where you stand is holy ground”, and says that he has seen the misery of his people: “I have come down to rescue them.”

Moses objects, again and again. “Who am I to go to Pharaoh, and to bring the Israelites out of Egypt?” God answers not by telling Moses who he is but by promising who will go with him: “I will be with you.” Then Moses asks what he should say when the people ask God’s name, and God answers with the sentence at the head of this introduction, “I AM WHO I AM.” The words come from the Hebrew verb “to be”, and the personal name of God, written in this translation as “the LORD”, is closely related to them. They have been read in more than one way: as God’s faithful presence (“I will be who I will be”), as his freedom from anyone’s control, and as his being itself, the one who simply is. They may mean all of these. God goes on: “That is my name for all time.”

Moses has more excuses, until he says plainly, “Please, Lord, send someone else.” God is angry, but he gives him his brother Aaron to speak for him, and sends him back.

“Release my people”

Pharaoh’s first answer sets the terms of everything that follows: “Who is the LORD, that I should do what he says and release Israel? I do not know the LORD.” The plagues are God’s reply. Water turns to blood; frogs, gnats, flies, disease, boils, hail, locusts and darkness fall on Egypt, until the tenth and last. Again and again the purpose is stated: “the Egyptians will know that I am the LORD.” The Nile, the land, the sun: the powers Egypt worshiped are shown to be creatures, and against “all the gods of Egypt” the LORD carries out judgment.

Readers have always noticed that Pharaoh’s stubbornness is described in two ways. Sometimes Pharaoh hardens his own heart; sometimes the LORD hardens it; sometimes it simply stays stubborn. Christians have weighed these sentences differently, some stressing God’s sovereign purpose (Paul quotes 9:16 in Romans 9:17), others that God confirmed a choice Pharaoh had already made. The text holds both together and does not let either Pharaoh or the reader off the hook.

The Passover

Before the last plague God gives Israel a meal. Each household is to kill a lamb, “a sound, unblemished male in its first year”, put its blood on the doorposts, and eat it in haste, dressed for the road, “robes tucked into your belts, sandals on, staffs in hand.”

{{OWB:EXO.12.13.s1}}

— Exodus 12:13

Notice that the meal is designed to be repeated, and to be explained. “In time your children will say to you, ‘What is this service of yours about?’” The story of the exodus is meant to be told at the table, by parents to children, in every generation. And notice that the deliverance comes through blood and through a substitute: the firstborn of Israel live because a lamb has died. The apostle Paul could write, with no further explanation, “For Christ has been sacrificed as our Passover lamb.” Jesus himself chose the Passover meal to give his disciples the bread and the cup.

Through the sea

Pharaoh lets the people go, then changes his mind, and the Israelites find themselves trapped between the sea and the chariots of Egypt. They panic, and turn on Moses with a bitter joke: “Did Egypt have no graves, that you had to bring us out here to die in the wilderness?” His answer is one of the great sentences of faith:

Bread from heaven

Three days later they are grumbling. The water at Marah is bitter; the food has run out; they look back on Egypt, of all places, with longing: “There we sat beside the pots of meat.” The wilderness chapters are honest about how quickly rescued people forget. They are just as honest about how patient God is. He makes the bitter water sweet. He rains down bread from heaven, a strange flaky food the people call manna, from their own question, “What is it?” Each day there is enough for that day, and on the sixth day enough for the Sabbath as well.

At Rephidim there is no water, and the people quarrel with Moses and put the LORD to the test by asking, “Is the LORD among us or not?” Moses strikes the rock at Horeb, and water pours out. Then the Amalekites attack, and Israel prevails only while Moses holds up his hands, until Aaron and Hur sit him on a stone and hold them up for him “until the sun set.” Even Moses cannot do it alone, as Jethro tells him in chapter 18: “The load is too heavy for you; you cannot carry it by yourself.”

Eagles’ wings: the covenant at Sinai

In the third month the people reach Sinai, and God speaks to them from the mountain. Before he gives them a single command, he reminds them what he has already done: “how I lifted you up on eagles’ wings and brought you here to me.” Only then does he offer them a covenant, to be his “own special treasure”, “a kingdom of priests and a holy nation”. The order matters. The law is not the way Israel earns God’s rescue. It is the shape of life for people who have already been rescued.

The Ten Commandments themselves begin the same way: “I am the LORD your God. I brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house where you were slaves.” Then come the words that Jews and Christians have learned by heart: no other gods, no idols, no misuse of the name, the Sabbath day, honour to parents, and no murder, adultery, theft, false evidence or coveting. The first commands are about God, the rest about the neighbour, and Jesus would later sum up the whole law in love for both.

Chapters 21–23, often called the Book of the Covenant, apply those commands to the life of a farming people: slaves and their release, injuries, property, loans and justice in court. Some of these laws are hard for modern readers, and they come from a world very unlike ours. But again and again they turn to the weak, with a reason drawn from Israel’s own story:

And the elders go up the mountain, and see the God of Israel, “and they ate and drank.” Centuries later, at another meal, Jesus took a cup and said, “This is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many.”

A tent for God

Moses goes up into the cloud for forty days, and what God gives him there is not more law but a building plan:

{{OWB:EXO.25.8.s1}}

— Exodus 25:8

Readers often stall in these chapters, with their curtains, acacia wood and gold. But they are among the most moving in the book, once you see what they are for. The God who thundered on the mountain means to travel with his people in a tent. At its centre is the ark, and over the ark the mercy seat, where God says, “I will meet with you there.” The priests’ garments, the lampstand, the altar of incense: everything is made to “the pattern” God shows Moses, as though the tent were a copy of something greater. And the reason is given plainly: “I brought them out of the land of Egypt for this purpose, to make my home among them.” The goal of the exodus was not only freedom from Pharaoh. It was life with God.

The golden calf

While God is giving Moses the plans for his dwelling, the people at the foot of the mountain are making a god of their own. Moses has been gone too long; they want gods “to lead the way for us”. Aaron collects their gold and makes a calf, and they cry, “Israel, these are your gods, who brought you up from the land of Egypt!” The covenant is broken within weeks of being made, and Moses, coming down the mountain, smashes the tablets.

What follows is one of the great passages on prayer in the Bible. God tells Moses to let him alone, that he may destroy the people and make a new nation from Moses. Moses will not let him alone. He pleads God’s reputation among the Egyptians and God’s promises to Abraham, Isaac and Israel, and “the LORD relented from the harm he had said he would do to his people.” He even offers himself: if God will not forgive them, “then blot me out of your book that you have written.”

The crisis is now about presence. God offers to send an angel ahead of them, but not to go himself. Moses answers, “Unless your presence goes with us, do not make us leave this place.” In the tent outside the camp, the LORD speaks with him “face to face, the way a person talks with a friend.” And Moses asks for the boldest thing of all: “Please, let me see your glory.” God hides him in a cleft of the rock, covers him with his hand, and passes by, proclaiming his own name:

{{OWB:EXO.34.6.s1}} {{OWB:EXO.34.7.s1}}

— Exodus 34:6–7

Few passages are echoed more often within the Old Testament itself. It echoes through the Psalms and the prophets, from Joel to Jonah. It is God’s own account of his character, given at the moment his people least deserved it, and it holds mercy and justice together without letting go of either.

Glory fills the tent

The covenant is renewed, and Moses comes down with his face shining, so that he has to cover it with a veil. Then the people bring their gifts, more than enough, and the tabernacle is built exactly as God commanded. The last scene of the book is the answer to everything that went before:

— Exodus 40:34

The book that began with God hearing the groans of slaves ends with God living in their camp, “at every stage of their travels.”

How to read Exodus

Read the story chapters, 1–20 and 32–34, at a good pace; they are some of the most gripping narrative in Scripture. Take the laws of chapters 21–23 slowly and ask what each one protects. In the tabernacle chapters, keep asking the question they answer: how can a holy God live among a sinful people? Leviticus, the next book, takes up that question at length.

Exodus does not name its author. It says that Moses wrote down some of its words (17:14; 24:4; 34:27), and Jewish and Christian tradition has received it as the second of the five books of Moses. Many scholars think it was drawn together from older sources and reached its final form much later. There is also debate about when the exodus happened: some date it to the fifteenth century BC, from the 480 years of 1 Kings 6:1; others to the thirteenth, from the city of Rameses named in 1:11. Christians hold a range of views on these questions, while agreeing that the God who acts in this book is the God who raised Jesus from the dead.

Watch for the echoes. Moses is drawn out of the water in an “ark”, the same word as Noah’s. The plagues undo creation, and the sea is split as the waters were divided in Genesis 1. Israel is called God’s firstborn son (4:22), and the firstborn of Egypt die. The people complain in the wilderness, and Moses prays for them. And notice how often the book tells you that something is to be remembered, retold, explained to children. Exodus means to become your story too.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Exodus are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” Exodus is the book in which God tells Moses his personal name, the four Hebrew letters often written Yahweh. Following long Jewish and Christian custom, this translation prints it as “the LORD” in capitals. At the burning bush it keeps the familiar “I AM WHO I AM” for God’s explanation of the name.

“Faithful love.” The Hebrew word hesed, older Bibles’ “mercy” or “steadfast love”, is the love that keeps covenant. Here it is “faithful love”: God shows it “to thousands who love me and keep my commandments”, and is “rich in faithful love and in truth.”

“The Ten Words.” What we call the Ten Commandments the Hebrew calls “the Ten Words”, and the translation keeps that name where it occurs (34:28).

Measures and names. Ancient measures are given in modern terms, so a day’s manna is “about 2 quarts” for every person. The sea Israel crossed keeps its long-familiar name, the Red Sea.
