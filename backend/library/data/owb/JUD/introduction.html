— Jude 1:3

Jude meant to write a different letter. He tells us so in his first lines: he was doing all he could to write about “the salvation we hold in common”, the joyful things all believers share. But news reached him that changed his plans, and he wrote this instead, a short, urgent, fiery warning, crowded with images and with names from Israel’s past.

It is one of the least read books of the New Testament, and one of the most often quoted. Many people who could not find it in their Bibles know its last two verses by heart, because churches have sung and spoken them as a blessing for centuries. Between the warning and the blessing lies a letter that is far richer than its size, and far more hopeful than its reputation.

A servant, and a brother

The writer introduces himself as “a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother of James”. Only one James in the early church needed no further description: James of Jerusalem, the brother of Jesus. The Gospels name Judas (in English, Jude) among Jesus’s brothers as well (Mark 6:3). So the writer of this letter grew up in the same house as Jesus, and yet he does not call himself the Lord’s brother. He calls himself his servant. That is a quiet act of faith, and of humility.

We know almost nothing else about him. The historian Eusebius preserves a story, from the second-century writer Hegesippus, that the Emperor Domitian once had Jude’s grandsons brought before him because they were descendants of David, and let them go when he saw their calloused farmers’ hands. Some scholars think the letter was written later by someone in Jude’s circle, using his name; most who accept it as his date it somewhere between the 50s and the 80s of the first century. It may have been written to Jewish Christians, who would know every story it alludes to.

Why he wrote

Some people, Jude says, “have slipped in among you without being noticed” (1:4). He describes them as “ungodly people who twist our God’s grace into an excuse for shameless living, and who disown Jesus Christ, our only Master and Lord.” They were not outsiders attacking the church but insiders eating at its table, and their error was moral as much as doctrinal. They took the freedom of grace and made it a licence. They claimed visions and spiritual insight, rejected authority, and grumbled and flattered their way to what they wanted.

So Jude calls his readers to “fight hard for the faith that was handed over to God’s holy people once for all.” The faith, in this sentence, is not our act of believing but what is believed: the message about Jesus, given once and not to be revised.

Three warnings from the past

Jude reminds his readers of things they already know (1:5–7). God saved a people out of Egypt and afterward destroyed those who did not believe. Angels who abandoned their proper place are kept for judgment. Sodom and Gomorrah lie before us as an example. Each of the three had been richly blessed and turned from it, and each stands as a warning that privilege is not the same as faithfulness.

Later he names three more, in a single cry of woe:

Cain, the jealous brother; Balaam, the prophet who could be bought; Korah, who rebelled against the leaders God had given. Jude says the intruders have done all three, and he writes as though their fate were already sealed.

Reefs, clouds, trees, waves, stars

Then comes a burst of pictures from the natural world, among the most vivid in the Bible (1:12–13). The intruders are “like reefs hidden under the water” at the church’s love feasts, waiting to wreck the unwary. They are “clouds that bring no rain”, promising much and giving nothing; “trees in late autumn with no fruit, twice dead and pulled up by the roots”; “wild waves of the sea, churning up their shameful deeds like foam”; “wandering stars”, which cannot be steered by. The images sweep across sea, sky and land. Wherever you look, there is something that seems to promise life and cannot give it.

Michael and Enoch

Two verses in Jude have puzzled readers more than any others. In verse 9 he tells how Michael the archangel, disputing with the devil over the body of Moses, did not dare to slander him, but said only, “May the Lord rebuke you.” The story is not in the Old Testament. Origen, early in the third century, says it comes from a book called the Ascension of Moses, which has not survived complete.

In verses 14–15 Jude quotes “Enoch, the seventh in line from Adam”, with words that closely match a passage in the book now called 1 Enoch, a Jewish writing well known in Jude’s day. Some in the early church, Jerome tells us, hesitated over Jude because of the quotation from Enoch; Eusebius lists it among the books disputed by some, though recognised by many. Christians have generally understood that quoting a book does not make it Scripture, any more than Paul made the Greek poets Scripture by quoting them. Jude uses stories his readers knew and honoured to make a point the whole Bible makes: God judges the ungodly, and humility, not slander, is the way of those who belong to him.

But you, dear friends

The turn comes in verse 17, with the words “But you, dear friends”. Jude has spent enough time on the intruders. Now he tells his readers what to do, and it is not to argue, but to build, pray, keep and wait:

— Jude 1:20–21

And they are to show mercy, even to those who have been led astray. “Be merciful to some who are wavering. Save others by pulling them out of the fire.” The fight for the faith is not a fight against the people who are going wrong. It is a fight for them.

Kept

One word ties the letter together. Jude writes to those who are “kept safe for Jesus Christ” (1:1). The rebel angels did not keep their place, and are kept for judgment (1:6); the blackest darkness has been kept for the wandering stars (1:13). And the readers are told, “Keep yourselves within God’s love” (1:21). Then, in the final blessing, the one who keeps is God himself, “the one who is able to guard you from stumbling” (1:24). We are to keep ourselves in his love; but it is he who keeps us.

How to read Jude

Read it with the Old Testament stories it names in mind, and with 2 Peter 2 beside it. The two passages share much of their material, and most scholars think 2 Peter drew on Jude, though some think the reverse, or that both drew on a common source. Notice the letter’s threes: mercy, peace and love; three warnings, three villains, three kinds of mercy. Notice how much of it is spent on hope. And do not skip to the doxology too quickly. It shines brightest at the end of the dark letter that leads to it.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Jude are worth knowing about.

“Jesus saved a people.” In verse 5 the manuscripts differ more than anywhere else in the letter, between “Jesus”, “the Lord” and “God”. This translation reads “Jesus”, the harder reading and that of some important early manuscripts, so that Jude speaks of Jesus as the one who brought Israel out of Egypt.

“Reefs hidden under the water.” In verse 12, where older Bibles say “spots”, this translation reads the Greek word as hidden rocks, a danger to ships.

“Fight hard for the faith.” In verse 3, where older Bibles say “earnestly contend”, it uses plain words for a strenuous struggle. And where the Greek says “Christ”, the Open Word Bible lets a reader choose “Christ” or “the Messiah”; this edition prints “Christ”.
