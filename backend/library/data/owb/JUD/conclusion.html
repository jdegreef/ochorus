— Jude 1:24–25

No letter in the New Testament ends more gloriously than this one, and none has a darker road leading up to it. Jude has written of intruders, judgment, fire and the blackest darkness. Then, in one long sentence, he lifts his readers’ eyes from all of it to God, who is able to bring them safely home and set them, without blemish and rejoicing, in the presence of his glory.

The question the book leaves with you

Jude asks whether we will hold on to the faith once handed over, or let it be quietly reshaped into something more comfortable. The intruders did not announce that they were abandoning Christ. They simply turned grace into an excuse, and went on eating at the love feasts. Jude’s warning is that this can happen to any church, without anyone noticing, unless someone cares enough to say so.

But the letter’s deeper question is where our confidence rests. Jude tells his readers to keep themselves in God’s love, and he means it. Yet he does not end by telling them to be strong. He ends by telling them who is strong. The faith is worth fighting for because God is able to keep those who hold it.

Where to go from here

Jude has been less preached than almost any book of the Bible, but its best verses run through the church’s classics, and several are in this library:

  • On the faith handed over (1:3). J. C. Ryle’s Knots Untied, whose papers on “divers and strange doctrines” and on the fallibility of ministers take Jude’s call to contend for the faith as a plain Christian duty.
  • On praying in the Holy Spirit (1:20). R. A. Torrey’s The Person and Work of the Holy Spirit, which calls this verse one of the most deeply significant in the Bible on prayer, and the reading for October 8 in Charles H. Spurgeon’s Evening by Evening.
  • On the one who is able to keep you (1:24). Spurgeon’s reading for October 9 in Morning by Morning.
  • On the man and his letter. Eusebius of Caesarea’s The Church History, which tells the story of Jude’s grandsons before Domitian and how the letter was received; and Origen’s On First Principles, which names the book behind the story of Michael and the body of Moses.
  • On the teachers Jude warned against. Irenaeus of Lyons’s Against Heresies, which describes a later sect that honoured Cain, Korah and the people of Sodom, the very names Jude holds up as warnings.

But the best next step may be to learn the doxology by heart. Christians have spoken it over one another for nearly two thousand years, at the end of services and at partings. Say it slowly, and let each phrase land: able to guard you; without blemish; rejoicing greatly; to God alone.
