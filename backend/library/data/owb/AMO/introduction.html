{{OWB:AMO.5.24.s1}}

— Amos 5:24

Amos was not a professional prophet, and he said so. He was a herdsman from Tekoa, a village in the hills of Judah south of Bethlehem, a man who bred sheep and tended sycamore-fig trees. Then the LORD took him from the flock and sent him north, across the border into the kingdom of Israel, to tell a prosperous and very religious people that God was about to bring their world to an end.

His book opens with a roar: “The LORD roars from Zion and thunders from Jerusalem; the shepherds’ pastures dry up, and the summit of Carmel withers.” A shepherd knows what a lion’s roar means. It means the lion has already found its prey.

A herdsman at the king’s sanctuary

The first verse dates Amos’s words to the reigns of Uzziah king of Judah and Jeroboam son of Joash king of Israel, “two years before the earthquake”. The earthquake was remembered for centuries; the prophet Zechariah still speaks of people fleeing from it. Most scholars place Amos’s preaching around 760 BC, a little before Hosea began, in the long and successful reign of Jeroboam II.

Those were good years for Israel, or seemed so. Its borders had been pushed back almost to where they had been in Solomon’s day; trade flourished; the wealthy built winter houses and summer houses, “houses of ivory”, and lay on couches eating the best lamb and drinking wine by the bowlful. The shrines at Bethel and Gilgal were crowded with worshippers and offerings. Everyone agreed that God was on their side.

But the wealth was not shared, and Amos saw who paid for it. The poor were sold into slavery for debts as small as the price of a pair of sandals; merchants cheated with false weights; the courts took bribes; the needy were pushed aside at the gate where justice was meant to be done. Amos preached at Bethel, the royal sanctuary, and the priest there, Amaziah, finally sent word to the king that “The land cannot endure all his words.”

Three crimes, even four

The book’s first two chapters are a masterpiece of preaching. Amos begins by pronouncing judgment on Israel’s neighbours, one by one, each in the same pattern: “For three crimes of Damascus, even for four, I will not hold back the punishment.” Damascus for its cruelty in war; Gaza and Tyre for selling whole communities into slavery; Edom for hunting down his brother with the sword; Ammon for ripping open pregnant women to enlarge its borders; Moab for desecrating a king’s bones.

We can imagine his audience at Bethel applauding. The circle closes on the nations around them, and then on Judah, their rival to the south. And then, without warning, it closes on them:

{{OWB:AMO.2.6.s1}}

— Amos 2:6

The neighbours were condemned for atrocities in war. Israel is condemned for what it does to its own poor in peacetime, and it receives the longest sentence of all. The nations sinned against common decency; Israel sinned against the God who brought it out of Egypt and gave it prophets, and against the poor whom his law protected.

“You alone have I known”

Israel believed that being God’s chosen people made it safe. Amos turns that belief inside out:

{{OWB:AMO.3.2.s1}}

— Amos 3:2

To be known by God, chosen and loved by him, is not a shelter from his judgment but the reason for it. The more light a people has been given, the more is asked of it. Amos follows this with a string of questions, each about cause and effect: does a lion roar with no prey, does a trap spring with nothing in it? So too with him: “The lion has roared; who will not be afraid? The Lord GOD has spoken; who can keep from prophesying?” He preached because he could not keep silent.

“Yet you did not return to me”

Chapter 4 begins with the wealthy women of Samaria, whom Amos calls “cows of Bashan”, after the famous fat cattle of the plain east of the Jordan: they “oppress the poor and crush the needy” and tell their husbands, “Bring us something to drink!” Then it mocks the busy worship of the shrines: “Go to Bethel and rebel!”

Next comes one of the saddest passages in the prophets. God lists the warnings he has already sent, famine, drought, blight, locusts, plague, war, and after each the same refrain tolls like a bell: “Yet you did not return to me, declares the LORD.” Five times it sounds. And then there is nothing left but this:

{{OWB:AMO.4.12.s1}}

— Amos 4:12

Amos does not leave it as a threat. He follows it at once with a hymn to the God they must meet: the one “who forms the mountains and creates the wind”, who “turns the dawn into darkness”. Short hymns like this one break into the book three times (4:13; 5:8–9; 9:5–6), each crowned with his name: “the LORD is his name”. The God of justice is the maker of the stars.

“Seek me and live”

Chapter 5 opens with a funeral song, sung over a nation that still thinks itself alive: “Fallen, never to rise again, is Virgin Israel.” Yet inside the lament is an invitation, repeated three times: “Seek me and live!” “Seek the LORD and live”. “Seek good and not evil, so that you may live”. To seek God, Amos insists, is not to go on pilgrimage to Bethel or Gilgal or Beersheba. It is to “Hate evil and love good; establish justice in the courts.” Even then he offers no easy promise, only a hope: “It may be that the LORD God of Armies will be gracious to the remnant of Joseph.”

Many in Israel longed for the day of the LORD, expecting it to be the day God would defeat their enemies. Amos tells them that it will be like a man who runs from a lion and meets a bear, then gets home, leans his hand against the wall, and is bitten by a snake:

{{OWB:AMO.5.18.s1}}

— Amos 5:18

And then comes the most famous passage in the book. God speaks of Israel’s festivals, offerings and songs, and his words are blunt: “I hate, I despise your festivals”; “Take the noise of your songs away from me”. What he wants instead is the verse at the head of this introduction, justice rolling on like water, righteousness like a stream that never runs dry. In a land where streambeds dry up in summer, Amos asks for a justice that never fails. In 1963 Martin Luther King Jr. took up this verse in Washington, in his speech “I Have a Dream”, and it has been a watchword of the struggle for justice ever since.

At ease in Zion

Chapter 6 turns to the leaders, “the complacent in Zion” and “those who feel secure on the mountain of Samaria”. They lie on beds inlaid with ivory, improvise songs to the harp, and anoint themselves with the finest oils, “but they do not grieve over the ruin of Joseph.” Amos’s charge is not only that they did wrong. It is that they did not care. So they will be the first to go into exile.

Five visions

The last three chapters are built around five visions. In the first two, God shows Amos a swarm of locusts and a consuming fire, and twice Amos prays, “How can Jacob survive? He is so small.” Twice the LORD relents. Amos is a prophet of judgment, but he is first an intercessor.

In the third vision the Lord stands beside a wall with a plumb line in his hand, the builder’s tool for testing whether a wall is straight. Israel has been measured, and it is crooked. This time there is no reprieve: “I will no longer pass them by.”

Here the story breaks in. Amaziah the priest of Bethel tells Amos to go home to Judah and do his prophesying there, “for it is the king’s sanctuary and a royal temple.” Amos’s answer is one of the great statements of a prophet’s calling:

{{OWB:AMO.7.14.s1}} {{OWB:AMO.7.15.s1}}

— Amos 7:14–15

The fourth vision is a basket of summer fruit. The point is a pun: the Hebrew for “summer fruit”, qayits, sounds like the word for “end”, qets. Israel is ripe, and “The end has come upon my people Israel.” With it comes one of the most chilling threats in Scripture, not a famine of bread but “for hearing the words of the LORD”: people will run back and forth searching for God’s word, “but they will not find it.” In the fifth vision Amos sees the Lord himself standing by the altar, commanding the shrine to be struck down. There is nowhere to hide from him, whether in Sheol, in the heavens or at the bottom of the sea.

The fallen booth of David

Then, in the book’s last verses, the light breaks through. God will shake Israel among the nations as grain is shaken in a sieve, but he will not destroy the house of Jacob completely. And after the judgment:

{{OWB:AMO.9.11.s1}} {{OWB:AMO.9.12.s1}}

— Amos 9:11–12

The book that began with withered pastures ends with mountains dripping sweet wine, ruined cities rebuilt, and a people planted in their land never to be uprooted again. Some scholars think these closing verses were added after the exile, because their tone is so different from what comes before; others answer that a prophet who prayed for Jacob could also foresee his healing, and that judgment without hope would not be the God of Amos at all. When the apostles met in Jerusalem to decide whether Gentile believers must keep the law of Moses, James quoted this promise, in a form close to the ancient Greek translation, as foretelling the very thing they were seeing: “so that the rest of humankind may seek the Lord, all the nations that are called by my name”.

How to read Amos

Amos is a book for reading aloud. Its patterns, the “For three crimes…, even for four”, the refrain “Yet you did not return to me”, the repeated “Seek”, are made for the ear. Read chapters 1–2 in one go to feel the trap closing; chapters 3–6 as a series of sermons, most beginning “Hear this word” or “Woe”; and chapters 7–9 as the visions, with the clash at Bethel in the middle.

As you read, notice how concrete Amos is. He names the sandals, the false weights, the garments taken in pledge, the ivory couches. Sin, for him, is never an abstraction; it has victims. And notice that the same prophet who denounces empty worship also sings the greatest hymns in the book. Amos does not want less worship. He wants worship that cannot be separated from the way we treat the poor.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Most of Amos is poetry, and this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out; the first four visions and the story of Amaziah are printed as prose.

“The LORD” and “the Lord GOD”. Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation writes “the LORD”. Amos often joins it to the word for “Lord” or “master”, and then, as in most English Bibles, it reads “the Lord GOD”. The title that older Bibles render “the LORD God of hosts” is translated “the LORD God of Armies”.

Three crimes and four. The refrain of chapters 1–2 keeps the Hebrew’s numbers, “For three crimes of Damascus, even for four”, rather than smoothing them away. It is a way of saying “crime upon crime”: the measure is full and more than full.

Amos’s trade. The first verse calls him one of “the sheep breeders of Tekoa”, and in 7:14 he says, “I am a herdsman, and I take care of sycamore-fig trees”, a kind of fig that has to be pierced to ripen.
