— Amos 9:15

Amos ends with a promise of roots. A herdsman who spent his life among growing things, figs and flocks and pastures, closes his book with God as a farmer planting his people in their land so deeply that nothing will pull them up again. It is a surprising last word from the sternest of the prophets, and it changes how the rest of the book sounds.

The question the book leaves with you

Amos asks what God sees when he looks at our worship. Israel’s festivals were crowded and its songs were loud, and God would not listen to them, because the same people who sang in the sanctuary sold the needy for a pair of sandals on the way home. Amos will not let us separate the two. He asks whether our religion has anything to do with the courts, the markets, the wages, the weights and measures, the people at the gate who have no one to speak for them.

And he asks whether we are at ease. His harshest words are kept not for open unbelievers but for the comfortable, for those who “do not grieve over the ruin of Joseph”. The opposite of the complacency he condemns is not frantic activity but grief, the kind that moved Amos himself to pray, “How can Jacob survive? He is so small.”

Where the story goes next

Within forty years of Amos’s preaching, the northern kingdom was gone. The Assyrians took Samaria in 722 BC, and the people Amos warned were carried into exile “beyond Damascus”, as he had said. The shrine at Bethel was later torn down by King Josiah of Judah.

But the promise of “the fallen booth of David” did not fall with it. The New Testament hears it kept in Jesus, the son of David, and in the church gathered from every nation. When James quoted it at the council in Jerusalem, he was explaining why Gentiles who turned to God did not have to become Jews first: the rebuilt house of David was always meant to have room for the nations.

Where to go from here

  • On walking with God (Amos 3:3). A. W. Tozer’s sermon How to Cultivate the Holy Spirit’s Companionship, which takes as its text Amos’s question, “Do two people walk together unless they have agreed to meet?”, and asks what it means to be agreed with God.
  • On God revealing his plans to the prophets (Amos 3:7). The chapter “The Prophets: The Revelation of God’s Secrets” in Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, which takes its title from this verse and traces the promises of restoration through all the prophets, Amos 9 among them.
  • On the sieve (Amos 9:9). Charles H. Spurgeon’s reading for June 20 in Morning by Morning, and his reading for September 23 in The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith, which read the verse, as the King James Version renders it, as a promise that not the least grain will be lost.
  • On turning back to God. The chapter on repentance in R. A. Torrey’s What the Bible Teaches, which sets the LORD relenting at Amos’s prayer beside the other great texts on repentance.
  • On Amos and the gospel. Augustine’s The City of God, book XVIII, chapter 28, on what Hosea and Amos foretold of Christ; and Jerome’s letter to Paulinus (Letter LIII in The Letters of St. Jerome), with its brief, vivid summary of the herdsman’s visions, from the plumb line to the famine of the word.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Amos in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

Then read the book again, and listen for the invitation inside the warnings. The God who roars from Zion is the God who says, three times over, that he would rather his people lived:
