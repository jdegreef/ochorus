{{OWB:JOL.2.13.s1}}

— Joel 2:13

Joel begins with a disaster everyone could see. A plague of locusts has swept over the land, wave after wave, and stripped it bare: the vines, the fig trees, the grain, the very bark of the trees. There is nothing left to offer in the temple, and the farmers and the priests are in mourning together. Out of that ruined landscape the prophet hears God speak, and what he hears runs from the next harvest all the way to the end of history.

The book is only three chapters long, yet the church has never stopped quoting it. On the day of Pentecost, when the Holy Spirit came on the disciples, Peter stood up and explained what the crowd was seeing in Joel’s words: “No, this is what was spoken through the prophet Joel”.

A land stripped bare

The first chapter is a call to lament. Joel summons the elders, the drunkards, the farmers, the vine growers and the priests, and asks whether anything like this has happened in their lifetime or their ancestors’. He names the plague four times over:

{{OWB:JOL.1.4.s1}}

— Joel 1:4

The four names may be stages in the locust’s life or simply four words for the same devouring swarm; either way the effect is total. Joel describes it as an invasion: “For a nation has invaded my land, powerful and too many to count. Its teeth are a lion’s teeth”. And he tells the priests what to do with their grief: “Proclaim a holy fast; call a solemn assembly”, and gather everyone at the house of the LORD to cry to him for help.

Then, for the first time, Joel names what this disaster is a foretaste of: “For the day of the LORD is close at hand, and it will come like ruin from the Almighty.”

The day of the LORD

The day of the LORD is Joel’s great theme. It means the day when God steps in to put the world right, the day of his judgment and his rescue. Many in Israel looked forward to it as a day of victory over their enemies. Joel, like Amos before or after him, warns that it is first a day of reckoning for God’s own people.

Chapter 2 opens with a trumpet in Zion and a vision of an army sweeping in like the dawn across the mountains. They look like horses and charge like warriors; they climb the walls and come in through the windows like a thief; before them the land is “like the garden of Eden”, behind them “a desolate wilderness”. And at their head is the LORD himself:

{{OWB:JOL.2.11.s1}}

— Joel 2:11

Readers differ over what this army is. Some take it as the same locust plague described again as an army; others as a human army, perhaps from the north, of which the locusts were a warning; others as the hosts of the final day itself, painted in the colours of the locusts. The poem may be all three at once, the present disaster opening a window onto the last one.

“Yet even now”

Then, at the darkest point, the tone changes:

{{OWB:JOL.2.12.s1}}

— Joel 2:12

Joel asks for tears and fasting, but he will not let them become a performance: “Tear your hearts, not your clothes”. The reason he gives for hope is the description of God that the LORD proclaimed to Moses on Mount Sinai: gracious, full of compassion, slow to anger, rich in loyal love. Jonah quotes the same words, with very different feelings. And Joel adds a humble “Who knows? He may turn back and relent”. He does not presume on God’s mercy; he simply throws himself on it.

Everyone is to come, the elders and the children and the nursing babies, even the bride and bridegroom from their wedding chamber. The priests are to weep between the porch and the altar and pray that God’s people not become a byword among the nations, who would ask, “Where is their God?”

The years the locust has eaten

And God answers. “Then the LORD became jealous for his land and took pity on his people.” The rain returns; the threshing floors are heaped with grain; the fig tree and the vine give their fruit. And God makes a promise that has comforted people in every kind of loss:

{{OWB:JOL.2.25.s1}}

— Joel 2:25

He calls the locusts “my great army”. Even the disaster was in his hand, and so is the restoring. Twice he says, “Never again will my people be put to shame.”

“I will pour out my Spirit”

Then the promise widens from rain on the fields to something far greater:

{{OWB:JOL.3.1.s1}} {{OWB:JOL.3.2.s1}}

— Joel 2:28–29

In Israel the Spirit of the LORD had come on chosen people for particular tasks: prophets, judges, kings. Joel foresees a day when he will be given to everyone, young and old, men and women, even the servants. Moses had once wished that all the LORD’s people were prophets; Joel says the day is coming. Peter announced at Pentecost that it had come, and Paul, quoting the line that follows, applied it to Jew and Gentile alike: “all who call on the name of the Lord will be saved.”

The valley of decision

The last chapter gathers the nations into “the valley of Jehoshaphat”, a name that means “the LORD judges”, to answer for what they have done to God’s people: scattering them, dividing their land, selling their children for wine. In a bitter reversal of the words of Isaiah and Micah, the nations are told to “Hammer your plow blades into swords” and come to battle. But the battle is a harvest, and it is God’s:

{{OWB:JOL.4.14.s1}}

— Joel 3:14

Joel’s “valley of decision” is often preached as the place where each of us must decide for God. In the book it is the place where God decides, giving his verdict on the nations. Yet the two readings are not far apart, for the call to turn back to him with all your heart comes before that verdict is given.

The book ends with the LORD roaring from Zion, mountains dripping with sweet wine, and a spring flowing out from the house of the LORD to water the dry valleys. The land stripped bare in chapter 1 becomes a garden, and its last words are a promise of presence: “The LORD dwells in Zion.”

How to read Joel

Read Joel in one sitting; it takes about fifteen minutes. Notice how it turns in the middle, at 2:18, from lament and warning to promise.

The book does not say when Joel lived. It names no king, and speaks of elders and priests, of the temple and its offerings, and of Judah’s people sold to the Greeks. Some scholars date it early, in the ninth century before Christ; many date it after the return from exile in Babylon, perhaps around 400 BC; others place it in between. Its message does not depend on the date. It speaks to any people who have seen their world stripped bare and wonder whether God is still there.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Joel is poetry throughout, and this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out. Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, it writes “the LORD”. The Hebrew word hesed is translated “loyal love”: God is “rich in loyal love”. The four locusts of 1:4 are named, as in the Hebrew, “the cutting locust”, “the swarming locust”, “the hopping locust” and “the stripping locust”.

The Hebrew divides Joel into four chapters, beginning a new one at the promise of the Spirit. This edition follows the three chapters of English Bibles, and the references in it follow the English numbering. For the reasoning behind every sentence, with the Hebrew beside it, see openwordbible.org.
