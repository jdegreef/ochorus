{{OWB:ECC.1.2.s1}}

— Ecclesiastes 1:2

There is no other book in the Bible quite like this one. It opens with a cry that sounds almost like despair, and it closes with the same cry. In between, a king who has tried everything reports on what he found: that wisdom does not save the wise from dying, that pleasure leaves the heart empty, that the hard work of a lifetime passes to someone who never lifted a finger for it, and that the righteous sometimes perish while the wicked live long. He does not look away from any of it.

And yet this book is Scripture. Generations of readers have found it the most honest voice in the Bible, the one that says aloud what they had been afraid to think. In the synagogue it is read at the Feast of Booths, the most joyful festival of the year, as if to say that joy is safest in the hands of those who know how brief life is. For Ecclesiastes is not finally a book of despair. Again and again, in the middle of its hardest chapters, it stops to say that bread and wine and work and love are gifts from the hand of God, to be received with gladness while there is time.

“The one who gathers”

The speaker of the book is called Qohelet, a word found nowhere else in the Bible. It comes from the Hebrew for an assembly, and means one who gathers people together and speaks to them; he also gathered and arranged wise sayings (12:9). The ancient Greek translation rendered it Ekklesiastes, a member or leader of the assembly, and that gave the book its English name. Older Bibles call him “the Preacher”. This translation calls him “the Teacher”, because, as the book says at its end, he “taught the people knowledge”.

He is introduced as “David’s son, king in Jerusalem”, and in chapter 2 he describes houses and vineyards, gardens and pools, silver and gold, singers and slaves, greater than all who were before him in Jerusalem. Readers have always heard Solomon in this, and Jewish and Christian tradition long received the book as his, written in old age. But the book never gives his name, and many scholars, noticing that its Hebrew is among the latest in the Bible, think a later sage wrote it, taking on Solomon’s place in order to test where wisdom, pleasure and wealth lead. Christians hold both views. Either way, the voice is that of someone who has had everything a person could want, and so can tell you what it is worth.

Two voices

Listen carefully and you will hear two voices in the book. Most of it is Qohelet speaking for himself, “I, the Teacher”. But the first verses speak about him from outside, “says the Teacher”, and so do the last ones, from 12:8 to the end. Once, in the middle, the outer voice breaks in again (7:27). Someone has framed the Teacher’s words, introduced him and then, at the close, commented on him:

{{OWB:ECC.12.9.s1}} {{OWB:ECC.12.10.s1}}

— Ecclesiastes 12:9–10

The frame does not argue with the Teacher or soften what he said. It honours him as wise and honest, and then it has the last word. That last word, as we shall see, is the key to reading the whole.

“Breath upon breath”

The book’s keyword is hevel, used nearly forty times. Older Bibles translate it “vanity”, which once meant emptiness but now suggests conceit; some newer ones say “meaningless”. But the word is a picture. Hevel is a breath, the puff of vapour you see on a cold morning, real enough and then gone. It is also the name of Abel, whose life was over almost as soon as it began.

The Teacher uses the picture in more than one way. Sometimes he means that a thing is brief: life is “a mere breath, which they pass like a shadow” (6:12). Sometimes he means that it cannot be grasped or understood, as when the righteous get what the wicked deserve (8:14). And sometimes he means that it comes to nothing, that all the effort leaves no lasting gain. This translation keeps the picture, so that the word can carry all three senses: “Everything is a breath.”

Beside it stand two other refrains. One is “chasing the wind”, the effort to catch what will not be caught. The other is the question the book asks in its third verse:

{{OWB:ECC.1.3.s1}}

— Ecclesiastes 1:3

The word for “gain” is a word from bookkeeping. It means what is left over when the accounts are done. The Teacher is not asking whether work achieves anything at all. He is asking whether anything remains to a person in the end.

Under the sun

The phrase “under the sun” comes twenty-nine times in Ecclesiastes and nowhere else in the Bible. It marks out the field of the Teacher’s search: life as anyone on earth can see and test it, where the sun rises and sets and hurries back to rise again. He looks at the world as it is, without leaning on a heavenly answer before he has faced the earthly question.

That does not make him an unbeliever. God is everywhere in the book: it is God who has given human beings their task, God who makes everything fitting in its time, God who will judge, God whose gifts are bread and joy. What the Teacher refuses to do is pretend that the ways of God are easy to read. The book always calls him simply “God”, never by his personal name, the LORD, as if to keep him at the distance from which he is seen “under the sun”.

The great experiment

In 1:12–2:26 the Teacher tells how he set out to find what is good for human beings “during the few days of their lives”. He tried wisdom and found that “with much wisdom comes much vexation”. He tried pleasure and laughter and wine. He built and planted and gathered treasure, and denied himself nothing. Then he took stock:

{{OWB:ECC.2.11.s1}}

— Ecclesiastes 2:11

Wisdom is better than folly, he grants, as light is better than darkness. “Yet I also came to know that one fate comes to them all” (2:14). The wise die like fools and are forgotten like them. What a person builds goes to the next owner, “And who knows whether that one will be wise or a fool?” (2:19). The experiment ends in the bleakest words of the book: “So I hated life.”

And then, at once, comes the first of the book’s turns:

{{OWB:ECC.2.24.s1}}

— Ecclesiastes 2:24

The pattern repeats through the book. The Teacher goes down into the hard facts, and comes up with the same quiet counsel: receive the day and its work and its food as God’s gift.

A time for everything

The best-known passage in Ecclesiastes is a poem of fourteen pairs, from birth and death to war and peace:

— Ecclesiastes 3:1–8

It is often read as a comfort, and it is one. But it is also humbling. We do not choose our times; they are set, and they come to us. The Teacher draws the lesson a few verses later, in one of the deepest sentences in the book:

{{OWB:ECC.3.11.s1}}

— Ecclesiastes 3:11

Human beings are made for more than the moment. We carry a sense of eternity, and so we cannot be satisfied by what passes. Yet we cannot see the whole of what God is doing. Ecclesiastes lives inside that tension, and it does not pretend to resolve it.

What the Teacher saw

The middle chapters move from one observation to the next, often in short sayings like those in Proverbs. He sees “the tears of the oppressed, with no one to comfort them” (4:1). He sees a man working endlessly with no one to work for: “For whom am I working so hard,” he asks, “and going without anything good?” (4:8). He sees that “Two are better than one”, and that “a cord of three strands does not snap quickly” (4:9, 12). He sees that “Whoever loves money never has enough money” (5:10), and that we leave the world as we entered it, “naked as he came” (5:15).

Most of all he sees death, which comes to all alike. “It is the same for everyone: one fate comes to the righteous and the wicked” (9:2). He even says that “a living dog is better off than a dead lion” (9:4). The Teacher does not have the resurrection to set against the grave. He looks at death squarely, and lets it teach him how to live. “It is better to go to a house of mourning than to go to a house of feasting,” he says, “because death is the end of everyone, and the living will take it to heart” (7:2).

Before God, few words

In the middle of the book comes a short passage on worship, and it is characteristic of the Teacher. He does not warn against too little religion but against careless religion: rash vows, hurried words, sacrifices offered by people who do not listen:

{{OWB:ECC.4.17.s1}} {{OWB:ECC.5.1.s1}}

— Ecclesiastes 5:1–2

The passage ends with the counsel that runs beneath the whole book: “For when dreams and words are many, they are only a breath. Instead, fear God” (5:7).

Joy as a gift

Seven times the Teacher stops to commend joy (2:24–26; 3:12–13; 3:22; 5:18–20; 8:15; 9:7–10; 11:7–10), and each time it comes more strongly. By chapter 9 it has become a command:

{{OWB:ECC.9.7.s1}}

— Ecclesiastes 9:7

He goes on: wear white, enjoy life with the one you love, and “Whatever your hand finds to do, put all your strength into it” (9:10). This is not the shrug of those who say, “Let us eat and drink, since tomorrow we die” (1 Corinthians 15:32). It is a man who has seen through every attempt to make life pay a lasting profit, and has found that the ordinary goods of food and work and love are still good, because God gives them. The breath is brief. That is why it is precious.

The book’s tensions

Ecclesiastes often sets sayings side by side that seem to pull against each other. Sorrow is better than laughter (7:3), yet eat your bread with joy (9:7). Wisdom gains more than folly, yet the wise die like fools. Toil is a breath, yet it is a gift. “Do not be too righteous,” he says, “and do not make yourself too wise” (7:16), and in the next breath, “Do not be too wicked.” Readers have puzzled over these tensions for centuries.

They have read the book in different ways. Some hear in the Teacher a man of faith clearing away false hopes, so that only God and his gifts are left. Some hear an honest sceptic whose doubts the frame answers at the end. Some hear a deliberate portrait of how life looks “under the sun”, set in Scripture to drive the reader to look higher. There is truth in each. What all agree on is that the tensions belong to the book. They are part of how it teaches, and a reader who smooths them away too quickly will miss what it has to say.

“Remember your Creator”

The last poem of the Teacher is addressed to the young: “Be glad, young man, while you are young” (11:9). But it goes on, “Remember your Creator in the days of your youth”, and then paints, in a series of images, the coming of age and death. The guards of the house tremble, the women who grind stop because they are few, the windows grow dim, the almond tree blossoms. Many readers have heard in these pictures the failing body: trembling hands, few teeth, dim eyes, white hair. Then the silver cord snaps, the golden bowl breaks, “and the dust goes back to the earth it came from, and the spirit returns to God, who gave it” (12:7).

And the Teacher’s last words are his first: “Breath upon breath, says the Teacher. Everything is a breath.”

Ecclesiastes and the New Testament

The New Testament never clearly quotes Ecclesiastes, but it answers it. When Paul writes that “Creation was made subject to futility” (Romans 8:20), he uses the Greek word by which the ancient translation rendered the Teacher’s “breath”, and he adds what the Teacher could not see: that creation itself will be set free and share the glory of God’s children. Jesus’s parable of the rich fool, who stores up his harvest and dies that night, ends with the Teacher’s own question: “And the things you have made ready, whose will they be then?” (Luke 12:20).

And to the question of 1:3, what anyone gains from all their hard work, Paul gives an answer grounded in the resurrection of Christ. After a long chapter on the risen body, he ends: “in the Lord your hard work is never wasted” (1 Corinthians 15:58). Under the sun, everything is a breath. In the Lord, nothing done for him is lost.

How to read Ecclesiastes

Ecclesiastes is short, and it is best read straight through, in one or two sittings, so that you hear its refrains and feel its turns. A few suggestions:

  • Do not lift out a verse on its own. Many of the Teacher’s sayings are answered a few verses later, and the book means them to be heard together.
  • Listen for the refrains. “Breath”, “under the sun”, “chasing the wind”, and the repeated “There is nothing better”.
  • Read the ending carefully. The frame’s last words (12:9–14) tell you how to hold everything before them.
  • Read it beside Proverbs and Job. Proverbs says how life usually goes; Job and Ecclesiastes take up the times when it does not. Each needs the others.
  • Let it do its work. Ecclesiastes strips away the things we lean on, so that we lean on God. It is not comfortable reading, but it is good medicine.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Ecclesiastes moves between prose and poetry; its poems, such as the round of nature in 1:4–11, the times and seasons of 3:1–8 and the closing poem of age in 12:1–7, are printed one line per line, as the Hebrew sets them out. A few of its choices are worth knowing before you begin.

“Breath.” The book’s keyword hevel is rendered “breath” throughout, not “vanity” or “meaningless”, so that its picture of something real but brief and beyond grasping stays in view. The opening cry piles breath on breath, as the Hebrew does: “Breath upon breath”.

“The Teacher.” The speaker’s Hebrew title, Qohelet, is translated by what he does, “the Teacher”, with a capital because it is the name the book calls him by.

The refrains. “Under the sun” and “chasing the wind” are kept word for word wherever they stand, and the book’s two other key words keep one English word each: “gain”, for what is left over, and “hard work”, for the toil that wears a person out.
