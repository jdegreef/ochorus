— Ecclesiastes 12:13–14

After twelve chapters of searching, the book ends in two sentences. They do not take back anything the Teacher said. Everything under the sun is still a breath; the wise still die like fools; the righteous still sometimes perish in their righteousness. But the frame steps forward and sets all of it inside something larger. God is, and God will judge. So the one thing that remains, when every other gain has blown away, is to fear him and keep his commandments. That, the book says, is “the whole of being human”.

It is a surprising place to arrive. The Teacher had complained that no one remembers those who came before, that the wise are forgotten as quickly as fools. The last verse answers him: God will bring every deed into judgment, “including everything hidden”. Nothing is lost to him. What looked like a breath is weighed after all.

The question the book leaves with you

Ecclesiastes asks every reader the question it asked in its third verse: what do you gain from all your hard work under the sun? It will not let you answer quickly. It has taken away the usual answers one by one: wisdom, pleasure, achievement, wealth, reputation, even a long life. None of them, held for its own sake, survives the grave.

What is left is not nothing. It is God, the giver, and his gifts, received with open hands: bread eaten with joy, work done with all your strength, life shared with the one you love, the light that is sweet to the eyes. And it is the fear of God, which holds those gifts in their place. The question Ecclesiastes leaves is whether you will build your life on what passes, or receive what passes from the hand of the one who does not.

Where Ecclesiastes points

The Teacher saw that God had “put a sense of eternity in their hearts”, and that no one could see the whole of God’s work. He could not see past the grave. The New Testament claims that someone has. The Son of God came under the sun, shared our flesh and blood, worked with his hands and wept at a friend’s tomb, and died; and by his death he set free “those whom the fear of death kept enslaved their whole life long” (Hebrews 2:15). On the third day he rose.

That is why Paul can write to people whose lives were as brief as anyone’s: “What can be seen lasts only a while; what cannot be seen is eternal” (2 Corinthians 4:18). Ecclesiastes does not give that answer. It clears the ground for it, so that when the answer comes, it is heard for what it is.

Where to go from here

Ecclesiastes has spoken to preachers and thinkers in every age, often to the restless and the disillusioned. Some of the best of what they wrote is in this library:

  • On “Breath upon breath” (Ecclesiastes 1:2). John Chrysostom’s sermon Against Eutropius, preached over a fallen court minister who clung to the altar of the church he had attacked.
  • On the crooked things God has made (Ecclesiastes 7:13). Thomas Boston’s The Crook in the Lot, on bearing the hard places of a life as God’s work.
  • On a world that is a breath. John Bunyan’s The Pilgrim’s Progress, whose Vanity Fair takes its name partly from Ecclesiastes (11:8); and Hannah Whitall Smith’s chapter “Ecclesiastes: The Vanity of the World” in Old Testament Types and Teachings.
  • On the restless heart. Augustine’s Confessions, which opens with the prayer that our heart is restless until it rests in God; and Blaise Pascal’s Pensées, whose section on the misery of man without God says that Solomon and Job have best known and spoken of it.
  • On numbering our days. Jeremy Taylor’s The Rule and Exercises of Holy Dying, which begins with the vanity and shortness of human life and turns it into daily preparation for death.
  • For reading with children. Tell Me the Story – Book 9: Songs and Wise Words, an Ochorus Original, with chapters on a time for everything (3:1) and remembering your Creator (12:1).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Ecclesiastes in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
