Romans ends as it began. In its first paragraph Paul says he received grace and apostleship “to bring about the obedience of faith among all the nations”; in its last sentence the mystery kept secret for long ages has been made known to all the nations “to bring about the obedience of faith”. Everything in between, the guilt of all, the gift of righteousness, the life of the Spirit, the grief over Israel and the welcome at the table, lies between those two phrases. And the whole of it ends, as Paul’s great arguments always do, in praise.

Where the letter points

It points, first, to God. Romans is often read as a book about salvation, and it is. But when Paul reaches the end of his hardest argument, in chapter 11, he does not sum it up. He worships:

{{OWB:ROM.11.33.s1}} {{OWB:ROM.11.36.s1}}

— Romans 11:33, 36

A reader who finishes Romans knowing more doctrine but loving God no more has missed what Paul was doing. The argument is meant to end on our knees.

It points, second, to a people. The letter that tells us God declares the ungodly righteous also tells the strong to carry the weaknesses of those who lack strength, and greets Phoebe, Prisca, Junia and Rufus’s mother by name. Justification by faith, in Romans, is never a private arrangement between one soul and God. It makes Jews and Gentiles into one family, who must now learn to eat together.

And it points outward, to the nations. Paul wrote, after all, because he was on his way to Spain. In chapter 10 he asks, “how are they to believe in him if they have never heard him?”, and the question has sent missionaries out ever since. He did reach Rome, though not as he planned: arrested in Jerusalem, he came as a prisoner, and Acts leaves him there, preaching in his rented lodgings, without hindrance. Whether he ever reached Spain no one knows for certain. Clement, writing from the church in Rome a generation later, says that Paul taught righteousness to the whole world and reached the farthest bounds of the West.

Where to go from here

Romans has drawn out the best of the church’s preachers and teachers for as long as there has been a church, and many of them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • Through the whole letter. John Chrysostom’s Homilies on Romans, preached at Antioch, and John Calvin’s Commentary on Romans, which Calvin called a way into all of Scripture.
  • On sin and the gift of righteousness (chapters 1–4). Martyn Lloyd-Jones’s The Wrath of God (1:18), John Wesley’s The Circumcision of the Heart (2:29), Charles H. Spurgeon’s Justification by Grace (3:24) and his All of Grace, and George Whitefield’s The Lord Our Righteousness (on Jeremiah 23:6). For the man who found the gospel in 1:17, The Key Teachings of Martin Luther and Luther’s own First Principles of the Reformation.
  • On peace with God (chapter 5). Alexander Maclaren’s Let Us Have Peace, which follows the other reading of 5:1, his The Sources of Hope and What Proves God’s Love; Thomas Chalmers’s The Sure Warrant of a Believer’s Hope; Christmas Evans’s Fall and Recovery of Man; and A. B. Simpson’s Grace Abounding.
  • On sin and the new life (chapters 6–8). John Owen wrote on these chapters more searchingly than almost anyone: The Dominion of Sin and Grace (6:14), Indwelling Sin in Believers (chapter 7), The Mortification of Sin in Believers (8:13) and The Grace and Duty of Being Spiritually Minded (8:6).
  • On Romans 8. Thomas Watson’s All Things for Good (8:28); two sermons called The Witness of the Spirit (8:16), by John Wesley and by Maclaren; Charles G. Finney’s The Spirit of Prayer (8:26–27); Maclaren’s Sons and Heirs and The Gift That Brings All Gifts; John Newton’s If God Be for Us; and Simpson’s More Than Conquerors.
  • On Israel, the nations and the gospel’s reach (chapters 9–11). Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s Our Duty to Israel and Believers Not Ashamed, both on 1:16; Spurgeon’s A Simple Sermon for Seeking Souls (10:13); and Simpson’s The Logic of Missions (10:14–15).
  • The lives it changed, and the church it was written to. Augustine of Hippo’s Confessions, whose eighth book ends in the garden at Milan; The Journal of John Wesley, with the evening at Aldersgate; and two letters from the generations after Paul: The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians, sent from the church in Rome, and Ignatius of Antioch’s letter to it, in The Epistles of Ignatius and His Life’s Story.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Romans in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.

But the best next step may be to read Romans again, and perhaps to read it aloud, as Phoebe’s hearers first heard it, all at once. The argument that seemed so hard the first time will begin to sound like what it is: good news, set out by a man who could not stop marvelling at it.
