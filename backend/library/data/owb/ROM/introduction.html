— Romans 1:16–17

Some time around AD 57, in the port city of Corinth, Paul sat down with a secretary named Tertius and dictated the longest letter he ever wrote. It was addressed to a church he had never visited, in the capital of the world, and it was carried there by a woman from the harbour town of Cenchreae named Phoebe.

No one in Corinth could have guessed what it would become. Few pieces of writing have been argued over, preached from and prayed through more. Augustine was converted reading it. Luther found the gospel again in it. Wesley felt his heart “strangely warmed” while someone read aloud a preface to it. Calvin called it a doorway into the whole of Scripture.

And yet it is a letter, not a textbook. It begins with greetings and ends with a list of friends. Between the two, Paul argues, pleads, asks himself questions and answers them, breaks off into praise, and grieves for his own people. To read it well is to hear a man thinking aloud, with all his heart, about what God has done in Jesus Christ.

A letter to a church Paul had never visited

Paul did not found the church in Rome, and no one knows who did. There were visitors from Rome in Jerusalem at Pentecost (Acts 2:10), and believers travelling the roads of the empire probably carried the news home. By the time Paul wrote, people throughout the world, he says, were speaking of its faith.

It was a church of Jews and Gentiles together, and that was not always easy. The Roman historian Suetonius records that the emperor Claudius expelled the Jews from Rome (probably around AD 49), and Acts tells of Aquila and his wife Priscilla, Paul’s Prisca, arriving in Corinth because of it (Acts 18:2). When Jewish believers returned after Claudius died in 54, they would have found congregations grown largely Gentile in their absence. Many readers think that lies behind the letter’s refrain, “the Jew first and the Greek as well”, and behind its plea in chapters 14 and 15 for believers who disagree about food and holy days to welcome one another.

Paul gives his own reasons near the end (15:22–33). His work in the east is done, “from Jerusalem as far as Illyricum” (15:19). He is about to take a gift from the Gentile churches of Greece to the poor believers in Jerusalem; after that he means to go west, to Spain, and to visit Rome on the way. He wants the Roman church to know him, to pray for him, and to help him on his way. So he sets out, more fully than anywhere else, the gospel he preaches.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapters 1–4: the gospel of God’s righteousness. Every human being, Jew and Gentile alike, stands guilty before God; God declares people righteous as a free gift, through faith in Jesus Christ; and Abraham shows that it was always so.
  • Chapters 5–8: the new life. Peace with God; Adam and Christ; dying and rising with Christ; the struggle with sin and the law; and life in the Spirit, which nothing can take away.
  • Chapters 9–11: Israel and the nations. If God’s promises were made to Israel, and most of Israel has not believed, has God’s word failed?
  • Chapters 12–16: a people shaped by mercy. Bodies offered as a living sacrifice, love in practice, the strong and the weak, Paul’s plans, and his greetings.

Each part leans on the one before. The commands of chapter 12 begin with a “so”, and that “so” rests on eleven chapters of God’s mercy.

All have sinned

Paul begins not with good news but with bad. From 1:18 to 3:20 he builds a case, and every reader is meant to find himself in it. First the Gentile world, which knew enough of God to thank him and did not, and “traded the truth about God for the lie”. Then, turning on anyone who has been nodding along, the moral person who judges others and does the same things. Then his own people, who have the law and the covenant and are no less in need. The case closes with a chain of quotations from the Psalms and Isaiah: “No one is righteous, not even one.”

This is not gloom for its own sake. If everyone is in the same condition, then everyone can be met by the same grace. The verdict and the remedy come in a single sentence:

— Romans 3:23–24

Righteousness from God, through faith

“But now”, Paul writes at 3:21, and the whole letter turns. God’s righteousness has come to light apart from the law, “through faith in Jesus Christ, for all who believe.” God put Christ forward as the means of atonement, by his blood, so that he would be both righteous himself and “the one who declares righteous whoever has faith in Jesus.”

This is what Christians call justification by faith. The Greek verb belongs to the law court: it is a judge’s verdict, declaring someone in the right. Paul’s astonishing claim is that God gives that verdict to the guilty, not for anything they have done, but as a free gift received by trusting him. God is the God “who declares the ungodly righteous” (4:5), and boasting is shut out.

Christians have understood this in more than one way. Catholic and Orthodox readers stress that the God who declares righteous also makes righteous; the Reformers stressed that the verdict rests wholly on Christ, received by faith alone, and that the new life flows from it rather than earning it. More recently, E. P. Sanders reassessed the Judaism of Paul’s day, and James Dunn and N. T. Wright have argued that “works of the law” means especially the marks that set Jews apart from Gentiles, so that Paul’s first concern is who belongs to God’s people; others answer that his argument reaches deeper, to the impossibility of anyone earning God’s favour. The letter itself is the best place to weigh them.

To show that none of this is new, Paul turns in chapter 4 to Abraham, the father of Israel:

{{OWB:ROM.4.3.s1}}

— Romans 4:3

Abraham was counted righteous before he was circumcised, Paul points out, so he is father both of Jews who believe and of Gentiles who believe. His faith was no vague optimism. He was about a hundred years old, and he trusted the God “who brings the dead to life and calls into being things that do not exist.” That is the God, Paul says, who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead.

Adam and Christ

Chapter 5 opens with the fruit of all this: “we are at peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ.” And it gives the deepest reason for that peace:

{{OWB:ROM.5.8.s1}}

— Romans 5:8

Then Paul steps back to take in the whole of human history. Through one man, Adam, sin and death came into the world. Through one man, Jesus Christ, came the free gift and life. The two are alike in shape and utterly unlike in size: “where sin multiplied, grace overflowed far beyond it.” Christians have drawn on this passage in thinking about original sin, and differed over it. But Paul’s emphasis falls on the second man. The point is not how far we fell, but how much further grace reaches.

Dead to sin, alive to God

If grace is so abundant, someone objects, why not go on sinning? “Certainly not!” Those baptized into Christ were baptized into his death and buried with him, so that they might “live a life that is new.” Paul’s picture is a change of masters: once slaves of sin, now slaves of righteousness, and of God.

Chapter 7 turns to the law. It is holy, Paul insists, and its commandment “holy, just and good”, and yet it could not give life. Then come some of the most personal words he ever wrote: “The things I want to do, I fail to do; the things I hate are what I end up doing.” And at the end, the cry: “Wretched man that I am! Who will set me free from this body of death?”

Who is the “I” of Romans 7? Christians have never fully agreed. Augustine in his later years, and Luther and Calvin after him, heard the believer, still fighting the sin that remains. Others, from Chrysostom to many interpreters today, hear the person under the law without the Spirit, described with Christian hindsight. Either way, the chapter ends not in despair but in thanksgiving: “Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!”

No sentence of condemnation

Many readers count Romans 8 the greatest chapter in the Bible. It begins:

{{OWB:ROM.8.1.s1}}

— Romans 8:1

It is the chapter of the Spirit. The Spirit gives life where the law could not; the Spirit leads God’s children and testifies that they belong to him; in the Spirit they call out “Abba, Father!” The whole creation groans, waiting for God’s children to be revealed, and so do believers, and the Spirit himself pleads for them “with groans that no words carry.”

Then, in rising questions, Paul gathers everything up. “If God is on our side, who can stand against us?” Who will accuse? Who will condemn? Who can tear us away from the love of Christ? The answer is the peak of the letter’s first half:

{{OWB:ROM.8.38.s1}}

— Romans 8:38–39

Israel and the nations

Readers sometimes treat chapters 9 to 11 as a detour. Paul does not. Straight after the joy of chapter 8 he speaks of the constant pain in his heart for his own people, most of whom have not believed in their Messiah. If God’s promises to Israel could fail, how could anyone trust the promises of chapter 8?

His answer moves in stages. God’s word has not failed, because God has always worked by choosing, by mercy and not by human striving. Israel has stumbled, pursuing righteousness as though it came by works; yet “all who call on the name of the Lord will be saved.” And God has not rejected his people: there is a remnant, chosen by grace, and Paul is one of them. Gentile believers are wild olive shoots grafted into Israel’s tree, and must not boast over the natural branches, which God can graft back in. A hardening has come upon part of Israel “until the full number of the Gentiles comes in. And in this way all Israel will be saved.”

These chapters are read in very different ways. In the tradition of Augustine and Calvin, chapter 9 teaches God’s choice of individuals for salvation; in the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions, it speaks of God choosing peoples and persons for a part in his purpose, with salvation resting on a faith open to all. “All Israel” has been read as the Jewish people turning to Christ at the last, as the whole people of God, or as all believing Israel through history. Every reading must keep where Paul lands: “the gifts and the call of God can never be taken back”, and God has locked everyone up in disobedience “so that he may show mercy to everyone.” Then Paul stops arguing and worships.

A living sacrifice

The last part of the letter turns all of this into a way of life:

{{OWB:ROM.12.1.s1}}

— Romans 12:1

What follows is practical, and searching. Believers are one body with many gifts. Their love is to be real, with no pretense. They are to bless those who persecute them, weep with those who weep, never avenge themselves, and feed their enemies: “Do not let evil defeat you; defeat evil with good.” Chapter 13 speaks of the governing authorities, a passage argued over in times of peace and of persecution alike, and of the one debt never paid off, the debt of love.

Chapters 14 and 15 come down to the dinner table. Some believers eat anything; others eat only vegetables. Some keep special days; others treat every day alike. Paul’s concern is that no one despise or judge another, since each belongs to the Lord: “So welcome one another, just as Christ has welcomed you, for the glory of God.” The great doctrines end in a church where Jews and Gentiles, the strong and the weak, sit down and eat together.

A real church

Romans 16 is the chapter most readers skip, and it is worth slowing down for. Paul commends Phoebe, “a deacon of the church in Cenchreae” and a patron to many. Then he greets twenty-six people in Rome, twenty-four of them by name, and nine of them women: Prisca and Aquila, who risked their necks for him; Andronicus and Junia, who were in prison with him and are “prominent among the apostles”; Mary, Tryphaena, Tryphosa and Persis, who have worked hard in the Lord; Rufus’s mother, “who has also been a mother to me.” Several of the names were common among slaves and freed slaves. There is a church that meets in a house.

The great argument was read aloud to small congregations of ordinary people, men and women, slave and free, Jew and Gentile, and here they are greeted one by one.

Romans in the life of the church

John Chrysostom, preaching on this letter at Antioch in the fourth century, said he heard Paul’s letters read twice every week, and was warmed each time, as if by the voice of a friend.

In a garden in Milan in 386, Augustine heard a child’s voice saying “Take up and read.” He opened Paul’s letters, and his eyes fell on the end of Romans 13:

{{OWB:ROM.13.13.s1}}

— Romans 13:13–14

He tells us in his Confessions that he read no further, nor needed to: the darkness of doubt was gone.

Eleven centuries later Martin Luther, teaching at Wittenberg, wrestled with “the righteousness of God” in 1:17. He had heard it as the righteousness by which God punishes sinners, and he hated it. When at last he understood it as the righteousness God gives, by which “the one who is righteous will live by faith”, he felt, he said, that he had been born again and had entered paradise through open gates. In his preface to the letter he called Romans the chief part of the New Testament and the purest gospel.

And on the evening of 24 May 1738, at a meeting in Aldersgate Street in London, John Wesley listened as someone read from that preface. “About a quarter before nine,” he wrote, “while he was describing the change which God works in the heart through faith in Christ, I felt my heart strangely warmed.” Wesley’s preaching in the revival that followed flowed out of that evening.

How to read Romans

Read it through once, in a sitting or two, to hear it as the Roman Christians did, aloud and all at once. Then read it slowly.

Notice Paul’s questions. Again and again he raises an objection (“What then are we to say?”, “Is the law sin?”) and answers it, often with “Certainly not!” He is anticipating his hearers’ questions, and yours. Notice his “so” and “therefore”: Romans is an argument, and its conclusions stand on what comes before. Notice how often he breaks into praise, at the end of chapter 8, of chapter 11, and of the letter. And do not stop at chapter 11. The letter is not finished until the doctrine has sat down at table.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Romans are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Declared righteous.” Where older Bibles say “justified”, this translation says what the word means in a court: God “declares righteous”. It keeps “righteous” and “righteousness” throughout, so that a reader can follow one family of words, plainly related in Greek, through the whole argument.

“Faith in Jesus Christ.” The Greek of 3:22 says literally “faith of Jesus Christ”. It can mean faith that has Jesus as its object, or Jesus’ own faithfulness to God, shown in his death. This translation follows the first reading, and its notes give the second, which many scholars hold.

“Flesh.” Where Paul means human life under the sway of sin, set against the Spirit, as in chapters 7 and 8, the translation keeps “flesh”, so that the contrast can be seen. Where he simply means descent, as when Christ is born of David’s line (1:3), it says “by human descent”.

Places where manuscripts differ. In 16:7 it reads Junia, a woman’s name, as early Christian writers did. In 5:1 it reads “we are at peace with God”; many early manuscripts have “let us have peace”, a difference of one vowel. And the closing praise (16:25–27), which stands in different places in different manuscripts, is printed at the end, where most Bibles have it.

“Christ.” The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose whether Christos reads “Christ” or “the Messiah”. This edition prints “Christ”, but it is worth remembering, especially in chapters 9 to 11, that the word means Israel’s anointed King.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Greek beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
