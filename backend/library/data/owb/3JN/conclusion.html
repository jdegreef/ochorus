Third John leaves us in the middle of a story. We never learn whether the elder came, whether Diotrephes gave way, or what became of Gaius and Demetrius. The letter survives because the church saw in it something more than one congregation’s quarrel: a picture of what the church is meant to be, and of what can spoil it.

The question the book leaves with you

The letter sets two kinds of people side by side and asks which we are. Gaius opens his door to strangers because they belong to the Name. Diotrephes shuts his door, and other people’s doors too, because he loves to be first. Both are church members. Both, presumably, would have said they served God. What divides them is not their doctrine but what they love.

The question reaches every Christian, but especially anyone with a little authority: a pastor, an elder, a leader of anything. Are we using our place to make room for others, or to keep it for ourselves? Do we rejoice when the gospel goes forward through someone else, or only when it goes through us? The elder’s answer is as plain as it is searching: “The person who does good is from God; the person who does evil has not seen God.”

Where to go from here

No book in this library is given wholly to 3 John, but several take up its two great concerns, the support of those who go out with the gospel and the danger of loving to be first:

  • On ambition in the church. Gregory the Great’s The Book of Pastoral Rule, whose first part warns against those who “covet superiority to others” and take up leadership “through lust of pre-eminence”; Richard Baxter’s The Reformed Pastor, which calls ministers away from self-seeking and pride; and Andrew Murray’s Humility.
  • On those who set out for the sake of the Name. Hudson Taylor’s A Retrospect, the story of a missionary who resolved to depend on God alone for his support, and John R. Mott’s The Evangelization of the World in This Generation.
  • On the elder and his letters. Eusebius of Caesarea’s The Church History, which preserves what Papias said of the two Johns of Ephesus and records the early questions about 2 and 3 John; and Augustine of Hippo’s Homilies on the First Epistle of John, on the longer letter from the same hand.

But the best next step may be simpler. Think of someone who has gone out for the sake of the Name, a missionary, a travelling preacher, a believer far from home, and do something to send them on their way “in a manner worthy of God”. That is how the elder would want his letter read. And then receive his blessing, as Gaius did:
