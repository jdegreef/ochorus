{{OWB:3JN.1.4.s1}}

— 3 John 1:4

Third John is the shortest book in the Bible. In Greek it runs to little more than two hundred words, a single sheet of papyrus, about the length of a note a friend might leave on a table. It is also the most personal of John’s letters, and in one way the most surprising of all the letters in the New Testament: it never once mentions Jesus or Christ by name. He is there, but only as “the Name” for whose sake the travelling preachers have set out.

That makes 3 John a precious window into the ordinary life of the first churches. There is no great doctrine here, no vision, no argument. There are three men, one good, one bad, one recommended, and a question that every church still faces: how should we treat the people who carry the gospel from place to place?

Gaius, the dear friend

{{OWB:3JN.1.1.s1}} {{OWB:3JN.1.2.s1}}

— 3 John 1:1–2

Gaius was one of the commonest names in the Roman world, and we cannot tell whether this Gaius is any of the others in the New Testament. What we know of him comes from this letter alone, and it is all good. Four times the elder calls him “dear friend”. Travelling believers have come back with news of his faithfulness, “how you are walking in the truth” (1:3), and that news is the elder’s greatest joy.

The wish in verse 2, for Gaius’s good health, has sometimes been read as a promise that God wants every believer to prosper in body and goods. In fact it is the ordinary kind wish with which letters of the time began, much as we might write “I hope you are keeping well”. What is unusual is the comparison: the elder hopes that Gaius’s body is doing as well as his soul.

For the sake of the Name

The heart of the letter is about hospitality. Gaius has been taking in travelling Christians, men he had never met before, and helping them on their way:

{{OWB:3JN.1.5.s1}}

— 3 John 1:5–6

These were missionaries, or something like them. “They set out for the sake of the Name, and they take nothing from the pagans” (1:7). They would not ask unbelievers to pay for the preaching of the gospel, so they depended on the churches along the road for a bed, a meal, and supplies for the next stage. To send them on “in a manner worthy of God” meant to treat them as you would treat God’s own messengers.

And the elder draws the lesson: “It is our duty, then, to take in and support such people, and so become fellow workers with the truth” (1:8). Not everyone is called to go. But those who stay at home and open their doors share in the work of those who go. A church that supports its missionaries is not an audience for the gospel; it is a partner in it.

Diotrephes, who loves to be first

Then the tone changes:

{{OWB:3JN.1.9.s1}}

— 3 John 1:9

This is the only time Diotrephes appears in history, and it is not a flattering portrait. He refuses the elder’s authority. He spreads “empty and wicked talk” about him. He will not receive the travelling believers himself, he stops others from receiving them, and “he drives them out of the church” (1:10).

Readers have long wondered what lay behind the quarrel. Some think Diotrephes was a leader of a house church who resented outside oversight; some see in him an early stage of the struggle over how churches should be governed; a few have wondered whether a dispute over teaching lay underneath. The letter does not say. What it does say is the root of the trouble, in a single Greek word: he “loves to be first”. Whatever else was at stake, the elder diagnoses it as ambition. It is the sin the disciples showed when they argued about which of them was the greatest, and it has troubled the church in every age since.

Do what is good

The elder does not tell Gaius to fight Diotrephes. He gives him a simple rule:

{{OWB:3JN.1.11.s1}} {{OWB:3JN.1.11.s2}}

— 3 John 1:11

It is the same test 1 John gives, put in a single line: a life shows where it comes from. And the elder sets a good example beside the bad one. “Everyone testifies in favor of Demetrius, and so does the truth itself” (1:12). Demetrius may have been one of the travellers, perhaps the one carrying this very letter, and the elder’s words are a letter of recommendation: here is someone you can receive.

Ink and pen

The close is very like that of 2 John. The elder has much more to say, but “I do not want to put it down in ink with a pen” (1:13). He hopes to come soon and talk face to face. Then come three short lines of greeting: peace to Gaius, greetings from “the friends here”, and greetings to “each of the friends there by name”. The church, in this letter, is a network of friends who know each other by name.

Who wrote 3 John?

As in 2 John, the writer calls himself only “the elder”, and the two letters are so alike in shape and phrasing, down to the closing lines, that nearly everyone agrees they come from the same hand. The church linked them with John the apostle, though, as Eusebius records, some in the early centuries were unsure whether they belonged to the evangelist “or to another person of the same name”, the John the Elder whom Papias mentions. They were probably written late in the first century, in the province of Asia, where tradition places John’s last years.

How to read 3 John

Read it as a real letter between real people, with the gaps a real letter has. Notice the three men and what each shows: the faithful host, the man who loves to be first, the one everyone speaks well of. Notice how often the word truth comes, even here. And read it beside 2 John. One letter tells a church whom not to receive; the other tells a friend whom he must receive. Together they show a church that guards its door and keeps it open, both for the sake of the truth.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 3 John are worth knowing about.

“The Name.” In verse 7 the translation gives the Name a capital letter. The Greek says simply “the name”, and the elder means the name of Jesus, as the apostles in Acts were glad to have been found worthy “to bear shame for the sake of the Name” (Acts 5:41).

“Pagans” and “brothers and sisters.” The word in verse 7 means the nations, those who were not God’s people; here it is rendered “pagans”. Where the Greek “brothers” means fellow believers of either sex, the translation says “brothers and sisters”.

The last verse. Bibles divide the closing lines differently. Some, like the King James Version, end the letter at verse 14; others make the final greetings a verse 15. References here follow the first numbering.
