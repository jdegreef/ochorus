— Lamentations 5:21–22

Lamentations ends without an answer. Its last word is not a promise but a fear, that God’s anger might have no limit, and the prayer before it is left hanging. Jewish readers repeat the request to be turned back so as not to close the book on that note. But the book itself leaves it open, and that is part of its gift. It does not pretend that every grief resolves by the last line.

The question the book leaves with you

What do you do when God seems to have become your enemy? Lamentations does not offer an argument. It offers a practice. It names the loss in full. It confesses sin where there is sin, without pretending that the suffering is therefore small. It complains to God, to his face, and asks him to look. And in the middle of the ruins it calls to mind what it knows of him: that his loyal love has not run out, that his mercies are new each morning, that he does not afflict from his heart, that his throne stands through every generation.

The hope of chapter 3 is not a feeling that arrives when things improve. It is something the sufferer brings back to mind, deliberately, while nothing has improved at all. “The LORD is my portion,” he tells himself, “and so I will wait for him in hope.” That is a choice anyone can make in the dark, and the book shows a man making it.

Where the book points

The prayer “make our days new” did not go unanswered. Within fifty years exiles began to come home, and some seventy years after its destruction the temple was rebuilt. But Christians have also heard in Lamentations a deeper sorrow and a deeper answer. Jesus wept over Jerusalem and foretold its second destruction. He was the man who saw affliction under the rod of God’s anger, cut off, mocked all day long, laid in a pit with a stone over it, and raised. In him God has shown that he does not cast off for ever, and that the morning comes. The last book of the Bible gathers up the tears of Lamentations and promises a city where “Every tear he will wipe from their eyes” (Revelation 21:4).

Where to go from here

Lamentations is a small book, but some of the best devotional writing in this library has grown from its central chapter. A few places to begin:

  • On the mercies new every morning (Lamentations 3:21–24). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Evening by Evening, for May 28, on the memory that gives hope (3:21), and his Morning by Morning, for November 16, on the Lord as the soul’s portion (3:24).
  • On waiting for God (Lamentations 3:25–26). Andrew Murray’s Waiting on God, whose chapters “To Know His Goodness” and “Quietly” take these two verses as their text.
  • On suffering and the yoke (Lamentations 3:27–31). Spurgeon’s The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith, for January 12, “Love unto the End” (3:31), and September 11, “Sufferers Make Strong Believers” (3:27).
  • On prayer and self-examination (Lamentations 3:40–58). Morning by Morning again, for October 11 (3:41) and November 20 (3:58), Spurgeon on lifting up the heart with the hands and on the Lord who pleads the soul’s cause.
  • On waiting in silence. Portraits of Courage – Elisabeth Elliot, an Ochorus Original whose chapter “The Waiting Years” takes Lamentations 3:26 as its keynote.
  • On Lamentations and Christ (Lamentations 4:20). Augustine’s The City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 33, which reads “the LORD’s anointed” caught in the enemy’s traps as a word about Christ.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Lamentations in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather what the great writers of the church have said about each passage.

Then read Lamentations beside Jeremiah, who saw the disaster coming, and the Psalms, whose laments speak the same language. And keep it for the day you need it. It was written for people who had lost everything, and it has never stopped finding them. At its centre is a sentence that has carried more sufferers through the night than almost any other:
