— Lamentations 1:1

Lamentations is the Bible’s book of grief. Its Hebrew name, Eikhah, is its first word, a cry that means “How!”, and the word returns at the head of the second and fourth poems as well: how the city sits alone, how the Lord has covered Zion with a cloud, how the gold has lost its shine. The English name comes from the old Greek and Latin titles, which call the book the laments, or dirges.

It was written after the worst thing that had ever happened to the people of God. In 587 or 586 BC, after a siege of a year and a half, the Babylonian army broke into Jerusalem, burned the temple Solomon had built, tore down the walls and carried the people away. The city of David, the place where God had set his name, was a heap of ruins with jackals roaming over it. Lamentations is what faith sounded like in the rubble.

It is a short book, five poems in five chapters, and it is not easy to read. It describes famine and slaughter without looking away. But it has given the church some of its most loved words of hope, and it has given generations of sufferers a voice for what they could not otherwise say.

Five poems, and an alphabet

The first four poems are acrostics: each verse, or group of verses, begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Chapters 1, 2 and 4 have twenty-two verses, one for each letter. Chapter 3 has sixty-six, three for each letter, and stands at the centre of the book. Chapter 5 has twenty-two verses again, but the alphabet is gone.

Why write grief in alphabetical order? Readers have offered several answers. An acrostic is easier to learn by heart and to sing together. It also says something: this is suffering from A to Z, the whole of it, nothing left out. And it puts order around what has no order. When a world has collapsed, shaping the cry letter by letter is itself an act of faith, a refusal to let chaos have the last word. That the last poem breaks the pattern, while keeping its length, may be the most eloquent touch of all: the alphabet runs out, and the prayer goes on.

Many of the lines also move in a particular rhythm, a longer half-line followed by a shorter one, which scholars have linked with the funeral dirge. The book is meant to be heard as mourning.

Daughter Zion: the city weeps (Lamentations 1–2)

The first poem begins by looking at Jerusalem from outside, as a widow, a princess put to forced labour, a woman weeping through the night with “not one to comfort her”. That phrase, or something like it, returns five times in the chapter. Halfway through, the city herself begins to speak, and her words have been heard in Holy Week for centuries:

{{OWB:LAM.1.12.s1}}

— Lamentations 1:12

Zion does not pretend to be innocent. “The LORD is in the right, for I rebelled against his word.” Lamentations agrees with the prophets that the disaster was judgement, the curse of the covenant falling at last on a people who had been warned for generations. But it does not let that answer close the conversation. Knowing why does not stop the pain, and the poems say both things at once.

The second poem is harder still. Here the enemy who has torn down the city is named as the Lord himself: “The Lord has become like an enemy.” He has strung his bow, cast off his altar, wiped out the memory of festival and Sabbath. The poet watches little children faint in the squares, asking their mothers, “Where is there grain and wine?”, and he can find nothing to compare it to: “the breach in you is as wide as the sea; who could heal you?” He blames the prophets who told the city comforting lies and never laid bare its guilt. Then he urges the city to do the only thing left:

— Lamentations 2:19

The poem ends not with comfort but with a question flung at heaven: “Look, LORD, and take note: whom have you ever dealt with like this?” Lamentations is a book of prayer, and this is what its prayer sometimes sounds like.

The man who has seen affliction (Lamentations 3)

The third poem changes voice. Instead of the city, a single man speaks: “I am the man who has lived through affliction beneath the rod of his anger.” For twenty verses he describes what God has done to him, and he holds nothing back. God has walled him in, weighed him down with chains, shut out his prayer, waited for him like a bear in ambush, fed him wormwood. “My strength is gone, and so is all I hoped for from the LORD.”

And then, at the very centre of the book, with no change in his circumstances at all, he turns:

{{OWB:LAM.3.21.s1}} {{OWB:LAM.3.22.s1}} {{OWB:LAM.3.23.s1}} {{OWB:LAM.3.24.s1}}

— Lamentations 3:21–24

Nothing outside him has changed. The city is still in ruins. What has changed is what he calls to mind. He remembers who God is: loyal in love, merciful, faithful, new every morning. The hymn “Great Is Thy Faithfulness” grew from these lines, and it is worth remembering where they were first spoken, by a man sitting in ashes.

The poem goes on to some of the deepest words in the Bible about suffering. “It is good to wait in silence for the LORD’s salvation.” “For the Lord does not cast people off for all time.” “For he does not bring affliction willingly or grief to human beings.” The Hebrew says, literally, that he does not afflict “from his heart”: judgement is something God does, but it is not what he delights in. Then the speaker becomes “us”, and leads the people in confession: “Let us look hard at the way we live and test it, and turn back to the LORD.”

Even so, the hope does not settle into calm. The poem returns to tears and to a pit with a stone over it, and the speaker remembers calling on God’s name “from the bottom of the pit”, and hearing him say, “Do not be afraid!” Many readers have heard in this man the voice of Jeremiah, who was lowered into a cistern; Christians have heard in him a greater sufferer still.

The gold grown dim (Lamentations 4)

The fourth poem looks back at the siege with a reporter’s eye. “How the gold has lost its shine!” Children who were worth their weight in gold are treated like clay pots. Princes once brighter than snow are blacker than soot and unrecognised in the streets. Mothers do what the covenant curses had warned of (Deuteronomy 28:53–57). Hope in Egypt’s help came to nothing; the king, “the very breath of our life, the LORD’s anointed”, was caught as he fled. And yet the poem ends with the first plain word of release in the book: “Your punishment is complete, Daughter Zion; he will not keep you in exile any longer.”

A people’s prayer (Lamentations 5)

The last poem is a prayer from first word to last, spoken by the whole community: “Remember, LORD, what has come upon us.” Its verses are short, and they list the losses of ordinary life under occupation: water bought for money, young men hauling millstones, elders gone from the gate, music fallen silent. Near the end it lifts its eyes to the one thing that has not fallen:

— Lamentations 5:19

Then it asks God to turn them back to himself, and ends on a sentence so stark that synagogues, reading the book aloud, repeat the verse before it so as not to finish there.

Who wrote Lamentations?

The book does not say. The ancient Greek translation opens with a note that Jeremiah sat weeping and composed this lament over Jerusalem, and Jewish and Christian tradition long followed it; English Bibles still place the book after Jeremiah. Jeremiah did write laments (2 Chronicles 35:25), lived through the fall, and speaks often of weeping. Many scholars think the poems come from one or more unnamed poets who saw the same events, pointing to differences of style and outlook. Either way, the poems were written soon after the fall of the city, by people who had seen it.

Jewish communities read Lamentations each year on the Ninth of Av, the fast that mourns the destruction of both the first and the second temples. Christians have long read it in Holy Week, hearing in it the sorrow of Christ, who wept over Jerusalem and was himself despised and cut off.

How to read Lamentations

Read it slowly, and preferably aloud. It is short enough to read in one sitting, and is best read that way at least once, to feel its shape: grief, deeper grief, the turn to hope at the centre, grief remembered, and prayer.

Do not hurry to the hopeful verses. They mean what they mean because of where they stand. Lamentations teaches that faith can be honest about pain, and that complaint addressed to God is a form of prayer, not a lack of it. Here Scripture itself gives the sufferer words to say to God that most of us would not dare to say on our own.

And read it beside the rest of the Bible. Jeremiah and Ezekiel tell how the disaster came; Lamentations tells what it felt like. The Psalms of lament speak the same language. And the book of Isaiah answers its question, “Why do you forget us?”, with “Bring comfort, bring comfort to my people” (Isaiah 40:1).

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Lamentations is poetry throughout, and each line is printed on its own, as it is laid out in the Hebrew. The acrostic pattern cannot be carried into English, but the verses keep their shape: twenty-two to a poem, and sixty-six in the third.

“The LORD” and “the Lord.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation writes “the LORD” in capitals; where it has the word for “master”, it writes “the Lord”. Lamentations uses both, sometimes in the same verse.

“Loyal love.” The Hebrew hesed, God’s faithful covenant love, is rendered “loyal love”: “It is by the LORD’s loyal love that we have not been wiped out.”

“Daughter Zion.” The poems personify Jerusalem as a woman, “Daughter Zion”, “Virgin Daughter Judah”, and this translation keeps the titles as they are, because the whole book is built on the picture of a bereaved city who weeps and speaks.
