{{OWB:2JN.1.6.s1}}

— 2 John 1:6

Second John is one of the two shortest books in the Bible. In Greek it is fewer than two hundred and fifty words, and it would have fitted on a single sheet of papyrus, rolled and tied and carried by hand. It can be read aloud in two minutes. But it was not written to be read in passing. It was written to a church in danger, by someone who loved it and could not yet come to it in person.

Readers who have just finished 1 John will feel at home at once. The words are the same: truth, love, command, from the beginning, remain, the Father and the Son, antichrist. In a sense 2 John is the longer letter in miniature, its whole message folded into a page and sent ahead.

The elder and the chosen lady

The letter begins like an ordinary letter of its day, with sender and recipient:

{{OWB:2JN.1.1.s1}}

— 2 John 1:1–2

Neither is named. The writer calls himself simply “the elder”, a title that means both an old man and a leader of the church, and he expects his readers to know who he is. The one he writes to is “the chosen lady and her children”, and here readers have long been divided.

Some take her to be a real Christian woman, perhaps a widow in whose house a church met, with her children grown in the faith. Others, probably the majority, take “the chosen lady” to be a church, personified as a woman the way Israel was called a daughter or a bride, and “her children” to be its members. The letter’s close supports this second reading: “Your chosen sister’s children send you their greetings” (1:13), which sounds like one congregation greeting another. The plural “you” that runs through the middle of the letter points the same way. Either reading makes good sense, and nothing in the letter’s message depends on the choice.

Truth and love

In the first four verses the word truth comes five times. The elder loves his readers “in truth”; so do “all who have come to know the truth”; they love “because of the truth that remains in us and will be with us forever”; grace, mercy and peace will be with us “in truth and love”; and he rejoices to find some of the lady’s children “living in the truth”.

Then comes his one request, and it is about love:

{{OWB:2JN.1.5.s1}}

— 2 John 1:5

It is the command Jesus gave on his last night, “love one another” (John 13:34), and the elder insists it is not new. What is new is the setting. He goes straight on to say what love is: “that we live by his commands” (1:6). Love and obedience are not rivals. And love and truth belong together too, because the danger that comes next is a danger to both.

The deceivers

{{OWB:2JN.1.7.s1}}

— 2 John 1:7

This is the same trouble 1 John speaks of: teachers who would not confess that Jesus Christ had truly come in a human body. Some of them, it seems, were travelling from church to church. The elder warns his readers to be on their guard, “so that you do not lose what we have worked to bring about, but receive your reward in full” (1:8).

He gives them a test, and it is a striking one. The false teachers no doubt presented their ideas as progress, a deeper and more advanced faith. The elder describes them as people who go “too far”:

{{OWB:2JN.1.9.s1}}

— 2 John 1:9

Progress in the Christian life, for the elder, is not moving beyond the teaching of Christ, but going deeper into it. Anyone who leaves it behind has left God behind with it.

Do not receive them

The hardest words in the letter come next. If anyone comes without bringing this teaching, “do not receive that person into your home, and do not give them a greeting” (1:10), because whoever greets such a person “becomes a partner in their evil deeds” (1:11).

These verses need to be read in their own world. Christians in the first century had no church buildings; they met in homes. Travelling teachers depended on the hospitality of believers for food and lodging and for a platform. To take a teacher into your house was to take him into the church that met there, and to send him on his way with a blessing was to recommend him to the next. The elder is not telling Christians to be cold to their neighbours, or to refuse kindness to people who disagree with them. He is telling a church not to give its home and its blessing to those who are spreading a false gospel. Ignatius of Antioch, a few years later, gave the same advice to the church at Smyrna.

Christians have weighed how far this applies today. But the elder’s point is plain: love that has no care for the truth is not love, and a church that opens its door to every teaching will soon have nothing left to protect.

Paper and ink

The letter ends almost as soon as it has begun. “There is a great deal I could write to you, but I chose not to do it with paper and ink” (1:12). The elder would rather come and speak “face to face, so that your joy may be complete”. It is a lovely, human close. Even an apostolic letter, he admits, is a poor substitute for being together.

Who wrote 2 John?

The elder does not give his name. From early times the church linked him with John the apostle, the author of the Gospel and of 1 John, and the language of the three is very close. But the short letters were slower to be accepted everywhere than 1 John; the historian Eusebius, early in the fourth century, lists them among the books disputed by some, “whether they belong to the evangelist or to another person of the same name”. Papias names the elder John among the Lord’s disciples, and Eusebius read him as a second John, distinct from the apostle; some ancient and modern readers have thought him the writer, though others dispute that reading. Either way, the letter belongs with 1 and 3 John, from the same circle and probably the same years, late in the first century.

How to read 2 John

Read it with 1 John open beside it, and notice how the longer letter’s themes reappear here, compressed. Notice the plain shape of a first-century letter: greeting (1:1–3), thanksgiving and request (1:4–6), warning (1:7–11), and close (1:12–13). And notice that the warning is framed on both sides by love. The elder guards the door because he loves the people inside.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Two of its choices in 2 John are worth knowing about.

“Goes too far.” In verse 9, where older Bibles say “transgresseth” or “runs ahead”, this translation says “goes too far”: the Greek word means to lead the way forward, and here it describes those who claim to have moved on past the teaching of Christ.

“The chosen lady.” The translation keeps the elder’s own phrase, without deciding for the reader whether he means a woman or a church. The Open Word Bible also lets a reader choose “Christ” or “the Messiah” for Christos; this edition prints “Christ”.
