Second John ends with a hope rather than a conclusion. The elder has said only what could not wait, and saved the rest for a visit. Whether he ever made it we do not know. But the letter he sent instead has outlasted any conversation, and it still says what he most wanted his children to hear.

The question the book leaves with you

The letter asks how truth and love belong together. Many people today assume they pull in opposite directions: that to love people is to stop insisting on what is true, or that to hold firmly to the truth is to stop loving. The elder will not accept either. He loves his readers “in truth”. He defines love as living by God’s commands. And he guards the church against false teachers precisely because he loves it.

So the question is not whether to choose truth or love, but whether our love is truthful and our truth is loving. A church can fail on either side. It can welcome everything and stand for nothing; or it can stand for the right things with a cold heart. The elder’s little letter calls us away from both.

Where to go from here

No book in this library is given wholly to 2 John, but several of the best stand very close to it:

  • The longer letter. Augustine of Hippo’s Homilies on the First Epistle of John, on the letter whose themes 2 John gathers into a page.
  • On loving one another. F. B. Meyer’s Thrice Bidden to Love (John 13:34), on the command the elder says is not new.
  • On guarding the church. Gareth Evans’s Guard the Truth, on Paul’s farewell to the elders of Ephesus (Acts 20:17–32), with its warning of wolves who would come in among the flock.
  • From the generation after John. Ignatius of Antioch, in The Epistles of Ignatius and His Life’s Story, warns the church at Smyrna against teachers who said Christ suffered only in appearance, men “whom not only should ye not receive, but, if it were possible, not so much as meet”. The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp repeats John’s warning about those who do not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh.
  • On the church’s long fight for the truth. Irenaeus of Lyons’s Against Heresies, which quotes verse 11 of this letter, and Eusebius of Caesarea’s The Church History, which records how some in the early church hesitated over 2 and 3 John, and why.

But the best next step may be to read all three of John’s letters together, at one sitting, and then this one again. Its last line, so ordinary, will sound different: a church greeting a church, one family of the truth to another.
