{{OWB:REV.1.3.s1}}

— Revelation 1:3

No book of the Bible has been more feared, more avoided, or more argued over than Revelation. It has been used to frighten people, to calculate the end of the world, and to put names to the beast; and many Christians, weary of all that, have simply left it closed. Yet it is the only book of the Bible that begins by promising a blessing to the person who reads it aloud and to those who listen. It was written to be heard in church, from beginning to end, by ordinary believers. It expected them to understand it, and to be strengthened by it.

The key is in the first words: “This is the revelation of Jesus Christ.” The Greek word is apokalypsis, an unveiling, the pulling back of a curtain. Revelation is not a book of riddles meant to hide the future. It is a book of pictures meant to show the present as heaven sees it: who truly rules the world, what the powers of the age really are, and how the story ends. Above all it is a revelation of Jesus himself, the one who was dead and is alive for all the ages to come.

A vision, a prophecy and a letter

Revelation is three kinds of writing at once. It is an apocalypse, a vision full of symbols, of a kind well known in Jewish writing of the time, with the book of Daniel as its great model. It is a prophecy, as John calls it in 1:3, a word from God to his people about their own day as well as the days to come. And it is a letter: “From John, to the seven churches in the province of Asia” (1:4), with a greeting of grace and peace like any letter of Paul.

Each matters. Because it is an apocalypse, its images are meant to be seen and felt rather than decoded one by one, as a political cartoon is read as a whole rather than line by line. Because it is a prophecy, it speaks with God’s authority and calls for a response. Because it is a letter, it was written to real churches in real cities, and whatever else it means, it must have meant something to them.

John on Patmos

{{OWB:REV.1.9.s1}}

— Revelation 1:9

Patmos is a small rocky island off the coast of what is now Turkey. John was there, it seems, in exile, as a punishment for his preaching. “On the Lord’s day I came to be in the Spirit”, he writes (1:10), and behind him he heard a voice like a trumpet.

Irenaeus, writing around AD 180, says the vision was seen “towards the end of Domitian’s reign”, that is, in the mid-90s, and most scholars still date it then. Others date it to the late 60s, after Nero’s persecution of the Christians in Rome. The writer calls himself simply John, a servant and a brother. From the second century most of the church identified him with John the apostle. But Dionysius, bishop of Alexandria in the third century, noticed how different the Greek of Revelation is from that of the Gospel and letters of John, and thought it the work of another John, a holy and inspired man; many scholars today agree. Either way, he writes as a prophet known to the churches of Asia, sharing their troubles and their hope.

The one like a son of man

The first thing John sees is not a beast or a battle but Jesus, standing among seven gold lampstands, which are the seven churches. He is “one like a son of man”, the figure of Daniel 7, with hair white as snow, eyes like flaming fire, and a face as bright as the sun. John falls at his feet like a dead man:

{{OWB:REV.1.17.s1}}

— Revelation 1:17–18

That is how the book begins, and everything else follows from it. Before John sees the terrors of the world, he sees that the one who holds the keys of death is the one who laid his hand on him and said, “Do not be afraid.”

Seven letters to seven churches

Chapters 2 and 3 hold seven short letters from the risen Jesus, to Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea. Each begins “I know your works”. Each describes Jesus in words drawn from the opening vision. Each ends with a promise “to everyone who overcomes”, and with the refrain, “Whoever has ears should listen to what the Spirit is saying to the churches.”

The churches are not alike. Ephesus works hard and guards the truth but has let go of “the love you had in the beginning” (2:4). Smyrna is poor and persecuted, and is told, “Stay faithful even to death, and I will give you the crown of life” (2:10). Pergamum and Thyatira have made peace with idolatry. Sardis has a name for being alive, but is dead. Philadelphia has little strength but has kept Christ’s word. Laodicea is rich and satisfied and “lukewarm, neither hot nor cold” (3:16), and to that comfortable church Jesus speaks the most tender words of all:

{{OWB:REV.3.20.s1}}

— Revelation 3:20

Two dangers run through the letters: persecution from outside, and compromise from within. Some of the churches are in danger of being crushed; others are in danger of being too comfortable to notice that anything is wrong. The rest of the book is written for both.

The throne and the Lamb

In chapter 4 John sees “a door standing open” in heaven, and is taken up to see a throne, with someone seated on it, surrounded by a rainbow, by twenty-four elders and by four living creatures who never stop saying, “Holy, holy, holy, Lord God, the Almighty, the one who was and who is and who is coming.” Before anything happens on earth, John is shown who is on the throne. The world is not out of control.

Then, in chapter 5, comes the turning point of the whole book. In God’s right hand is a scroll sealed with seven seals, and no one is worthy to open it. John weeps bitterly. One of the elders tells him to stop weeping:

{{OWB:REV.5.5.s1}}

— Revelation 5:5

John turns to see the Lion, and sees instead a Lamb:

{{OWB:REV.5.6.s1}}

— Revelation 5:6

This is the heart of Revelation. The Lion of Judah, the conquering King Israel longed for, has conquered as a slaughtered Lamb. God’s power to bring history to its goal is the power of the cross. From here on the book calls Jesus “the Lamb” nearly thirty times, and all heaven sings to him a new song: “at the cost of your blood you bought for God those from every tribe and language and people and nation” (5:9).

Seals, trumpets and bowls

The Lamb opens the seals, and a series of judgments begins: the four horsemen of conquest, war, famine and death; the cry of the martyrs under the altar, “how long”; the great earthquake; and at the seventh seal, silence in heaven “for about half an hour” (8:1). Then seven trumpets sound, and then seven bowls of God’s fury are poured out. Many of these plagues echo the plagues of Egypt, and the people of God who come through them sing “the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb” (15:3). Revelation is telling the story of a new exodus.

The three series are not simply one long timeline. Each seems to bring us to the edge of the end and then start again, more intense each time, as though the same reality were being shown from three angles. And between them come pauses, in which the people of God are shown safe. In chapter 7, before the winds of judgment blow, God’s servants are sealed on their foreheads. John hears their number, 144,000, from every tribe of Israel; then he turns and sees “a crowd so vast that no one could number it”, from every nation, standing before the throne. As with the Lion and the Lamb, what John hears and what he sees are, many readers think, the same people described two ways.

Numbers that mean

Revelation is full of numbers, and almost all of them are symbols. Seven is the number of completeness: seven churches, spirits, seals, trumpets, bowls. Twelve is the number of God’s people, the twelve tribes and the twelve apostles; twelve times twelve times a thousand is 144,000. Three and a half, half of seven, is the time of trouble, cut short: it appears as 1,260 days, as forty-two months, and as “a time, and times, and half a time”, a phrase from Daniel. A thousand is a great and complete number. Reading these as a calendar has led to much confusion; reading them as John’s first hearers would have done opens the book up.

The dragon, the beasts and Babylon

Chapters 12 to 18 pull back the curtain further, to show the conflict behind the conflicts. A woman clothed with the sun gives birth to a son who is to rule the nations, and a great red dragon waits to devour him. The child is caught up to God; there is war in heaven; and the dragon, “the serpent of old, the one called the devil and Satan, deceiver of the whole world” (12:9), is thrown down. He has lost, and knows his time is short. How do God’s people overcome him?

{{OWB:REV.12.11.s1}}

— Revelation 12:11

Then the dragon calls up two beasts: one from the sea, a monstrous empire that blasphemes God and makes war on his people, and one from the land, with horns like a lamb’s and a voice like a dragon’s, which makes everyone worship the first. The beast is a grim parody of the Lamb: it too has a wound that was healed; it too is worshipped by every tribe and nation. Its mark, and its number, 666, have been fitted to countless names over the centuries. Irenaeus warned long ago that the number “is capable of being fitted to many names”. Many scholars think John’s first readers would have heard in it the name of Nero, whose name in Hebrew letters adds up to 666; others take it as a symbol of a power that always falls short of seven. Either way, John’s point is less a puzzle than a warning: “Here is where God’s holy people need endurance and faith” (13:10).

And then there is Babylon, “Babylon the great, mother of prostitutes and of the earth’s abominations” (17:5), seated on seven mountains, drunk on the blood of God’s people, rich with the trade of the whole world. For John’s first hearers, a city on seven hills could only be Rome. But Babylon is more than one city. She is every power that seduces the world with wealth and luxury and is built on the suffering of others; the long list of her cargoes in chapter 18 ends with “slaves, human lives”. God’s word to his people is not to fight her but to leave her: “Leave her, my people, so that you have no part in her sins” (18:4).

The rider, the thousand years and the throne

When Babylon falls, heaven breaks into song: “Hallelujah! For the Lord God, the Almighty, has begun to reign” (19:6). The wedding of the Lamb has come. Heaven opens, and a rider on a white horse comes out, named Faithful and True, his robe dipped in blood, and “the name by which he is called is the Word of God” (19:13). His only weapon is the sword that comes from his mouth.

Chapter 20 tells of Satan bound for a thousand years, of martyrs who come to life and reign with Christ, of a last rebellion, and of the great white throne where the dead are judged. Few chapters of the Bible have been read in more ways. Some Christians, called premillennialists, believe Christ will return and then reign on earth for a thousand years; Irenaeus and Justin Martyr held a form of this view. Others, amillennialists, following Augustine in The City of God, take the thousand years as the present age of the church, between Christ’s first coming and his return. Still others, postmillennialists, expect a long age of the gospel’s triumph before he comes. All agree on the end: Christ returns, the dead are raised, evil is judged, and death itself is thrown into the lake of fire.

Everything new

And then the last vision, the most beautiful in the Bible:

{{OWB:REV.21.1.s1}} {{OWB:REV.21.2.s1}}

— Revelation 21:1–2

The holy city does not rise from earth to heaven; it comes down from heaven to earth. God makes his home with human beings, and wipes every tear from their eyes. The one on the throne says, “See, I am making everything new!” (21:5). The city is a perfect cube, like the holy of holies in Solomon’s temple, and so “There was no temple to be seen in the city: its temple is the Lord God, the Almighty, and the Lamb” (21:22). Through it flows the river of the water of life, and beside it grows the tree of life, whose “leaves bring healing to the nations” (22:2).

The Bible that began in a garden ends in a garden-city. The tree of life, lost in Genesis, is given back. The curse is lifted: “Nothing accursed will be found there any longer” (22:3). And the thing every human heart was made for is given at last: “they will look upon his face” (22:4).

Four ways of reading

Christians have read Revelation in four broad ways, and most thoughtful readers draw something from more than one. Preterists read it chiefly as a message about John’s own time, the struggle of the early church with Rome. Historicists read it as a map of church history from the first century to the end. Futurists read most of it, from chapter 4 on, as describing events still to come, just before Christ’s return. Idealists read it as a picture of the struggle between God and evil in every age. Each has insight to offer, and each can be pressed too far. Whatever one’s view, the book’s message to every age is the same: Jesus is Lord, evil will not have the last word, and those who stay faithful will share his victory.

How to read Revelation

Read it aloud, as it was meant to be read; it takes about an hour and a quarter. Its shape is simple enough to hold: the vision of Christ and the seven letters (1–3); the throne and the Lamb (4–5); the seals, trumpets and bowls, with their pauses (6–16); the fall of Babylon (17–19:10); the final victory, the thousand years and the judgment (19:11–20:15); and the new creation (21–22).

Listen for the Old Testament. Revelation hardly quotes it directly, yet nearly every verse alludes to it: to Exodus, Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, Zechariah and the Psalms. Listen for the songs, which come at every turning point and have filled the church’s hymnbooks. And listen for the seven blessings scattered through the book (1:3; 14:13; 16:15; 19:9; 20:6; 22:7; 22:14). They show what the book is for. It is not written to make its readers anxious, but blessed.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Revelation are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Affliction” and “overcomes.” Where older Bibles say “tribulation”, this translation says “affliction”, the same word John uses of the troubles he shares with his readers (1:9); so the “great tribulation” of 7:14 is here “the great affliction”. And it keeps “overcome” for the Greek word of victory, from the promises to the churches to the Lamb who overcomes the kings of the earth, so that a reader can follow the theme through the book.

Places where manuscripts differ. In 1:5 it reads that Christ “by his blood released us from our sins”; some later manuscripts have “washed”, a difference of one letter in Greek. In 22:14 it reads “How blessed are those who wash their robes!”, where the King James Version, following later manuscripts, has “do his commandments”. The number of the beast is 666; a few early witnesses read 616, as Irenaeus already knew.

Measures. The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose modern measures or the original ones. This edition prints the modern ones, so the holy city measures “about 1,400 miles” and its wall “about 216 feet”. In the Greek they are 12,000 stadia and 144 cubits, numbers built on twelve, and in Revelation that matters.

Numbering. The verse about the dragon standing on the seashore is 12:18 in some Bibles and the start of 13:1 in others. References here follow the King James numbering. And where the Greek says Christos, the Open Word Bible lets a reader choose “Christ” or “the Messiah”; this edition prints “Christ”.
