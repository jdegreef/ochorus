— Revelation 21:3–4

Revelation ends the Bible where the Bible began, with God and human beings together in a garden, and nothing between them. Everything that went wrong in Genesis has been put right. The tree of life is given back; the curse is gone; death is no more; and God himself wipes away the tears. The book that so many people fear turns out to be, at its end, the most tender book in the Bible.

The question the book leaves with you

Revelation asks its readers whom they will worship. That is the dividing line through the whole book. On one side are those who worship the one on the throne and the Lamb; on the other, those who worship the dragon and the beast, and take its mark. There is no third side. Babylon is beautiful and rich, and the beast seems unbeatable: “Who is equal to the beast? Who could fight a war against it?” (13:4). The pressure to bow down, or simply to go along, is very great.

Against it, Revelation sets a vision. It shows John’s hearers, and us, that the throne is not empty, that the Lamb who was slaughtered has already overcome, and that the powers which look so strong are already falling. And it calls for the response the risen Jesus asks of every one of the seven churches: to overcome, not by force, but by faithfulness. “Stay faithful even to death, and I will give you the crown of life.”

Where the book points

It points to Jesus, who is both the first word and the last: “I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end” (22:13). It points to worship, since every vision in the book leads to a song, and the songs of Revelation have become the church’s songs: “Holy, holy, holy”, “Worthy is the Lamb”, “Hallelujah”, “King of kings and Lord of lords”. And it points forward, to a hope that is not escape from the world but its renewal. The last vision is not of souls going up to heaven but of a city coming down, and of God making his home with us.

Where to go from here

Revelation has filled the church’s sermons, hymns and poems for nineteen centuries, and many of the best are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the risen Christ and the seven churches (chapters 1–3). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Till He Come, whose address “Swooning and Reviving at Christ’s Feet” is on 1:17–18; and William J. Seymour’s Christ’s Messages to the Church (1:5–7; 2:1–7).
  • From the churches John wrote to. Ignatius of Antioch’s letters to Ephesus, Philadelphia and Smyrna, in The Epistles of Ignatius and His Life’s Story; and The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp, the story of Smyrna’s old bishop, who said at his trial, “Eighty and six years have I served Him”, and was faithful even to death.
  • On the Lion and the Lamb (chapter 5). Jonathan Edwards’s The Excellency of Christ (5:5–6) and John Newton’s Worthy Is the Lamb (5:12).
  • On the war in heaven and the faithful witnesses (chapters 6–13). John Milton’s Paradise Lost, whose sixth book tells of Michael’s war with the rebel angels, and John Foxe’s Fox’s Book of Martyrs, whose opening chapters tell of the persecutions under which John was sent to Patmos.
  • On the thousand years and the judgment (chapter 20). Augustine of Hippo’s The City of God, whose twentieth book sets out the reading of the thousand years that much of the church has followed since; and Irenaeus of Lyons’s Against Heresies, whose fifth book reads them differently and discusses the number of the beast.
  • On who wrote the book, and when. Eusebius of Caesarea’s The Church History, which records John’s return from Patmos and Dionysius of Alexandria’s careful case that the writer was another John.
  • On the city and the invitation (chapters 21–22). John Bunyan’s The Pilgrim’s Progress, which ends at the gates of the Celestial City; Richard Baxter’s The Saints’ Everlasting Rest; and Spurgeon’s Come and Welcome, on the Spirit and the bride saying “Come!” (22:17).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Revelation in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

But the best next step may be to read Revelation again, aloud, all the way through, and to let it do what it was written to do: not to satisfy curiosity about the future, but to give courage for the present, and to turn the heart toward the one who is coming.
