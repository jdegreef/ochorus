— Colossians 1:15–20

Colossians is a short letter with the largest possible subject. It was written to a small church in a country town that Paul had never visited, and it was carried there by a friend named Tychicus, who travelled with a slave named Onesimus. Yet in its first chapter it speaks of Christ as the one through whom everything in heaven and on earth was created, in whom all things are held together, and through whom God will reconcile all things to himself.

Paul writes that way because the church at Colossae was being told it needed something more than Christ: a deeper philosophy, stricter rules, visions, the help of angels. His answer fills four chapters and can be put in one line from the third: “Christ is everything, and he is in all.”

Colossae and its church

Colossae lay inland from Ephesus, in the valley of the river Lycus, some ten miles from two larger cities, Laodicea and Hierapolis. It had been an important town once, on the road from the coast to the east, but by Paul’s day it was overshadowed by its neighbours. Laodicea, rich and confident, is the church that Revelation later calls lukewarm (Revelation 3:14–22).

Paul did not found the church. He speaks of the readers as among “everyone who has never met me in person” (2:1). The gospel came to them through Epaphras, one of their own: “You learned it from Epaphras, our dear fellow servant” (1:7). Most likely Epaphras heard it during the years Paul spent in Ephesus, when, as Acts says, all the people of the province of Asia heard the word of the Lord (Acts 19:10). Now Epaphras is with Paul, and the news he has brought is mixed: the church is growing in faith and love, but it is being unsettled by teachers who offer it something more.

Who wrote it

The letter comes from Paul and Timothy (1:1), and Paul signs it in his own hand at the end, adding, “Remember that I am in chains” (4:18). Its closest companion is the little letter to Philemon. The same people are with Paul in both, Timothy, Epaphras, Aristarchus, Mark, Luke and Demas; Archippus is named in both; and Onesimus, the subject of Philemon, travels with Tychicus to Colossae (4:9). Most readers have taken the two as written and sent together, from prison, probably in Rome around AD 60–62, though some place them in an earlier imprisonment at Ephesus.

Some scholars think Colossians was written in Paul’s name by one of his disciples, pointing to differences of style and to its vision of Christ as head of the whole church and of the universe. Others answer that a new problem called for new words, and that the letter’s ties to Philemon, which almost everyone accepts as Paul’s, make his authorship the simplest explanation. The church has always read it as his.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapter 1: Christ above all. Thanksgiving and prayer for the Colossians; the great passage on the Son, the image of the unseen God; and Paul’s own work, to make known “Christ in you, the hope of glory.”
  • Chapter 2: Christ is enough. A warning against an empty philosophy, and the answer to it: in Christ all the fullness of God dwells, and in him you have been made full.
  • Chapters 3–4: life hidden with Christ. Setting the mind on what is above; the old self and the new; a household in the Lord; prayer, wise speech and a long list of friends.

The image of the unseen God

Paul opens, as he usually does, with thanks and prayer. Then, almost without a pause, he begins to speak of the Son, in the lines that open this introduction. Many readers think they were an early Christian hymn, and they fall into two halves. In the first, the Son is Lord of creation: everything was made in him, through him and for him, and in him all things are held together. In the second, he is Lord of the new creation: the head of the church, the first to be born from among the dead, the one through whom God makes peace “by the blood of his cross.”

No passage in Paul says more about who Christ is. The Son is not one heavenly power among many, to be added to the others. Every power in the universe, “whether thrones or dominions, rulers or authorities,” was made through him and for him. In the fourth century the followers of Arius pointed to the word “firstborn” to argue that the Son was the first of God’s creatures, and Athanasius answered them from the passage itself: if all creation came to be through the Son and holds together in him, how can he be one of the things that hold together in him?

Paul then tells his readers what this means for them. They were once estranged from God; now they have been reconciled by Christ’s death. And the mystery hidden for ages has been revealed, among Gentiles of all people: “Christ in you, the hope of glory.” Paul says that in his own sufferings he is “completing what is still lacking in Christ’s afflictions, for the sake of his body, the church” (1:24). Readers of every tradition agree that he cannot mean Christ’s saving death was not enough; most take him to mean the sufferings that Christ’s people must still bear, which Christ counts as his own.

The philosophy and the fullness

In chapter 2 Paul turns to the danger. He warns against “philosophy that is empty deceit,” which follows human tradition and “the elemental forces of the world,” not Christ. He mentions food and drink, festivals, new moons and the sabbath; self-humbling and “the worship of angels”; visions; and rules like “Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch.”

No one is quite sure what the Colossian teaching was, since Paul never sets it out. Some think it was a form of Jewish mysticism, in which strict fasting and observance prepared the soul for visions of the angels worshipping in heaven; others, a blend of Jewish practice and local folk religion. Paul’s answer does not depend on the details:

The Colossians need nothing more, because in Christ they have everything. God has forgiven all their trespasses, wiped out the bond of debt that stood against them, “nailing it to the cross,” and led the hostile powers captive in his victory procession. Rules about food and festivals are “a shadow of what was coming; the substance is Christ.” And the hard regulations of self-denial, for all their look of wisdom, are worth nothing for holding back the indulgence of the flesh.

Raised with Christ

What does work, then? Not rules, but a new life:

— Colossians 3:1–4

From that, everything else follows. The old ways are put to death and taken off like old clothes, and the new self is put on, “being made new, growing in knowledge, in the image of its Creator.” In this new humanity it no longer counts whether one is Greek or Jew, slave or free. Believers are to put on compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness and patience, and to forgive as the Lord has forgiven them. “Over all of these put on love.” Christ’s peace is to settle their disputes, his word is to make its home among them richly, and everything they say and do is to be done in the name of the Lord Jesus, with thanks.

A household in the Lord

Like Ephesians, Colossians then speaks to each member of a household: wives and husbands, children and fathers, slaves and masters (3:18–4:1). Christians apply these words to marriage today in different ways, and the words to slaves belong to a world where slavery was taken for granted. But it is striking how much more Paul says to slaves than to anyone else, telling them that they will receive an inheritance from the Lord and that it is the Lord Christ they serve. And he tells masters to give their slaves “what is right and fair,” because they too have a Master in heaven. These words were read aloud in a church where Onesimus, a slave returning to his master, now sat as “our faithful and dear brother, who is one of your own.”

A circle of friends

The letter ends with prayer, with a word about speech that is gracious and “seasoned with salt”, and with greetings that let us glimpse the people around Paul. Aristarchus, his fellow prisoner. Mark, “the cousin of Barnabas,” once the cause of a quarrel between Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39), now commended. “Our dear friend Luke, the doctor.” Epaphras, still wrestling in prayer for his home church. And Nympha, with the church that meets in her house. The Colossians are to swap letters with Laodicea (4:16); the letter from Laodicea has not survived, unless, as some think, it is the one we call Ephesians.

How to read Colossians

Read it alongside Philemon, which was carried on the same journey, and alongside Ephesians, which shares so much of its wording. Read it first straight through, in a quarter of an hour, and then slowly.

Notice the words “all” and “fullness”, which keep returning, and the phrases “in him” and “with him”: buried with him, raised with him, hidden with him, revealed with him. Notice how Paul answers every offer of something more with Christ himself. And notice how a letter that begins with the creation of the universe ends with a list of ordinary people, greeted by name.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Colossians are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Firstborn.” In 1:15 the translation reads “the firstborn who stands above all creation,” making clear that the word is about rank, the place of the heir, and not about the Son being the first thing created.

“Elemental forces.” Paul’s phrase in 2:8 and 2:20 is hard to pin down. It may mean the basic principles of religion, or the powers thought to rule the world. The translation says “the elemental forces of the world,” which leaves room for both.

“The umpire in your hearts.” In 3:15 Paul uses a word from the games, for the judge who settles a contest, and the translation keeps the picture: “Let the peace that Christ gives be the umpire in your hearts.”

Places where manuscripts differ. In 1:14 it reads, with the oldest manuscripts, “In him we have redemption, which is the forgiveness of our sins”; some later manuscripts add “through his blood”, from Ephesians 1:7. And in 4:15 it reads Nympha, a woman, and “the church that meets in her house”; other manuscripts have a man’s name.
