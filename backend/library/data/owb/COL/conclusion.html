— Colossians 4:18

At the end of the letter Paul takes the pen from his secretary and writes three short sentences of his own. After the soaring lines about the Son through whom all things were made, the last thing the Colossians see is a prisoner’s handwriting, and a request not to forget his chains. It is a fitting close. The Lord of all creation is known in this letter by people in very ordinary places: a country town, a house church, a prison cell.

The question the letter leaves

Colossians asks whether Christ is enough. The teachers at Colossae did not deny Christ; they simply offered more. A deeper wisdom, a stricter discipline, a higher experience, a way to the angels. Every age has its own version of that offer, and it is always attractive, because it promises progress and gives people something to do.

Paul’s answer is not that wisdom, discipline and experience are bad. It is that they are found in Christ, or not at all. In him are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge. In him the whole fullness of God dwells bodily, and in him believers have been made full. To look for something beyond him is not to go deeper but to lose hold of the head. The question the letter leaves is whether we will let Christ be everything, or go on looking for something more.

Where the letter points

It points upward. Those who have been raised with Christ are to seek what is above, where Christ sits at the right hand of God, and to fix their thoughts there. Their life is hidden with him, and when he is revealed they will be revealed with him in glory.

And it points to the church as a new kind of household. Greek and Jew, slave and free, sit together under one Master. A slave who had left Colossae under a cloud comes home as a faithful and dear brother. The letter is to be read aloud, then passed to Laodicea. Christ is not only above all things; he is in all, and he makes a people.

Where to go from here

Colossians has always drawn preachers to its picture of Christ, and many of their sermons and books are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On Christ above all (chapter 1). Athanasius’s Against the Arians, whose second Discourse, in the chapter “Our Lord Not Said to Be Created”, answers those who took “firstborn” in 1:15 to make the Son a creature; A. B. Simpson’s Himself (1:27); and George MacDonald’s “The Inheritance” (1:12), in his Unspoken Sermons.
  • On Christ as enough (chapter 2). Andrew Murray’s Abide in Christ, whose chapter “As You Came to Him, by Faith” is on 2:6–7; and Charles H. Spurgeon’s “Complete in Christ” (2:10), in The Saint and His Saviour.
  • On Christ as everything (3:11). Two sermons on the same three words: J. C. Ryle’s Christ Is All and Dwight L. Moody’s Christ All in All.
  • On the hidden life (chapter 3). Andrew Murray’s “Christ Our Life” (3:4), in The Master’s Indwelling, and “Who Are You?” (3:2–3), in The Inner Chamber; and John Owen’s The Mortification of Sin in Believers, which sets out the duty of 3:5, putting sin to death, beside Romans 8:13.
  • On Paul’s friends (chapter 4). Devoted to Prayer, whose chapter “Always Wrestling in Prayer” is about Epaphras (4:12); and J. C. Ryle’s “Luke, the Beloved Physician” (4:14), in The Upper Room.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Colossians in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.
