— Nahum 1:7

Nahum is a book about one city. Its first words are “An oracle about Nineveh”, and from there to its last line it never looks away. There is no call to Nineveh to repent and no word of hope for her, only the news, told in some of the most vivid poetry in the Bible, that the great capital of Assyria is about to fall.

The prophet’s name means “comfort”, and that is a clue to how the book should be read. It is written not to Nineveh but to the people Nineveh had crushed, for whom its end was good news.

Nineveh

For more than a century Assyria was the terror of the ancient Near East. Its armies destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel and carried its people into exile in 722 BC. Twenty years later they swept through Judah, took its fortified towns and shut King Hezekiah up in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage”, as the Assyrian king boasted in his own records. Assyrian kings lined their palaces with carved scenes of conquered cities and captives, and boasted of their cruelty in inscriptions. Nahum’s picture of Nineveh as a lion’s den, “where the lion and lioness prowled with their cubs, and no one disturbed them”, is not exaggeration.

The book can be dated within a few decades. It speaks of the fall of No-amon, the Egyptian city of Thebes, “who sat by the streams of the Nile”, as something already past (3:8–10); the Assyrians sacked Thebes in 663 BC. And it looks forward to the fall of Nineveh itself, which came in 612 BC, when an alliance of Babylonians and Medes took the city. Nahum prophesied somewhere between the two, while Assyria still seemed unbeatable. Of the prophet himself we know only that he came from Elkosh, a village whose place no one can now find with certainty.

The shape of the book

  • 1:1–8: the God who judges. A hymn to the LORD as a warrior and judge, before whom the mountains quake. Its lines begin, more or less in order, with the first half of the letters of the Hebrew alphabet.
  • 1:9–15: two audiences. Short words turn back and forth between Nineveh, which is told it is finished, and Judah, which is told its yoke will be broken.
  • Chapter 2: the fall of the city. The attack, told as if the reader were standing on the walls: red shields, racing chariots, river gates thrown open, treasure looted.
  • Chapter 3: woe to the city of bloodshed. The reasons for the judgement, the example of Thebes, and a mocking dirge over the king of Assyria.

Slow to grow angry

The book begins not with Nineveh but with God. Before a single word about Assyria, Nahum sets out who it is that Assyria has to deal with:

{{OWB:NAM.1.3.s1}}

— Nahum 1:3

These words come from the oldest confession of God’s character in the Bible, the one he proclaimed to Moses on Mount Sinai, “a God of tender compassion and grace, slow to grow angry”, who forgives wrongdoing, “Yet he will never acquit the guilty” (Exodus 34:6–7).

Another prophet had once quoted the same confession about the same city. When Nineveh repented at Jonah’s preaching, Jonah sulked, because, he said, “I knew that you are a God of grace and tender compassion, slow to grow angry, rich in faithful love” (Jonah 4:2). The two little books belong together. Jonah shows the first half of the confession: God is slow to anger, and spares a city that turns. Nahum, a century or so later, shows the second: he is slow, but he is not indifferent, and he will not acquit the guilty for ever. Both books end with a question about Nineveh.

Good news on the mountains

For Judah, the fall of Assyria meant freedom. In the middle of the first chapter the LORD speaks to his people directly: “I have afflicted you, but I will afflict you no more.” Then comes the announcement:

— Nahum 1:15

Isaiah uses almost the same picture for the end of the exile in Babylon: “What a welcome sight on the mountains, the feet of one who brings good news!” (Isaiah 52:7). Paul quotes Isaiah’s line for the preaching of the gospel itself (Romans 10:15). In the Greek translation of the Old Testament, the word for the messenger who “brings good news” is the word from which the New Testament gets its word gospel.

A hard book

Many readers find Nahum difficult. The poetry of chapters 2 and 3 is the poetry of a sacked city: horses galloping, “heaps of corpses”, a city “stripped and carried away”, and, in the memory of Thebes, little ones dashed to pieces at every street corner. Can this be the word of the God of Jonah, who cared for Nineveh’s people and even its animals?

Three things help. First, the book is about God’s justice, not Judah’s revenge. Judah lifts no sword in it; the LORD acts, and his people are told only to keep their festivals. Scripture forbids people to take revenge for themselves, and leaves it to God, who alone can repay justly (Romans 12:19).

Second, the book says why. Nineveh is “the city of bloodshed”, which has looted nations and sold peoples. Its last line is not a cheer but a question that every nation Assyria had trampled could answer: “for who has not felt your endless cruelty?”

Third, the hymn at the start sets limits. The God who brings Nineveh down is the same one who is “a stronghold in the day of trouble”, who “knows those who take refuge in him”. His anger is the settled opposition of the good to what destroys his creatures. For anyone who has lived under a cruel power, the message that it will not last for ever is not bloodthirsty. It is comfort.

How to read Nahum

Nahum is short, three chapters, and best read in one sitting, aloud if you can. Its poetry is fast, full of short cries, and meant to be heard. Read Jonah beside it, and notice how differently the two books speak about the same city and the same God.

As you read, keep in mind who first heard it: a small kingdom that had lived for a century under the shadow of the most powerful army on earth. Then let it widen: Babylon falls in Isaiah and Jeremiah, and a greater Babylon in Revelation. Every empire that trusts in its own strength is Nineveh. The question the book puts to its readers is the one in its first hymn: “Who can stand before his indignation?” And its answer is the verse at the head of this introduction: those who take refuge in him.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Nahum is poetry almost from beginning to end, and it is printed here one line per line, as the poetry it is. A few of the translation’s choices are worth knowing.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”. Twice the LORD speaks in his own voice to Nineveh: “I am against you, declares the LORD of Armies.” “The LORD of Armies” renders the title older Bibles translate “the LORD of hosts”: he commands the armies of heaven, and no earthly army can stand against him.

An old confession. The translation renders Nahum 1:3 in the same words it uses for God’s self-description to Moses in Exodus 34:6–7, “slow to grow angry” and “will never acquit the guilty”, so that the echo can be heard in English as it is in Hebrew.

Numbering. Hebrew Bibles count the verse about the feet on the mountains as the first verse of chapter 2, so chapter 2 has one more verse than in English. References in this edition follow the numbering of English Bibles.
