— Obadiah 1:21

The shortest book in the Old Testament ends with one of its largest claims. After twenty verses about a small nation’s pride and a terrible day in Jerusalem, Obadiah lifts his eyes to the end of all things, and what he sees there is not a nation but a King.

The question the book leaves with you

Obadiah asks where we stand on the day of someone else’s disaster. Edom’s guilt began with standing at a distance, and went on to gloating, boasting and looting. Most of us will never hand a refugee to his enemies, but every one of us has felt the small, ugly pleasure of watching a rival fall. Obadiah calls that pleasure by its name, and reminds us that the one we watch is a brother.

And it asks what we trust in. Edom trusted its heights, its allies and its wise men, and all three failed it. The book’s final answer to pride is not humiliation for its own sake but the kingdom of God, in which the high are brought low and the scattered are brought home.

Where the story goes next

Obadiah’s words came true. In the centuries after the exile, the Edomites were driven from their mountains and pushed west into southern Judah, where they became known as the Idumeans. Herod the Great, the king who tried to kill the child born in Bethlehem, was of Idumean descent. After the first century the Edomites disappear from history. But the kingdom Obadiah foresaw did not disappear. The book of Revelation hears loud voices in heaven announce it: “The rule over the world now belongs to our Lord and to his Christ, and he will reign forever and ever.”

Where to go from here

  • On standing by (1:11). Charles H. Spurgeon’s reading for July 23 in Morning by Morning, on the brotherly kindness Edom owed and refused.
  • On pride and its fall (1:3–4). Jerome’s letter to Eustochium (Letter XXII in The Letters of St. Jerome), which hears Obadiah’s eagle among the stars as a warning against the pride by which Lucifer fell.
  • On deliverance and holiness (1:17). Thomas Watson’s The Ten Commandments, whose chapter on the preface to the commandments takes up Obadiah’s promise that on Mount Zion there will be both.
  • On the two brothers. “I Will Not Let You Go”, the chapter on Jacob at the Jabbok in The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original, which tells how Jacob went to meet the brother he had wronged.
  • On the conqueror from Edom. Christmas Evans’s sermon The Triumph of Calvary (Isaiah 63:1), on the one who comes from Edom “with clothing dyed red”, read as Christ returning from the cross.
  • On Obadiah and the gospel. Augustine’s The City of God, book XVIII, chapter 31, on the deliverers going up to Mount Zion; and Jerome’s letter to Paulinus (Letter LIII in The Letters of St. Jerome).
