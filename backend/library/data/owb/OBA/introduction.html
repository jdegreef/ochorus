Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament: twenty-one verses, a single chapter, readable in five minutes. It is a vision about one nation, Edom, Israel’s neighbour to the south-east, and about one dreadful day when Jerusalem fell and Edom stood by and cheered. Yet in that small compass it holds some of the Bible’s deepest convictions: that pride goes before a fall, that cruelty to a brother does not go unanswered, and that in the end the kingdom belongs to God.

We know nothing about the prophet except his name. Obadiah means “servant of the LORD”, and a dozen men in the Old Testament bear it. An old Jewish tradition identified him with the Obadiah who hid a hundred prophets from Queen Jezebel, but the book itself says only, “The vision of Obadiah.”

Two brothers

To understand Obadiah you have to go back to Genesis. Edom was the nation descended from Esau, and Israel the nation descended from his twin brother Jacob. Their quarrel began in the womb, where the boys struggled together, and it ran through the stolen birthright and the stolen blessing to the night Jacob spent dreading his brother’s four hundred men. That story ended in a reconciliation: Esau ran to meet Jacob and embraced him, and they wept.

The nations did not do as well as their fathers. When Israel came out of Egypt, Edom refused to let it pass through its land. Through the centuries of the kings the two peoples fought again and again. And when the Babylonians captured Jerusalem in 586 BC, burned the temple and carried its people into exile, the Edomites did not simply stand aside. Psalm 137 remembers what they shouted: “Strip it, strip it bare, right down to its foundations!” Obadiah is God’s answer to that day.

The book calls Edom by both its names, Edom and Esau, and Israel by its father’s name, Jacob. The prophet wants us to hear, all the way through, the word “brother”.

The pride of your heart

Edom’s homeland was a rugged plateau of red sandstone mountains, cut by deep gorges and full of caves, its cities set high on the heights. It seemed impregnable, and the Edomites believed it. The LORD speaks to them:

— Obadiah 1:3–4

Notice where the deception lies. Edom’s enemies have not fooled it; its own pride has. A nation that asks “Who can bring me down?” has forgotten that there is someone above the eagle and beyond the stars.

Then the poem describes how complete the fall will be. Thieves take only what they want; grape pickers leave a few grapes for the gleaners. But Esau will be ransacked, “his hidden treasures hunted down!” His allies will turn on him, his friends will deceive him, and those who eat at his table will set a trap for him. Edom was famous for its wise men, and Teman, in its heartland, for its wisdom; but “There is no understanding in Edom.”

On the day of your brother’s misfortune

Now Obadiah names the crime:

{{OWB:OBA.1.10.s1}} {{OWB:OBA.1.11.s1}}

— Obadiah 1:10–11

“You were like one of them.” Edom did not have to raise a sword to share in the guilt. Standing at a distance and watching was already a choice. And it went further. In three verses the prophet says eight times what Edom should not have done: gloated over its brother’s day, rejoiced over Judah’s ruin, boasted, marched through the gate of God’s people, gloated again, seized their wealth, stood at the crossroads to cut down those who escaped, and handed the survivors over to the enemy. The word “day” rings through the passage like a bell, ten times in four verses: the day of his misfortune, of their ruin, of their distress, of his disaster.

It is a searching list, because the sins it names begin so small. Looking on. Taking pleasure in someone else’s fall. Talking big. Only then come looting and betrayal. Obadiah traces the whole slope, from the bystander’s smile to the hand that delivers a refugee to his killers.

The day of the LORD

Then the vision widens. Edom’s day of reckoning is part of a greater day, “the day of the LORD”, which is “close at hand for all the nations.” Its rule is the one in the verse at the head of this introduction: “As you have done, so it will be done to you.” As there was drinking on God’s holy mountain, so all the nations will drink the cup of his judgment, until they are as if they had never existed.

This is not revenge for its own sake. It is the moral order of a world with a just God at its centre, the same truth Jesus put in different words when he said that the measure we use will be measured back to us. Obadiah’s comfort to a crushed and humiliated people is that their humiliation has been seen, and that it will not have the last word.

The kingdom will belong to the LORD

The last verses turn to the future of the house of Jacob. Where Edom’s survivors will be cut off, “on Mount Zion there will be survivors, and it will be holy”. The exiles will come home and possess the land again, from the Negev in the south to Gilead in the east and as far north as Zarephath. And the book ends with its great conclusion:

— Obadiah 1:21

The last word is not Israel’s victory over Edom but God’s kingship over all. Mount Zion and Mount Esau, the two mountains of the two brothers, end under one rule, and it is the LORD’s.

How to read Obadiah

Read it aloud, all at once, and then read the story of Jacob and Esau in Genesis 25–33 beside it. Most scholars date the book soon after the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC, since its description of that day matches the laments of the exile so closely. Some place it earlier, at one of the other times when Jerusalem was plundered and Edom joined in. Verses 1–9 are very close to a passage about Edom in Jeremiah 49, and the two prophets seem to draw on a shared oracle.

As you read, let the book search you as well as comfort you. Its hard words are aimed at Edom, but its warnings are for anyone: against the pride that thinks itself out of reach, and against the quiet pleasure of watching a brother fall.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Obadiah is poetry, and this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out. Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, it writes “the LORD”, and where it joins that name to the word for “Lord”, as in the first verse, “the Lord GOD”. Edom’s mountain country is called, as in the Hebrew, “Mount Esau”. Sepharad, where some of Jerusalem’s exiles are found, is a place no one has identified for certain; later Jewish tradition gave the name to Spain. For the reasoning behind every sentence, with the Hebrew beside it, see openwordbible.org.
