Genesis does not end with a wedding or a victory. It ends with a coffin. The book that opened with God breathing life into dust closes with Joseph’s body embalmed and laid in a box in a foreign country. It is an honest last sentence, but not a hopeless one, because of what Joseph said just before it.

The question the book leaves with you

Joseph’s family is safe and fed in Egypt, but it is not home, and the land they were promised belongs to others. Abraham owned only a field with a burial cave in it. Isaac and Jacob lived there as foreigners; old Jacob told Pharaoh, “My years have been few and hard.” After three generations, the great nation is seventy people living on someone else’s land. And yet Joseph’s last words are not about Egypt at all:

{{OWB:GEN.50.24.s1}}

— Genesis 50:24

He makes his brothers swear to carry his bones with them. Joseph, who had more of Egypt than anyone, wanted to be buried in a promise. That is the question Genesis leaves with every reader: what will you trust when the promise has not yet arrived? Abraham trusted it when it seemed impossible; Joseph held to it in a pit, a prison and a palace, and at the last staked his body on it.

The letter to the Hebrews says of all of them that they died still holding to faith, not having received what was promised: “they saw it from far off and welcomed it.” Abraham, it says, “was waiting for the city with firm foundations, a city planned and built by God.”

Where the story goes next

The very next book opens with Jacob’s family multiplying in Egypt until a new king comes to the throne, “one who had never heard of Joseph”, and makes them slaves. When they cry out, Exodus says, “God remembered his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.” Generations later, when Moses led the people out, he took Joseph’s bones with him, and they were buried at last at Shechem, in the land.

But the promise to Abraham was larger than a land. It was that “all the families of the earth” would be blessed through him, and the New Testament says plainly where that came true. Paul writes to the Galatians:

“Scripture saw in advance that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, and so it announced the gospel ahead of time to Abraham: ‘In you all the nations will be blessed.’”

— Galatians 3:8

The offspring of the woman who would strike the serpent’s head, the descendant of Abraham through whom the nations would be blessed, the ruler from Judah who would hold the scepter: the New Testament gathers all these threads into Jesus Christ. And the promise now belongs to everyone who trusts him: “if you belong to Christ, it follows that you are Abraham’s offspring, heirs in keeping with the promise.”

Where to go from here

Genesis has been preached for as long as there has been a church, and some of the best of that work is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On creation (Genesis 1). Basil of Caesarea’s The Hexaemeron, nine homilies on the six days; the closing books of Augustine’s Confessions, on time and eternity and the opening words of Genesis; and the second book of Theophilus of Antioch’s To Autolycus, on the prophets and the creation.
  • On the fall and its long shadow (Genesis 3–11). John Milton’s Paradise Lost, the English epic of the fall; and Augustine’s The City of God, whose books XI–XVI trace the two cities from the creation and the fall, through Cain and Abel and the flood, to Abraham and beyond.
  • On Enoch and Noah (Genesis 5–7). George Whitefield’s sermon Walking with God (Genesis 5:24) and D. L. Moody’s Come Thou and All Thy House into the Ark (Genesis 7:1).
  • On Abraham. Alexander Maclaren’s Jehovah-Jireh (Genesis 22:14), and his The Gift That Brings All Gifts, which hears Abraham and Isaac behind Romans 8:32; E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, with its chapter on Abraham, the man of prayer; and William J. Seymour’s Rebecca: Type of the Bride of Christ (Genesis 24).
  • On the faith of the patriarchs. Jonathan Edwards’s The Christian Pilgrim, on Hebrews 11:13–14 and the patriarchs who called themselves strangers on the earth; Gareth Evans’s He Holds My Tomorrows, which walks through the faith of Hebrews 11 from Abel onward; and The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original that begins with Abraham pleading for a city and Jacob wrestling through the night.
  • On the covenant. Andrew Murray’s The Two Covenants, which begins its comparison of the old covenant and the new in Abraham’s household; and Charles H. Spurgeon’s The Blood of the Everlasting Covenant.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Genesis in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
