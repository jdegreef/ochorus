{{OWB:GEN.1.1.s1}}

— Genesis 1:1

The Bible begins where everything begins. In Hebrew this book is called Bereshit, after its first word, which means “In the beginning”. The name we use, Genesis, comes from the ancient Greek translation and means “origin”. Both are fitting. Genesis tells of the beginning of the world, of the human race, of sin and death, of nations, and of the people of Israel. And it tells of the beginning of a promise that the rest of the Bible spends itself keeping.

It opens with God and nothing else. There is no argument for his existence, no account of where he came from, no rival for him to defeat. Before Genesis tells us anything about ourselves, it tells us whose world this is.

And it ends, fifty chapters later, in a very different place: with Joseph dying in Egypt, far from the land his family had been promised, asking his brothers to carry his bones home when God comes to help them. The book that began with “Let there be light” closes with a coffin. Between those two ends lies one of the great stories ever told, and one of the most honest.

Two halves

Genesis falls into two parts of very different pace. The first, chapters 1–11, covers an enormous sweep of time in a few pages: creation, the garden, the first murder, long lists of generations, the flood, the scattering of the nations at Babel. Its subject is the whole human race.

Then, at the end of chapter 11, the camera stops and turns to one family. A man named Terah sets out from Ur of the Chaldeans with his son Abram and his daughter-in-law Sarai, of whom we are told only this: “Now Sarai was barren; she had no children.” From chapter 12 to chapter 50 the book slows almost to walking pace. It follows four generations of this one family: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Jacob’s twelve sons.

The change is the point. The first half shows a world that has gone wrong. The second shows what God begins to do about it. And he begins, strangely, not with an empire or an army but with an elderly couple who cannot have children.

“This is the story of…”

Genesis has its own table of contents, woven into the text. Ten headings, eleven times counting the repeat at 36:9, read in Hebrew “these are the toledot of…”, a word older translations rendered “generations”. It means something like “what came from”: a person’s descendants, and the story of what became of them. This translation renders it naturally in each place, “This is the story of…” or “This is the account of…”, but the pattern is there to be found:

  • the heavens and the earth (2:4)
  • Adam (5:1)
  • Noah (6:9)
  • the sons of Noah (10:1)
  • Shem (11:10)
  • Terah (11:27)
  • Ishmael (25:12)
  • Isaac (25:19)
  • Esau (36:1, and again at 36:9)
  • Jacob (37:2)

Two things are worth noticing. First, each heading names a father but tells the story of his children. The account of Terah is the story of Abraham; the account of Isaac is the story of Jacob and Esau; the account of Jacob is the story of Joseph and his brothers. Genesis is always looking at what comes next.

Second, the headings narrow. Again and again the book briefly lists a branch of the family that will not carry the promise (Ishmael’s, Esau’s) and then sets it aside to follow the one that will. The lists of names that tempt readers to skip are doing real work: they show a God steering, through all the branching of the human family, toward a single promise.

Creation and the image of God

The first chapter is built like a piece of music. Day by day God speaks, and it is so; he names what he has made; he sees that it is good; and a refrain closes each movement: “Evening came, and then morning.” The first three days make spaces, and the next three fill them. On the seventh day God rests, and blesses the day, and sets it apart as holy.

At the climax of the sixth day the rhythm changes. Instead of “Let there be”, God says “Let us make”, and makes human beings:

{{OWB:GEN.1.27.s1}}

— Genesis 1:27

In the ancient world, kings set up images of themselves in the lands they ruled, to show whose those lands were. Genesis says that God has already placed his image in the world, and it is every human being, man and woman alike. That is why, after the flood, the taking of a human life is forbidden “for God made human beings in his own image”.

Chapter 2 tells the story again from closer up. The LORD God shapes the man out of “dust from the ground” and breathes into him “the breath of life”; he puts him in a garden “to work it and take care of it”; he says, for the first time, that something is “not good”, the man being on his own, and makes the woman. Human beings are made of dust and made in God’s image, and Genesis never lets us forget either half. At the end of the sixth day, God looks at everything he has made, “and it was very good indeed.”

The fall and the spread of sin

Chapter 3 begins with a question: “Did God really tell you not to eat from any tree in the garden?” The serpent twists what God said, then contradicts it outright: “You certainly will not die.” The temptation is to be “like God, knowing good and evil”, to decide for oneself what good is. The woman eats, and gives some to her husband, “who was with her”, and he eats.

Their eyes are opened, and what they see is their own nakedness. They hide. And the first words God speaks to fallen humanity are not a sentence of judgement but a question:

{{OWB:GEN.3.9.s1}}

— Genesis 3:9

The man blames the woman, and God for giving her to him; the woman blames the serpent. Judgement comes: pain, sweat, and the return to dust. But inside the judgement there is a promise. God tells the serpent that the woman’s offspring will strike its head, though it will strike his heel (3:15). Christians since Irenaeus in the second century have read this as the first gospel: a descendant of Eve who would undo what the serpent had done, at cost to himself. And before God sends the man and woman out of the garden, he makes them “garments of skin” and clothes them. Even in exile, they are not left uncovered.

Then sin spreads, with grim speed. Cain is warned that “sin is crouching at your door”, and he kills his brother anyway. Asked “Where is your brother Abel?”, he answers, “Am I my brother’s keeper?” Chapter 5 lists the generations from Adam to Noah, and its refrain tolls like a bell: “and then he died”. Only once is it broken: “Enoch walked with God, and then he was no more, because God took him away.” By chapter 6 the LORD sees that every inclination of the human heart is “only evil all the time”, and his heart is deeply troubled.

Noah and the covenant

Into that darkness comes a single sentence: “But Noah found favor with the LORD.” The flood story is told with a careful symmetry, the waters rising and falling, and its turning point is a sentence that echoes through the Bible: “But God remembered Noah.” The waters go down, and Noah’s first act on dry ground is to build an altar.

God resolves that as long as the earth lasts, seedtime and harvest, summer and winter, day and night will not cease (8:22). Then he makes a covenant, not only with Noah but with every living creature: never again will a flood destroy the earth. And God gives a sign:

{{OWB:GEN.9.13.s1}}

— Genesis 9:13

The word is the one used for a warrior’s bow, and many readers have seen in it a weapon of war hung up in the sky. But Genesis is too honest to pretend that the flood has cured the human heart. A few verses later Noah, the righteous man, lies drunk and uncovered in his tent. The waters have washed the world. They have not washed us.

Babel

The first half of the book ends with the human race united, speaking one language, and deciding to build:

{{OWB:GEN.11.4.s1}}

— Genesis 11:4

It is a project of fear as much as pride. God comes down to see their tower, which is the Bible’s gentle joke about how high it really reached, confuses their language, and scatters them anyway. The city is called Babel, which sounds in Hebrew like the word for confusion.

Read the next chapter beside this one. The builders wanted to make a name for themselves; God promises Abram that he will “make your name great”. The builders feared being scattered; God promises that through Abram “all the families of the earth will be blessed.” What human beings tried to seize, God means to give.

Abraham: called, and counted righteous

The second half of Genesis begins with a call and a promise:

{{OWB:GEN.12.1.s1}} {{OWB:GEN.12.2.s1}} {{OWB:GEN.12.3.s1}}

— Genesis 12:1–3

Everything else in the Bible can be read as the working out of those three verses. And the next verse is just as important: “Abram set out as the LORD had told him.” He was seventy-five, with a wife who could not have children and a promise of a great nation.

Genesis does not make Abraham a plaster saint. In a famine he goes down to Egypt and, afraid for his life, passes Sarai off as his sister. Later, when the promised child has not come, he and Sarai try to bring the promise about themselves through her servant Hagar, and the household is torn by it. But in chapter 15, when Abram complains that he is still childless, God takes him outside and tells him to count the stars, if he can. “Your descendants will be like that.” Then comes one of the most important sentences in Scripture:

{{OWB:GEN.15.6.s1}}

— Genesis 15:6

Abram has done nothing yet but believe. He has not been circumcised; the law of Moses is centuries away. The apostle Paul builds his letters to the Romans and the Galatians on this verse: God counts as righteous the one who trusts him, and Abraham is the father of everyone who believes.

God renames him Abraham, “a father of many nations”. Sarah laughs to hear she will bear a son in her old age, and is answered with a question: “Is anything too wonderful for the LORD?” Isaac is born, and his name means “he laughs”. Then, in chapter 22, comes the hardest test in the book: God tells Abraham to take “your son, your only son, the one you love, Isaac” and offer him on a mountain in Moriah. On the way up, the boy asks where the lamb is:

{{OWB:GEN.22.8.s1}}

— Genesis 22:8

God stops Abraham’s hand and provides a ram, and Abraham names the place “The LORD Will See to It.” Christians have never been able to read this chapter without thinking of another Father, another only Son, and another hill, where God himself saw to the lamb.

Isaac, Jacob and the God of the unlikely

Isaac is the quietest of the patriarchs, and the promise passes through him to his twin sons, Esau and Jacob. Before they are born, their mother is told that “the elder will be servant to the younger”. It is a pattern Genesis repeats too often to be an accident: Abel over Cain, Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Joseph over his older brothers, Ephraim over Manasseh. God’s promises do not run along the lines of human advantage.

Jacob’s name sounds like “he grasps the heel”, and he lives up to it. He bargains away his brother’s birthright and deceives his blind father to steal his brother’s blessing, then flees for his life. And it is on the run, sleeping on the ground, that God meets him in a dream of a stairway reaching to the sky, with angels going up and down on it, and says, “I am with you.” Jacob wakes and says what many have said since in the low places of their lives: “The LORD is truly here in this place, and I had no idea!”

Twenty years later, returning home to face the brother he wronged, Jacob spends a night alone by the river Jabbok, and a man wrestles with him until dawn. Jacob will not let go: “I will not let you go unless you bless me.” He comes away limping, and with a new name:

{{OWB:GEN.32.29.s1}}

— Genesis 32:28

He calls the place Peniel, “the face of God”, because “I have seen God face to face”. The next morning Esau, whom he had dreaded, runs to meet him, and they weep together. The grasper has learned that what he wanted most had to be received as a gift.

Joseph: “Good was what God planned”

The last and longest story in Genesis belongs to Joseph, Jacob’s favourite son, the boy with the long-sleeved robe and the dreams. His brothers sell him to traders bound for Egypt. There he is a slave, then falsely accused and imprisoned, then forgotten. Yet the narrator keeps saying one quiet thing: “The LORD was with Joseph.” In the end he is set over all Egypt, and saves the land, and his own starving family, from famine.

The story is also about the brothers. When they come to buy grain, Joseph tests whether the men who sold one brother will abandon another. And Judah, who once proposed selling Joseph, now offers himself as a slave in place of the youngest, Benjamin. Then Joseph can hold back no longer: “I am Joseph!” He tells his terrified brothers not to be angry with themselves, because “It was God who sent me on ahead of you, to keep people alive.”

After their father dies, the brothers fear that Joseph will take his revenge at last. He weeps, and answers them with the sentence that sums up the whole book:

{{OWB:GEN.50.20.s1}}

— Genesis 50:20

Joseph does not pretend the evil was not evil. He says that God was at work in it and through it, toward a good the brothers never intended, and that this leaves no room for revenge: “Do I stand in the place of God?” It is one of the deepest things the Bible says about suffering.

How to read Genesis

Genesis is a long book, but it is made almost entirely of stories, and it reads quickly. It is worth reading once straight through to feel its shape: the world (1–11), Abraham (12–25), Jacob (25–36), and Joseph (37–50).

The book does not name its author. Tradition, Jewish and Christian, has received it as the first of the five books of Moses; many scholars think it drew on older sources and took its final form over a long period. Christians hold a range of views on how it came together, but agree that it belongs at the head of the Scriptures.

Readers also ask how the first chapters relate to what the sciences say about the age of the world, and faithful Christians answer differently: some read the six days as ordinary days, others as long ages, others as a frame that arranges God’s work into a week to show its order and goal. All of them agree on what the chapter is most concerned to say: that there is one God, that the sun and moon and sea are not gods but his creatures, that the world he made is good, and that human beings bear his image. Whatever else it is, Genesis 1 is a declaration of who is Lord.

As you read, watch for the patterns: barren women who become mothers, younger sons chosen over older, promises delayed for years, altars built at every turning point, names given and changed. Notice how honestly the book tells its heroes’ failures. And listen to the questions God asks, because they are asked of the reader too. “Where are you?” “Where is your brother Abel?” “Is anything too wonderful for the LORD?”

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Two of its choices in Genesis are worth knowing about before you begin.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”. Where it uses the ordinary word for God, it writes “God”. Genesis 2 joins the two for the first time: “the LORD God”.

Human beings. In chapter 1 the Hebrew word adam means humankind, “male and female”, and is translated “human beings”. Only gradually does it become the name of the first man. The word is close to adamah, “ground”, from whose dust the man is shaped.
