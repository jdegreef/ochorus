Second Peter is a farewell. Its writer knows that he must soon “lay this tent aside”, as the Lord Jesus had shown him, and he writes so that after he is gone his readers will remember what they have been taught. It is a short letter with three concerns: grow in the knowledge of Christ; do not be led astray by false teachers; and do not give up waiting for the Lord’s return because it seems slow in coming.

Who wrote it?

The letter names its author as “Simeon Peter, a servant and apostle of Jesus Christ”. It speaks as Peter: he heard the voice from heaven on the holy mountain at the transfiguration, he expects to die soon, and this is his “second letter” to the same readers.

Even so, no book of the New Testament has had its authorship questioned so long. It is hardly quoted in the second century. Origen in the third century knew that some doubted it, and Eusebius in the fourth listed it among the books spoken against, though known to many. Jerome noted that many thought it was not Peter’s because its style differs from that of 1 Peter, and suggested Peter had used a different secretary. Many modern scholars think it was written after Peter’s death, around AD 80–130, by a follower who set down his teaching in the form of a last testament, a kind of writing well known at the time. Others hold that Peter wrote it, perhaps with help, shortly before his martyrdom in the mid-60s. The church, after long weighing, received it as Scripture, and it has been read as such ever since.

Readers also notice that much of chapter 2 is very close to the Letter of Jude, so close that one has surely drawn on the other, or both on a common source.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapter 1: growing in the knowledge of Christ. God’s gifts and promises, the qualities faith should add, and the sure word of the apostles and prophets.
  • Chapter 2: false teachers. Their greed and arrogance, God’s judgment in the past, and the misery of turning back.
  • Chapter 3: the day of the Lord. The scoffers’ question, God’s patience, new heavens and a new earth, and a closing call to grow.

Faith, goodness, love

The letter opens with breathtaking promises. Through knowing Christ, God has given his people everything they need for life and godliness, so that they may “come to share in the divine nature”. The Eastern Orthodox churches have treasured this verse as a summary of salvation: God became human so that human beings might share in his life. Western Christians have spoken more often of union with Christ, and all agree that Peter does not mean people become gods in their own right, but that they share God’s life, free at last from the decay that desire has brought into the world.

Gifts like that call for effort, and Peter sets out a ladder of qualities, each built on the one before:

The ladder begins in faith and ends in love. It is not a way of earning God’s favour; it is how his gifts become fruitful. “So, brothers and sisters, work even harder to make sure that God’s call and choice of you stand firm”.

Not clever stories

Peter’s teaching rests on what he saw. The message of Christ’s power and coming is not made up: he was on the mountain, he saw Christ’s greatness and heard the Father’s voice. And alongside the apostles’ witness stands the word of the prophets, “like a lamp that shines in a murky place, until the day breaks and the morning star rises in your hearts.” No prophecy came because a human being willed it; “rather, people spoke from God as the Holy Spirit bore them along.” Christians have often turned to these verses in thinking about the inspiration of Scripture.

False teachers

Chapter 2 is the letter’s sternest. False teachers will arise in the church as false prophets arose in Israel, greedy, arrogant, promising freedom while “they themselves are slaves of corruption.” Peter reminds his readers of God’s judgments in the past, on rebellious angels, on the ancient world in the flood and on Sodom and Gomorrah, but also of his rescues: Noah kept safe, and Lot, the righteous man, delivered. The lesson cuts both ways: “the Lord knows how to rescue those who are godly from their trials”. The chapter closes with the misery of those who have known the way of righteousness and turned from it.

Where is this promise?

Then the scoffers’ question. “Where is this promise of his coming?” Everything goes on as it always has. Peter answers in three ways. The world has not always gone on unchanged: God’s word made it and God’s word brought the flood. God does not measure time as we do. And the delay is not slowness but mercy:

The day will come, unexpectedly, like a thief. Christians understand its fire differently, as the destruction of the present world, as its purifying and renewal, or as the laying bare of all things before God. But Peter’s concern is not to satisfy curiosity. It is to ask what kind of people his readers ought to be, and to lift their eyes to “new heavens and a new earth, where righteousness is at home.”

The letter ends with a remarkable mention of Paul, “our dear brother”, who wrote with the wisdom God gave him. “Some things in them are hard to grasp,” Peter says of his letters, and untaught people bend them out of shape, “as they bend the other Scriptures”. It is one of the earliest signs that Paul’s letters were being read alongside the Scriptures of Israel.

How to read 2 Peter

Read it as a last testament, the words of a man setting his affairs in order and leaving his friends what matters most. Read it alongside Jude, to see how the two letters use the same warnings. And notice how it begins and ends in the same place, with growing in grace and in the knowledge of Jesus Christ.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 2 Peter are worth knowing about.

“Our God and Savior Jesus Christ.” In 1:1 the translation reads both titles as belonging to Jesus, so that the letter calls him God from its first sentence.

Tartarus. In 2:4 Peter uses a Greek name for the deepest place of punishment, where the rebellious angels are held. The translation keeps the word “Tartarus” rather than flattening it to “hell”.

“Found out.” In 3:10 many later manuscripts say the earth and its works “will be burned up”. The oldest read that they “will be found”, and the translation follows them: the earth and the deeds done on it “will be found out”, laid bare before God.
