— Zechariah 14:20–21

Zechariah ends in the kitchen. After the visions and the oracles, the earthquake on the Mount of Olives and the living water flowing east and west, the last picture in the book is of horses’ bells and cooking pots. The words that once were engraved only on the high priest’s turban are now on the harness of the war horses, and the ordinary pots of every household in Jerusalem and Judah are as holy as the bowls before the altar. The line between holy and common has not been erased. It has been pushed out until it covers everything.

It is a fitting end for a book first spoken to a few builders on a ruined hill, worried that their work was too small to matter. Zechariah told them that God remembers, that the temple they were building was the start of something that would fill the world, and that one day there would be nothing in it that was not his.

The question the book leaves with you

The book’s first question was put to the people of 520 BC: will you return? “Return to me, declares the LORD of Armies, and I will return to you.” Its last chapters ask a second question, sharper and more personal: what will you do with the king when he comes? He comes humble, on a donkey. He comes as a shepherd and is valued at thirty pieces of silver. He is struck, and his sheep scatter. He is pierced, and those who pierced him look on him and mourn.

Zechariah does not let the reader stand outside that story. The mourning of 12:10 is the mourning of the very people who pierced him, “each clan on its own”, and the fountain of 13:1 is opened for them, “to wash away their sin and impurity”.

Where it points

Few books of the Old Testament are woven so closely into the story of Jesus’s last days. He rides into Jerusalem on a donkey as Zechariah’s king; he speaks of the shepherd struck and the sheep scattered on the way to the Mount of Olives; he is betrayed for thirty pieces of silver; he is pierced on the cross. And the last chapters of the Bible pick up Zechariah’s own last chapter: in Revelation there is no more night, a river of the water of life flows from the throne, and the nations walk by the city’s light. The LORD is king over all the earth.

Where to go from here

A few places in this library to take Zechariah further:

  • On the night visions (1:7–6:8). A. B. Simpson’s sermon Words for Discouraged Workers, which walks through the visions as encouragement for the few builders of the temple; and two chapters of his The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, “The Holy Spirit in the Days of the Restoration” and “Olive Trees and Golden Lamps”, on Zechariah 4:6.
  • On “Not by might” (4:6). Portraits of Courage – Mary Slessor, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “Not by Might” takes the verse as its text.
  • On the day of small beginnings (4:10). George Whitefield’s sermon on Zechariah 4:10, preached before the governor and assembly of Georgia in 1770, in Selected Sermons of George Whitefield.
  • On the Accuser and the pupil of his eye (2:8; 3:1–5). Portraits of Courage – Praying Hyde, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “The Accuser of the Brethren” tells how Zechariah 2:8 stopped John Hyde in the middle of a prayer.
  • Morning and evening readings. Charles H. Spurgeon’s Morning by Morning has readings on the myrtle trees (September 26, 1:8), Joshua the high priest (November 27, 3:1), the one who will build the temple (June 22, 6:13), light at evening time (October 4, 14:7) and the living waters (July 1, 14:8); his Evening by Evening on the comforting answer (February 24, 1:12–13), the four craftsmen (December 5, 1:20), the plummet in Zerubbabel’s hand (November 26, 4:10) and the fallen cedar (September 26, 11:2).
  • On the coming king (9:9). A. B. Simpson’s sermon Personal Responsibility, on Christ’s entry into Jerusalem.
  • On the shepherd struck (13:7). John Henry Newman’s The Shepherd of Our Souls (John 10:11), and A. W. Tozer’s Prepare by Prayer (Matthew 26:31–46).
  • On the fountain and the living water (13:1; 14:8). Horatius Bonar’s Christ the Cleanser (John 13:10), Hudson Taylor’s Unfailing Springs (John 4:14) and William J. Seymour’s River of Living Water (John 4:10).
  • On Zechariah and Christ in the early church. Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, chapters 53 (on the king riding a donkey) and 115 (on Zechariah 2:10–3:2); and Augustine’s The City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 35, on Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi.

And read Haggai in this same edition, spoken to the same builders in the same months, and the last week of Jesus’s life in Matthew and John.
