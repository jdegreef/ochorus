{{OWB:ZEC.1.3.s1}}

— Zechariah 1:3

Zechariah is the longest of the twelve Minor Prophets and the strangest. It has horsemen among myrtle trees, a flying scroll thirty feet long, a woman shut in a basket and carried off by women with wings like a stork’s, and a golden lampstand fed by two olive trees. Jerome, who translated it into Latin, counted it among the most obscure books of the Bible. And yet the gospel writers turned to it more than to almost any other prophet when they told the story of Jesus’s last week: the king riding on a donkey, the thirty pieces of silver, the shepherd struck and the sheep scattered, the one they pierced.

The prophet’s name means “the LORD remembers”, and the whole book is a sermon on it. To a small, discouraged people who had come home from exile to a ruined city, it says that God has not forgotten them, or his promises, or the nations.

The prophet and his time

Zechariah was the son of Berechiah and grandson of Iddo, the head of a priestly family that had come back from Babylon (Nehemiah 12:4, 16), so he was probably a priest as well as a prophet. The book of Ezra calls him simply “Zechariah son of Iddo”, and names him with the older prophet Haggai as the two men whose preaching got the temple rebuilt (Ezra 5:1; 6:14).

His first message is dated “In the eighth month of the second year of Darius’s reign”, the autumn of 520 BC, two months after Haggai first spoke. Cyrus of Persia had let the exiles go home eighteen years before, but the city was still largely in ruins and the temple unfinished. The world, as the first vision puts it, was “still and at rest”: Persia was secure, and nothing seemed to be happening to bring about the glorious future the earlier prophets had promised. Zechariah was sent to tell the people that God was at work, and that it was worth building.

Two halves

The book falls into two parts that feel very different. Chapters 1–8 are dated, mostly prose, and tied closely to the years 520–518 BC. They are full of names, Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest, and of the work at hand: rebuilding the temple and the community. Chapters 9–14 are undated, open with poetry and move into visions of the far future. They speak of a coming king, a rejected shepherd, a day when the LORD will be king over all the earth.

Many scholars think that the second half comes from a later time and another hand, and some divide it further; others argue that its differences come from its subject, not its author, and that Zechariah himself, older now, wrote it. Christians have held both views. Either way, the two halves share a great deal: the gathering of the scattered, the cleansing of the land, the nations coming to Jerusalem, and the LORD himself living among his people. And the church has always received them as one book.

  • 1:1–6: a call to return.
  • 1:7–6:15: eight visions in one night, and the crowning of the high priest.
  • Chapters 7–8: fasting turned to feasting. A question about fasts, and ten promises for Jerusalem.
  • Chapters 9–11: the first oracle. The coming king, the gathering of the people, and the rejected shepherd.
  • Chapters 12–14: the second oracle. The pierced one, a fountain for cleansing, and the day when the LORD is king.

“Return to me”

Zechariah’s first word sets the tone for everything else. God was angry with their ancestors, who would not listen to the earlier prophets. “Your ancestors, where are they now? And the prophets, do they live forever?” The prophets died; the people who ignored them died; but God’s words caught up with them. The lesson is not despair but invitation: “Return to me, declares the LORD of Armies, and I will return to you.”

Eight visions in one night

Three months later, on the twenty-fourth day of Shebat, Zechariah saw eight visions in one night. In each he asks what he is seeing, and an angel “who talked with me” explains. Read together, they move from heaven’s view of the world to the cleansing of the land and back again.

  • The horsemen among the myrtles (1:7–17). Patrols report that the whole earth is at rest. The angel of the LORD asks, “LORD of Armies, how long will you hold back your compassion from Jerusalem and the cities of Judah”, and the answer comes: “I have returned to Jerusalem with compassion.”
  • Four horns and four craftsmen (1:18–21). The powers that scattered Judah will themselves be thrown down.
  • The man with the measuring line (2:1–13). Jerusalem will be too full for walls, and God himself will be “a wall of fire around her”. Whoever touches his people “touches the pupil of his eye”, and “Many nations will join themselves to the LORD on that day”.
  • Joshua and the Accuser (3:1–10). The high priest stands in filthy clothes, accused, and is cleansed.
  • The lampstand and the olive trees (4:1–14). The temple will be finished, not by might but by God’s Spirit.
  • The flying scroll (5:1–4). God’s curse goes out against theft and false oaths.
  • The woman in the basket (5:5–11). Wickedness is shut in a basket and carried away to Babylon.
  • Four chariots (6:1–8). The winds of heaven go out to the ends of the earth, and God’s spirit is at rest.

Two visions stand at the centre, and each is about one of the community’s two leaders. In the fourth, Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the LORD, and the Accuser stands at his right side to accuse him. The Accuser is given no words. The LORD rebukes him: “Is this man not a burning stick snatched from the fire?” Then:

{{OWB:ZEC.3.4.s1}}

— Zechariah 3:4

Joshua is cleansed not because he is clean but because God has chosen Jerusalem. And the vision looks beyond him to “my servant, the Branch”, and to a day when God “will remove the guilt of that land in a single day”.

In the fifth vision, Zechariah sees a golden lampstand with two olive trees beside it that feed it with oil. The word is for Zerubbabel, who had a mountain of work in front of him and very little to do it with:

{{OWB:ZEC.4.6.s1}} {{OWB:ZEC.4.7.s1}}

— Zechariah 4:6–7

The hands that laid the foundation would finish it. And to those who looked at the modest work and sneered, God asks a question that has comforted builders ever since: “Who has despised the day of small beginnings?”

After the visions comes an action. Zechariah is told to make a crown from silver and gold brought by exiles from Babylon and set it on Joshua’s head, with a word about the future: “Here is the man whose name is the Branch.” He will build the temple of the LORD, and rule, and “There will be a priest on his throne”. Whether the verse speaks of one figure or of two in harmony, kingship and priesthood, offices kept apart in Israel, stand together. Christians have heard in it the high priest who, as the letter to the Hebrews says, “has taken his seat at the right hand of the throne” (Hebrews 8:1).

Fasting and feasting

Two years later, in 518 BC, a delegation from Bethel came with a practical question. For seventy years they had fasted in the fifth month to mourn the burning of the temple. Now that it was being rebuilt, should they go on? God answers with a question of his own: “did you truly fast for me?” Then he repeats what the earlier prophets had said:

{{OWB:ZEC.7.9.s1}} {{OWB:ZEC.7.10.s1}}

— Zechariah 7:9–10

Their ancestors had refused, and made their hearts “as hard as diamond”, and the land had become a wasteland. But chapter 8 turns to promise. Ten times it says “The LORD of Armies says this” or “The LORD says this”, and the promises are among the gentlest in the prophets. God will live in Jerusalem again. Old men and old women will sit in its squares, each with a cane, “And the city squares will be full of boys and girls playing there.” Then the question about fasting is answered: the fasts “will become times of joy and gladness and happy festivals”. And the chapter ends with the nations:

{{OWB:ZEC.8.23.s1}}

— Zechariah 8:23

The coming king

The second half of the book opens with the LORD marching down from the north, through Syria, Tyre and the Philistine cities, to camp around his house. Then a king arrives, and he is not what anyone expected:

{{OWB:ZEC.9.9.s1}}

— Zechariah 9:9

Conquerors rode horses and chariots. This king comes humble, on a donkey, and takes the chariots and war horses away. “He will proclaim peace to the nations; his rule will stretch from sea to sea”. On the Sunday before he died, Jesus rode into Jerusalem on a donkey, and Matthew and John both quote this verse (Matthew 21:5; John 12:15). He was not making a vague gesture. He was claiming to be this king.

The poetry goes on to promise freedom for “you prisoners who have hope”, rain from the LORD instead of lying diviners, and a scattered people whistled home like a flock.

The rejected shepherd

Chapter 11 is one of the hardest passages in the prophets. Zechariah is told to act out the part of a shepherd for “the flock that is marked for slaughter”, whose own shepherds show them no pity. He takes two staffs, named Favor and Union. But the flock loathes him, and when he asks for his wages they weigh out thirty pieces of silver, the price of a slave in the law of Moses (Exodus 21:32). The LORD’s comment is bitter: “Throw it to the potter, that fine price they set on me!” So the prophet throws the silver into the house of the LORD, to the potter, and breaks his staffs.

Matthew saw this fulfilled when Judas threw back the thirty silver coins he had been paid to betray Jesus, and the priests used them to buy a potter’s field (Matthew 27:3–10). Matthew attributes the words to Jeremiah, perhaps because he joins Zechariah’s words to Jeremiah’s accounts of a potter and of a field bought for silver (Jeremiah 18–19; 32).

The one they pierced

The second oracle begins with the nations gathered against Jerusalem and the LORD defending it. Then, at its centre, comes a verse of extraordinary strangeness:

{{OWB:ZEC.12.10.s1}}

— Zechariah 12:10

The LORD speaks, and says “They will look on me, the one they pierced”, and then “mourn for him”. The pierced one is somehow both the LORD and another. Jewish interpreters have read the verse in several ways. John, telling how a soldier pierced Jesus’s side, writes: “And another passage of Scripture also says, ‘They will look at the one they pierced.’” (John 19:37). Revelation takes it further: when Christ comes with the clouds, “those who pierced him” will see him (Revelation 1:7).

The mourning is followed at once by cleansing: “On that day a fountain will be opened for the house of David and the people of Jerusalem, to wash away their sin and impurity.” And then the shepherd appears once more, now as one whom the LORD calls “the man who stands next to me”:

{{OWB:ZEC.13.7.s1}}

— Zechariah 13:7

Jesus quoted these words on the night of his arrest, on the way to the Mount of Olives, when he told his disciples that they would all fall away (Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27). Yet the chapter ends with a refined people, brought through fire, who call on God and are answered: “I will say, ‘This is my people,’ and they will say, ‘The LORD is my God.’”

The LORD will be king

The last chapter is the most dramatic in the book. The nations attack Jerusalem; the LORD goes out to fight; his feet stand on the Mount of Olives, which splits in two. There is a day “without day or night; and when evening comes, there will be light”; living water flows out from Jerusalem, east and west, “in summer and in winter alike”. And at the centre:

{{OWB:ZEC.14.9.s1}}

— Zechariah 14:9

Even the nations that attacked the city go up each year to worship the King and keep the Festival of Booths. The bells on the horses are engraved “Holy to the LORD”, the words once engraved on the gold plate of the high priest’s turban (Exodus 28:36); every cooking pot in Jerusalem is as holy as the temple bowls; and there is no longer a trader in the house of the LORD.

Christians read this chapter in different ways. Some expect a literal future fulfilment in the land of Israel, connected with Christ’s return to the Mount of Olives (compare Acts 1:11–12). Others read its pictures as the prophet’s way of describing what the gospel brings and the new creation completes: living water flowing to the nations, worship of the one God by every people, and the whole of life made holy. They agree on its heart: the LORD will be king over all the earth.

How to read Zechariah

Do not try to read Zechariah in one sitting. Take the first eight chapters with Haggai and Ezra 1–6 beside them, and remember the people who first heard them: a few builders on a ruined hill, wondering whether anything they did mattered. Then read chapters 9–14 slowly, with the accounts of Jesus’s last week open alongside: Matthew 21 and 26–27, and John 12 and 19. Much that seems obscure becomes clear when you see who the gospel writers thought it was about.

In the visions, do not worry if every detail is not explained; Zechariah himself kept asking “What are these?” Look for what the angel does explain, and for the direction of travel: from guilt to cleansing, from scattering to gathering, from small beginnings to a world where everything is “Holy to the LORD”.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. The poetry of chapters 9–11, and of a few passages elsewhere, is printed one line per line. A few of the translation’s choices are worth knowing.

“The Accuser.” In chapter 3 the Hebrew has “the satan”, with the article, a title meaning the one who brings a charge in court, as in Job 1. The translation renders it “the Accuser”, so that its link with the verb that follows, “to accuse him”, can be heard. Later Scripture uses the word as a name, and Revelation 12:10 speaks of “the one who accused our brothers and sisters”.

“The LORD of Armies.” Zechariah uses this title for God more than fifty times. It renders what older Bibles call “the LORD of hosts”: the LORD who commands the armies of heaven.

“Loyal love.” In 7:9 the Hebrew hesed, the faithful love that keeps covenant, is “loyal love”.

“A trader.” The last word of the book, in 14:21, is literally “Canaanite”. Since the Canaanites of the coast were famous merchants, the word came to mean a trader, and the translation reads it that way. When Jesus drove the traders out of the temple courts, he acted in the spirit of this verse.

Numbering. In Hebrew Bibles the vision of the four horns (1:18–21 in English) begins chapter 2, so the verses of chapter 2 are numbered four higher. References in this edition follow the numbering of English Bibles.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
