— Ezekiel 36:26

Ezekiel is the strangest of the prophets, and one of the most hopeful. It opens with a storm out of the north, wheels within wheels, and the glory of God on a throne above the living creatures. It goes on to a prophet lying on his side for over a year, shaving his head with a sword, and eating a scroll that tastes like honey. It contains some of the hardest pages in the Bible. And it ends with dry bones rising to their feet, a river flowing out of the temple to make the Dead Sea fresh, and a city whose name is “The LORD Is There.”

Its prophet was a priest who, as far as we know, never served in the temple. Ezekiel son of Buzi was carried off to Babylon in 597 BC with King Jehoiachin and the leading families of Jerusalem, and lived among the exiles at Tel-abib, by the Kebar Canal. Five years later, in 593, the heavens opened. For more than twenty years he spoke to people who had lost their home and were about to lose their temple too, and his book asks what Jeremiah’s and Lamentations’ ask in their own ways: if God’s house is gone, is God gone too?

The shape of the book

Ezekiel is the most orderly of the prophetic books. It is told almost entirely in the first person, and more than a dozen of its sections are dated by the year of Jehoiachin’s exile, mostly in sequence, from the call in the fifth year (593 BC) to a last oracle in the twenty-seventh (571). The fall of Jerusalem divides it neatly in two:

  • Chapters 1–24: the call, and judgement on Jerusalem, spoken before the city fell.
  • Chapters 25–32: oracles against the nations around Judah, from Ammon to Tyre and Egypt.
  • Chapters 33–48: after the news of the fall, promises of restoration, ending in the great vision of the new temple and the land (40–48).

The hinge is a single sentence in 33:21. In the twelfth year of the exile a survivor arrives from Jerusalem and says, “The city has fallen!” Up to that point Ezekiel has been tearing down false hope. From that point he builds true hope.

Through the whole book runs one refrain, heard more than sixty times: “Then you will know that I am the LORD.” Everything God does in Ezekiel, in judgement and in mercy, is so that he will be known for who he is.

The vision by the canal (Ezekiel 1–3)

The book opens with an overwhelming vision. Out of a storm come four living creatures, each with four faces, a human face, a lion’s, an ox’s and an eagle’s; beside them are wheels whose rims are “full of eyes all around”, going wherever the Spirit goes; above them is a vault “glittering like ice”, and above that a throne, and on the throne “a figure that looked like a human being”, surrounded by fire and brightness. Ezekiel piles up “something like” and “what looked like” as language strains at its limits, and then says what he has seen:

{{OWB:EZK.1.28.s1}}

— Ezekiel 1:28

The point of all the strangeness is where it happens. The glory of the LORD, which had filled the temple in Jerusalem, has appeared by a canal in Babylon, among exiles. The throne has wheels. God is not confined to his house, and he has not abandoned his people.

Then God speaks, and his first words set the tone of the whole book: “Son of man, stand up on your feet.” The title, used over ninety times, means “human being”, “mortal”, and it reminds the prophet of the distance between himself and the glory he has seen. He is sent to “a rebellious house”, told that they may not listen, and given a scroll written on both sides with “laments and mourning and woe”. He is told to eat it, and “in my mouth it tasted as sweet as honey.” Then the Spirit carries him off, and he sits among the exiles for seven days, stunned.

He is appointed a watchman (3:17). If he warns the wicked and they do not turn, they die for their sin; if he fails to warn them, God will hold him responsible. The image returns at the turning point of the book (33:1–9), and it has shaped how preachers have thought about their calling ever since.

A prophet as a sign (Ezekiel 4–24)

Ezekiel does not only speak his message; he acts it out. He builds a model of Jerusalem under siege and lies beside it on his left side, and then on his right, bearing the years of his people’s guilt. He eats rationed bread cooked over dung. He shaves his head and beard with a sword, burns a third of the hair, strikes a third with the sword and scatters a third to the wind, keeping only a few strands tucked into his robe. He digs through a wall at night with his baggage on his shoulder, like a refugee. For long periods he is struck silent, able to speak only when God gives him a word (3:26–27).

The hardest sign of all is in chapter 24. God tells him that “the delight of your eyes” will be taken from him at a stroke, and that he must not mourn: “in the morning I spoke to the people, and that evening my wife died.” His silent grief becomes a sign for the exiles of the grief that will be too great for mourning when the temple, the delight of their eyes, is destroyed. No prophet’s life was more completely given over to the message.

The glory leaves the temple (Ezekiel 8–11)

In chapter 8 the Spirit lifts Ezekiel by a lock of his hair and carries him in visions to Jerusalem. He is shown what is happening in the temple: an idol at the north gate, elders burning incense to images in a dark room, women weeping for the god Tammuz, men bowing to the sun with their backs to the sanctuary. God asks him whether he sees the things that “will drive me far from my sanctuary”.

And then the glory goes. Step by step it rises from the inner sanctuary to the threshold, from the threshold to the cherubim, out to the east gate, and finally “went up from the middle of the city and stopped over the mountain east of the city.” The holiest thing in Israel’s life has left of its own accord. The Babylonians will burn an empty house.

Yet in the same vision comes a word for the exiles whom the people in Jerusalem despised: “for a little while I have still been a sanctuary to them in the lands they have gone to.” God himself will be their temple in Babylon. And he promises what he will do for them there: “I will take the heart of stone out of their bodies and give them a heart of flesh.”

Unfaithful Jerusalem, and a just God (Ezekiel 16–23)

Some of Ezekiel’s parables are the most shocking pages in Scripture. Chapter 16 tells of a baby girl abandoned at birth, “thrashing about in your blood”, whom the LORD passes by and tells to live, raises, marries and adorns, and who turns to prostitution with every passer-by. Chapter 23 tells of two sisters, Samaria and Jerusalem, in even more graphic language. Ancient Jewish teachers restricted the public reading of some of these passages, and readers today find them painful. Their purpose is to make the exiles feel what idolatry is: not a change of religious taste, but the betrayal of a love that saved them. And even chapter 16 ends with a promise: “I myself will remember the covenant I made with you in your youth.”

Chapter 18 answers a proverb the exiles were repeating: “The parents eat sour grapes, but it is the children whose teeth are set on edge.” They believed they were being punished for their ancestors’ sins and could do nothing about it. God replies that every life belongs to him, and each person answers for their own way. The wicked who turn will live; the righteous who turn to wickedness will not be saved by their past. And beneath the logic is a plea:

{{OWB:EZK.18.31.s1}} {{OWB:EZK.18.32.s1}}

— Ezekiel 18:31–32

The command to make a new heart in chapter 18 and the promise to give one in chapter 36 sit side by side in the book. Christians have discussed ever since how the two belong together. Most have answered that God commands what only he can give, and gives what he commands.

The nations (Ezekiel 25–32)

While the siege goes on, Ezekiel turns to the neighbours who were gloating over Judah. The longest oracles are against Tyre, the rich trading city on the coast, pictured as a great ship that sinks with all its cargo (27), and against Egypt, the power Judah kept trusting for rescue. The lament over the king of Tyre in chapter 28 pictures him as a figure in Eden, “on God’s holy mountain”, perfect until wickedness was found in him and cast out for his pride. Some early Christian writers read the passage as a description of the fall of Satan behind the king; others read it as a portrait of a human ruler in the language of Eden. Either way the lesson is the same as the charge God lays against the prince of Tyre in 28:2: “You are a mortal and not a god.”

The good shepherd and the new heart (Ezekiel 33–36)

When the news of the fall arrives, Ezekiel’s silence ends, and his message turns. Chapter 34 condemns Israel’s rulers as shepherds who fed themselves and left the flock to be scattered: “You have not strengthened the weak or healed the sick or bandaged the injured.” Then God says he will do it himself:

{{OWB:EZK.34.11.s1}} {{OWB:EZK.34.15.s1}} {{OWB:EZK.34.16.s1}}

— Ezekiel 34:11, 15–16

He will also set over them “one shepherd, my servant David”. Six centuries later, Jesus stood in Jerusalem and said, “I am the good shepherd.”

Chapter 36 is the heart of Ezekiel’s hope. God will act, he says, not for Israel’s sake but for the sake of his holy name, which they have dragged through the mud among the nations. He will gather them, and then:

{{OWB:EZK.36.25.s1}} {{OWB:EZK.36.26.s1}} {{OWB:EZK.36.27.s1}}

— Ezekiel 36:25–27

It is the same promise Jeremiah heard, of a covenant written on the heart, with one addition that changes everything: “I will put my Spirit within you.” Many readers have heard it behind Jesus’s words to Nicodemus, that no one can enter God’s kingdom unless they are “born of water and the Spirit” (John 3:5).

The valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37)

The best-known vision in the book follows at once. The hand of the LORD sets Ezekiel down in a valley full of bones, “and they were very dry.” God asks him, “Son of man, can these bones live?” and he gives the only honest answer: “Lord GOD, only you know.” He is told to prophesy to the bones, and they come together with a rattling, bone to bone, and sinews and flesh and skin cover them; but there is no breath in them. Then he prophesies to the breath:

{{OWB:EZK.37.9.s1}} {{OWB:EZK.37.10.s1}}

— Ezekiel 37:9–10

In Hebrew one word, ruach, means breath, wind and spirit, and the chapter plays on all three. God explains the vision: the bones are the whole house of Israel, who say, “Our bones have dried up, our hope is gone; we are completely cut off.” He will open their graves and bring them home, and put his Spirit in them so that they live. The vision is first of all a promise of national rebirth after the death of exile. But it is told as a resurrection, and Jews and Christians alike have long read it as a pledge that God can and will raise the dead.

Gog, and the new temple (Ezekiel 38–48)

Chapters 38–39 tell of a last assault on restored Israel by Gog, of the land of Magog, at the head of a host from the ends of the earth, and of God’s decisive victory over him. Revelation takes up the names for the final rebellion against God (Revelation 20:8). Christians disagree about how to read these chapters, as a forecast of particular events at the end of history or as a picture, in the language of Ezekiel’s day, of the final defeat of every power that sets itself against God’s people. The chapters end on the promise, “I will pour out my Spirit upon the house of Israel.”

The last nine chapters are a vision dated to the twenty-fifth year of the exile. An angelic guide with a measuring rod shows Ezekiel a new temple, wall by wall and gate by gate, with its altar, its priests and its offerings, and the land divided anew among the tribes. Then the glory that left by the east gate in chapter 11 comes back the same way, “and the earth shone with his glory”, and God says, “Here I will live among the Israelites forever.”

From under the threshold a stream begins to flow. It deepens from ankle-deep to a river too deep to cross, flows down to the Dead Sea and makes its salt water fresh: “Wherever the river goes, everything will live.” Trees grow on its banks, whose “leaves will never wither” and whose leaves are “for healing”. And the book ends with the name of the city.

Here too readers differ. Some expect a literal temple still to be built; many read the vision as a picture of the restored worship of God, fulfilled in Christ, who called his body the temple, and in the new Jerusalem of Revelation, where there is no temple, for “its temple is the Lord God, the Almighty, and the Lamb”, and the river of the water of life flows with the tree of life on its banks.

How to read Ezekiel

Ezekiel is long and often strange, but it is well ordered, and the dates are a help: watch for them. Read the call (1–3), the departure of the glory (8–11), the turning point (33), the shepherds, the new heart and the dry bones (34–37), and the return of the glory and the river (43 and 47) first; they are the spine of the book.

Do not be put off by the visions. Ezekiel himself can barely describe them, and the details matter less than what they show: a God whose holiness cannot be domesticated, and who will not let his people go. Take the hard chapters slowly and in context; they are meant to shock. And listen for the refrain, “Then you will know that I am the LORD.” It tells you what the whole book is for.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Ezekiel are worth knowing before you begin.

“The Lord GOD.” Ezekiel’s favourite way of naming God joins the word for “Lord” to God’s personal name. Where the name stands alone, this translation writes “the LORD”; where the two come together, it writes “the Lord GOD”, as in “Lord GOD, only you know.”

“Son of man.” God’s constant name for the prophet is kept as “Son of man”, so that readers can hear the phrase that Daniel and Jesus would later fill with new meaning; here it marks Ezekiel as a mortal before the glory of God.

The Spirit and the breath. The Hebrew ruach is translated by context: “the Spirit” who lifts the prophet, “breath” that enters the bones, the “four winds” from which it comes. In chapter 37 all three meet.
