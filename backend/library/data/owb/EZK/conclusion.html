— Ezekiel 48:35

Ezekiel ends with a name. After nine chapters of measurements, gates and tribal boundaries, the last words of the book give the city its title, and it is not Jerusalem. It is “The LORD Is There.” The book that began with the glory of God appearing to exiles far from home, and went on to watch that glory leave the temple, closes with a city whose whole identity is that God lives in it and will not leave again.

The question the book leaves with you

Ezekiel’s first hearers asked whether God had abandoned them. The book answers in two ways that belong together. First, he had left the temple, because they had filled it with idols and driven him out; his holiness is not to be taken for granted or tamed. Second, he had never left them. He was a sanctuary to them in Babylon. His throne had wheels. He would gather them, cleanse them, give them a new heart and his own Spirit, and dwell among them for ever.

So the question Ezekiel leaves is the one God asked him in the valley: “can these bones live?” It is asked of every life that has gone dry, every church that has lost its fire, every people who say “our hope is gone; we are completely cut off.” Ezekiel’s answer was not optimism. It was “Lord GOD, only you know,” and then obedience: he prophesied as he was told, and the breath came. The book’s other question is pressed with equal force: “Why would you die, house of Israel?” God takes no pleasure in the death of anyone. “So turn back and live!”

Where the book points

Much of what Ezekiel promised began to come true when the exiles returned. But the New Testament sees its fullness in Christ. Jesus called himself the good shepherd who searches for the lost and lays down his life for the sheep, the shepherd of David’s line that Ezekiel foretold. He spoke of the Spirit as rivers of living water, and of being born of water and the Spirit. On the day of Pentecost God put his Spirit within his people, and Paul could call the church God’s temple.

The last book of the Bible is full of Ezekiel. John sees the four living creatures around the throne, eats a scroll that is sweet in his mouth, hears of Gog and Magog, watches an angel measure the holy city, and is shown at last a river of the water of life flowing from the throne, with the tree of life on its banks, whose “leaves bring healing to the nations.” Ezekiel’s city called “The LORD Is There” becomes the new Jerusalem, where “God’s dwelling is with human beings.”

Where to go from here

Ezekiel has given preachers some of their most searching texts, and many of the best sermons on them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On turning and living (Ezekiel 18 and 33). Richard Baxter’s A Call to the Unconverted, a whole book on 33:11, one of the most widely read evangelistic books of the seventeenth century; and Charles G. Finney’s sermon Sinners Bound to Change Their Own Hearts (18:31).
  • On the new heart and the Spirit within (Ezekiel 36). Charles H. Spurgeon’s sermons The New Heart (36:26), Free Grace (36:32) and Prayer—the Forerunner of Mercy (36:37), and “The Necessity of the Spirit’s Work” (36:27) in Spurgeon on the Holy Spirit; Andrew Murray’s chapter “New Covenant Obedience” in The Two Covenants, also on 36:27.
  • On the valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37). Cyril of Jerusalem’s The Catechetical Lectures, whose Lecture XVIII opens with Ezekiel 37 and teaches the resurrection of the body from it.
  • On the prophet and his visions. A. B. Simpson’s chapter “The Holy Spirit in Ezekiel”, in The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, Part I; and Augustine’s The City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 34, on what Ezekiel and Daniel foretold of Christ.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Ezekiel in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

Then read Ezekiel beside Jeremiah, his older contemporary, who was saying the same things in Jerusalem while Ezekiel said them in Babylon, and beside the Revelation to John, which takes up his visions at the end of the Bible.
