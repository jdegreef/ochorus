— Zephaniah 3:17

No book in the Bible travels further in three chapters than Zephaniah. It begins with God sweeping away everything on the face of the earth, people and animals, birds and fish. It ends with God singing. In between lies the fullest description of “the day of the LORD” in all the prophets, and one of the tenderest promises in the Old Testament.

The prophet and his time

Zephaniah gives us more of his family tree than any other prophet: he is the son of Cushi, son of Gedaliah, son of Amariah, “son of Hezekiah”. Since he traces his line back four generations, the Hezekiah he names is very probably the good king of that name, which would make Zephaniah a distant member of the royal house, and may explain how well he knows the officials and “the king’s sons” of Jerusalem.

He prophesied “during the reign of Josiah son of Amon, king of Judah” (640–609 BC). Josiah is remembered as the king who turned to the LORD “with his whole heart” and swept away the idols of Judah (2 Kings 22–23). Zephaniah describes a Jerusalem where that has not yet happened: Baal is still worshipped, people bow on the rooftops to the stars, and they swear by the LORD “while they also swear by their king”, probably the god Milcom. So many think he preached before Josiah’s reform of 622 BC, and may have helped prepare the way for it. Jeremiah began his ministry in the same years.

The shape of the book

  • 1:1–2:3: the day of the LORD. Judgement on the whole earth, and especially on Judah and Jerusalem, with a call to seek the LORD before it comes.
  • 2:4–15: the nations. Philistia to the west, Moab and Ammon to the east, Cush to the south, and Assyria to the north.
  • 3:1–8: Jerusalem. Woe to the city that would not listen.
  • 3:9–20: the day after. The peoples purified, a humble remnant, and the LORD rejoicing over his people.

The day of the LORD

Amos and Isaiah had spoken of the day of the LORD before, but no one paints it as Zephaniah does. “The great day of the LORD is close at hand”, he cries, and then strikes the word “day” like a bell:

— Zephaniah 1:15–16

In the Latin Bible the first words of verse 15 are dies irae, “day of wrath”, and they gave their name to a famous medieval hymn on the Last Judgement, sung for centuries at funerals in the Western church.

The sin Zephaniah exposes most sharply is not open rebellion but complacency. God will search Jerusalem “with lamps” for those who are “like wine left to thicken on its dregs”, people who have settled into comfort and say to themselves:

{{OWB:ZEP.1.12.s1}}

— Zephaniah 1:12

They do not deny God; they simply expect nothing from him. Zephaniah’s answer is that wealth will not shelter them: “Neither their silver nor their gold will be able to save them on the day of the LORD’s fury.”

“Seek the LORD”

Yet even here a door stands open. Before the decree takes effect, the prophet calls:

{{OWB:ZEP.2.3.s1}}

— Zephaniah 2:3

“It may be” is honest. Zephaniah does not promise an easy escape. But the people he addresses, the humble, are the ones the whole book is moving toward. At the end, God will leave in Jerusalem “a people humble and lowly, who will take refuge in the name of the LORD.”

The nations, and the proud city

Zephaniah’s judgement reaches in every direction from Judah, and ends with the greatest power of the day. Nineveh, still standing when he spoke, is pictured as a ruin where owls roost on the pillars. Her sin is the oldest one: she “thought in her heart, ‘I am, and there is no other but me.’” But the oracles against the nations also carry a surprise. The LORD will make the gods of the earth waste away, and “Then they will bow down to him, each in its own place, all the coastlands of the nations.”

From judgement to song

The turn comes at 3:9, and it is a reversal of Babel. There the human race was scattered by the confusion of its language. Here God says, “Then I will purify the speech of the peoples, so that all of them may call on the name of the LORD and serve him shoulder to shoulder.” His scattered people will come home from beyond the rivers of Cush.

Then the prophet calls Jerusalem to sing, “Sing for joy, Daughter Zion”, because the LORD, the King of Israel, is with her. And in the verse at the head of this introduction the singing changes sides. The God who began the book sweeping the earth clean ends it rejoicing over his people “with singing”. Between two lines of loud joy stands a quiet one: “be quiet in his love”. Some hear in it a love too deep for words; others, a love that no longer speaks of the city’s sins.

How to read Zephaniah

Read it in one sitting, and let the first chapter be as dark as it is, so that the last can be as bright. Notice the repeated phrases: “on that day”, “the day of the LORD”, “at that time”. Watch how the judgement that seemed to swallow everything leaves a remnant, and how the humble, who have nothing to boast of, are the ones who remain.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Zephaniah is mainly poetry, and it is printed here one line per line, as the poetry it is. Two of the translation’s choices are worth knowing.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”, and “the LORD of Armies” for the title older Bibles render “the LORD of hosts”.

“Be quiet in his love.” In 3:17 the Hebrew verb means “be silent”. The translation keeps that sense; others read the line as “he will quiet you by his love”.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
