— Mark 16:7

Mark’s Gospel ends at an empty tomb, with a message the women are too frightened to pass on. But the message itself is the last word Jesus has for his disciples, and it is full of grace. They abandoned him; Peter denied him. He is going ahead of them, back to Galilee, where it all began, and he will meet them there. The road goes on. And Peter, of all people, is named.

The question the book leaves with you

Mark began with a title: “The beginning of the gospel about Jesus Christ.” By the end it is clear that the whole book has been only the beginning. The rest of the story, the meeting in Galilee, the message carried to the ends of the earth, is left for the reader to know and, in a sense, to continue. Mark has told us that the gospel must first be proclaimed “to all the nations”, and that wherever it is proclaimed in the whole world, a woman’s act of love will be told. He does not tell us how. He leaves the women silent at the tomb, and the reader holding the news.

So the question Mark leaves is the one Jesus asked on the road to Caesarea Philippi:

{{OWB:MRK.8.29.s1}}

— Mark 8:29

Peter gave the right answer and did not yet understand it. A Roman centurion gave it at the cross, after watching Jesus die. Mark wants his readers to answer as the centurion did, knowing what it costs: the Christ is the one who came to serve and to give his life as the ransom price for many, and to follow him is to take up a cross.

Where the book points

It points, first, to the cross, not as a defeat that the resurrection reverses but as the place where Jesus is most fully revealed. In Mark the glory of God is seen in a servant.

It points, second, to discipleship as a journey, not an arrival. The disciples in Mark are always on the road, usually behind, often confused. Bartimaeus, who could see nothing, ends by following Jesus along the road. That is what faith looks like in this book: not a perfect understanding, but a desperate cry for mercy, a cloak thrown off, and a man walking after Jesus toward Jerusalem. “I believe! Help my unbelief!” is as good a prayer as any disciple has prayed.

And it points to hope for those who fail. The book does not hide the disciples’ fear or Peter’s denial, because the church that first heard it, very likely in Rome in the years of persecution, knew what it was to be afraid and to fall. Mark tells them that the risen Jesus goes ahead of his frightened followers, and waits for them.

Where to go from here

Mark has long been the quiet Gospel, but some of the church’s finest preachers have found in its vivid scenes exactly the details that open a sermon. A few places to begin in this library:

  • On the man behind the book. Eusebius of Caesarea’s The Church History, whose third book preserves what Papias said about Mark and Peter; and Hurlbut’s Life of Christ for Young and Old, which tells the Gospel story as one connected narrative.
  • On the touch and the look of Jesus. Alexander Maclaren preached on Mark more vividly than almost anyone: Christ’s Touch (1:41), The Secret of Gladness (2:19), The Toiling Christ (4:36–38), The Power of Feeble Faith (5:25–28) and The Looks of Jesus (5:32).
  • On prayer. Jesus, Teacher of Prayer, whose chapter “Very Early in the Morning” follows Jesus out to pray long before daylight (1:35).
  • On faith. A. B. Simpson’s The Possibilities of Faith, on Jesus’s words to the father of the boy with an unclean spirit (9:23).
  • On giving. Andrew Murray’s Christ’s Estimate of Money, on the widow’s two small coins (12:41–44).
  • On the empty tomb. Martin Luther’s Of Christ’s Resurrection, on the women at the tomb (16:1–8); and, from the longer ending, Catherine Booth’s Aggressive Christianity (16:15).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Mark in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this Gospel.

But the best next step may be to read Mark again, aloud and all at once, and then to turn to the other Gospels. Matthew and Luke both follow Mark’s outline closely, and you will see how each fills it out. Yet Mark’s plain, urgent telling has a power of its own. It ends by sending you back to the beginning, to Galilee, where Jesus first walked by the lake and called four fishermen, and where he still calls:

“Come and follow me.”

— Mark 1:17
