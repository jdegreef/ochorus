{{OWB:MRK.1.1.s1}}

— Mark 1:1

Mark has no time for a nativity. There are no shepherds, no wise men, no family tree. His first sentence is a title, and by the fourteenth verse Jesus is already grown, baptized, tested in the wilderness and walking into Galilee with a message: the time has come, the kingdom of God has come near. From then on the story hardly pauses for breath.

It is the shortest of the four Gospels, and for a long time it was the least read. Nearly everything in it is also in Matthew or Luke, usually at greater length, and early readers tended to prefer the fuller versions. The modern world has rediscovered it. Mark is vivid, blunt and fast. It shows Jesus angry and grieved and deeply moved; it shows his disciples baffled, frightened and slow; and it ends, in its oldest form, in a way no one expects. It reads less like a book about Jesus than like an eyewitness telling you what it was like to be there.

And the first word of its title is the right one. This is “the beginning of the gospel”, the good news. It may be from Mark’s use of the word that the four books came to be called Gospels.

Peter’s memories

The Gospel does not name its author. From the earliest days it has been called Mark’s, and the church has identified him with John Mark of the book of Acts, whose mother’s house in Jerusalem was a meeting place for the first believers. He set out with Paul and his cousin Barnabas on their first journey, turned back halfway, and became the cause of a quarrel so sharp that the two missionaries parted company. Years later Paul writes of him as a fellow worker and asks for him by name, “because he is helpful to me in my work”. The first letter of Peter sends greetings from Rome, under the name Babylon, from “my son Mark”.

It is Peter that the early tradition links him with most closely. Papias, a bishop in Asia Minor early in the second century, passed on what he had been told by an older teacher:

“Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ.”

Irenaeus, around 180, says the same: Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, handed down in writing what Peter had preached. Many readers have felt Peter’s voice behind the book. Peter is the first disciple called and the last named; the story often seems told from where he stood; and his failures are told without softening. The details are those of someone who remembered: the cushion in the stern of the boat where Jesus slept in the storm, the green grass where the five thousand sat down, the young man in a linen sheet who ran away naked on the night of the arrest.

Most scholars date the book to the late 60s or around AD 70, and the tradition places it in Rome, among Christians who had recently suffered under Nero. Mark explains Jewish customs and translates Aramaic words, so his first readers were not, for the most part, Jews of Judea. Most scholars also think it was the first of the Gospels to be written, and that Matthew and Luke both used it, though some hold, as Augustine did, that Matthew came first. On either view it is the Gospel closest to the way the apostles first told the story aloud.

At once

Mark’s favourite word is a small Greek word meaning “immediately”, and he uses it more than forty times. Jesus comes up out of the water and at once sees the heavens torn open; the Spirit at once drives him out into the wilderness; the fishermen at once leave their nets; he goes at once into the synagogue. The effect is of a story told breathlessly, by someone who cannot wait to get to what happened next.

Mark also loves to tell stories in the present tense, and to sandwich one story inside another. Jairus begs Jesus to come and heal his dying daughter; on the way a woman who has been bleeding for twelve years touches his cloak; by the time Jesus has found her, word comes that the girl is dead. Jesus curses a fig tree, then drives the traders out of the temple, and the next morning the fig tree is found withered. Peter follows Jesus into the high priest’s courtyard; inside, Jesus is tried and confesses who he is; outside, Peter denies three times that he knows him. Each story helps us read the other.

Who is this?

The first half of Mark is driven by a single question. Jesus teaches with an authority the scribes do not have. He orders unclean spirits out, and they go. He forgives a paralysed man’s sins and then, to show that he has the right to, tells him to get up and walk. He touches a leper, eats with tax collectors, and calls himself the Lord of the Sabbath. And one night on the lake he wakes in the stern of a sinking boat and speaks to the wind:

{{OWB:MRK.4.39.s1}} {{OWB:MRK.4.39.s2}}

— Mark 4:39

The disciples are more frightened afterwards than they were in the storm. “Who is this, then,” they ask one another, “that wind and sea alike obey him?” Every reader of the Old Testament knew who commands the sea. The disciples do not yet dare to say it.

Strangely, those who see most clearly in these chapters are the demons, who shout out that he is God’s Holy One and the Son of the Most High God, and Jesus silences them. He tells the people he heals to tell no one, and later he orders his disciples not to say who he is. Scholars have called this the “messianic secret”. Part of the reason may be practical: a crowd that wanted a miracle-worker or a warrior king could easily take him for the wrong kind of Messiah. But Mark’s deeper point is that no one can understand who Jesus is until they have seen the cross.

And yet no Gospel shows Jesus more plainly as a man. In Mark the people of Nazareth know him as “the carpenter, the son of Mary”, and take offence. His own family set out to take charge of him, saying, “He has lost his senses.” He sleeps from exhaustion, groans, is angry with his own disciples, and looks at a rich man and loves him. Of the day and hour of the end he says that nobody knows it, “neither the angels in heaven nor the Son.” Mark does not set the man and the mystery against each other. The one who commands the sea is the one asleep on the cushion.

On the way

The middle of the book is framed by two healings of blind men. At Bethsaida a man is healed in two stages: at first he sees people “like trees moving about”, and only with a second touch can he see everything clearly. That is exactly where the disciples are. At Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks who they say he is, and Peter answers for them all:

{{OWB:MRK.8.29.s2}}

— Mark 8:29

He is right, and he cannot yet see what it means. As soon as Jesus begins to teach that the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected and be killed, and rise again after three days, Peter takes him aside and rebukes him, and Jesus rebukes him back: “Get behind me, Satan!”

Three times in these chapters Jesus foretells his death, and three times the disciples miss the point. After the first, Peter protests. After the second, they argue on the road about which of them is the greatest. After the third, James and John ask for the best seats in his glory. And each time Jesus teaches them what it means to follow him: to say no to themselves and take up their cross, to be last of all and servant of all. The section reaches its summit in a single sentence, perhaps the key to the whole Gospel:

{{OWB:MRK.10.45.s1}}

— Mark 10:45

Then, at Jericho, a blind beggar named Bartimaeus shouts for mercy, throws off his cloak, receives his sight, and follows Jesus “along the road”. The word for road is the same word Mark has used for “the way” all through these chapters. The man who sees follows Jesus on the way to Jerusalem, and to the cross.

The disciples

No Gospel is harder on the Twelve than Mark. They are called, sent out with power, and given the mystery of the kingdom. And they are, again and again, without understanding. They panic in the storm. They forget the loaves the day after the feeding. Jesus asks them, “Do you still not see it or understand? Have your hearts been hardened?”, words Mark used earlier of Jesus’s opponents. In Gethsemane they sleep. At the arrest “they all left him and ran.” Peter, who promised to die with him, swears he does not know him.

Others in Mark see more clearly: a leper, a desperate father who cries, “I believe! Help my unbelief!”, a woman who pours a flask of costly perfume over Jesus’s head and is told that wherever the gospel is proclaimed in the whole world, what she has done will be told in memory of her. And when the men have fled, it is the women who stay, watching from a distance at the cross and coming to the tomb.

Mark tells this not to shame the apostles, who were the church’s leaders by the time he wrote, but to give hope to every disciple who has failed. If Jesus could make apostles of these men, there is hope for anyone. The first message from the empty tomb includes a name: “go and tell his disciples, and Peter too”.

The cross

About a third of Mark is taken up with the last week of Jesus’s life, and his account of the cross is the starkest of the four. There are no words of comfort from the cross. Jesus is mocked by the passers-by, the chief priests and the men crucified with him. Darkness falls at noon. At three in the afternoon he cries out in Aramaic, in the words of Psalm 22, and then dies with a loud cry.

Mark draws two lines from the beginning of his book to this moment. At the baptism the heavens were “torn apart” and a voice said, “You are my dearly loved Son.” Now the curtain of the temple is split in two, the same Greek word, and a voice is heard again, this time from the most unlikely person in the story:

{{OWB:MRK.15.39.s1}}

— Mark 15:39

The first human being in Mark to call Jesus the Son of God is a Gentile, a Roman officer, and he says it not after a miracle but after watching him die. That is what the whole book has been leading to. Jesus is most clearly the Son of God not when he stills the storm but when he gives his life as a ransom for many.

The ending

On the first day of the week, three women come to the tomb with spices and find the stone rolled away. A young man in white tells them that Jesus has been raised and is going ahead of them to Galilee. And in the oldest manuscripts, that is where the Gospel ends:

{{OWB:MRK.16.8.s1}}

— Mark 16:8

It is a startling last line, and in Greek it ends on the word “for”. Whether Mark meant to stop here, or his ending was lost, no one knows. Early copyists clearly felt that something more was needed, and two later endings were written to round it off. But many readers have come to think Mark meant exactly this. The promise has been given; the reader knows the message was in the end delivered, or there would be no Gospel to read. And the book leaves us standing at the empty tomb, with the women, asking what we will do with the news.

How to read Mark

Mark is short enough to read aloud in a little over an hour, and that is the best way to meet it first, as its first hearers did. Notice its shape: the beginning by the Jordan (1:1–13); the work in Galilee, with its question “Who is this?” (1:14–8:26); the way to Jerusalem, with its answer and its teaching on the cross (8:27–10:52); and the last week in Jerusalem, from the entry into the city to the empty tomb (11–16).

Then read it slowly. Watch Jesus’s face: Mark tells us when he looks around, when he groans, when he is angry, when he looks at a rich young man and loves him. Watch the disciples, and ask where you would have been standing. And watch for the moments when Mark speaks over the heads of the characters to the reader, as in the middle of Jesus’s words about the end: “What I tell you, I tell everyone: Keep watch!”

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Mark are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Immediately.” Rather than repeat one English word forty times, the translation varies it (“the moment”, “straight away”, “at once”, “on the spot”), so that Mark’s pace is felt without becoming monotonous.

The first verse. Many manuscripts add “the Son of God” to the title in 1:1; a few early ones lack it. This translation follows the shorter text, “The beginning of the gospel about Jesus Christ.” Mark gives Jesus that title at the key moments all the same, from the voice at the baptism to the centurion at the cross.

Jesus’s anger. In 1:41, where most manuscripts say Jesus was moved with pity for a leper, this translation follows a few manuscripts that say he was “Stirred with anger”, the harder reading, which copyists were more likely to soften than to invent. Mark speaks of Jesus’s anger elsewhere too.

The endings. The two later endings are printed after 16:8, set apart and in brackets, with a note: a short ending, in which the women report to Peter and his companions, and the longer ending (16:9–20), found in most manuscripts but not in the earliest, which tells of appearances to Mary Magdalene, to two disciples and to the eleven. Their style and vocabulary are not Mark’s, and they read like a summary of appearances told in the other Gospels and Acts. They are part of the church’s Bible and are printed here, as in most Bibles, with that note.

Verses not in the oldest manuscripts. Mark 7:16, 9:44, 9:46, 11:26 and 15:28, which older Bibles print, are missing from the earliest copies and were probably repeated from other verses or other Gospels. They are not in the text, and the verse numbers are skipped.
