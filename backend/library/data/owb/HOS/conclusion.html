— Hosea 14:9

Hosea ends like a book of wisdom, not like a book of prophecy. After fourteen chapters of grief and anger and astonishing tenderness, a last voice steps back and speaks to whoever has read this far. These things must be understood. The ways of the LORD are right, and the same road that the righteous walk in is the one on which the rebels stumble.

The question the book leaves with you

Hosea’s hearers did not doubt that God existed. They went to the shrines, kept the festivals, and cried out, “My God, we, Israel, know you!” What Hosea asked them was whether they knew him at all: whether their love was a covenant or a mood, the steady faithfulness of a marriage or “like the morning mist, like the dew that vanishes early in the day.” It is the question the book leaves with every reader. Not whether we are religious, but whether we know the God who says, “I want loyal love, not sacrifice.”

The book’s answer to that question is not first an instruction but a portrait. Before it tells Israel to return, it shows the God they would be returning to: a husband who goes into the wilderness to win back an unfaithful wife, a father who taught his son to walk, a Holy One whose heart turns over within him rather than let his people go. The call to “Take words with you and return to the LORD” is only possible because he has already said, “I will love them freely.”

Where the story goes next

Hosea’s warnings came true within his lifetime. The second book of Kings tells how Samaria fell to Assyria after a three-year siege and how the people of the northern kingdom were carried away, “because the Israelites had sinned against the LORD their God”. The southern kingdom, Judah, which Hosea warned more than once, would follow it into exile a little over a century later.

But the promises did not fall with Samaria. Paul and Peter heard them being kept in their own day, as people who had never been God’s people were called “children of the living God”. Matthew saw the Son called out of Egypt. Jesus took Hosea’s “mercy, not sacrifice” as a summary of what God had always wanted. And the New Testament takes up Hosea’s great picture and completes it: the church is the bride, and Christ the bridegroom who gave himself for her.

Where to go from here

Hosea has given preachers some of their most searching texts, and several of the best sermons on them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the love that buys back (Hosea 3). Frances Ridley Havergal’s Christ for Us, which takes Hosea 3:3 as Christ’s promise of himself to the soul he redeems; and Charles H. Spurgeon’s reading for February 4 in Morning by Morning, on the LORD’s love for Israel in Hosea 3:1.
  • On wilderness and betrothal (Hosea 2). Spurgeon’s readings for July 18 and July 22 in The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith, on Hosea 2:14 and 2:19–20.
  • On love that does not last (Hosea 6–7). Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s sermon The Impressions of Natural Men (Hosea 6:4), on the difference between being moved and being changed; and Spurgeon’s reading for June 23 in Morning by Morning, on the loaf not turned (Hosea 7:8).
  • On seeking the LORD (Hosea 10:12). Charles G. Finney’s Breaking Up the Fallow Ground, a practical call to self-examination and confession, and Spurgeon’s reading for April 1 in Evening by Evening.
  • On the ropes of love (Hosea 11). Spurgeon’s reading for May 20 in Evening by Evening, on Hosea 11:4.
  • On coming home (Hosea 14). M’Cheyne’s What Have I to Do Any More with Idols? (Hosea 14:8), preached at the close of a communion Sabbath; Alexander Maclaren’s The Dew and the Plants (Hosea 14:5–6); and Spurgeon’s reading for October 22 in Morning by Morning, on “I will love them freely” (Hosea 14:4).
  • On Hosea and the gospel. Augustine’s The City of God, whose eighteenth book, chapter 28, gathers what Hosea and Amos foretold of Christ and the calling of the nations; and Jerome’s letter to Paulinus (Letter LIII in The Letters of St. Jerome), which sketches the hidden meaning of each of the twelve prophets, beginning with Hosea.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Hosea in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
