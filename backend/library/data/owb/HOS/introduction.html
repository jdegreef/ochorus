{{OWB:HOS.11.8.s1}}

— Hosea 11:8

Hosea is the book in which God speaks like a betrayed husband and a heartbroken father. Other prophets thunder; Hosea grieves. He was sent to the northern kingdom of Israel in its last, feverish decades, and he told its people the hardest thing a prophet can say: that they did not know the God they worshipped. But he said it as no one had said it before, through the story of his own marriage.

The book is first of the twelve shorter prophets, the ones English readers call the Minor Prophets, meaning short rather than unimportant. It is also one of the most difficult books in the Bible. Its Hebrew is compressed and in places obscure, its poems change speaker without warning, and its images tumble over one another: a moth, a lion, a dove, a half-baked loaf, morning mist, a bear robbed of her cubs. Augustine, who wrote about it in The City of God, admitted that Hosea “speaks so very profoundly that it is laborious work to penetrate his meaning.”

Yet its heart is not hard to find at all. It beats in the verse above. Israel has earned the fate of the cities of the plain, and God cannot bring himself to give it up.

The last days of a kingdom

The book’s first verse sets Hosea in the reign of Jeroboam son of Joash over Israel, and of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah over Judah. That places the beginning of his work around the middle of the eighth century before Christ, and its end within a few years of the fall of the northern kingdom.

When Hosea began, Israel had never seemed stronger. Jeroboam II reigned for some forty years, pushed back his neighbours and brought wealth to the land. Amos, who preached in the same years, describes houses of ivory and couches of silk. Hosea saw the same prosperity and named what it had done: “When they had pasture, they ate their fill; they were full, and their hearts grew proud; that is why they forgot me.”

After Jeroboam died, the kingdom came apart. In the thirty years that followed, six kings sat on the throne of Samaria, and four of them were murdered by the men who replaced them. Hosea watched it happen: “All their kings have fallen, and not one of them calls on me.” Israel’s rulers lurched between paying tribute to Assyria and seeking help from Egypt, and Hosea has an image for that too: Ephraim “has become like a dove, easily fooled and without sense; they call to Egypt, they go to Assyria.” In 722 BC, after a siege of three years, Samaria fell to the Assyrians and its people were carried into exile. The book stands at the edge of that cliff.

Hosea is the northern kingdom’s own prophet. Amos came up from Judah to preach at Bethel; Hosea seems to have been one of the people he addressed, and he speaks of its towns, its shrines and its history from the inside. He often calls the nation Ephraim, after its largest tribe, and the name comes back almost forty times.

A marriage that preached

The book begins with a command that has troubled readers ever since:

{{OWB:HOS.1.2.s1}}

— Hosea 1:2

Hosea marries Gomer daughter of Diblaim, and she bears three children, each named by God as a sermon. The first is Jezreel, the valley where King Jehu had seized the throne in a bloodbath nearly a century earlier; the dynasty that began there would now be brought to account. The second is a daughter named No Compassion, “for I will no longer show compassion to the house of Israel.” The third is a son named Not My People, “because you are not my people, and I will not be yours.” The last name undoes the covenant itself, the bond by which the LORD had made Israel his people and himself their God.

Then, before the first chapter is over, the sentence is reversed. The people will be as countless as the sand, and “In the very place where they were told, ‘You are not my people,’ they will be told, ‘You are children of the living God.’” The pattern of the whole book is there in miniature: judgment spoken in full, and then a love that refuses to let judgment have the last word.

Chapter 2 turns the marriage into a courtroom poem. The wife has run after lovers, believing that they gave her grain and wine and oil, when “it was I who gave her the grain, the new wine, and the olive oil.” In Hosea’s day Baal was worshipped as the storm god who sent the rain and made the fields fruitful, and Israel had come to give him the thanks that belonged to the LORD. So the LORD will take back his gifts until she remembers who gave them. And then, astonishingly, the punishment becomes a courtship:

{{OWB:HOS.2.16.s1}} {{OWB:HOS.2.17.s1}}

— Hosea 2:14–15

The valley of Achor, “trouble”, was where Achan was stoned when Israel first entered the land. The place of the first disaster will become the door. And in the wilderness, where the LORD first took Israel as his own, he will betroth her again:

{{OWB:HOS.2.21.s1}} {{OWB:HOS.2.22.s1}}

— Hosea 2:19–20

Notice the dowry. The bridegroom pays it, and what he pays is himself: righteousness, justice, loyal love, compassion and faithfulness. The chapter ends by renaming the children. No Compassion will be shown compassion; to Not My People God will say “You are my people,” and he will say “You are my God.”

In chapter 3 Hosea is told to “Go again, love a woman who is loved by another man and is an adulteress, just as the LORD loves the people of Israel.” He buys her back, for silver and barley, and she must wait with him many days, as Israel will wait without king or sacrifice before it turns back to “the LORD their God and David their king.”

Readers have long asked what really happened. Some take the story at its plainest: God told the prophet to marry a woman known to be unfaithful. Others think Gomer was faithful when Hosea married her and only later went astray, and that chapter 1 tells the story with its end already in view. Some interpreters, Jewish and Christian, have read the marriage as a vision or an acted parable rather than a literal command. Most readers think the woman of chapter 3 is Gomer herself, taken back; a few think she is another woman. The book does not settle these questions. What it makes unmistakable is that the prophet’s pain is meant to let us feel God’s. Israel had broken God’s heart, and Hosea knew what that meant from the inside.

“No knowledge of God in the land”

From chapter 4 to the end the book is a long series of poems, hard to divide neatly, that move back and forth between accusation and appeal. It opens like a court case: “the LORD has a charge to bring against those who live in the land: there is no faithfulness, no loyal love, and no knowledge of God in the land.”

Those three missing things are Hosea’s great words. Faithfulness is being true to one’s word. Loyal love, the Hebrew hesed, is the love that keeps covenant when keeping it costs something. And knowledge of God, for Hosea, is not information about him but intimacy with him, the knowing of a husband and wife. Its absence explains everything else: “Cursing, lying, murder, stealing and adultery!”

Hosea lays much of the blame at the feet of the priests, who should have taught the people and instead fed on their sins: “My people are perishing for lack of knowledge.” And so it will be “like people, like priest.” Worship had gone wild on every hilltop. People consulted pieces of wood; the shrines at Bethel and Gilgal were full of sacrifice and empty of God. Hosea’s scorn for the golden calf set up at Bethel is withering: “a craftsman made it; it is not God.” He even renames Bethel, “house of God”, as Beth-aven, “house of wickedness”.

Politics fares no better. “They set up kings, but not by me.” Israel sought safety in treaties, in fortified towns, in horses, in anyone but God. Hosea’s images for this drift are some of the sharpest in Scripture: “Ephraim is a flat loaf left unturned”, burnt on one side and raw on the other; strangers have eaten up his strength, and “gray hairs are scattered on his head, but he does not know it.” And over it all stands a proverb that has passed into common speech: “They sow the wind, and they will reap the whirlwind.”

Loyal love, not sacrifice

At the centre of the book stands a moment of apparent repentance. The people say to one another:

{{OWB:HOS.6.1.s1}} {{OWB:HOS.6.2.s1}} {{OWB:HOS.6.3.s1}}

— Hosea 6:1–3

It sounds beautiful, and it is. But God’s answer shows that it is not enough. The people expect God to come back as surely as the sunrise; God replies that their love comes and goes like the dew:

{{OWB:HOS.6.4.s1}} {{OWB:HOS.6.5.s1}} {{OWB:HOS.6.6.s1}}

— Hosea 6:4–6

The last verse is one Jesus loved. Twice in Matthew’s Gospel he quotes it to critics who cared more for ritual than for people: when he ate with tax collectors and sinners, “Go and learn what this saying means: ‘I want mercy, not sacrifice’”, and when his disciples picked grain on the Sabbath. Hosea did not mean that sacrifice was worthless. He meant that ritual without loyalty is like a wedding ring worn by someone who has gone off with another.

The promise of being raised up “on the third day” was read by many early Christian writers as a hint of the resurrection. Hosea himself was speaking of the nation’s recovery, and readers differ on how far to press the words; but the shape of Israel’s hope, death and then life from God, is the shape of the gospel.

“How can I give you up?”

Chapter 11 is the summit of the book, and one of the tenderest passages in the Old Testament. Here the picture changes from husband to father:

{{OWB:HOS.11.1.s1}}

— Hosea 11:1

God remembers Israel’s childhood the way a parent does: “it was I who taught Ephraim to walk, holding them by the arms”, leading them “with ropes of love”, bending down to feed them. And the more he called, the further they ran. Justice would leave them to the fate of Admah and Zeboiim, towns destroyed with Sodom and Gomorrah. Then comes the verse at the head of this introduction, and the reason God gives for mercy:

{{OWB:HOS.11.9.s1}}

— Hosea 11:9

We might have expected the opposite. A human being, wronged like this, would take revenge; it is because God is holy, not in spite of it, that he will not come in fury. His holiness is not only his purity but his otherness, and part of what makes him other is a love that outlasts betrayal.

Matthew’s Gospel hears in “out of Egypt I called my son” a pattern fulfilled when the child Jesus is brought back from Egypt. The Son who is called out of Egypt this time is the one who will be faithful where Israel was not.

Jacob, the wilderness, and death

The last chapters reach back into Israel’s past to explain its present. Chapter 12 recalls Jacob, the nation’s ancestor: “In the womb he grabbed his brother by the heel, and in his full strength he contended with God.” Yet at the Jabbok “he wept and begged for his favor.” His children have inherited his grasping without his tears. Chapter 13 recalls the wilderness, where God cared for them “in a land of burning drought”, and how they forgot him the moment they were full. There is a stern claim at its centre: “there is no savior except me.”

The judgments grow darker: the lion, the leopard, the bear robbed of her cubs. And then a verse of strange double edge:

{{OWB:HOS.13.14.s1}}

— Hosea 13:14

In its setting it may be a summons to death to do its worst, and many translations, this one among them, read the first lines as questions. But the apostle Paul heard in it something more. Writing of the resurrection, he takes up its words and turns them into a taunt over a defeated enemy: “Death, where is your victory? Death, where is your sting?”

Return

Hosea’s last chapter is a call to come home, with the very words to say:

{{OWB:HOS.14.2.s1}} {{OWB:HOS.14.3.s1}}

— Hosea 14:1–2

The prayer God gives them renounces every false hope Hosea has named: “Assyria cannot save us; we will not ride on horses. Never again will we say ‘Our god’ to what our own hands have made, for in you the orphan finds compassion.” And God answers it before it is finished:

{{OWB:HOS.14.5.s1}} {{OWB:HOS.14.6.s1}}

— Hosea 14:4–5

The dew that once stood for Israel’s fickle love now stands for God’s faithful one. The nation that was a withered root will blossom like a lily and grow like the cedars of Lebanon. And the book that began with a broken marriage ends with God saying, “your fruit comes from me.”

Hosea in the New Testament

Beyond the sayings already mentioned, the apostles found in Hosea one of their clearest pictures of the gospel going out to the nations. Paul, in Romans 9, and Peter, in his first letter, take up the renaming of Hosea’s children, “Not My People” becoming “My People”, and apply it to Gentiles who had never belonged to God and now do: “At one time you were no people at all; now you are God’s people.” Jesus, on the way to the cross, quotes the cry of 10:8, people calling on the mountains to cover them and the hills to fall on them, and the book of Revelation echoes it. Through the long story of Christian reading, Hosea’s marriage has been heard as the story of Christ and his church: a bridegroom who goes after an unfaithful bride and pays the price to bring her home.

How to read Hosea

Read the first three chapters slowly, as one piece, since everything else depends on them. Then read chapters 4–14 in larger stretches rather than verse by verse. The poems are short and change direction quickly, and their power is cumulative, like listening to someone pour out a grief that keeps breaking into hope. Chapters 6, 11 and 14 are the places to pause.

Expect to be puzzled now and then. Hosea refers to places and events his first hearers knew well: Gibeah, where a terrible crime once led to civil war; Baal-peor, where Israel first worshipped the gods of Moab; Gilgal and Bethel, the great shrines of his own day. When a line is obscure, read on; the main thread is never lost for long.

Listen for the two great pictures, the husband and the father, and for the words that keep returning: return, know, loyal love, Egypt, Assyria, prostitution and healing. Above all, listen for the voice of God, which in Hosea is passionate, wounded and stubbornly kind. The book is less a set of arguments than a love letter written in tears.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Hosea are worth knowing before you begin.

Poetry. Almost all of Hosea is poetry, and this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out. The narrative of chapters 1 and 3 is printed as prose.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”. In 12:5 the name is joined to a title that older Bibles render “hosts”: “The LORD is the God of Armies”.

“Loyal love.” The Hebrew hesed, which older Bibles call “mercy”, “goodness” or “lovingkindness”, is translated “loyal love” throughout, so that Hosea’s key word can be heard each time it comes: “For I want loyal love, not sacrifice.”

The children’s names. The names of Gomer’s second and third children, often left in Hebrew as Lo-ruhamah and Lo-ammi, are translated, “No Compassion” and “Not My People”, so that every reader hears what Hosea’s hearers heard. In 2:16 the same is done with a play on words: “you will call me ‘My husband’; you will no longer call me ‘My Baal’”, since baal was also an ordinary word for a husband or master.

Numbering. The Hebrew divides Hosea’s chapters a little differently from English Bibles, in chapters 1–2 and 11–14. This edition follows the English chapters, and the references in it follow the English numbering.
