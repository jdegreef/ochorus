— Ruth 1:16

The book of Ruth begins with a date that should make a reader wary: “In the days when the judges judged”. Anyone who has just read Judges knows what those days were like. Idols in private shrines, tribes at war with one another, a Levite’s concubine from Bethlehem abused to death in Gibeah. Everyone did what they thought was right.

And then, in the same years and from the same town, comes this. Four short chapters, a story of famine and funerals, of harvest fields and a night on a threshing floor and a meeting of elders at the town gate. There are no battles, no kings, no miracles. There are two widows, a wealthy farmer, and a baby. And by the last verse the story has quietly arrived at the name of Israel’s greatest king.

Ruth is one of the most beautifully told stories in the Bible. It is also one of the most carefully built, and much of its meaning lies in the way it is put together. It rewards slow reading.

From full to empty

The first five verses are a catalogue of loss. A famine drives Elimelech of Bethlehem, whose town’s name means “house of bread”, to Moab with his wife Naomi and their two sons. There Elimelech dies. The sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. Then the sons die too, “and the woman was left alone, without her two sons and without her husband.” In a world where a widow’s safety lay in her husband and her sons, Naomi has nothing.

When she hears that “the LORD had come to the help of his people and given them food”, she sets out for home, and urges her daughters-in-law to go back to their mothers. Orpah, weeping, goes. Ruth will not:

{{OWB:RUT.1.17.s1}}

— Ruth 1:17

With the words just before this, “Your people are now my people, and your God is now my God”, a Moabite widow binds herself to an old woman with nothing to offer, to a foreign people, and to the God of Israel. She calls on the LORD by name. It is a conversion as well as a promise.

The women of Bethlehem scarcely recognise Naomi when she arrives. Her name means “pleasant”, and she will not answer to it: “Call me Mara, for the Almighty has made my life very bitter. I went away full, but the LORD has brought me home empty.” She is not wrong about what has happened to her. But she is wrong about one thing. She has not come home empty. Ruth is standing beside her. And the chapter ends with a quiet note of hope: they arrive “as the barley harvest was beginning.”

“As it happened”

Chapter 2 introduces Boaz, a relative of Elimelech, “a man of wealth and standing”. Ruth goes out to glean, to pick up the grain left behind by the harvesters, which the law of Moses reserved for the poor, the widow and the foreigner. And the narrator says, with a straight face: “As it happened, she came to the part of the field that belonged to Boaz.”

It is a lovely piece of understatement. The book almost never tells us that God is acting. It does so only twice: at the beginning, when the LORD gives his people food, and at the end, when he gives Ruth a son. In between, everything seems to happen by chance, and by the choices of ordinary people. But no reader believes it is chance. Ruth is the Bible’s great story of providence, God working out his purposes through the hidden turns of daily life, through a famine, a field, and a kindness.

Boaz notices Ruth at once. He tells her to stay in his field, orders his young men not to touch her, gives her water and bread and roasted grain, and quietly tells his workers to pull out stalks for her to find. When she asks why a foreigner should find such favour, he answers with a blessing that the rest of the book will answer:

— Ruth 2:12

Ruth comes home with about twenty quarts of barley, far more than any gleaner could expect. And when Naomi hears whose field it was, the bitterness breaks: “The LORD has not stopped showing his faithful love to the living and to the dead.” Then she adds a word that changes the whole story: “He is one of our family redeemers.”

Faithful love

The key word of the book is the Hebrew hesed, translated here “faithful love”. It means the kindness that goes beyond what duty requires, the loyalty that keeps faith with someone when it would be easier to let go. It appears three times, at the three turning points of the story.

  • Naomi prays that the LORD will show her daughters-in-law faithful love, “just as you showed it to the men who died and to me” (1:8).
  • Naomi blesses the LORD, who “has not stopped showing his faithful love to the living and to the dead” (2:20).
  • Boaz tells Ruth that the faithful love she has now shown “is greater than what you showed before” (3:10).

The book is a study of what hesed looks like in human lives. Ruth shows it to Naomi by refusing to leave her. Boaz shows it to Ruth by going far beyond the law. And behind them both, unseen but never absent, the LORD shows it to all of them. The faithful love of God is often carried by the faithful love of his people.

The threshing floor

Chapter 3 is the most delicate scene in the book. Naomi, now planning for Ruth’s future, tells her to wash, dress well, and go down by night to the threshing floor where Boaz is winnowing barley. When he lies down, she is to uncover his feet and lie there, “and he will tell you what you should do.”

It is a bold plan, and readers have long noticed how the scene is charged with possibility. But the book is careful to show that nothing improper happens. When Boaz wakes at midnight to find a woman at his feet, it is Ruth who tells him what to do:

{{OWB:RUT.3.9.s1}}

— Ruth 3:9

The word for “wing” is the same one Boaz had used in the field, when he blessed her for coming to take refuge beneath the LORD’s wings. Ruth is asking Boaz to be the answer to his own prayer. To spread the corner of one’s cloak over a woman was a way of claiming her in marriage, and Boaz understands her at once. He praises her, calls her “a woman of real worth”, and promises to act. But there is a complication: another relative is more closely related, and has the first right.

The family redeemer

To understand chapter 4, it helps to know what a family redeemer was. In Israel’s law, land was not to be lost from a family for ever. If a man fell into poverty and had to sell his land, “the relative closest to him, his redeemer, is to come and buy back what his kinsman sold.” Another law, in Deuteronomy, provided that if a man died childless, his brother should marry the widow, and her first son would carry the dead man’s name. Ruth’s story brings the two customs together.

At the town gate, before ten elders, Boaz puts the case to the nearer relative, whom the narrator pointedly does not name: Boaz calls him “Mr. So-and-so”. He is happy to buy Elimelech’s field. But when he hears that the field comes with Ruth, and that her son would inherit it in Mahlon’s name, he backs away: “I would ruin my own inheritance.” He takes off his sandal, the sign of a transfer, and Boaz buys the land and takes Ruth as his wife, “so that the dead man’s name may be kept alive on his inheritance.”

The man who would not risk his inheritance is forgotten; we do not even know his name. The man who took on another family’s loss at his own cost is remembered wherever the Bible is read.

A Moabite in Bethlehem

The book never lets us forget where Ruth came from. She is “Ruth the Moabite” again and again, even after she has made Israel her home. That matters, because Moab was no friendly neighbour. Genesis traces its origin to the incest of Lot’s daughters; Moab had hired Balaam to curse Israel in the wilderness; and Deuteronomy says that “No Ammonite or Moabite may enter the assembly of the LORD.”

And yet here is a Moabite woman, coming to shelter under the wings of Israel’s God, received by the people of Bethlehem, blessed by the elders in the names of Rachel and Leah, and made the great-grandmother of David. Readers have long seen in her a sign that the promise to Abraham, that all the families of the earth would be blessed through him, was never meant to stop at Israel’s borders. Faith, not birth, brings her in.

From empty to full

Ruth bears a son, and the story ends where it began, with Naomi. The women of Bethlehem who had heard her say “the LORD has brought me home empty” now gather around her:

{{OWB:RUT.4.14.s1}}

— Ruth 4:14

They speak of Ruth as one who “loves you and means more to you than seven sons”, and they name the baby in Naomi’s honour: “Naomi has a son!” The woman who went away full and came back empty is full again. And then, in a single sentence, the book opens out: the child is Obed, who “became the father of Jesse, David’s father.”

The short genealogy that closes the book traces the line from Perez, the son of Judah and Tamar, through Boaz, to David. Nothing in the story had pointed to a king. That is the point. While Israel’s leaders were doing what they thought was right, God was preparing a king in a farmhouse in Bethlehem, through a foreign widow’s loyalty and a farmer’s kindness.

How to read Ruth

Read it in one sitting; it takes perhaps twenty minutes. Then read it again, and watch for the echoes: empty and full, the wings, the word “redeem”, the women of Bethlehem at the beginning and the end. Notice how much of the story is told in speech, and how kindly the characters speak to one another. In Jewish practice Ruth is read at the Feast of Weeks, the harvest festival Christians know as Pentecost.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition assigned it to Samuel. Since its genealogy ends with David, it was written no earlier than his reign; some scholars place it in the time of the kings, and others after the exile, when its welcome of a foreign wife would have spoken to a sharp debate about marriage outside Israel. Christians hold different views on its date while agreeing on its place among the Scriptures and its link to the story of David.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Ruth are worth knowing before you begin.

“Faithful love.” The Hebrew hesed, rendered in older Bibles “kindness” or “mercy”, is translated “faithful love” throughout the book.

“Family redeemer.” The Hebrew go’el, often rendered “kinsman” or “kinsman-redeemer”, is translated “family redeemer”: the close relative whose duty was to buy back what a family had lost.

Names of God. “The LORD” in capitals stands for God’s personal name; Naomi also speaks of God as “the Almighty”, the old title Shaddai.

Measures and plain speech. Ancient measures are given in modern terms, so that Ruth’s day of gleaning comes to “about 20 quarts of barley”, and Boaz’s greeting to the nameless relative, an idiom for someone the speaker will not name, becomes “Mr. So-and-so”.
