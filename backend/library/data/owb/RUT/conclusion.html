— Ruth 4:22

The book of Ruth ends with a list of names, and the last name is David. A story that seemed to be about two widows and their next meal turns out, in its final line, to have been about the throne of Israel all along. The people in it never knew. Ruth probably never saw her great-grandson. Naomi held Obed on her lap and could not have guessed whose grandfather he would be.

The question the book leaves with you

That is the question Ruth leaves with its readers: do you believe God is at work in the ordinary days? The book contains no visions, no prophets, no voice from heaven. It has a famine, a long walk, a day’s work in someone else’s field, a conversation at night, and a meeting at the town gate. Its characters make decisions of loyalty and kindness without knowing where they lead. And through all of it, “as it happened”, God is building a family and preparing a king.

Naomi had decided that “the hand of the LORD has turned against me.” She was grieving, and the book does not scold her for it. But it lets her be proved wrong. The emptiness was real, and it was not the end. The one who seemed to have nothing to give, a foreign daughter-in-law, turned out to be the gift.

Where the story goes next

In the English Bible, Ruth sits between Judges and 1 Samuel, and that is where it belongs in the story. 1 Samuel will tell how Israel got its first king, and then how God chose another, a shepherd boy from Bethlehem, the son of Jesse, the grandson of Obed. Many centuries later, the prophet Micah would say that a ruler was still to come from “Bethlehem Ephrathah, too small to be counted among the clans of Judah”.

The New Testament names Ruth in the very first chapter. Matthew’s list of the ancestors of Jesus includes four women besides Mary, and two of them belong to this very household: “Salmon was the father of Boaz by Rahab. Boaz was the father of Obed by Ruth.” A Canaanite and a Moabite, in the line of the Messiah. And the shape of Boaz’s kindness has long been read as a picture of Christ: the near relative who takes on our loss at his own cost, redeems what we could not buy back, and gives a stranger a home and a name. Jesus himself used the image of the wings, lamenting over Jerusalem: “How many times I have wanted to gather your children together, the way a hen draws her chicks under her wings!”

Where to go from here

Ruth is short, but the church has never stopped preaching it, and some of the best of that preaching is in this library:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Ruth: Union with Christ” reads the marriage of Ruth and Boaz as a picture of the union of Christ and the church.
  • On Ruth’s choice (Ruth 1). Jonathan Edwards’s sermon “Ruth’s Resolution”, on Ruth 1:16, in Selected Sermons of Jonathan Edwards.
  • On the field of Boaz (Ruth 2). Hudson Taylor’s A Full Reward, on Boaz’s blessing in Ruth 2:12; and Charles H. Spurgeon’s chapter “Spiritual Gleaning”, on Ruth 2:15, in Talks to the Farmer.
  • On the redeemer (Ruth 3–4). A. B. Simpson’s sermon Our Kinsman Redeemer, which reads Boaz as a picture of Christ.
  • For reading with children. “Ruth Stays with Naomi” and “Boaz the Redeemer” in Tell Me the Story – Book 2: Out of Egypt, an Ochorus Original.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Ruth in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
