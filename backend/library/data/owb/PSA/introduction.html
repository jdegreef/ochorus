— Psalm 22:3

Most of the Bible is God speaking to his people. The Psalms are his people speaking back: a hundred and fifty prayers and songs, written over something like a thousand years, in which shepherds and kings, temple singers and exiles, the guilty and the falsely accused all turn and say something to God. They shout, they weep, they argue, they confess, they remember, they give thanks. And the church, receiving them from Israel, has found nothing better than to say the same words after them.

No book of the Old Testament is quoted more often in the New, and none has been sung, prayed or memorised more. Monks have recited the whole of it every week for fifteen centuries; the dying have asked for the twenty-third Psalm when they could ask for nothing else. It is the book people turn to when they do not know what to say, and they find that someone has already said it.

“Praises”

The Hebrew name of the book is Tehillim, “Praises”. Our word “psalm” comes from Greek, where it meant a song sung to a stringed instrument, and the collection is often called the Psalter.

The Hebrew name is a surprise to anyone who reads the book through, because the largest single group of psalms is not praise at all. It is lament: complaint, protest, grief, the cry of someone in trouble asking how long God will wait. More than a third of the Psalms are of this kind. One psalm calls the first part of the collection by a different name, “the prayers of David”.

Yet the name is not a mistake. In the Psalms, even the bitterest complaint is spoken to God, and that is already a kind of praise: it treats him as the one who hears, who is able to act and who will be held to his word. The verse at the head of this introduction comes from the psalm that begins “My God, my God, why have you abandoned me?”, and in the same breath it calls Israel’s praises the throne where God sits. Lament and praise belong to one conversation. The book’s name tells you where that conversation is going.

Five books and their doxologies

The Psalms were gathered over centuries from smaller collections, and the final editors arranged them into five books, each closing with a short blessing of God, a doxology:

  • Book I (Psalms 1–41), almost all headed “of David”, ending with a blessing of the God of Israel “from ages past to ages to come” (41:13).
  • Book II (Psalms 42–72), opening with the sons of Korah and ending with a psalm for the king, a doxology and a note: “Here end the prayers of David son of Jesse” (72:20).
  • Book III (Psalms 73–89), largely the psalms of Asaph and Korah, ending with Psalm 89’s anguish over the fallen line of David and a two-line blessing (89:52).
  • Book IV (Psalms 90–106), opening with the only psalm headed with the name of Moses and gathering the psalms that cry, “The LORD is King!”, and ending with a blessing to which all the people answer “Amen!” (106:48).
  • Book V (Psalms 107–150), the longest, holding the psalms of ascents and Psalm 119, and ending not with a single doxology but with five psalms of praise in a row, Psalm 150 the last of them.

The first of these doxologies sets the pattern:

— Psalm 41:13

Ancient Jewish teachers saw the five books as Israel’s answer to the five books of Moses. Either way, the shape tells a story. Book III ends with the throne of David in ruins and the question of whether God has forgotten his promises. Book IV answers by going back behind David to Moses, and behind the kingdom to God himself: “Lord, you have been our home through every generation.” Human kings fail; the LORD is King. And the whole Psalter climbs, through grief and confession and long waiting, toward its last word, which is praise.

The gateway: Psalms 1 and 2

The first two psalms have no titles. They stand at the door of the book like two pillars, and most readers in the ancient church and synagogue read them as an introduction to the whole. The first is about a person:

{{OWB:PSA.1.1.s1}} {{OWB:PSA.1.2.s1}}

— Psalm 1:1–2

The word translated “instruction” is torah, the same word as for the Law. Psalm 1 tells you how to read what follows: slowly, often, aloud, as something to be taken in and lived by. And it sets two paths before you, the path of the righteous that the LORD knows and cares for, and the path of the wicked that ends in ruin. Much of the Psalter is the cry of people on the first path who find it harder than Psalm 1 made it sound.

The second psalm is about a king. The nations plot “against the LORD and against his anointed king”, and God speaks a decree to his king: “You are my son. This is the day I have become your father.” Psalm 1 begins with “How blessed”; Psalm 2 ends with it: “How blessed are all who make him their refuge!” Between those two blessings stand the two great themes of the book, the one who delights in God’s word and the king God has chosen. The New Testament will find both fulfilled in one person.

Kinds of psalm

The Psalms are not all alike, and it helps to know what kind you are reading. The main families are easy to recognise:

  • Praise, or hymns, which call people to praise God and give the reason: his greatness in creation (8, 19, 104), his faithfulness to Israel (105, 136), or simply who he is (100, 145–150).
  • Lament, by one person (3, 13, 22, 42) or the whole people (44, 74, 79, 80), which pour out a trouble, plead with God and usually end in trust.
  • Thanksgiving, which tell what God has done in answer to a lament: “I waited and kept on waiting for the LORD” (40, 30, 34, 116, 118).
  • Royal psalms, about the king in David’s line, his coronation, battles and wedding (2, 18, 20, 21, 45, 72, 110), and the psalms that celebrate the LORD himself as King (93, 95–99).
  • Wisdom and the law, which reflect on the two paths and the puzzle of the wicked prospering (1, 37, 49, 73), and delight in God’s word (19, 119).
  • Psalms of trust, quiet and confident (23, 62, 121, 131).
  • The penitential psalms, seven psalms of confession the church has long grouped together (6, 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, 143).

One more family needs a word of its own. The imprecatory psalms (among them 35, 58, 69, 109 and 137) call down judgment on enemies, sometimes in words that shock a Christian reader. Psalm 137, which begins so tenderly by the rivers of Babylon, ends by blessing whoever smashes Babylon’s children on the rock.

Christians have read these psalms in several ways, often together. They are the honest cry of people who had suffered terrible wrongs, handing vengeance over to God instead of taking it themselves, and refusing to pretend that cruelty does not matter. Many in the church have prayed them against sin, death and the devil rather than against people. A Christian, taught by Jesus to love enemies, may not pray them as a curse on a neighbour. But neither should they be cut out: they teach us that God hears even our rage, and that justice is his.

Hebrew poetry

Hebrew poetry does not rhyme, and its rhythm is hard to carry into English. What does carry is its most distinctive feature, parallelism: a thought is said once, and then said again in a second line that echoes, sharpens, extends or contrasts with the first.

{{OWB:PSA.19.2.s1}}

— Psalm 19:1

The second line does not simply repeat the first; it looks at the same thing from a little further along. Sometimes the second line is the opposite of the first, as at the end of Psalm 1: the LORD knows the path of the righteous, “but the path of the wicked ends in ruin.” Sometimes a third line climbs higher still. Once you hear this pattern, you will hear it everywhere.

Some psalms are acrostics, each line or stanza beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet (25, 34, 111, 145). Psalm 119, the longest chapter in the Bible, gives each of the twenty-two letters a stanza of eight lines: an alphabet of devotion to God’s word.

Because the Psalms are poetry, this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out, rather than running the lines together as prose. There are no verse numbers, so that you can read a psalm as a song and not as a list. The small word Selah, which appears seventy-one times, is printed where it stands. Its meaning is uncertain; it was probably a musical direction.

The titles

Above most psalms stands a short heading: “For the music director. A psalm of David.” Some name an author, some a tune or instrument (“On the gittith”), some a kind of song (“A maskil”), and about a dozen the moment in David’s life that the psalm belongs to: “A psalm of David, when he fled from his son Absalom.” In this edition these titles are printed in italics at the head of the psalm.

By the titles, David is linked with seventy-three psalms; Asaph, one of David’s temple musicians, with twelve; the sons of Korah, a family of temple singers, with eleven; Solomon with two; Moses, Heman and Ethan with one each. About a third have no name at all.

The titles are very old. They are part of the Hebrew text, they were already translated in the Greek Septuagint more than a century before Christ, and they appear in the Psalms scrolls found near the Dead Sea. Most scholars think they were added by the editors who gathered the collections rather than written by the poets themselves, and the little Hebrew word translated “of David” can mean “by David”, “for David”, or “belonging to the Davidic collection”. Even so, the tradition behind them is ancient and weighty, and Jesus himself quotes Psalm 110 as words David spoke by the Holy Spirit. The titles are worth reading: they set a psalm in a real life.

The Hebrew counts a title as verse 1 (or 1 and 2), so its verse numbers often run ahead of English Bibles. This edition prints no verse numbers, and the references in it follow the English numbering.

Lament that turns to trust

Psalm 13 is only six verses long, and it shows in miniature the shape of a great many psalms. It begins in the dark:

{{OWB:PSA.13.2.s1}}

— Psalm 13:1

Four times the psalmist asks “How long?” Then he pleads: “Turn and look, LORD my God, and answer me!” And then, with nothing in his circumstances changed, the psalm turns:

{{OWB:PSA.13.6.s1}}

— Psalm 13:5–6

That turn happens again and again in the Psalms, often on a few words: “But you, LORD” or “Yet you”. It is not a pretence that all is well, but a decision to remember who God is while the trouble is still there. The psalms of lament give us permission to bring God our worst, and then they teach us where to look.

Not every lament makes the turn. Psalm 88 ends in darkness, its last line “my one companion is the dark.” It is a gift that the Bible contains such a psalm: no one is too low to pray, and even this prayer is addressed to “God who saves me”.

The songs of ascents

Fifteen psalms in a row (120–134) are headed “A song of ascents”. They were almost certainly sung by pilgrims going up to Jerusalem, which stands high in the hills, for the great festivals. They move like a journey: from a cry of trouble far from home (120), to a look toward the mountains and the question where help will come from (121), to joy at the gates of the city (122), to the house of the LORD and the blessing of those who serve in it at night (134). Along the way come some of the best-loved psalms of all: tears sown and sheaves brought home (126), a cry “from the deep waters” (130), a soul as calm as a weaned child (131), and the goodness of brothers and sisters living in harmony (133).

The Psalms in the life of Jesus

Jesus grew up with the Psalms, singing them in the synagogue and on the road to the festivals; after the last supper, the Gospels say, he and his disciples sang a hymn before going out to the Mount of Olives, most likely the psalms of praise sung at Passover (113–118). After his resurrection he told his disciples that everything written about him in the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms had to be fulfilled.

On the cross he prayed the Psalms. Matthew and Mark record that he cried out in his own Aramaic the first line of Psalm 22:

{{OWB:PSA.22.2.s1}}

— Psalm 22:1

Psalm 22 goes on to describe a man mocked by onlookers who shake their heads, his hands and feet attacked, his clothes shared out by lot, and the Gospels echo it all through the crucifixion. But the psalm does not end in abandonment: its second half breaks out into praise in the great assembly and the promise that the farthest ends of the earth will turn to the LORD.

Luke records Jesus’s last words as another line from the Psalms, a prayer of trust that has long been part of Jewish prayer at nightfall:

{{OWB:PSA.31.6.s1}}

— Psalm 31:5

The rest of the New Testament reads the Psalms in the same light. The voice from heaven at Jesus’s baptism echoes Psalm 2: “You are my son.” Jesus asks how David can call the Messiah his lord in Psalm 110 (“The LORD declares to my lord: ‘Take your seat at my right hand’”), the Old Testament verse the New quotes most often. Psalm 118’s stone that the builders cast aside, now “the stone at the head of the corner”, becomes the apostles’ picture of the rejected and risen Christ. Peter at Pentecost preaches Psalm 16, “nor allow your loyal one to see the pit”, as a promise of resurrection. The early church heard Christ in the Psalms in two ways at once: as the one they speak about, and as the one who prays them with his people.

How to pray the Psalms

The Psalms are best understood by praying them. Athanasius, in the fourth century, said that most of Scripture speaks to us, while the Psalms are like a mirror in which we see ourselves. Martin Luther called the book a little Bible. John Calvin called it “an anatomy of all the parts of the soul”, because there is no feeling a person can have that is not pictured somewhere in it.

A few ways in:

  • Read them aloud. They were made for the voice.
  • Read them through. An old custom reads five psalms a day, taking the day of the month and adding thirty each time (on the 3rd, Psalms 3, 33, 63, 93 and 123), to finish the book in a month.
  • Pray the psalm that does not fit your mood. On a happy day, a lament teaches you to pray for those who suffer; on a hard day, a psalm of praise reminds you of what is still true.
  • Let a psalm say what you cannot. When there are no words of your own, Psalm 51 can confess for you, Psalm 23 can trust for you, and Psalm 88 can sit with you in the dark.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in the Psalms are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” When you see LORD in capital letters, it stands for God’s personal name, the four Hebrew letters YHWH, probably pronounced Yahweh, which Jewish readers out of reverence did not say aloud. Following a very old custom, this translation, like most English Bibles, prints it as “the LORD”. Where the Hebrew has the ordinary word for “master”, it reads “Lord” in small letters, and where the two come together, “Lord GOD”. So Psalm 8 begins, “LORD, our Lord, how glorious is your name across the whole earth!”

“Loyal love.” The Hebrew word hesed, which older Bibles render “mercy”, “lovingkindness” or “steadfast love”, means the faithful love that keeps a promise and will not let go. This translation calls it “loyal love”, and you will meet it on almost every page: “his loyal love lasts forever.”

“How blessed.” The first word of the Psalter, and a favourite of the psalmists, is an exclamation, not a statement: “How blessed is the one…” It is printed that way throughout.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
