— Psalm 150:1

The Psalms begin with a single person on a path, delighting in God’s instruction. They end with everything that breathes, praising. In between lies the whole of human life: enemies and exile, sin and forgiveness, sickness and long nights, festivals and harvests, kings who rose and kings who fell. The book has never once looked away from trouble. And yet it ends here.

Psalm 150 is the shortest possible summary of everything the Psalter has learned. It gives no new reason for praise; the hundred and forty-nine psalms before it have given them all. It says only where God is to be praised (in his holy place, and in the great expanse of his power), why (“for the mighty things he has done”), how (with the ram’s horn, harp and lyre, hand drum and dance, strings and pipes, and cymbals that ring out like a shout) and who. The answer to that last question is the book’s last line, and it leaves no one out.

Where the Psalms point

The ending is not a mood. The psalmists who wrote “How long, LORD?” did not stop meaning it when they wrote “Praise the LORD!” The book ends in praise because it believes that is where history ends: with the LORD as King over all the earth, the nations gathered to him, and his loyal love proved true. Again and again the Psalms look forward to that day, and their hope was larger than anything Israel had yet seen.

Christians read that hope as fulfilled in Jesus. He is the blessed one of Psalm 1 who delighted in God’s law, and the son of Psalm 2 whom the nations raged against. He prayed Psalm 22 on the cross, and its second half, the praise in the great assembly and the ends of the earth turning to the LORD, began to come true when he rose. He is the lord of Psalm 110, seated at God’s right hand; the shepherd of Psalm 23, who said, “I am the good shepherd”; the stone the builders cast aside. And the church has never stopped singing the Psalms, from the first Christians who were urged to speak to one another in “psalms, hymns and Spirit-given songs” to every congregation and every lonely reader who has prayed them since.

So the call of Psalm 150 is not only to the choirs of the temple. It reaches the reader of this book, wherever the Psalms have found you: in a lament, in a confession, on a pilgrimage, or already singing.

Where to go from here

Preachers and writers have lived in the Psalms for as long as there has been a church, and many of the best of them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the shepherd psalm (Psalm 23). Alexander Maclaren’s The Shepherd King of Israel reads it as the old king David looking back on his shepherd years; Hudson Taylor’s Blessed Adversity takes up its last verse.
  • On the two paths (Psalm 1). Hudson Taylor’s Blessed Prosperity.
  • On longing and lament. Maclaren’s Thirsting for God (Psalm 42), Spurgeon’s Waiting Only Upon God (Psalm 62) and Elisabeth Elliot’s I Keep a Quiet Heart (Psalm 131).
  • On God our refuge (Psalms 46 and 91). A. W. Tozer’s God Is Our Refuge and Strength, and Maclaren’s The City and River of God and The Sheltering Wing.
  • On pilgrimage (Psalms 84 and 121). Elisabeth Elliot’s Hearts Set on Pilgrimage, A. B. Simpson’s From Strength to Strength and Maclaren’s Looking to the Hills.
  • On the cross (Psalms 22 and 31). Maclaren’s Into Thy Hands and Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s The Spirit Committed to God, both on the prayer Jesus prayed as he died; Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, for how the earliest Christians read Psalm 22 and Psalm 110.
  • On praise. Maclaren’s The Satisfier of All Desires (Psalm 145), and on Psalm 147, Spurgeon’s Healing for the Wounded and Tozer’s The Infinite God.
  • On praying and singing the Psalms. William Law’s A Serious Call to a Devout and Holy Life, with its chapter on chanting psalms in private devotion; Andrew Murray’s The Inner Chamber, on Psalm 119; Hannah Whitall Smith’s The Christian’s Secret of a Happy Life, on Psalm 139; and The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original on the prayers of the Old Testament, David’s psalms among them.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to the Psalms in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, psalm by psalm, what the great writers of the church have said about each one.

But the best next step may simply be to keep reading the Psalms, one a day or five, and to pray them as you go. They were never meant to be finished. Readers who have prayed them all their lives say they understand them better each time, because each time they bring more of their own life to them.
