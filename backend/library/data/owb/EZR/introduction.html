{{OWB:EZR.7.10.s1}}

— Ezra 7:10

The book of Ezra begins where the story of Israel’s kings ended: in ruins. Jerusalem had been burned by the armies of Babylon in 586 BC, the temple Solomon built pulled down, the gold and silver vessels of the LORD’s house carried off to stand in the house of a foreign god. The people of Judah had been marched east, and in Babylon they learned to sing a new kind of song: “Beside the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down; we wept as we thought of Zion.”

But the prophets had said that exile would not be the last word. Jeremiah had even named a span of time: “When Babylon’s seventy years have been completed,” the LORD would come to their aid and bring them back. The book of Ezra is the story of that coming back. It is not a story of armies or miracles, but of permits and letters, lists of families, budgets, delays, a dispute before the Persian court, and a scribe who loved the law of God. It is about how a broken people begins again.

The name Ezra means “help”, probably a short form of Azariah, “the LORD has helped”. It is a fitting name for the man and for the book. Again and again, at every turning point, the narrator steps back to say where the help came from: “the hand of the LORD his God was on him.”

One story in two books

In the Hebrew Bible, Ezra and Nehemiah were counted as a single book, and they are best read together. The division into two came later, through the Christian church, and passed into printed Hebrew Bibles only in the Middle Ages. Read as one, the work covers roughly a century, from the decree of Cyrus in 538 BC to Nehemiah’s second term as governor of Judah, some time after 433 BC.

Its subject is the rebuilding of three things: the house of God (Ezra 1–6), the people of God under God’s law (Ezra 7–10 and Nehemiah 8–10), and the city of God with its walls (Nehemiah 1–7 and 11–13). Ezra tells the first two parts of that story; Nehemiah carries it on.

The last verses of 2 Chronicles and the first verses of Ezra are almost word for word the same. Chronicles breaks off in the middle of Cyrus’s proclamation, with the words “let them go up.” Ezra begins by telling us that they did.

Two returns, sixty years apart

The book has two halves, and it helps to know that a long gap lies between them.

  • Chapters 1–6 tell of the first return, in the reign of Cyrus, led by Sheshbazzar and then by Zerubbabel, a descendant of King David, and Jeshua the priest. They rebuild the altar and the temple, which is finished, after long opposition, in the sixth year of Darius, 516 or 515 BC.
  • Chapters 7–10 jump forward to the seventh year of King Artaxerxes and the arrival of Ezra himself, a priest and scribe sent from Babylon with the king’s authority to teach God’s law in Judah. Most date his journey to 458 BC, under Artaxerxes I; some scholars place it in 398 BC, under Artaxerxes II, and so after Nehemiah.

Ezra himself does not appear until chapter 7, and part of the second half is told in his own words, in the first person (7:27–9:15). The story of Esther falls in the gap between the two halves.

“The LORD stirred the spirit of Cyrus”

The book opens with an emperor:

{{OWB:EZR.1.1.s1}} {{OWB:EZR.1.2.s1}} {{OWB:EZR.1.3.s1}}

— Ezra 1:1–3

Cyrus the Great had taken Babylon in 539 BC. A clay cylinder from his reign, found in Babylon in 1879, records his policy of sending conquered peoples home and restoring their sanctuaries; it does not mention the Jews, but Ezra 1 fits it well. Cyrus was not a worshipper of Israel’s God in any sense the prophets would have recognised. Yet Isaiah had called him by name, and had heard the LORD say of him, “He is my shepherd,” and speak to him as “his anointed”.

That is the first lesson of Ezra. The God of Israel is not a local god who lost his land when his people lost theirs. He moves the hearts of emperors. And he moves the hearts of ordinary people too: those who went up to Jerusalem were “everyone whose spirit God had stirred.” The same verb is used of the king and of the returning families. Behind the politics of the Persian court, the book wants us to see the hand of God.

Why the lists matter

Chapter 2 is a long list of the families who came back, town by town and clan by clan, with the totals at the end: “The whole assembly together numbered 42,360.” It is the kind of chapter readers skip. But a list like this is a kind of answer to the exile. A people that had been scattered and counted out is counted again, by name. Each family can say: we were there; we came home.

The list is honest about its edges: some families “could not show their family or their descent, whether they belonged to Israel”. That mattered, because the returning community understood itself as the continuation of Israel, the people to whom God had made his promises. The lists say that the story has not been broken off.

Weeping and shouting

The first thing the returned exiles build is not a house or a wall, but an altar. They set it on its old foundations in the seventh month and begin the morning and evening offerings again, “because they were afraid of the peoples of the lands.” Worship comes first, and it comes out of fear as much as faith.

The next spring they lay the foundation of the temple, and the priests and Levites sing in response to one another the old refrain of Solomon’s temple. Then comes one of the most moving scenes in the Old Testament:

{{OWB:EZR.3.11.s1}} {{OWB:EZR.3.12.s1}} {{OWB:EZR.3.13.s1}}

— Ezra 3:11–13

The old men remembered what had been lost. The young had never seen it and could only rejoice at what was beginning. Ezra does not say which group was right. It simply records that both sounds rose together, and that no one could tell them apart. Many readers have found in it a picture of the life of faith: gratitude for what God is doing, mingled with grief for what is not yet as it should be.

Opposition and a long silence

Chapter 4 brings trouble. The people already living in the land, settled there long before by the kings of Assyria, offer to help build: “we worship your God as you do.” The leaders refuse: “You have no part with us in building a house for our God.” The refusal can sound harsh, but the leaders feared a worship mixed with other gods, the kind that had ruined the old kingdom. The people of the land respond by frightening the builders and bribing officials against them.

Here the book confuses many readers. In 4:6–23 it leaps ahead, past the reign of Darius, to later complaints under Ahasuerus and Artaxerxes about rebuilding the city walls. This is not a mistake: the writer gathers the opposition into one place to show its pattern across the generations, and then, at 4:24, returns to where he left off:

{{OWB:EZR.4.24.s1}}

— Ezra 4:24

For about sixteen years the foundation lay there with nothing on it. The prophet Haggai, who preached in that second year of Darius, put a finger on what had happened in the meantime. The people had stopped building God’s house and turned to their own: “Is it a time for you yourselves to live in paneled houses, while this house lies in ruins?”

Prophets, an archive, and a finished house

Chapter 5 begins again with the word of God. Haggai and Zechariah prophesy, and Zerubbabel and Jeshua set to work again. “The prophets of God were with them, helping them.” Zechariah had a word for Zerubbabel that has comforted builders ever since: “Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit.” And another for those tempted to look down on so small a temple: “Who has despised the day of small beginnings?”

When Tattenai, the Persian governor, asks who gave them permission, the elders answer with a short sermon: “We are the servants of the God of heaven and earth.” They tell him of their sin and exile, and appeal to the decree of Cyrus. Tattenai writes to the king, and the narrator adds a quiet sentence: “But the eye of their God was watching over the elders of the Jews.”

The search through the archives turns up Cyrus’s memorandum, not in Babylon but in the fortress at Ecbatana. Darius confirms it and orders the officials who came to question the work to pay for it from the royal taxes. The temple is finished, and the people celebrate:

{{OWB:EZR.6.16.s1}}

— Ezra 6:16

The first half of the book ends at Passover, the festival of the first deliverance from slavery. Notice who eats it: the returned exiles, “together with all who had separated themselves from the uncleanness of the nations of the land to join them in seeking the LORD, the God of Israel.” The door is not closed to outsiders who turn to the LORD. And the half closes on joy: “the LORD had filled them with joy.”

Ezra the scribe

Ezra arrives in chapter 7 with a genealogy that runs all the way back to “Aaron the chief priest”. He is a priest by birth, but the book is more interested in what he is by calling: “a scribe well versed in the law of Moses”. The verse that opens this introduction sums him up. Ezra had set his heart first to study the law, then to practise it, and only then to teach it. The order matters. He did not teach what he had not first lived.

Artaxerxes gives him a letter of remarkable scope: silver and gold, supplies for the temple, and authority to appoint judges and teach the law of God throughout the province. Ezra’s response is not to praise the king but to bless God, “who put it into the king’s heart to bring honor to the house of the LORD in Jerusalem.” And then he speaks for the first time in his own voice: “Because the hand of the LORD my God was on me, I took courage”.

A fast by the Ahava canal

Chapter 8 lists those who went up with Ezra and tells of their preparations. When Ezra reviews the company, he finds that no Levites have volunteered, and he will not set out until some are found. Then, before a journey of some nine hundred miles with a fortune in silver and gold, he faces a problem of his own making:

{{OWB:EZR.8.22.s1}} {{OWB:EZR.8.23.s1}}

— Ezra 8:22–23

It is a very human scene. Ezra has told the king that God protects those who seek him, and is ashamed to ask for soldiers as though he did not believe it. So the whole company fasts and prays. Ezra weighs out the treasure to the priests: “You are holy to the LORD, and the vessels are holy.” Nearly four months later they arrive, and everything is weighed again, “everything by number and by weight”. Faith in Ezra is not careless. It prays, and it also counts.

The crisis of the marriages

The last two chapters are the hardest in the book. The leaders come to Ezra to report that many of the people, priests and Levites among them, and the leaders first of all, have married women from the surrounding peoples. Why does this land on Ezra like a blow? The law had warned that such marriages would turn Israel’s heart to other gods, and that is what happened to Solomon: “his wives turned his heart to follow other gods.” The exile was the end of that road. A small community, barely re-established, could within a generation dissolve into the nations around it, and its faith with it.

Ezra tears his clothes, pulls hair from his head and beard, and sits down, stunned, until the evening offering. Then he prays one of the great prayers of confession in the Bible:

{{OWB:EZR.9.6.s1}}

— Ezra 9:6

Ezra has not done this himself, yet he says “our sins” and “our guilt”. He stands with his people, not above them. And in the middle of the confession he thanks God for grace already given:

{{OWB:EZR.9.9.s1}}

— Ezra 9:9

A crowd gathers and weeps with him, and a man named Shecaniah speaks up: “We have been unfaithful to our God by marrying foreign wives from the peoples of the land. But even so, there is still hope for Israel.” He proposes that the people make a covenant to send the foreign wives and their children away. The people gather in the open square in the ninth month, at the start of the winter rains, “trembling because of this matter and because of the heavy rain,” and agree. A commission works for three months, and the book ends with a list of the men involved, priests first.

Christian readers have found these chapters painful. Some read Ezra’s reform as a necessary, costly act of faithfulness in an emergency, when the very survival of the people of God was at stake; others think the book records it without holding it up as a pattern, noting that it reports dissent (10:15) and that its last verse dwells on the children. What the book does not do is make the issue one of race. The concern is worship: outsiders who turned to seek the LORD shared the Passover (6:21), and long before, Ruth the Moabite had become the great-grandmother of David. In the New Testament, Paul tells a believer married to an unbeliever not to seek a divorce (1 Corinthians 7:12–14). Whatever one concludes, the chapters show how seriously the returned exiles took the danger of losing God, and how much it cost them.

How to read Ezra

Ezra is short, ten chapters, and can be read in an evening. It helps to keep a rough timeline in mind: the return under Cyrus (chapters 1–2), the altar and foundation (3), the long opposition, gathered from several reigns (4), the completion of the temple under Darius (5–6), and then, after a gap of almost sixty years, Ezra’s journey and reforms under Artaxerxes (7–10).

Read it alongside Haggai and Zechariah, who preached while the temple was being built. And read it straight on into Nehemiah, where Ezra reappears, standing on a wooden platform to read the law aloud to the people.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition held that Ezra wrote it, and the first-person sections may well come from his own hand. Many scholars think a later editor gathered Ezra’s memoir, the official letters and the lists into the book we have. Either way, it is a book built from documents, and it reads like one.

As you read, watch for the refrain of God’s hand: on Ezra (7:6, 9, 28), on the travellers (8:18, 22, 31). Watch for the theme of joy, which runs from the shout at the foundation to the Passover. And notice how often the book turns to prayer and to Scripture at the moments of crisis. The returned exiles had no king, no army and a poor province. What they had was the word of God, and the book shows them learning to build their life on it.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and Aramaic and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few things are worth knowing before you begin.

Two languages. Most of Ezra is written in Hebrew, but two long sections, 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26, are in Aramaic, the common language of the Persian empire’s letters and records, which the exiles also spoke. These are the sections built around the official correspondence with the Persian kings. The translation is made from both languages as they stand.

“The LORD” and “the God of heaven.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation writes “the LORD”, following long Jewish and Christian custom. The Persian kings and officials speak of “the God of heaven”, a title that recurs throughout the book, and the Jews themselves use it when speaking to outsiders.

“Faithful love.” The Hebrew word hesed, God’s love that keeps faith with his covenant, appears in the song at the foundation, “his faithful love for Israel lasts forever,” and again when Ezra speaks of the favour God has shown through the kings of Persia (7:28; 9:9).

Places and measures. The Persian province west of the Euphrates, which included Judah, is called “Beyond the River”, a translation of its official name. Weights and sizes are given in modern equivalents, so the gifts for the temple are “61,000 darics of gold, about 3 tons of silver” and the temple Cyrus authorised is “about 90 feet” high.
