Ezra does not end with a celebration. It ends with a list of names and a sentence about children. The book that began with an emperor’s proclamation and a people going home closes in a cold square in the rain, with families coming apart. It is an honest ending, and it leaves the reader with a question the book does not try to answer.

The question the book leaves with you

Is this, then, the restoration the prophets promised? In one sense, yes. The people are home; the altar smokes again; the temple stands; the law is taught. God has kept his word through Jeremiah, and he has done it by moving the heart of a pagan king. Ezra wants us to see that, and to give thanks for it.

But in another sense, plainly not. The old men wept when they saw the new foundation. The province is poor, and it is still ruled from Susa. Ezra himself prays, “We are slaves.” And no sooner is the temple built and the law brought than the people slide back toward the very sin that had sent them into exile. The returned exiles have their house of God again. What they cannot do, it seems, is make themselves the holy people the house was built for.

So the question Ezra leaves is not whether God is faithful. He plainly is. The question is what it will take for his people to be faithful in return, and whether walls, laws and reforms can ever be enough.

Where the story goes next

The next part of the same story is Nehemiah, where Ezra reappears to read the law to the whole people and a governor rebuilds the walls of the city. But the prophets of these years were already looking further. Haggai, speaking to those who thought the new temple looked like nothing, promised more than its builders could see:

“The glory of this house in the future will be greater than its glory in the past, says the LORD of Armies.”

— Haggai 2:9

A generation or two later, Malachi announced: “suddenly the Lord you are seeking will come to his temple”. The New Testament says that he did. Zerubbabel, the son of David who laid the foundation, appears by name in the family line of Jesus at the start of Matthew’s Gospel. Jesus walked in the courts of this second temple, rebuilt and greatly enlarged by Herod, and spoke of a temple that would replace it: “Tear this temple down, and I will raise it up again within three days.” John explains that he meant his own body. In him, God “made his home among us”, as he had once done in the tent in the wilderness and the house in Jerusalem.

And the problem Ezra could not solve, a people who could not keep themselves faithful, is the one the prophets said God himself would solve. Ezekiel heard the promise: “I will give you a new heart, and I will place a new spirit within you.” The returned exiles had the law in their hands. The new covenant writes it on the heart.

Where to go from here

Ezra is not often preached on its own, but its scenes have fed the church’s prayer and preaching for a long time, and some of the best of that work is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On faith for the journey (Ezra 8). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon Heroic Faith (Ezra 8:22–23), on Ezra’s refusal to ask the king for soldiers.
  • On Ezra’s prayer (Ezra 9–10). E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, whose chapter “Ezra, the Praying Reformer” takes Ezra 10:1, the scribe weeping and confessing before the house of God, as its text.
  • On the return as a picture of renewal. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Ezra: Restoration from Backsliding” reads the faithful remnant’s return as a picture of every heart that seeks a closer walk with God.
  • On the whole story of the return. The Big Story, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “Coming Home” tells the story of Ezra 1, Nehemiah 8 and Esther 4 together.
  • On Ezra’s reading of the law (Nehemiah 8). Two sermons on what happened when Ezra opened the book before the people: Alexander Maclaren’s The Joy of the Lord and A. B. Simpson’s The Joy of the Lord, both on Nehemiah 8:10.
  • On the builders who came after. Bounds’s chapter “Nehemiah, the Praying Builder”, in the same book, and the chapter “Arrow Prayers and Long Fasts” in The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original, on Nehemiah’s prayers before the king.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Ezra in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
