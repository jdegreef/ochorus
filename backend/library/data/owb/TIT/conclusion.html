Titus ends where it has been heading all along: with people who have believed in God, devoting themselves to doing good. The letter has no patience for a faith that is only talk. Its opponents “claim to know God, but by what they do they deny him.” Its hope is a church on an island with a bad name, whose ordinary life, in homes and workplaces and before the authorities, makes the teaching about God our Savior attractive.

The question the letter leaves

Does what you believe show? Titus answers that it must, and it explains why it can. Good works are not the root of salvation; the letter could hardly say so more plainly: “not because of righteous things we had done, but because of his mercy.” They are its fruit. The grace that saves is the same grace that teaches. A people washed, made new and given the Spirit are being made into a people “eager to do what is good”, and they wait, while they work, for the appearing of their great God and Savior.

Where to go from here

A few places in this library to take the letter further:

  • On the grace that has appeared (2:11–15). Martin Luther’s Christmas Eve sermon The Appearing of the Grace of God, from his Church Postil, which opens by noting Paul’s charge in 1:9 that a pastor must both teach and refute.
  • On salvation by mercy, not works (3:4–7). John Wesley’s sermon Salvation by Faith, and Charles H. Spurgeon’s All of Grace.
  • On the new birth (3:5). J. C. Ryle’s sermon Are You Born Again? and Dwight L. Moody’s The New Birth, both on Jesus’s words to Nicodemus in John 3.
  • On elders and their work (1:5–9). The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians, which tells how the apostles appointed overseers and deacons in the churches they founded; John Chrysostom’s On the Priesthood, which quotes Paul’s instruction to Titus that an overseer must hold firmly to the trustworthy message; and Gregory the Great’s The Book of Pastoral Rule.

And read 1 and 2 Timothy, in this same edition, the two letters most like this one.

Every reference to Titus in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather what the great writers of the church have said about each passage. But the best next step may be to read the letter again, and to receive its last words as if they were written to you:
