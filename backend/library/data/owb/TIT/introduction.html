— Titus 2:11–14

Paul had left his co-worker Titus on the island of Crete with an unfinished job: to put the churches there in order and to appoint elders in every town. This short letter tells him how. It is brisk and practical, full of instructions for older men and younger women, slaves and rulers, and stern about troublemakers. But twice it stops to say why any of it matters, and those two passages, on the grace of God that has appeared, are among the richest summaries of the gospel anywhere in Paul’s letters.

Titus

Titus is never named in Acts, but Paul’s letters show how much he trusted him. He was a Greek, uncircumcised, and Paul took him to Jerusalem as living proof that Gentile believers need not become Jews (Galatians 2:1–3). Later he was Paul’s envoy to the troubled church at Corinth, where he probably carried a painful letter, brought back good news, and organized the collection for Jerusalem (2 Corinthians 7–8). “He is a partner of mine, working with me for you,” Paul wrote of him there (8:23). The last we hear, he has gone to Dalmatia, on the Adriatic coast (2 Timothy 4:10). Eusebius records a tradition that he was the first bishop of the churches in Crete.

Crete

Acts mentions Crete only as a coast Paul’s ship sailed along on its way to Rome (Acts 27). The letter implies a mission there that Acts does not record, perhaps after Paul’s first imprisonment, though the island had Jewish residents and Cretans were in Jerusalem at Pentecost (Acts 2:11). Like 1 and 2 Timothy, Titus is one of the Pastoral Letters, and scholars differ over whether Paul wrote it himself or a follower wrote it in his name; the introduction to 1 Timothy in this edition says more.

The churches faced teachers “of the circumcision group”, full of empty talk, Jewish myths and quarrels about the law, who were “ruining whole households” for gain. Paul quotes a line attributed to the Cretan poet Epimenides: “Cretans are always liars, evil brutes, lazy gluttons.” “What he said is true,” Paul adds, a blunt use of a famous insult. But the letter is not finally about Cretans. It is about what the grace of God can make of anyone.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapter 1: elders and opponents. The greeting, what an elder must be, and why false teachers must be silenced.
  • Chapter 2: teaching that fits. How believers of every age and station are to live, and the grace that teaches them.
  • Chapter 3: good citizens, made new. Life among outsiders, the kindness of God our Savior, and final instructions.

Elders in every town

An elder must be “blameless, the husband of one wife”, with a household in order. Paul then calls the same leader an overseer, and lists what he must not be, “arrogant or quick-tempered, not a drunkard or violent, not greedy for money”, and what he must be: hospitable, self-controlled, holy, and holding firmly to the trustworthy message, able to encourage with sound teaching and to correct those who oppose it. The list is close to the one in 1 Timothy 3. Character comes first; the ability to teach the truth follows.

Teaching that fits

“You must teach what fits with sound teaching,” Paul tells Titus, and he goes through the church: older men and older women, younger women and younger men, slaves. The instructions reflect the household order of the first-century world, including the call for wives to submit to their husbands. Christians differ on how such instructions apply in other times and cultures. What the letter makes plain is their purpose. Each group is to live so that “no one will speak evil of the word of God”, and slaves, the people with least status of all, are to “make the teaching about God our Savior attractive”.

The grace of God has appeared

Then comes the first of the letter’s two great summaries. Grace has appeared in Jesus Christ, bringing salvation to all people. And grace is not only a gift; it is a teacher. It trains believers to say no to ungodliness and to live “self-controlled, righteous and godly lives in this present age”, while they wait for “the blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ.” The Christian life stands between two appearings, the first in humility and the second in glory.

He saved us

In chapter 3 Paul reminds the believers what they once were, “foolish, disobedient and led astray”, and then turns to what God has done:

— Titus 3:4–7

Here, in a few lines, is nearly the whole gospel: the kindness and love of God, mercy not merit, new birth and the Spirit poured out, grace and the hope of eternal life. The Father, the Son and the Spirit are all named. Many see in “the washing of new birth” a reference to baptism; others a cleansing that baptism pictures. All agree that it is God’s work, “not because of righteous things we had done”.

Then the letter’s refrain: those who have believed must “devote themselves to doing good.” The phrase or one like it comes again and again in these three chapters. Grace that saves without good works produces a people “eager to do what is good.”

How to read Titus

Read it in one sitting; it takes about ten minutes. Then read 2:11–14 and 3:4–7 slowly, and notice how every practical instruction around them depends on them. Notice too how the title “Savior” passes back and forth between God and Jesus Christ (1:3–4; 2:10, 2:13; 3:4, 3:6).

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Titus are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ” (2:13). With one article governing both nouns, the Greek most naturally names one person, Jesus Christ, as God and Savior, as in 2 Peter 1:1. Some read two persons, God the Father and Christ. This translation follows the first reading.

“The washing of new birth” (3:5). Older Bibles say “regeneration”. The Greek means “becoming again”, and this translation uses the plainer English.

“The husband of one wife” (1:6). Literally “a one-woman man”, as in 1 Timothy 3:2. It may require faithfulness in marriage, rule out polygamy or rule out remarriage. Here the translation keeps the familiar phrase.
