— Micah 7:20

Micah ends by reaching back to the beginning. The prophet who watched Samaria fall and saw Jerusalem’s ruin coming closes his book with Jacob and Abraham, and with an oath God swore long before any of it. Whatever happens to the cities, the promise stands, because it rests not on the people’s faithfulness but on God’s.

The question the book leaves with you

Micah leaves two questions, and they belong together. The first is ours: “What shall I bring when I come before the LORD?” It is the question of anyone who has felt the weight of their own wrongdoing and wondered what could possibly make it right. Micah’s answer is not more sacrifice but a different life: to do justice, to love loyalty, and to walk humbly with your God. It sounds simple, and it is the work of a lifetime.

The second question is the prophet’s, and it is the answer to the first: “Who is a God like you?” Micah knew that no one keeps the requirements of 6:8 perfectly, not the rulers of Jerusalem and not the prophet himself. So the book ends not with what we bring to God but with what God does with what we bring him: he forgives, he passes over rebellion, he tramples our wrongdoings underfoot and hurls our sins into the sea. Justice, loyalty and humility are the shape of a life lived in response to a God like that.

Where the story goes next

Micah’s warning was heard. Hezekiah repented, and Jerusalem was spared when the Assyrians besieged it. A century later, when the city had forgotten again, Micah’s words saved Jeremiah’s life. And seven centuries later, his promise about Bethlehem was read aloud in Herod’s palace, while a child lay in a house in the village Micah had named, the ruler “whose origins are from of old”, who would shepherd his flock in the strength of the LORD and be his people’s peace.

Where to go from here

  • On what the LORD asks (Micah 6:6–8). Alexander Maclaren’s God’s Requirements and God’s Gift, which shows how deep the requirements of 6:8 go and how God’s gift in Christ meets our failure to keep them; and Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s The Good Way of Coming Before the Lord, which answers the question of 6:6 with the way of coming in Christ.
  • On the ruler from Bethlehem (Micah 5). Charles H. Spurgeon’s reading for February 27 in Evening by Evening, on the one whose origins are from of old (5:2), and his reading for August 19 in Morning by Morning, on the shepherd-king who stands firm in the strength of the LORD (5:4); and, for children, “The Promise of a Child” in Tell Me the Story – Book 3: Kings and Prophets, an Ochorus Original.
  • On the one who breaks through (Micah 2:13). Spurgeon’s reading for August 24 in Morning by Morning.
  • On hope in the dark (Micah 7:7–8). Spurgeon’s readings for January 31 and May 4 in The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith.
  • On sins thrown into the sea (Micah 7:19). Spurgeon’s reading for December 21 in The Cheque Book of the Bank of Faith; and Corrie ten Boom’s talk The Greatest of These Is Love, in which she tells how she learned to forgive and how God casts our confessed sins into the depths of the sea.
  • On the nations streaming to Zion (Micah 4). Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, in the chapter “The conversion of the Gentiles has been predicted by Micah”; and Augustine’s The City of God, book XVIII, chapter 30, on Micah’s mountain and his prophecy of Bethlehem.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Micah in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
