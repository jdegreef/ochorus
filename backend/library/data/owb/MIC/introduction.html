{{OWB:MIC.6.8.s1}}

— Micah 6:8

Micah was a countryman. He came from Moresheth, a small town in the low hills of Judah’s south-west, the farming country between the mountains and the coastal plain, and he spoke for the people of such places: farmers losing their fields to rich men from the city, families driven out of their homes, villagers who saw the soldiers of every invading army pass by first. His book is angry, and it is full of hope, and it contains what may be the best-known summary of what God wants from human beings in the whole Old Testament.

His name is a question. Micah is a shortened form of Micaiah, which means “Who is like the LORD?”, and the book ends by asking it of God himself: “Who is a God like you, who forgives wrongdoing and passes over rebellion for the remnant of his inheritance?”

The prophet of Moresheth

The first verse places Micah in the reigns of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah, kings of Judah, roughly from 740 to 700 BC. He was a younger contemporary of Isaiah, and the two prophets share a vision of the nations coming to Zion that appears in both their books almost word for word. Isaiah moved among kings in Jerusalem; Micah looked at Jerusalem from the countryside, and he did not like what he saw.

These were the decades of Assyria’s great advance. Samaria, the capital of the northern kingdom, fell in 722 BC, and in 701 the Assyrian king Sennacherib swept through Judah, taking its fortified towns one by one, among them Lachish, a few miles from Micah’s home, before laying siege to Jerusalem. Micah’s book opens with God coming down to tread on the heights of the earth, mountains melting beneath him, because of the sins of both capitals:

{{OWB:MIC.1.5.s1}}

— Micah 1:5

Then Micah walks the reader through the towns of his own district, a lament built out of puns that are mostly lost in translation. “Do not tell it in Gath”, he begins, echoing David’s lament for Saul; in Beth-leaphrah, “house of dust”, they are to roll in the dust; Lachish, the great fortress of the region, is told to harness the horses to the chariot. The disaster that struck Samaria “has reached the gate of my people, Jerusalem itself.”

Three times, from judgment to hope

The book is built in three movements, each beginning with a summons to listen: “Listen, all you peoples” (1:2), “Listen, you leaders of Jacob” (3:1), and “Listen to what the LORD says” (6:1). Each moves from judgment to hope. Chapters 1–2 end with God gathering a remnant like sheep in a pen; chapters 3–5 with a ruler from Bethlehem; chapters 6–7 with a God who throws sins into the sea.

Those who covet fields

Micah’s first target is the land-grabbers:

{{OWB:MIC.2.1.s1}} {{OWB:MIC.2.2.s1}}

— Micah 2:1–2

In Israel the land was God’s gift, divided among families as their inheritance, never to be sold away for good. To seize a family’s fields was to rob it of its place among God’s people. So Micah tells the grabbers that God will take their fields too, and that they will have no one left to stretch out a measuring line for them “in the assembly of the LORD.”

His hearers did not want to hear it. “Stop preaching,” they preach. “No one should preach about such things.” Micah answers with a bitter joke: if someone came along, full of wind and lies, and promised to preach about wine and beer, “he would be just the preacher for this people!”

Leaders who devour

Chapter 3 turns on the leaders of Jacob, the very people who should have known what justice is. Micah’s language is shocking on purpose. They “tear the skin off my people and the flesh off their bones”, chop them up “like meat for the pot”. He turns next to the prophets, who cry “Peace!” to anyone who feeds them, and to the priests and judges who work for pay. Yet they all lean on the LORD and say, “Is not the LORD among us? No disaster will come upon us.” Against them Micah sets his own calling:

{{OWB:MIC.3.8.s1}}

— Micah 3:8

And then comes a sentence no prophet before him had spoken so bluntly against Zion and its temple: “Zion will be plowed as a field, Jerusalem will be a mound of ruins”. It was remembered. A century later, when Jeremiah was on trial for his life for predicting the temple’s fall, some of the elders quoted Micah’s words in his defence. King Hezekiah, they said, had not put Micah to death; he had feared the LORD and sought his favour, and the LORD had relented.

The mountain of the LORD

Immediately after the ruined temple mount, Micah sees the same mountain raised high above all others, and the nations streaming to it:

{{OWB:MIC.4.2.s1}} {{OWB:MIC.4.3.s1}}

— Micah 4:2–3

The same vision stands at the beginning of Isaiah. Whether one prophet took it from the other, or both drew on a psalm sung in the temple, it was clearly treasured. Micah adds a line of his own, the countryman’s picture of peace: “Everyone will sit under their own vine and under their own fig tree, with no one to frighten them”. It is the opposite of chapter 2, where the powerful seized the fields of the poor. In God’s kingdom every family is home, and safe.

The rest of chapter 4 holds hope and pain together. God will gather “those who limp” and make them a strong nation. Daughter Zion must first writhe like a woman in labor and go to Babylon, but “There the LORD will redeem you”. It is striking that a prophet of Micah’s time, when Assyria was the great power, names Babylon as the place of exile and rescue. Some scholars think the line was added after the exile; others note that Babylon was already courting Hezekiah in Micah’s day, as the second book of Kings records.

Bethlehem

Then, out of a city under siege and a ruler struck on the cheek, comes one of the great promises of the Old Testament:

{{OWB:MIC.5.1.s1}}

— Micah 5:2

Bethlehem was David’s town, small and unimpressive. Micah says that God will begin again where he began with David, not in the palace but in the village. The coming ruler will “shepherd his flock by the power of the LORD”, his greatness will reach to the ends of the earth, “And he will be our peace.” Centuries later, when wise men from the east came to Jerusalem asking where the king of the Jews had been born, the chief priests and scribes turned to this verse and answered, “In Bethlehem in Judea”.

The phrase about his origins, “from of old, from ancient days”, has been read in two ways: as pointing back to the ancient line of David, or, as many Christians have read it, to a ruler whose goings forth are from eternity. The chapter goes on to promise that the remnant of Jacob will be among the nations “like dew sent by the LORD”, and that God will cut off from his people every horse and chariot, every idol and sorcery they trusted instead of him.

What does the LORD ask?

The last movement opens in a courtroom. God calls the mountains to be the jury and brings his case against his people. But the charge is a lament:

{{OWB:MIC.6.3.s1}} {{OWB:MIC.6.4.s1}}

— Micah 6:3–4

These words became the heart of the Reproaches, the ancient Good Friday chant in which Christ speaks from the cross: “My people, what have I done to you?”

The people answer with a frantic question. What can they bring? Burnt offerings, year-old calves, thousands of rams, ten thousand streams of olive oil, even their firstborn child? And the prophet answers with the verse at the head of this introduction. God has already told them. Justice, loyalty, and a humble walk with God: none of these can be bought, and all of them can be practised by anyone, in any village, every day.

“Who is a God like you?”

The final chapter begins in desolation. Micah looks for one upright person and finds none, “like one who comes when the summer fruit is gathered” and finds no grapes left. Trust has collapsed even in the home: “a person’s enemies are the people of their own household.” Jesus quoted those words when he warned his disciples of what following him might cost.

Then the prophet turns, and speaks for his people as one of them:

{{OWB:MIC.7.7.s1}} {{OWB:MIC.7.8.s1}}

— Micah 7:7–8

And the book closes with a hymn on Micah’s own name:

{{OWB:MIC.7.18.s1}} {{OWB:MIC.7.19.s1}}

— Micah 7:18–19

These verses have a long life in worship. Jews read them on the Day of Atonement, after the book of Jonah, and recite them at the New Year beside running water, in a custom called Tashlikh, “you will hurl”, after the verb in verse 19. Christians have found in them one of the clearest pictures of forgiveness: sins not merely covered but thrown into the depths of the sea.

How to read Micah

Read it in its three movements, 1–2, 3–5 and 6–7, and watch each turn from judgment to hope. Keep the setting in mind: a small country caught between empires, its villages plundered from outside and its poor exploited from within. Notice how concrete Micah’s sins are, fields and houses, bribes and false weights, and how concrete his hopes, a vine and a fig tree, a shepherd, a village. Some scholars think the book’s hopeful passages were added by later hands; others see the alternation of doom and hope as the prophet’s own design. Either way, the book as it stands insists that judgment is never God’s last word.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Most of Micah is poetry, and this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation writes “the LORD”, and where it joins that name to the word for “Lord”, “the Lord GOD” (1:2). The title older Bibles render “the LORD of hosts” is “the LORD of Armies” (4:4).

Loyalty and loyal love. The Hebrew hesed, which older Bibles render “mercy”, is “loyal love” in the closing hymn (“he delights in loyal love”) and “loyalty” in 6:8, where God asks us “to love loyalty”. In the same verse the Hebrew adam, “human being”, is rendered “mortal”: “He has told you, mortal, what is good.”

Numbering. The Hebrew counts the first verse of English chapter 5 as the last verse of chapter 4, so the promise about Bethlehem is 5:1 in Hebrew and 5:2 in English. This edition follows the English chapters, and the references in it follow the English numbering.
