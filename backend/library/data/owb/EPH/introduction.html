— Ephesians 2:8–10

Most of Paul’s letters were written into the middle of a quarrel. Someone at Corinth has asked a question, the Galatians are being talked into circumcision, the Thessalonians are grieving and confused. Ephesians is different. There is no crisis in it, no rival teacher named, no list of friends to greet. For three chapters Paul hardly argues at all. He blesses God, he prays, he marvels, and then he tells a church how to live in the light of what he has marvelled at.

Readers have loved it for exactly that reason. Samuel Taylor Coleridge called it one of the divinest compositions of man. It is the letter of the church: of Jews and Gentiles made into one people, of a body with Christ as its head, of a household and a temple and a bride. And it was written, the letter says three times, by a man in chains.

Ephesus and the churches of Asia

Ephesus was the chief city of the Roman province of Asia, on the western coast of what is now Turkey: a harbour, a great theatre, and the temple of Artemis, one of the wonders of the ancient world. Paul stayed there longer than anywhere else on his travels. Acts tells of two years of daily teaching in a lecture hall, so that “all the people of the province of Asia, Jews and Greeks alike, heard the word of the Lord” (Acts 19:10), and of a riot in the theatre, where the crowd cried out for about two hours, “Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!” When Paul later said goodbye to the Ephesian elders on the beach at Miletus, he reminded them that “for three years I did not stop, night or day, admonishing each one of you with tears” (Acts 20:31).

It is a surprise, then, that the letter sounds as though Paul has never met its readers. He has “heard of your faith” (1:15); he supposes they have heard how God entrusted him with his grace (3:2). And some of the oldest manuscripts do not have the words “in Ephesus” in the first verse at all. Many readers think the letter was meant for several churches in the province, Ephesus chief among them, and carried from one to the next. In the second century the teacher Marcion knew it as the letter “to the Laodiceans”; Tertullian, answering him, insisted that the church had always received it as written to the Ephesians. Paul tells the Colossians to read “the letter that comes from Laodicea” (Colossians 4:16), and some have wondered whether this is that letter.

Who wrote it

The letter names Paul as its author, twice (1:1; 3:1), and speaks of him as a prisoner (3:1; 4:1) and as “an ambassador, though in a chain” (6:20). It is to be carried by Tychicus (6:21), who also carries Colossians, and the two letters are so alike in shape and wording that they must be read together.

Here Christians differ. The church has always received the letter as Paul’s, probably written from prison in Rome around AD 60–62, and many scholars still hold that view. Many others think it was written after Paul’s death by a disciple who knew his letters well, pointing to its long, piled-up sentences, its vocabulary, its use of Colossians, and its picture of the church as one worldwide body rather than a congregation in a house. Readers on both sides agree on what matters most for reading it: this is Paul’s gospel, gathered up and looked at whole.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapters 1–3: what God has done. A long blessing of God for every gift in Christ; a prayer for eyes to see it; the dead made alive by grace; Jew and Gentile made one; the mystery revealed to Paul; and a second prayer, which ends in praise.
  • Chapters 4–6: how to live in it. The unity of the body and its gifts; the old self and the new; children of light; the Spirit’s fullness; wives and husbands, children and parents, slaves and masters; and the armour of God.

The hinge is the first word of chapter 4: “So”. Everything Paul asks of the church in the second half stands on what God has given it in the first.

Every blessing in Christ

In Greek, verses 3 to 14 of chapter 1 are a single breathless sentence. God “chose us in Christ” before the world was founded; he marked us out to be adopted as his own sons and daughters; in Christ we have redemption, the forgiveness of our wrongdoing; and his purpose is “to bring all things together under one head in Christ, things in heaven and things on earth.” The phrase “in Christ”, or “in him”, comes again and again. It is the key to the whole letter: every gift of God is found in union with his Son.

Then Paul turns to his Gentile readers and tells them they too have been included:

— Ephesians 1:13–14

Christians have long argued over what it means that God “chose us” and “marked us out beforehand”. In the tradition of Augustine and Calvin, this is God’s choice of particular people for salvation; in the Arminian and Wesleyan traditions, God chooses a people in Christ, and those who believe are found in him. Paul does not stop to settle it. His concern is praise: three times in the passage, everything happens so that God’s glory will be praised.

Dead, and made alive

Chapter 2 begins in the grave: “you were dead in your trespasses and sins.” Paul does not soften the picture; “we all once lived among them too”, he adds, and by nature deserved God’s wrath. Then come four of the best words in the letter: “But God is rich in mercy.” He made us alive together with Christ, raised us with him, and seated us with him in the heavenly places.

The sentence that opens this introduction follows. Salvation is by grace, through faith, and none of it comes from us; we are God’s own work. Yet it is not a salvation that leaves us idle. We were created in Christ Jesus “for good works, which God made ready in advance.” Grace is the root, and good works are the fruit, never the other way round.

The wall broken down

In the temple at Jerusalem a low stone barrier kept Gentiles out of the inner courts, with warning notices threatening death to any who went further. Paul knew it well: he was arrested in Jerusalem because some thought he had taken Trophimus, an Ephesian, past it (Acts 21:29). Now he tells Gentile believers that they, once “far away”, have been brought near:

{{OWB:EPH.2.14.s1}}

— Ephesians 2:14–16

This is the heart of the letter. The gospel does not only bring individuals to God; it makes enemies into “one new humanity”. In chapter 3 Paul calls it a mystery, hidden for ages and now revealed: that “the Gentiles are fellow heirs, members of the same body, and sharers together in the promise in Christ Jesus.” And he dares to say that through this mixed, unlikely church, God’s wisdom in all its rich variety is being shown to the powers in the heavenly places. The chapter ends in a prayer that the readers may grasp “the breadth and length and height and depth” of a love that goes beyond knowledge, and in praise:

— Ephesians 3:20–21

One body

Paul’s first request, now that he turns to their life together, is for unity: they are to “live in a way that is worthy of God’s call”, humble, gentle and patient, keeping the unity the Spirit gives.

— Ephesians 4:4–6

Unity is not sameness. The risen Christ has given gifts to his church, apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds and teachers, to equip all God’s holy people for the work of serving, so that the whole body grows up into its head, “as we speak the truth in love.” Then the old life is stripped off like clothing and the new self put on, and Paul gets very practical: tell the truth, “Do not let the sunset find you still angry”, work and share, speak words that build up, and forgive as God has forgiven you. “Once you were darkness itself; now, in the Lord, you are light.”

Households and the Spirit

The Spirit’s fullness, in 5:18–21, shows itself in songs, in thanksgiving, and in believers submitting to one another in the reverent fear of Christ. From there Paul speaks to each member of a household in turn: wives and husbands, children and fathers, slaves and masters. It is one of the fullest passages on marriage in the New Testament, and it ends by revealing what marriage was always a picture of: “I am speaking about Christ and the church.”

Christians read these verses in more than one way. Some hold that the husband’s headship, modelled on Christ’s self-giving love, is a lasting pattern for marriage; others hold that mutual submission (5:21) is the governing idea and that Paul is reshaping the household customs of his day from within. All agree on the startling demand made of husbands, to love their wives as Christ loved the church and handed himself over for her. The words to slaves and masters belong to a world where slavery was everywhere; it is worth noticing that Paul speaks to slaves as people answerable to Christ, and tells masters that their own Master is in heaven and shows no favouritism.

The armour of God

The letter ends on a battlefield. “Our fight is not with flesh and blood”, but with spiritual powers, and so believers are to clothe themselves in God’s complete armour: truth as a belt, righteousness as a breastplate, the gospel of peace for shoes, faith as a shield, salvation as a helmet, and the word of God as the Spirit’s sword. Much of it comes from Isaiah, where God himself puts on righteousness and the helmet of salvation (Isaiah 59:17). A prisoner chained to a Roman soldier hardly needed to look far for the picture. And the armour ends in prayer, for all God’s holy people and for Paul himself, that he may speak boldly.

How to read Ephesians

It takes about twenty minutes to read aloud, and it is worth hearing that way, as its first hearers did. Then read it slowly, a paragraph at a time.

Notice how often Paul prays, and pray his prayers (1:15–23; 3:14–21). Notice the little words “in Christ” and “in him”, and the three movements Watchman Nee summed up as sit, walk and stand: seated with Christ (2:6), living in a way worthy of God’s call (4:1), and standing your ground (6:13–14). Notice how the first half is full of what God has done and the second half of what we are to do, and do not read the commands of chapters 4 to 6 without the gifts of chapters 1 to 3 beneath them.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Ephesians are worth knowing about before you begin.

Long sentences. Paul’s Greek runs on for verse after verse in 1:3–14, 1:15–23 and 3:1–12. The translation keeps each passage together but breaks it into English sentences that can be read aloud.

“God’s holy people.” Where older Bibles say “saints”, this translation says “God’s holy people”, which is what the word means: not a few outstanding Christians, but all who belong to God.

“In Ephesus.” The translation keeps these words in 1:1, as almost all later manuscripts do and most Bibles print them, though some of the earliest leave them out.

“Let the Spirit fill you.” In 5:18 it presents the Spirit as the one who fills, and it prints verses 18 to 21 as one passage, so that the songs, the thanksgiving and the mutual submission are all seen to flow from the Spirit’s filling.

“Christ.” As elsewhere, the Open Word Bible lets a reader choose whether Christos reads “Christ” or “the Messiah”. This edition prints “Christ”, but in a letter about Gentiles being brought into Israel’s hope, it is worth remembering that the word means Israel’s anointed King.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Greek beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
