— Ephesians 6:23–24

Ephesians ends without a single name except that of the man who carries it. There are no greetings to friends, only peace, love and faith for the brothers and sisters, and grace for everyone who loves the Lord Jesus Christ with a love that will never perish. It is a fitting close for a letter about the whole church. Its last word reaches past Ephesus, past the province of Asia, to anyone, anywhere, who loves him.

The question the letter leaves

Ephesians asks what kind of people the church is. Its answer is very large. The church is Christ’s body and his fullness, the household of God, a temple still being built, a bride being made ready. Through it God’s wisdom is being shown to the powers of heaven.

And yet the church Paul knew was small and unremarkable: a few households of Jews and Gentiles who had once despised one another, slaves and masters sharing the same bread, people who still lied, lost their tempers and stole. The letter does not pretend otherwise. That is why it ends its great vision with plain commands. The question it leaves is whether the church will live as what God has made it. Will those who have been brought near keep the unity the Spirit gives? Will they speak the truth in love, and forgive as they have been forgiven? The wall has been broken down. It is all too easy to build it again.

Where the letter points

It points first to praise. Paul begins with “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ” and cannot finish his first prayer without breaking into worship. A reader who closes Ephesians with more ideas about the church, and no more love for its Lord, has missed the point.

And it points to a walk. Grace saves us apart from works, and then sets us walking in the good works God made ready in advance. The last picture in the letter is not of a church sitting still but of one standing its ground, armoured in God’s own strength and praying for those who carry the gospel.

Where to go from here

Ephesians has been a favourite of preachers and teachers in every age, and many of them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • The first readers. Ignatius of Antioch’s letter to the same church, written about fifty years later, in The Epistles of Ignatius and His Life’s Story, which calls them “associates in the mysteries with Paul”; and Tertullian’s Against Marcion, whose fifth book defends the title “to the Ephesians”.
  • On grace through faith (chapter 2). John Wesley’s Salvation by Faith (2:8), Charles H. Spurgeon’s All of Grace, J. C. Ryle’s Living or Dead? (2:1), and John Flavel’s The Method of Grace, with sermons on 1:6, 1:7 and 2:1.
  • On Paul’s prayers (chapters 1 and 3). Dwight L. Moody’s The Way to God, whose first chapter, “Love That Passeth Knowledge”, takes up 3:19; and Devoted to Prayer, with a chapter on each prayer, “That You May Know Him Better” and “Rooted Deep in Love”.
  • On the Spirit (chapters 4 and 5). Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s Grieve Not the Holy Spirit (4:30) and Charles G. Finney’s Be Filled with the Spirit (5:18).
  • On sitting, walking and standing. The Key Teachings of Watchman Nee, whose chapters “Sit”, “Walk” and “Stand” follow Nee’s reading of the whole letter; John Wesley’s sermon Of the Church (4:1–6), in his Sermons on Several Occasions; and Andrew Murray’s Abide in Christ, whose chapter “As Your Strength” is on 6:10.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Ephesians in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.

But the best next step may be to read Ephesians again, and to pray its two prayers for your own church, by name. And when you come to the end, hear its first command for the battle as spoken to you:
