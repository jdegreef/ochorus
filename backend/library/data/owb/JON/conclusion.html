— Jonah 4:11

Jonah ends with a question. God has the last word, but it is not a verdict; it is an invitation to see the world as he sees it. A hundred and twenty thousand people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and many animals as well. Should he not take pity on them?

The question the book leaves with you

The question is put to Jonah, and through him to every reader who has ever been glad to see God’s judgment fall on someone else. Jonah believed every word of Israel’s creed about God’s grace and compassion. He simply wanted it kept for his own people. The book asks whether we want it kept for ours.

It is easy to laugh at Jonah, and the book means us to. But it is harder to put down the book and not recognise him. Most of us have a Nineveh, a person or a people whose repentance we would not welcome, and a plant whose loss would grieve us more than their ruin. The book does not tell us whether Jonah ever came round. It leaves that part of the story for us to finish.

Where the story goes next

Nineveh’s repentance did not last forever. A century or so later the prophet Nahum announced its fall, and in 612 BC it was destroyed. But the book of Jonah had already shown that God’s mercy was not meant for Israel alone, and the rest of the Bible follows that thread to its end. Jesus said that the people of Nineveh would stand up at the judgment and condemn those who heard him and did not repent, “Yet here is something greater than Jonah.” He went down into death for three days and rose again, and then sent his disciples, as Jonah was sent, to call the nations to repentance.

Where to go from here

  • On running away (Jonah 1). Charles H. Spurgeon’s reading for February 25 in Evening by Evening, on Jonah fleeing to Tarshish.
  • On the prayer from the depths (Jonah 2). “A Prayer from the Depths”, the chapter on Jonah in The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original; and Spurgeon’s reading for February 26 in Morning by Morning, on the line that salvation belongs to the LORD.
  • On Nineveh’s repentance (Jonah 3). The chapter on repentance in R. A. Torrey’s What the Bible Teaches, which sets Jonah 3 beside Jesus’s words about the people of Nineveh; and Irenaeus of Lyons’s Against Heresies, whose third book reads Jonah’s rescue as a picture of God’s patience with humankind.
  • On Jonah’s anger (Jonah 4). Spurgeon’s reading for July 13 in Morning by Morning, on God’s question to Jonah; Thomas Watson’s The Lord’s Prayer, whose chapter on the prayer’s third petition sets Jonah’s anger over the plant against true submission to God; and A. B. Simpson’s sermon The Death of Self (Galatians 2:20), which holds up the sulking prophet as a warning against a ministry full of self.
  • On the sign of Jonah. Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, in the chapter “The same is taught from the history of Jonah”; Augustine’s The City of God, book XVIII, chapter 30; and Jerome’s letter to Paulinus (Letter LIII in The Letters of St. Jerome), on “Jonah, fairest of doves, whose shipwreck shews in a figure the passion of the Lord”.
  • For children. “Jonah Runs Away” and “Jonah and the Great City”, two chapters of Tell Me the Story – Book 3: Kings and Prophets, an Ochorus Original.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Jonah in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
