{{OWB:JON.4.2.s1}}

— Jonah 4:2

Jonah is the one prophetic book that is almost entirely a story. The other prophets give us their sermons; Jonah gives us one sentence of preaching, five words in Hebrew, and four chapters about the preacher. And the preacher is not a hero. He runs from God, sleeps through a storm, sulks when his message succeeds, and is still arguing with God when the book ends.

Everyone knows the fish. But the fish is not the heart of the story; it occupies three verses. The heart of the story is the verse above, in which a prophet complains to God that he is too merciful. Jonah knew exactly who God was. That was his problem.

Who was Jonah?

Jonah son of Amittai appears only once elsewhere in the Old Testament. The second book of Kings tells how King Jeroboam II of Israel restored Israel’s borders, in the eighth century before Christ, “by the word of the LORD the God of Israel, which he had spoken through his servant Jonah son of Amittai, the prophet from Gath-hepher.” Gath-hepher was a village in Galilee. So Jonah was a prophet of good news for Israel, a patriot whose word had come true in the expansion of his nation.

Nineveh was a great city of Assyria, the empire that would destroy the northern kingdom of Israel within a few decades and was already feared across the Near East for its cruelty. Its kings boasted in their inscriptions of the cities they burned and the prisoners they impaled and flayed. The prophet Nahum would later call Nineveh “the city of bloodshed”. To be sent there with a warning was to be sent to the enemy, and Jonah understood at once that a warning might mean a chance to repent.

Running away

The word of the LORD comes to Jonah: “Get up, go to Nineveh, that great city, and call out against it, because their wickedness has come up before me.” Nineveh lay hundreds of miles to the east. Jonah gets up and goes west, to the port of Joppa, and buys a passage to Tarshish, a city at the far end of the Mediterranean, the edge of the known world. The story is told with a steady downward motion. Jonah goes down to Joppa, down into the ship, down into the hold, where he lies down and falls into a deep sleep. Later he will go down to the roots of the mountains.

But “the LORD hurled a great wind onto the sea”, and the ship is about to break apart. The pagan sailors pray, each to his own god, and throw the cargo overboard, while the prophet of the LORD sleeps. It is the captain who has to wake him and tell him to pray. When the lot falls on Jonah, he makes a confession of faith that condemns him out of his own mouth:

{{OWB:JON.1.9.s1}}

— Jonah 1:9

He fears the God who made the sea, and he is running from him across it. The sailors understand the absurdity better than he does: “What is this that you have done?” Jonah tells them to throw him overboard. They try instead to row to shore; they pray not to be held guilty of his blood; only then do they hurl him into the sea, and it grows calm. Watch what happens to the sailors as the chapter goes on. At first they are “terrified”; then “seized with great fear”; at the end they are “filled with great fear of the LORD”, offering sacrifices and making vows. Jonah has not preached to them at all, and the first converts in his book are already on their knees.

From deep in Sheol

{{OWB:JON.2.1.s1}}

— Jonah 1:17

The fish is not a punishment but a rescue. Jonah, sinking to his death, is swallowed and kept alive, and from inside the fish he prays. His prayer, in chapter 2, is a psalm of thanksgiving, full of the language of the Psalms: the waters closing over him, the seaweed wrapped around his head, the bars of the earth closing behind him, and then, “But you brought my life up from the pit, LORD my God.” It ends with a line that sums up the whole book:

{{OWB:JON.2.10.s1}}

— Jonah 2:9

There is a faint irony in the prayer. Jonah says that “Those who devote themselves to useless idols abandon their loyal love”, while above him the pagan sailors he left behind are worshipping the LORD. But the prayer is real, and God hears it. He speaks to the fish, and the fish obeys at once, as everything in this book obeys God except his prophet.

“Forty more days”

Then comes the most important phrase in the book: “The word of the LORD came to Jonah a second time”. God gives second chances to prophets, and the rest of the story will show that he gives them to cities too.

This time Jonah goes. Nineveh is “an exceedingly great city, a three days’ walk across”, and he walks one day into it and delivers his message: “Forty more days, and Nineveh will be overthrown!” He gives no reason and offers no hope. And the impossible happens:

{{OWB:JON.3.5.s1}}

— Jonah 3:5

The king himself rises from his throne, lays aside his robe and sits in ashes. He decrees a fast for people and animals alike, and calls everyone to “turn from their evil ways and from the violence they do.” His reasoning is humble and uncertain: “Who knows? God may yet turn and change his mind”. He has no promise to stand on. He simply throws his city on the mercy of a God he does not know. And God sees, and relents, and does not do it.

“Are you right to be angry?”

Any other prophet would have rejoiced. Jonah is furious. Now we learn why he ran in the first place: he knew that God is “a God of grace and tender compassion, slow to grow angry, rich in faithful love, and one who relents from sending disaster.” These are the words the LORD himself proclaimed to Moses on Mount Sinai, Israel’s most treasured confession of who God is. Jonah throws them back at God as an accusation. He would rather die than live in a world where God is that merciful to Nineveh.

God answers with a question, “Are you right to be angry?”, and then with an object lesson. Jonah sits east of the city to see what will happen, and God appoints a leafy plant to shade him, and Jonah is overjoyed. Then God appoints a worm to kill the plant and a scorching east wind, and Jonah begs to die again. “Are you right to be angry about the plant?” “I am right to be angry,” he says, “angry enough to die!”

And the book ends with God’s reply, the last question in it:

{{OWB:JON.4.10.s1}} {{OWB:JON.4.11.s1}}

— Jonah 4:10–11

There is no answer from Jonah. The story stops there, with the question hanging in the air, and that is deliberate. The reader is left in Jonah’s place, under the withered plant, looking at the city, and must answer for himself.

A book about mercy

Jonah is a book about the reach of God’s mercy, and about how much we resent it when it reaches people we think do not deserve it. Every pagan in the book, the sailors, the king, the people, even the animals in their sackcloth, turns toward God more readily than his prophet does. Jonah cared more for a plant that gave him shade than for a city of a hundred and twenty thousand people. God cares even for the animals.

It is also a book about grace to Jonah himself. God does not abandon his runaway prophet. He sends a storm to stop him, a fish to save him, a second call to recommission him, a plant to shade him, and patient questions to reason with him. The same compassion that Jonah resents for Nineveh is shown to him on every page.

The sign of Jonah

When some of his hearers asked Jesus for a sign, he told them that the only sign they would be given was “the sign of Jonah the prophet”. In Matthew he explains: “Jonah spent three days and three nights inside the great sea creature, and in the same way the Son of Man will spend three days and three nights deep in the earth.” And he adds that the people of Nineveh will stand up at the judgment and condemn his own generation, “They turned from their sins when Jonah preached. Yet here is something greater than Jonah.”

The early church took up the image gladly. Jonah thrown up from the sea is one of the most common pictures in the art of the Roman catacombs, painted beside the graves of Christians as a sign of resurrection.

How to read Jonah

Read it as a story, in one sitting, and enjoy its craft. Words are repeated with purpose: “great” (the great city, the great wind, the great storm, the great fish, the great fear, and the “great wrong” Jonah feels); “hurled”, used of the wind, the cargo and Jonah; “appointed”, used of the fish, the plant, the worm and the east wind, all of them obeying their Maker. Notice the questions, especially God’s.

Christians differ over what kind of story this is. Many have read it as a straightforward account of events, pointing to the historical prophet of 2 Kings and to Jesus’s words about Jonah and the Ninevites. Others read it as an inspired parable or teaching story built around a real prophet, pointing to its artful shape, its open ending and its gentle humour. Both readings take it as the word of God, and both hear its question.

In the synagogue the book is read every year on the afternoon of the Day of Atonement, the most solemn day of the Jewish year: a story of repentance, and of a God who relents.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Most of Jonah is narrative and is printed as prose; Jonah’s prayer in chapter 2 is poetry, and this edition prints each of its lines on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation writes “the LORD”; where it uses the ordinary word for God, “God”. Jonah moves between them with care. The sailors at first cry to their gods and then to the LORD; the Ninevites believe “God”, the one name they could know.

Loyal love. The Hebrew hesed is translated “loyal love” in Jonah’s prayer (2:8) and “faithful love” in 4:2, where Jonah repeats the words of Exodus 34:6.

The fish and the plant. The Hebrew says only “a great fish”, and so does this translation. The plant God appoints in chapter 4 is “a leafy plant”; its exact kind is uncertain, and older Bibles called it a gourd.

Numbering. The Hebrew begins chapter 2 one verse earlier than English Bibles, with the fish. This edition follows the English chapters, and the references in it follow the English numbering.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
