The letter ends with one word of command: guard. Timothy has been given something that is not his own, the gospel, the “sound teaching” that agrees with “the glorious gospel of the blessed God”, and his task is to keep it safe and hand it on whole. Everything else in the letter, the prayers, the qualifications for leaders, the care of widows, the warnings about money, serves that one charge.

The question the letter leaves

What does a healthy church look like? 1 Timothy answers in an unexpected way. It says little about programmes or growth or eloquence. It talks about character: leaders who are self-controlled, gentle and faithful at home; members who pray for everyone, care for their own families, honour the old and protect the vulnerable; a community content with food and clothing and generous with everything else. And it talks about truth: teaching that is sound, that is, healthy, because it leads to “love that comes from a pure heart, a good conscience and a faith without pretense.”

That aim, stated in the first chapter, is the test of every other instruction. Teaching that produces quarrels, pride and greed has missed it, however clever. Teaching that produces love has not.

And at the centre of it all stands not a rule but a person: Christ Jesus, the one mediator, who “came into the world to save sinners”. A church that forgets the mercy shown to the worst of them has forgotten why it exists.

Where to go from here

For nearly two thousand years pastors have gone back to this letter to learn their work, and many of the best of them are in this library:

  • On mercy to the worst of sinners (1:15). John Bunyan’s Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners, whose title comes from this verse.
  • On prayer for all and the one mediator (2:1–7). John Calvin’s sermon God Will Have All Men to Be Saved, on 2:3–5.
  • On the mystery of our faith (3:16). Calvin’s sermon The Mystery of Godliness.
  • On the work of a pastor (3:1–7; 4:16; 5:22). Gregory the Great’s The Book of Pastoral Rule, which weighs Paul’s words on those who want to be overseers; John Chrysostom’s On the Priesthood; Richard Baxter’s The Reformed Pastor, on Paul’s charge to the elders of Ephesus (Acts 20:28); and Charles H. Spurgeon’s Lectures to My Students, whose lecture “The Minister’s Self-Watch” takes 4:16 as its text.
  • On contentment and wealth (6:6–19). John Henry Newman’s sermon Temporal Advantages (6:7–8); Chrysostom’s Homily II on the Statues, preached in a terrified Antioch on the charge to the rich (6:17); Thomas Watson’s The Art of Divine Contentment; and Clement of Alexandria’s Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?
  • The church of the next generation. The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp, in which Polycarp echoes this letter on the love of money, and Ignatius of Antioch’s letter to the church at Ephesus, in The Epistles of Ignatius and His Life’s Story.

Then read 2 Timothy, in this same edition: Paul’s last letter to the same young man, written from prison.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 1 Timothy in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.
