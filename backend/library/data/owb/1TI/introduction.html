{{OWB:1TI.1.15.s1}}

— 1 Timothy 1:15–16

Most of Paul’s letters were written to churches. This one is written to a man: Timothy, his younger colleague, whom he had left behind in Ephesus to deal with a church in trouble. Teachers there were spreading “a different doctrine”, arguing over myths and family trees, forbidding marriage and certain foods. Timothy was young, perhaps timid, and often unwell. Paul writes to tell him how people ought to live “in God’s household, which is the church of the living God, the pillar and foundation of the truth.”

Together with 2 Timothy and Titus, this letter has been called one of the Pastoral Letters since the eighteenth century, because all three concern the care of churches and those who lead them. They have shaped Christian thinking about ministry more than any other part of the New Testament.

Timothy

Timothy came from Lystra, in what is now central Turkey. His mother was a Jewish believer and his father a Greek (Acts 16:1). Paul took him on as a companion on his second journey, and from then on Timothy appears everywhere: in Thessalonica, Corinth, Philippi and Ephesus, and as co-sender of six of Paul’s letters. Paul trusted him like no one else. “I have no one else of the same heart,” he told the Philippians (2:20). In this letter he calls him “my true son in the faith”.

Ephesus was the greatest city of the province of Asia, home of the vast temple of Artemis. Paul had spent more than two years there, and the church had spread through the region (Acts 19:10). Before he left, he had warned its elders that savage wolves would come in among them, and that some of their own would distort the truth (Acts 20:29–30). That is the situation this letter addresses.

Who wrote the Pastoral Letters?

The letter opens “From Paul”, and for most of Christian history that settled the question. Since the nineteenth century many scholars have argued that the Pastoral Letters were written after Paul’s death by a follower writing in his name. They point to a vocabulary different from Paul’s other letters, a more settled church order, and travels that are hard to fit into Acts.

Others, including many scholars today, hold that Paul wrote them, perhaps with more freedom given to a secretary, late in his life. They note that the letters are full of personal detail, and that the church historian Eusebius records a tradition that Paul was released from his first imprisonment in Rome, worked again in the east, and was imprisoned a second time. On that view 1 Timothy was written in the early or middle 60s. Readers on both sides agree that these letters carry the voice of Paul’s teaching to the next generation, and the church has read them as Scripture from the second century on.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapter 1: sound teaching and the grace that saved Paul. A charge to stop the false teachers, and Paul’s own story.
  • Chapter 2: prayer and worship. Prayers for everyone, and how men and women are to conduct themselves when the church gathers.
  • Chapter 3: leaders. What overseers and deacons must be, and a hymn to Christ.
  • Chapter 4: Timothy’s own life and teaching. False asceticism refused, and godliness trained.
  • Chapters 5–6: the household of God. Older and younger, widows, elders, slaves and masters, the danger of money, and a closing charge.

The worst of sinners

Before he gives Timothy a single instruction about the church, Paul tells his own story. He was once a man who spoke against Christ and persecuted his people. He was shown mercy. And he sums up the whole gospel in a saying he calls one “you can rely on”: “Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, and I am the worst of them.” If Christ could be so patient with Paul, no one is beyond his reach.

The verse has a long history of its own. In Cambridge, a few years after 1516, a young scholar named Thomas Bilney, reading Erasmus’s new Greek and Latin New Testament, came upon these words and found, he later wrote, that his bruised bones leapt for joy. Through Bilney, Hugh Latimer was won to the Reformation, and both men later died at the stake. John Bunyan took his own title, Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners, from the same verse in the older English Bible.

Prayer for everyone

The first thing Paul asks of the church is prayer: for everyone, including kings and all who hold high office. The reason is God himself, who “wants everyone to be saved and to come to know the truth”:

{{OWB:1TI.2.5.s1}}

— 1 Timothy 2:5–6

Christians have debated how to hold together God’s will that all be saved and his choice of some; Calvin, preaching on this passage, read “everyone” as all kinds and conditions of people, while Wesley and many others read it as every person without exception. Both agreed on what follows from it: the church must pray for all and preach to all.

Men and women in worship

Paul asks men to pray without anger or quarrelling, and women to adorn themselves not with braided hair and gold but with good works. Then come two verses that have been argued over more than almost any in the New Testament: “A woman should learn quietly and with full submission. I do not allow a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; she is to remain quiet.” Paul gives a reason from Genesis, from the order of creation and the deception of Eve.

Christians read these words in different ways. Many churches, Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant, take them as a lasting rule that the ordained office of teaching and governing the church belongs to men. Others, noting that the rare Greek verb for “exercise authority” may mean to domineer, and that false teachers in Ephesus had made inroads among some women (5:13–15; 2 Timothy 3:6–7), read them as Paul’s answer to a particular situation, and point to women such as Priscilla, Phoebe and Junia who taught and served in his churches. Readers on every side should notice that the first command is that a woman should learn, a striking thing to say in the ancient world. The puzzling promise that “she will be saved through childbearing” (2:15) has no agreed explanation.

Overseers and deacons

Chapter 3 lists what a church’s leaders must be. The overseer (in Greek episkopos, from which “bishop” comes) must be “faithful to his one wife, clear-headed, self-controlled, respected, welcoming to guests, able to teach”. Almost every quality is one any Christian should have; what stands out is how little is said about talent and how much about character, home and reputation. Deacons must be tested first, and “the women likewise” must be worthy of respect, either deacons’ wives or women who served as deacons, as Phoebe did (Romans 16:1).

Then, at the centre of the letter, Paul breaks into what looks like an early Christian hymn:

{{OWB:1TI.3.16.s1}}

— 1 Timothy 3:16

The church’s order matters because the church holds this truth. Its leaders are chosen with care because of who it is they serve.

Train yourself in godliness

The false teachers forbade marriage and certain foods. Paul answers that “everything God created is good, and nothing should be refused if it is received with thanks.” The discipline he wants is of another kind: “Train yourself in godliness.” The word godliness runs through the letter. It means a life shaped through and through by reverence for God, and it has to be practised, as an athlete trains. Then he speaks to Timothy himself:

{{OWB:1TI.4.12.s1}}

— 1 Timothy 4:12

Read Scripture to the church, preach, teach, use your gift, and “keep a close watch on yourself and on your teaching.”

The household of God

Chapter 5 treats the church as a family: older men as fathers, younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, younger women as sisters. It gives careful rules for supporting widows who have no one else, urges families to care for their own, and tells Timothy to honour elders who lead well, pay those who preach and teach, and never accept an accusation against one without witnesses. There are instructions for slaves, written within an institution the letter does not directly challenge, which remind believing slaves that their believing masters are “brothers and sisters” and “dear to them”.

Godliness and gain

Some of the false teachers treated religion as a way to make money. Paul turns the idea around:

{{OWB:1TI.6.6.s1}} {{OWB:1TI.6.7.s1}}

— 1 Timothy 6:6–7

Then come the words so often misquoted. Not money but “the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil”, and those eager for it have “pierced themselves with many griefs”. To the rich he gives not condemnation but a charge: to put their hope in God, “who richly gives us everything to enjoy”, to be rich in good deeds, and so to “take hold of the life that is truly life.” The letter ends as it began, with Timothy told to guard what has been entrusted to him.

How to read 1 Timothy

Read it as a letter between friends, an older man to a younger one he loves, not as a church manual. Notice the outbursts of praise (1:17; 3:16; 6:15–16): even its instructions are written by someone overwhelmed by God. Notice the five sayings in these letters introduced as trustworthy (1:15; 3:1; 4:9; 2 Timothy 2:11; Titus 3:8), short sayings the churches already knew. And on the hardest passages, read slowly, read the whole letter around them, and read with charity toward those who understand them differently.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 1 Timothy are worth knowing about before you begin.

“He was revealed in a human body” (3:16). Many later manuscripts read “God was revealed in the flesh”, the wording of older English Bibles. The earliest manuscripts read “who”, which in early Greek writing differs from “God” by two small strokes. This translation follows them, and begins the line “He”. The line still speaks of Christ.

“Faithful to his one wife” (3:2, 3:12). The Greek is literally “a one-woman man”. It may mean faithful in marriage, married only once, or not having more than one wife. The translation takes the first sense.

“To exercise authority” (2:12). The Greek verb occurs only here in the New Testament. The translation gives its usual sense, while its notes record that it can also mean to domineer.

“A root of all kinds of evil” (6:10). There is no article before “root” in the Greek, and the translation does not supply one: Paul is not saying every evil comes from the love of money, but that every kind can grow from it.
