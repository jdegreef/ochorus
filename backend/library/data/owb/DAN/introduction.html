Daniel is two books in one. The first half is made of stories that children learn before they can read: four young men who will not eat the king’s food, three friends in a blazing furnace, a hand writing on a palace wall, an old man thrown to the lions. The second half is made of visions that have puzzled scholars for two thousand years: four beasts rising out of the sea, a ram and a goat, seventy sevens, and a long and detailed account of kings of the north and the south. Both halves say the same thing. Empires rise and fall, and some of them set themselves against God and his people, but “the Most High rules over human kingdoms”, and his kingdom will never be destroyed.

Its hero is a young man of Judah taken to Babylon, who spends his whole life serving pagan kings without ever ceasing to serve his God. Daniel’s name means “God is my judge”, and the book shows a man who lived as if that were true, in a palace where every other judge was hostile.

Two halves, two languages

The book divides into two halves of six chapters each:

  • Chapters 1–6: stories of Daniel and his friends at the courts of Babylon and Persia, told mostly in the third person.
  • Chapters 7–12: four visions given to Daniel, told mostly in his own voice, reaching from his own day to “the time of the end”.

It also divides another way. Daniel is written in two languages. It begins in Hebrew, switches in the middle of 2:4, when the king’s advisers speak to him “in Aramaic”, the common language of the empire, and stays in Aramaic until the end of chapter 7, before returning to Hebrew for the rest. The Aramaic chapters form a pattern of their own: a dream of four kingdoms (2) and a vision of four kingdoms (7) on the outside; two rescues of the faithful, from the furnace (3) and from the lions (6); and at the centre, two proud kings judged (4 and 5). The book is more carefully built than it first looks.

In the Hebrew Bible, Daniel stands among the Writings, with Psalms, Esther and Ezra, rather than among the Prophets. In Christian Bibles, following the ancient Greek translation, it comes after Ezekiel as the last of the major prophets. The Greek also included additional passages, the prayer of the three young men in the furnace and the stories of Susanna and of Bel and the Dragon, which Catholic and Orthodox Bibles keep; this translation, made from the Hebrew and Aramaic, does not include them.

Faithful in Babylon (Daniel 1)

The book opens in 605 BC, “in the third year of Jehoiakim’s reign”, when Nebuchadnezzar comes to Jerusalem and carries off young men of the royal family and the nobility to be trained for his service. Among them are Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah, renamed Belteshazzar, Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego. They are given a Babylonian education, Babylonian names, and food from the king’s table.

They accept the education and the names. They draw the line at the food: “Daniel resolved not to make himself unclean with the royal food or with the wine the king drank.” He does not rebel or make a scene; he proposes a ten-day test, vegetables and water, and the four come out healthier than everyone else. At the end of their training the king finds them “ten times wiser than all the magicians and enchanters in his entire kingdom.” The chapter is a pattern for living faithfully in a culture that is not your own: learn its language, serve it well, and know where you will not bend.

Its last verse quietly spans the whole book: “Daniel remained there until the first year of King Cyrus.” He outlived the empire that took him.

The statue and the stone (Daniel 2)

Nebuchadnezzar has a dream that troubles him, and demands that his wise men tell him not only what it means but what it was. They protest that no one on earth could do such a thing. Daniel and his friends pray, the mystery is revealed to Daniel in a vision at night, and his first response is praise:

— Daniel 2:20–21

The king had seen a great statue: a head of gold, chest and arms of silver, belly and thighs of bronze, legs of iron, and feet partly of iron and partly of clay. A stone “cut out, though no human hand touched it” struck its feet, and the whole statue crumbled like chaff and blew away, while the stone “grew into a great mountain and filled the whole earth.” Daniel explains: “You are the head of gold.” Other kingdoms will follow, each lesser than the last, and then the God of heaven will set up “a kingdom that will never be destroyed.”

The furnace (Daniel 3)

Nebuchadnezzar sets up a gold statue on the plain of Dura and commands everyone to bow down to it when the music plays, on pain of the furnace. Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego will not. Brought before the furious king, they give one of the great answers of faith in the Bible:

{{OWB:DAN.3.17.s1}} {{OWB:DAN.3.18.s1}}

— Daniel 3:17–18

“But even if he does not.” Their obedience does not depend on rescue. They are thrown into a furnace so hot that it kills the men who throw them in. And the king, looking in, jumps to his feet: “Look! I see four men, untied, walking around in the fire, and they are unharmed! And the fourth looks like a son of the gods.” The fire has burned only their ropes. Nebuchadnezzar can only call the fourth figure an angel; Christian readers from the early centuries on have wondered whether it was the Son of God himself, walking with his people in the fire.

Two proud kings (Daniel 4–5)

Chapter 4 is told by Nebuchadnezzar himself, as a letter to all peoples. He dreams of a great tree cut down, and Daniel warns him that it is a picture of himself, and pleads with him to break with his sins “by showing mercy to the oppressed”. A year later, walking on his palace roof, the king boasts, “Is this not the great Babylon that I have built as a royal capital by my own mighty power and for the glory of my majesty?” At that moment his mind is taken from him, and he lives like an animal in the fields until he looks up to heaven and his sanity returns. The king who would be worshipped ends his letter in worship: “Now I, Nebuchadnezzar, praise and exalt and honor the King of heaven… and he can bring down those who live in pride.”

Belshazzar does not learn the lesson. At a great feast he drinks from the gold and silver vessels taken from the temple in Jerusalem, and praises the gods of gold and silver. The fingers of a human hand appear and write on the wall. Daniel, now an old man, is brought in, and reads the words: MENE, MENE, TEKEL and PARSIN. God has numbered the days of his kingdom; “you have been weighed on the scales and found too light”; his kingdom is given to the Medes and Persians. “That very night Belshazzar, king of the Chaldeans, was killed.” Babylon fell to Cyrus in 539 BC.

Belshazzar was long unknown outside the Bible, until inscriptions were found naming him as the eldest son of Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon, who left him to rule in the city while he himself was away for years. That is why Belshazzar could offer Daniel no more than to be “the third ruler in the kingdom”.

The lions’ den (Daniel 6)

Under the new ruler, Darius the Mede, Daniel rises so high that the other officials look for a charge against him and find none, except in “the law of his God”. They persuade the king to sign a decree that for thirty days no one may pray to any god or human being except the king. Daniel’s response is simply to go on as before:

{{OWB:DAN.6.11.s1}}

— Daniel 6:10

He prays toward Jerusalem, as Solomon had asked exiles to do (1 Kings 8:48), though the city and its temple lie in ruins. He is thrown to the lions, and the king, who cannot undo his own law, spends the night without food or sleep. At dawn he runs to the den and calls out in anguish, and hears the old man answer: “My God sent his angel and shut the lions’ mouths.” Darius ends the story, as Nebuchadnezzar ended his, by praising “the living God, who endures forever”, whose “kingdom will never be destroyed.”

Who Darius the Mede was is still discussed. He is not named outside the Bible, and scholars have identified him with Cyrus himself, with a governor under Cyrus, or with others; some think the book uses a traditional figure. What the story asks of its reader is clear either way.

The beasts and the son of man (Daniel 7)

The second half of the book turns from stories to visions. In the first year of Belshazzar, Daniel dreams of four beasts rising from a stormy sea: a lion with eagle’s wings, a bear with ribs in its mouth, a four-headed leopard, and a fourth beast “terrifying, frightening and very strong”, with iron teeth and ten horns, among which a small horn grows up “and a mouth that spoke boastfully.” Then the scene changes to a courtroom in heaven. Thrones are set, “and the Ancient of Days was seated”, his clothes white as snow, his throne a flame of fire. The beast is destroyed. And then:

{{OWB:DAN.7.13.s1}} {{OWB:DAN.7.14.s1}}

— Daniel 7:13–14

The beasts rise from the sea, the Bible’s picture of chaos; the one like a son of man comes with the clouds of heaven, from God. Human empires are bestial; God’s kingdom is truly human. The angel explains that the kingdom will be given to “the holy ones of the Most High”, who will possess it forever.

No passage in Daniel matters more to the New Testament. “The Son of Man” was the name Jesus used most often for himself, and at his trial, asked by the high priest if he was the Messiah, he answered: “I am. And you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of the Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven” (Mark 14:62). The high priest tore his clothes.

Rams, goats and seventy sevens (Daniel 8–12)

The remaining visions narrow their focus. In chapter 8 a two-horned ram is trampled by a goat with a great horn; the angel Gabriel explains that they are the kings of Media and Persia and the king of Greece, and from the goat comes a horn that grows great, stops the daily offering and throws down the sanctuary. In chapters 10–12 a heavenly messenger describes, in remarkable detail, the wars of the kings of the north and the south after the Greek empire divided. Many details fit closely the history of the second century BC, and of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who outlawed the Jewish faith and set up an altar to Zeus in the temple in 167 BC, “the abomination that brings ruin.”

Between these comes Daniel’s great prayer in chapter 9. Reading Jeremiah, he understands that the desolation of Jerusalem is to last seventy years, and the years are nearly done. He does not wait. He fasts and prays in sackcloth and ashes, confessing his people’s sin as his own: “We have sinned and done wrong.” He asks not on the basis of their righteousness “but because of your great mercy”. Gabriel comes “about the hour of the evening offering”, though no offering had been made for decades, and tells him that “Seventy sevens have been decreed”, to put an end to sin and “to bring in everlasting righteousness”, with “an anointed one” who “will be cut off”.

The book ends with one of the clearest promises of resurrection in the Old Testament: “Many who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, these to everlasting life, and those to shame and everlasting contempt.” And it ends with Daniel himself, who does not understand all he has seen, told to “go your way until the end”, with the promise that he will rise.

When was Daniel written?

Christians disagree about the date of the book, and the question is bound up with how the visions are read. The traditional view, held by Jewish and Christian readers for most of history and by many today, is that the book comes from Daniel himself in the sixth century BC, and that its detailed visions are true predictive prophecy. Many scholars date the final form of the book to the 160s BC, during the persecution under Antiochus, holding that older stories of Daniel were joined to visions written to encourage the faithful in that crisis. Others take mediating positions. Readers on every side agree that the book was written to strengthen God’s people under hostile powers, and that it has done so ever since.

The visions themselves have also been read in different ways. The four kingdoms of chapters 2 and 7 have traditionally been taken as Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece and Rome, with God’s kingdom arriving in Christ; many modern interpreters count Babylon, Media, Persia and Greece. The seventy sevens of chapter 9 have been calculated to the coming and death of the Messiah, to the time of Antiochus, and to a final period at the end of history. Readers should hold these schemes humbly. The book itself says that Daniel was “dismayed by the vision; it was beyond understanding.”

How to read Daniel

Read the stories first, and read them as stories: they are told with great skill and humour, and they teach courage by showing it. Notice that in every one God’s people are under pressure from a power far greater than they are, and that God’s rescue sometimes comes and sometimes is only trusted for: “even if he does not.”

Then read the visions as the stories told again in another key. The kings who demand worship become beasts; the furnace and the den become persecution on a vast scale; and the end is the same, a kingdom given by God that will never be destroyed. Do not let the puzzles of the numbers keep you from the plain message: history is in God’s hands, the arrogant will fall, and those who stay faithful will “shine like the brightness of the sky”.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and Aramaic and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. The prayers and visions that are poetry in the original are printed one line per line. A few of its choices are worth knowing.

“One like a son of man.” The Aramaic phrase in 7:13 is kept as “one like a son of man”, a figure who looks human, so that readers can see the phrase that Jesus took up as his own title.

“The Most High.” The title by which Daniel and even the pagan kings speak of God is kept throughout, and so is the vivid name of Daniel 7, “the Ancient of Days”.

“The abomination that brings ruin.” The phrase from 9:27, 11:31 and 12:11, older Bibles’ “abomination of desolation”, is rendered “the abomination that brings ruin”, the same words used where Jesus speaks “of which the prophet Daniel spoke” (Matthew 24:15).

Units. Measures are given in modern terms, so that Nebuchadnezzar’s statue is “about 90 feet high and about 9 feet wide”.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew and Aramaic beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
