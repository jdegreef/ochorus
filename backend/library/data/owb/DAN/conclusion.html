— Daniel 12:13

Daniel ends with a word to one old man. He has seen empires rise and fall, served four kings, survived the lions and been shown visions that left him ill and bewildered. He has asked how it will all turn out, and has not been told everything. What he is given instead is a promise: go your way, rest, and rise at the end to receive your inheritance. The book that surveyed the kingdoms of the world comes down at last to one faithful life held safe in God’s hands.

The question the book leaves with you

Daniel asks every reader the question it asked its first readers: who really rules? Nebuchadnezzar thought he did, until he ate grass like cattle. Belshazzar thought he could drink from God’s vessels and praise his own gods, and was weighed and found too light the same night. Darius found that the law he had signed could not save the man he valued, and that God could. Again and again the kings themselves are made to say it: “the Most High rules over human kingdoms”, “his kingdom will never be destroyed.”

And if God rules, the next question is how to live under rulers who do not acknowledge him. Daniel’s answer is neither withdrawal nor surrender. He learns Babylon’s literature, serves its kings honestly, and rises high in its government. But he will not eat what defiles him, and he will not stop praying. His friends will not bow to the statue, “even if he does not” rescue them. Their faith does not depend on a happy ending, and the book insists that a happy ending is coming anyway.

Where the book points

The stone cut without hands, the kingdom that will never be destroyed, the one like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven to receive an everlasting kingdom: the New Testament sees all of these fulfilled in Jesus. He called himself the Son of Man, announced that the kingdom of God had come near, and before the high priest claimed the vision of Daniel 7 as his own. He spoke of “the abomination that brings ruin, of which the prophet Daniel spoke” in foretelling the fall of the temple. The Revelation to John borrows Daniel’s beasts, its time, times and half a time, and its picture of one whose hair is white as wool. And Daniel’s promise that those who sleep in the dust will awake is fulfilled, the church believes, in the resurrection of Christ, the firstfruits of those who have died.

Christians still differ over how the visions’ details map onto history, and the book itself counsels patience: the words were “kept secret and sealed until the time of the end.” But they do not differ over its hope.

Where to go from here

Daniel has been loved by preachers for its stories and studied by scholars for its visions, and both are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the furnace (Daniel 3). Charles H. Spurgeon’s sermon Consolation in the Furnace (3:25), on the fourth man in the fire.
  • On the writing on the wall (Daniel 5). R. A. Torrey’s sermon Found Wanting (5:25–27).
  • On Daniel’s prayers (Daniel 6 and 9). E. M. Bounds’s chapter “Daniel, the Praying Captive” in Prayer and Praying Men; “Windows Open Toward Jerusalem” in The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original on the lions’ den and the prayer of chapter 9; and the chapter “Confession” in D. L. Moody’s Prevailing Prayer, on Daniel’s confession for his people.
  • On the seventy sevens (Daniel 9). George Whitefield’s “The Righteousness of Christ, an Everlasting Righteousness” (9:24), in Selected Sermons of George Whitefield.
  • On the Son of Man and the coming kingdom (Daniel 7). Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, chapters 31–32, which quote Daniel 7 at length and distinguish Christ’s two comings; and Cyril of Jerusalem’s The Catechetical Lectures, Lecture XV, on Christ’s return in glory, which opens with Daniel 7:9.
  • On the end and the resurrection (Daniel 7 and 12). Augustine’s The City of God, Book XX, chapter 23, on what Daniel foretold of the last judgement and the kingdom of the saints, and Book XVIII, chapter 34, on Daniel and Ezekiel; and D. L. Moody’s sermon True Wisdom (12:3), on those who lead many to righteousness.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Daniel in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

Then read Daniel beside Jeremiah, whose seventy years he counted, and Ezekiel, who names a Daniel beside Noah and Job among the righteous (Ezekiel 14:14), and beside the Revelation to John, which takes up his visions.
