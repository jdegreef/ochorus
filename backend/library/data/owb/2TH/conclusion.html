— 2 Thessalonians 3:5

A letter that contains one of the most puzzling passages in the Bible ends with one of its simplest prayers. Paul does not pray that the Thessalonians will understand the man of lawlessness or name the power that holds him back. He prays that their hearts will be led deeper into God’s love and into the endurance of Christ. That is what a frightened church needs, and it is what every reader of this letter needs, whatever they make of chapter 2.

The question the letter leaves

How should Christians wait? The Thessalonians had been tempted two ways at once: to be shaken by every new report that the end had come, and to give up ordinary life because of it. Paul’s answer to both is the same. Do not be shaken; keep a firm grip on what you were taught. Do not be idle; go about your work quietly. “As for you, brothers and sisters, do not lose heart in doing what is right.” Hope in the Lord’s coming, rightly held, makes a person steadier, not more anxious, and more faithful in the work in front of them.

Where to go from here

Readers have puzzled over this letter and preached from it for as long as there has been a church, and several of them are in this library:

  • On growing faith (1:3). Charles H. Spurgeon’s A Lecture for Little-Faith, on Paul’s thanks that the church’s faith was growing beyond measure.
  • On the day of the Lord and the man of lawlessness (2:1–12). Augustine of Hippo’s The City of God, whose twentieth book weighs the guesses of his day and admits he does not know; Cyril of Jerusalem’s The Catechetical Lectures, whose fifteenth lecture, on Christ’s coming in glory, expounds the passage for new believers; and the fifth book of Irenaeus of Lyons’s Against Heresies.
  • On the hope of glory (1:10). Richard Baxter’s The Saints’ Everlasting Rest, which takes up the day when Christ is glorified among his people.
  • On work (3:6–13). Spurgeon’s John Ploughman’s Talk, whose homely chapter “To the Idle” makes Paul’s point in a farmer’s voice.

And go back to 1 Thessalonians, in this same edition, if you have not read it; the two letters are best read side by side.
