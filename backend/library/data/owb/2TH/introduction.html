— 2 Thessalonians 2:1–2

The young church in Thessalonica had been told to wait for the Lord’s coming. Now some of its members were told that the waiting was over. Someone, by a prophecy, a message or even a letter said to be from Paul, was claiming that the day of the Lord had already arrived. Believers who were already suffering persecution were thrown into alarm, and some had stopped working altogether.

This short letter answers them. It is the shortest of the letters to churches that bear Paul’s name, three chapters long, and it does three things: it encourages a persecuted church, it corrects a mistaken idea about the end, and it tells the idle to go back to work.

A second letter

The letter opens with the same three names as the first, Paul, Silvanus and Timothy, and much of its language echoes 1 Thessalonians. The traditional view, still held by many scholars, is that Paul wrote it a few weeks or months after the first letter, from Corinth, around AD 51, when news reached him that the church’s hope had been knocked off balance.

Many other scholars think it was written later by a follower of Paul, pointing to its cooler tone, its close imitation of the first letter, and its teaching that certain events must come before the day of the Lord, where 1 Thessalonians says the day comes like a thief. Defenders of Paul’s authorship answer that a writer facing a new error would naturally say something new, and that the letter’s closing signature (3:17) is hard to explain otherwise. A few have suggested that this letter was actually written first. The church has always received it as Paul’s.

The shape of the letter

  • Chapter 1: suffering and justice. Thanks for a church whose faith is growing under persecution, and the promise that God will set things right when the Lord Jesus appears.
  • Chapter 2: the day has not yet come. What must happen first, and why believers need not be shaken.
  • Chapter 3: prayer and work. A request for prayer, a command to the idle, and a greeting in Paul’s own hand.

Suffering and justice

The church was still being persecuted, and its faith was growing anyway. Paul is proud of them. He also speaks to a question every suffering believer asks: will the wrong done to us ever be put right? His answer is yes. When the Lord Jesus appears from heaven “in a blaze of fire”, God will repay those who make his people suffer, and give rest to those who suffer. The language is drawn from the prophets, and it is severe: those who refuse the gospel will be “cut off from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his power”.

Paul does not tell the Thessalonians to take revenge. He tells them that justice belongs to God, and that the day of judgment is also the day when the Lord comes “to be glorified among his holy people”. Then he prays for them, that God would count them worthy of the life he called them to.

The day has not yet come

Chapter 2 is the heart of the letter, and one of the most puzzling passages in the New Testament. Paul tells the Thessalonians not to be shaken. The day of the Lord cannot already be here, because certain things must come first:

{{OWB:2TH.2.3.s1}}

— 2 Thessalonians 2:3–4

Something, or someone, is holding him back for now, and the Thessalonians know what it is. Paul had told them while he was with them. We were not there, and we do not know. Augustine wrote frankly that he did not know what Paul meant, and then set out the guesses of his day.

Christians have proposed many answers. Early writers such as Irenaeus and Cyril of Jerusalem saw the man of lawlessness as the Antichrist who would appear at the end, and many took the restraining power to be the Roman empire and its law. Some recalled the emperor Gaius (Caligula), who about ten years earlier had ordered his statue set up in the temple at Jerusalem. The Reformers often identified the man of lawlessness with the papacy, a view few hold today. Others think of a figure still to come, or of a pattern of rebellion against God that repeats through history. Proposals for the restrainer have included the empire, the preaching of the gospel, an angel, the Holy Spirit and God himself.

What is not in doubt is Paul’s point. Evil is real and will come to a head, but it is on a leash. It is held back until its time, and when it is revealed, “the Lord Jesus will destroy him with the breath of his mouth and bring him to nothing by the brightness of his coming.” So the believers need not be shaken. They can give thanks, stand firm and hold on to what they were taught.

Anyone who will not work

The last chapter returns to a problem the first letter had touched on gently (1 Thessalonians 4:11; 5:14). Some members were living undisciplined lives, “not busy working” but “busy with everyone else’s business”, and expecting the church to feed them. Paul reminds them that he worked night and day so as not to be a burden, though he had a right to support, and he repeats a rule he had given them in person:

{{OWB:2TH.3.10.s1}}

— 2 Thessalonians 3:10

It is a rule about those who will not work, not those who cannot. And Paul is careful about how it is applied: keep your distance from such a person, but “do not treat that person as an enemy; correct them as a brother or sister.”

Then Paul takes the pen from his secretary and writes the last lines himself, as the sign of a genuine letter. After the warning about letters claiming to come from him, the signature matters.

How to read 2 Thessalonians

Read 1 Thessalonians first, and then this letter straight after, to hear what has changed. Do not let chapter 2 swallow the rest. Its details are disputed, but its purpose is pastoral: to steady a frightened church. Read chapter 1 as comfort for anyone who suffers unjustly, and chapter 3 as a reminder that hope in the Lord’s coming is lived out in ordinary, faithful work.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 2 Thessalonians are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Already here” (2:2). The Greek verb means “has arrived” or “is present”. The false claim was not that the day was near, which Paul himself taught, but that it had already come, and the translation says so.

“The man of lawlessness” (2:3). Many manuscripts read “the man of sin”, the phrase in older English Bibles; this translation follows those that read “lawlessness”, which links him to “the mystery of lawlessness” in verse 7.

“As the first of a harvest” (2:13). Two readings, a single letter apart in the oldest manuscripts, say either that God chose the Thessalonians “from the beginning” or “as firstfruits”, the first of a harvest offered to God. This translation follows the second, and explains the word.

“Undisciplined” (3:6, 3:11). The word was used of soldiers out of rank. Older Bibles have “disorderly”; the context makes plain that these members had stopped working.
