First John ends where it began, with knowing. The writer has spent five chapters giving anxious believers ways to recognise the truth and to know that they belong to it, and in the last verses the word comes again and again: we know, we know, we know. He has not given them a new teaching. He has taken them back to what they heard “from the beginning”, and shown them that it is enough.

The question the book leaves with you

The letter’s question is not only “Is it true?” but “Do I belong to it?” Every reader of 1 John, sooner or later, lays its tests against their own life. Do I believe that Jesus is the Christ, come in the flesh? Do I keep his commands? Do I love my brothers and sisters, not in words but in deeds?

Those questions can wound, and the writer knows it. That is why he never lets them stand alone. Beside every test he sets the love that came first: “We love because he loved us first” (4:19). The tests are not there to make the reader wonder whether God could ever love them. They are there so that a reader who has been told they are on the outside can see that they are on the inside after all, and stop being afraid.

Where the letter points

It points to Jesus, the one who was heard, seen and touched, who came “by water and blood”, and who stands now as our advocate in the Father’s presence. It points to the church, the small family of “dear children” who must love one another because they are loved. And it points ahead, to a day when God’s children will see him face to face:

Not yet, but surely. That hope, the writer adds, makes a person pure, just as he is pure.

Where to go from here

First John has been loved by the church’s preachers for as long as there has been a church, and many of them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • Through the whole letter. Augustine of Hippo’s Homilies on the First Epistle of John, ten sermons preached one Easter season, in which he says, “Love, and do what thou wilt.”
  • On light, sin and forgiveness (chapters 1–2). J. C. Ryle’s Are You Forgiven? (2:12) and Thomas Chalmers’s The Expulsive Power of a New Affection (2:15), on why love for the world is driven out only by a greater love.
  • On remaining and on being God’s children (chapters 2–3). A. B. Simpson’s How to Abide (2:28), Andrew Murray’s Abide in Christ, and Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s Adoption (3:1).
  • On God’s love (chapter 4). R. A. Torrey’s The Greatest Sentence That Was Ever Written (4:8) and Charles H. Spurgeon’s Love’s Logic (4:19).
  • On faith that overcomes (chapter 5). Ryle’s Victory (5:4–5).
  • On testing the reality of faith. John Wesley’s A Plain Account of Christian Perfection, which leans on this letter at every turn, and Jonathan Edwards’s A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections, on telling true grace from its counterfeits.
  • From the generation after John. The Epistle and Martyrdom of Polycarp, whose letter to the Philippians repeats this letter’s warning that whoever does not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is antichrist.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 1 John in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this letter.

But the best next step may be to read the Gospel of John beside it, and then to read the letter again. The Gospel tells how the Word became flesh; the letter tells what it means to live in his light. And its last words, so abrupt on a first reading, may come to sound like a father’s blessing at the door:
