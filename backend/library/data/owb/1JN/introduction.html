{{OWB:1JN.4.16.s2}}

— 1 John 4:16

First John is called a letter, but it does not begin like one. There is no greeting, no name of the sender, no name of the church, no “grace and peace”. And it does not end like one either: no news, no list of friends, no farewell, only a single abrupt line, “Dear children, guard yourselves against idols.” What lies between reads less like correspondence than like a pastor speaking to a congregation he has known for years, whose names he no longer needs to say.

He calls them “dear children” again and again. Something has shaken them badly, and he writes to steady them: to tell them what is true, and how to know that they belong to it.

The vocabulary is as small as the Gospel of John’s, and many of the words are the same: light and darkness, truth and lies, life and death, love, remain, know, the world. The sentences are short and plain enough for a beginner in Greek to read. Yet few books of the Bible are harder to outline, because the writer does not move in a straight line. He circles. He takes up a theme, lays it down, and comes back to it a chapter later from another side, each time a little deeper. The repetitions are the point.

Something we touched with our hands

The opening is one long, breathless sentence that reaches across the first three verses:

{{OWB:1JN.1.1.s1}}

— 1 John 1:1–3

Every reader of John’s Gospel will hear the echo. The Gospel begins “In the beginning was the Word”; the letter begins with “what was there from the beginning” and calls it “the word of life”. But the stress has moved. The Gospel announces that the Word became flesh; the letter insists, with three senses piled on one another, that this flesh was really there to be heard, seen and handled. That insistence is the first clue to why the letter was written.

Why it was written

The church the writer addresses has just been through a split:

{{OWB:1JN.2.19.s1}}

— 1 John 2:19

The letter never names the people who left, and we cannot know exactly who they were. But it lets us see what they said and how they lived. They denied “that Jesus is the Christ” (2:22). They would not confess “Jesus Christ as one who has come in the flesh” (4:2). They claimed to know God and to be in the light, and to be free from sin, while caring little for keeping his commands or for the brothers and sisters they had left behind.

Many readers connect this with teachings known soon afterward. Some taught that the divine Christ only seemed to have a human body, and so could not truly have suffered; Ignatius of Antioch, writing to churches in the same region, argues against them hard. Others, like Cerinthus, whom Irenaeus says the apostle John met at Ephesus, taught that the heavenly Christ came upon the man Jesus at his baptism and left him before the cross. That may be why the letter speaks of Jesus Christ as “the one who came by water and blood” (5:6): not by the water of baptism only, but by the blood of his death as well. Whether the people who left held either view, scholars still debate. What is clear is that the dispute was over whether God had truly come among us in a human life and death, and that it left those who stayed wondering whether they were in the wrong.

So the writer says plainly why he wrote:

{{OWB:1JN.5.13.s1}}

— 1 John 5:13

It is worth setting that sentence beside the purpose of the Gospel, which was written “so that you may believe Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and by believing have life in his name” (John 20:31). The Gospel was written so that its readers might come to believe. The letter was written to people who already believe, so that they might know.

The tests of life

How can they know? A Scottish scholar, Robert Law, called his study of this letter The Tests of Life, and the name has stuck. Three tests run through the book, woven together and returning again and again.

  • What you believe about Jesus. The true confession is that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, come in the flesh. “Anyone who denies the Son is without the Father as well; anyone who confesses the Son has the Father too” (2:23).
  • Whether you keep his commands. “We can be sure that we have come to know him in this way: if we keep his commands” (2:3). Anyone who claims to remain in him “ought to live in the same way that Jesus lived” (2:6).
  • Whether you love your brothers and sisters. “We know that we have moved out of death into life, because we love our brothers and sisters” (3:14).

The tests are not hurdles set up to keep people out. They are signs given to the anxious, so that they can stop wondering. Belief, obedience and love are not three different religions. They are one life, seen from three sides, and in 3:23 the writer ties them together in a single sentence: we are to believe in the name of God’s Son Jesus Christ, and to love one another.

God is light, God is love

Twice the letter says what God is, in three words each. The first comes near the start: “God is light, and in him there is no darkness, not the least” (1:5). The second comes near the end, and twice: “God is love” (4:8, 16). Together they carry the whole letter. Because God is light, no one can walk in darkness and claim to be with him. Because God is love, no one can hate a brother or sister and claim to know him.

And the letter is careful to say where love is found. It is not first our feeling toward God, but his action toward us:

{{OWB:1JN.4.10.s1}}

— 1 John 4:10

Love has a shape, and its shape is the cross: “This is how we have come to know what love is: he laid down his life for us” (3:16). Then, at once, the writer brings it down to the ground. If someone has enough of this world’s goods and shuts their heart against a brother or sister in need, “How can the love of God remain in such a person?” (3:17). Love that only talks is not love. “Dear children, let us love not by what we say or with our tongues, but by what we do and in truth” (3:18).

The puzzle of sin

Every careful reader of 1 John runs into the same puzzle. In the first chapter the writer says, “If we claim that we are free from sin, we are fooling ourselves, and the truth is not in us” (1:8). In the third he says, “Whoever has been born of God does not go on sinning” (3:9). How can both be true?

Part of the answer is in the tense of the Greek verbs, which in chapter 3 describe a settled way of life, a person who keeps on sinning as a matter of course. The Christian is not sinless, but neither is sin any longer their home. Christians have weighed these verses differently. John Wesley found in them a promise that believers may be so filled with love as to be free from wilful sin, and drew heavily on this letter for his teaching on Christian perfection; others, in the Reformed tradition, have read them as describing the direction of a life rather than its every step. Both sides take seriously what the letter holds together: a high call to holiness, and a sure remedy when we fall short.

{{OWB:1JN.2.1.s1}} {{OWB:1JN.2.1.s2}}

— 1 John 2:1–2

The writer does not want his children to sin. But if they do, he does not want them to despair. “If we confess our sins, he is faithful and righteous: he will forgive our sins and cleanse us from every kind of wrongdoing” (1:9).

Remain

The word to watch in 1 John is remain, more than twenty times in five short chapters: God’s word remains in us, we remain in him and he in us. It is the word Jesus uses of the vine in John 15: “Remain in me, and I will remain in you.” The people who left did not remain. The letter’s whole appeal is that those who stayed should go on staying, because they have found where life is.

When our hearts condemn us

The letter’s tests could be turned into a weapon against the tender-hearted, who are always ready to fail themselves. The writer sees that danger and meets it in one of the kindest sentences in the New Testament:

{{OWB:1JN.3.19.s1}}

— 1 John 3:19–20

Assurance rests finally not on how we feel about ourselves but on God, who knows everything. “Love has no room for fear; instead, complete love throws fear out” (4:18).

Who wrote 1 John?

The letter, like the Gospel, never names its author. He writes as one of a group of eyewitnesses (“what we have seen with our own eyes”) and as a father to his readers. Its language and thought are so close to the Gospel of John that most readers, ancient and modern, have taken them to come from the same hand or the same circle. From the second century the church received it as the work of John the apostle, the son of Zebedee, writing late in life at Ephesus, probably in the last decades of the first century. Some scholars think it was written by a leader in the community that treasured John’s witness, perhaps the “elder” of 2 and 3 John, of whom Papias spoke as a second John at Ephesus.

How to read 1 John

Read it aloud, at one sitting; it takes about twenty minutes. The prologue (1:1–4) announces the life that was seen and touched. Then the letter moves in widening circles: walking in the light (1:5–2:17); the antichrists and the anointing (2:18–27); children of God (2:28–3:24); testing the spirits and the love of God (4:1–21); and faith, testimony and assurance (5:1–21).

Notice the families of the letter: the “dear children”, and the fathers, young men and children of 2:12–14. Notice how often it says “we know”. Notice that its tests always come back to Jesus. And notice that its last word, about idols, is not an afterthought. A false picture of God is an idol, however spiritual it sounds. The letter has spent five chapters showing us the true one: “This one is the true God, and eternal life” (5:20).

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 1 John are worth knowing about before you begin.

“Remain.” Where older Bibles vary between “abide”, “continue” and “dwell”, this translation keeps “remain” throughout, so that a reader can follow one Greek word through the whole letter and back into John 15.

“Go on sinning.” In 3:6–9 and 5:18 the translation gives the force of the Greek tense: the one born of God “does not go on sinning”. It does not make the letter say that Christians never sin, which 1:8–10 denies.

“The sacrifice that atones for our sins.” In 2:2 and 4:10 a single Greek word, rendered in older Bibles “propitiation”, is translated by what it means: a sacrifice that deals with sin and turns away its judgment.

1 John 5:7–8. Readers of the King James Version will miss a line here: “the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost: and these three are one”. Those words are not found in any early Greek manuscript; they entered printed Greek New Testaments in the sixteenth century. This translation, like nearly every modern Bible, reads only “For there are three that testify: the Spirit, the water and the blood, and the three are in agreement.”

“Brothers and sisters.” Where the Greek “brother” means any fellow believer, as it almost always does in this letter, the translation says “brother or sister”. The Open Word Bible also lets a reader choose “Christ” or “the Messiah” for Christos; this edition prints “Christ”.
