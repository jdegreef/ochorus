— Deuteronomy 6:4–5

Deuteronomy is the last will and testament of Moses. Forty years after the exodus, Israel is camped on the plains of Moab, across the Jordan from the land God promised. Moses is a hundred and twenty years old, and he knows he will not cross the river with them. So he gathers the people one last time, and he preaches. The book is made almost entirely of his words, and in Hebrew it is named after them: Devarim, “Words”, from its opening, “These are the words Moses spoke to all Israel”.

The name we use comes from the ancient Greek translation and means “second law”. It arose from a verse in chapter 17, where the king is told to write out “a copy of this law”, which the Greek rendered as “this second law”. The name is not quite right, because Deuteronomy is not a new law. But it is not quite wrong either. It is the law of Sinai preached a second time, to a new generation, on the edge of a new life.

Few books of the Old Testament mattered more to Jesus. When the devil tempted him in the wilderness, he answered three times from Deuteronomy. When he was asked for the greatest commandment, he quoted Deuteronomy 6. And for centuries Jewish men and women have said its central words every morning and every evening: “Hear, Israel.”

The shape of the book

Deuteronomy is built mainly from three speeches of Moses, followed by an account of his last days:

  • the first speech: remembering the journey from Horeb to Moab (chapters 1–4)
  • the second speech: the commandments (5–11), the laws for life in the land (12–26), and the blessings and curses (27–28)
  • the third speech: the covenant renewed in Moab, and the call to choose life (29–30)
  • Moses’ last days: Joshua appointed, the song of Moses, the blessing of the tribes, and his death on Mount Nebo (31–34)

Many scholars have noticed that this shape resembles the treaties that ancient kings made with their subject peoples: a history of what the great king has done, the obligations of the people, blessings and curses, witnesses, and arrangements for the treaty to be kept and read. Deuteronomy is a covenant document. But it is a covenant preached with tears, by a pastor who loves his people and fears for them.

“Remember”

The first speech is a retelling. Moses goes back over the forty years: the appointment of judges, the scouts and the refusal at Kadesh, the long years of wandering, and the first victories east of the Jordan. Most of the people listening were children when those things happened, or not yet born. Moses insists that the story is theirs anyway:

{{OWB:DEU.5.3.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 5:3

Two words run all through the book: “remember” and “do not forget”. Israel is to remember that they were slaves in Egypt, remember the wilderness, remember what their own eyes have seen, and tell it to their children. Moses reminds them how God carried them in the wilderness “as a father carries his son”, and he asks a question that could only be asked of Israel:

{{OWB:DEU.4.7.s1}} {{OWB:DEU.4.8.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 4:7–8

The great lesson of the history is that there is only one God. “So know this today, and take it to heart: the LORD is God in the heavens above and on the earth below, and there is no other.” Moses also warns them that if they turn to idols in the land, they will be scattered among the nations. Yet even there, he says, “you will seek the LORD your God, and you will find him, if you search after him with all your heart and with all your soul.”

“Hear, Israel”

The second speech begins with the Ten Commandments, spoken again. They are almost the same as in Exodus 20, but one difference is telling. In Exodus the Sabbath is grounded in creation. Here it is grounded in rescue:

{{OWB:DEU.5.15.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 5:15

Then comes the Shema, named from its first word, “Hear”: the words at the head of this introduction. The LORD is one, and he is to be loved with the whole heart, the whole being and all one’s strength. It is a command to love, which is a strange thing to command. But Deuteronomy believes that love is something you do as well as feel: something you keep, teach and practise. So Moses goes on at once to the everyday means of keeping it:

{{OWB:DEU.6.6.s1}} {{OWB:DEU.6.7.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 6:6–7

The words are to be tied on the hand and written on the doorposts, and many Jewish homes still fix a small case containing them to the doorframe. Faith in Deuteronomy is passed on at the kitchen table and along the road, by parents who answer their children’s questions. “Some day your son will ask you, ‘What do they mean, these decrees, statutes and rulings that the LORD our God has commanded you?’” And the answer begins not with a rule but with a story: “In Egypt we were slaves to Pharaoh.”

Chosen in love

Why Israel? Deuteronomy answers with disarming honesty. Not because they were great, and not because they were good:

{{OWB:DEU.7.7.s1}} {{OWB:DEU.7.8.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 7:7–8

God loved them because he loved them. Two chapters later Moses makes the same point about the land: “it is not because you are righteous that the LORD your God is giving you this good land to possess, for you are a stiff-necked people.” Grace comes first in Deuteronomy, as it does in Exodus. The commandments are the grateful response of a people who have been loved.

“Do not forget”

Moses knows that the greatest danger lies not in the wilderness but in the land, when life becomes easy. The wilderness years were meant to teach dependence:

{{OWB:DEU.8.3.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 8:3

In the land there will be fine houses, growing herds and silver and gold, and the temptation will be to say, “My own power and the strength of my own hands have won me this wealth.” Moses answers, “Remember the LORD your God instead. It is he who gives you the ability to grow wealthy.” It is one of the most searching warnings in the Bible for anyone who has prospered.

And then, in chapter 10, Moses gathers it all into a single question:

{{OWB:DEU.10.12.s1}} {{OWB:DEU.10.13.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 10:12–13

The God who owns the highest heavens “sees that orphans and widows get justice, and he loves the outsider”. So must Israel: “You too must love the outsider, since you were outsiders yourselves in the land of Egypt.” Moses even asks for something no law can produce: “Circumcise your hearts, then, and stop stiffening your necks.”

Laws for life in the land

Chapters 12–26 set out the laws by which Israel is to live. They begin with worship: sacrifice is to be brought not anywhere but to “the place that the LORD your God will choose out of all your tribes to put his name there”. Then come festivals and tithes, judges, kings, priests and prophets, war, family life, and the treatment of neighbours, workers, animals and the poor.

Running through them is a passion for justice and generosity. “Pursue what is right, nothing but what is right.” The poor are not to be met with a closed hand: “open your hand to him generously and lend him as much as he needs to meet his want.” At harvest, a forgotten sheaf is to be left “to the outsider, the fatherless child and the widow”. Even the ox treading grain is not to be muzzled. And again and again the reason is the same: “Remember that you were slaves in Egypt.”

The king, too, stands under the law. He must not pile up horses, wives or gold, and he must write out his own copy of the law and read it all his life, “so that he does not think himself above his fellow Israelites.” And Moses promises a successor of a different kind:

{{OWB:DEU.18.15.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 18:15

The words can be read of the line of true prophets who would follow Moses. But the last chapter says that no prophet like him had yet arisen, and by the time of Jesus many were waiting for “the Prophet”. Peter, preaching in the temple, says plainly that Jesus is the one Moses meant (Acts 3:22).

Some of these laws are hard for modern readers, above all the commands to devote the nations of Canaan “wholly to destruction”. Christians have wrestled with them in different ways: as a unique judgment on peoples whose wickedness the book describes, limited to one time and place; as the language of ancient warfare, not always meant literally; or as a hard part of a story that finds its answer only in the cross. The book’s own stated reason is that Israel should not learn to copy the practices of those nations. It is right to read these chapters soberly, and not to pretend they are easy.

The laws end with a liturgy. When an Israelite brings the first fruits of the harvest, he is to recite the story of his people, beginning, “My ancestor was an Aramean, a wanderer”, and ending with thanksgiving for “a land that flows with milk and honey.” Then he is to rejoice, with the Levite and the outsider beside him.

Blessing and curse, life and death

Chapters 27–28 set out the blessings that will follow faithfulness and the curses that will follow rebellion. Six tribes are to stand on Mount Gerizim for the blessing, six on Mount Ebal for the curse. The curses are long and terrible, and they end in exile. But chapter 30 looks beyond exile to a return, and to a change that only God can make:

{{OWB:DEU.30.6.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 30:6

What Moses commanded in chapter 10, God here promises to do. Jeremiah and Ezekiel would take up the same hope, of a new heart and a new covenant. And Moses insists that God’s word is not far away or beyond reach:

{{OWB:DEU.30.14.s1}}

— Deuteronomy 30:14

Paul quotes these words in Romans 10 and applies them to the gospel of Christ, “the message of faith that we proclaim.” And then Moses sets the choice before them, heaven and earth as witnesses: “I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. So choose life, that you and your descendants may live”.

Moses’ last days

The last chapters gather up Moses’ final acts. He commissions Joshua: “Be strong and courageous.” He writes down the law and orders it to be read aloud to all Israel every seven years, “men, women and children, and the outsiders who live in your towns”. He teaches the people a song, in which God is “The Rock: his work is perfect”, and Israel is the child he found in the desert, guarded “as the pupil of his eye”, carried like an eagle’s young. He blesses the tribes one by one, and ends with a promise: “The God of old is a refuge, and underneath are arms that last forever.”

It is a hard ending for the man who had carried this people for forty years. Early in the book Moses tells how he begged to cross: “Please let me go across and see the good land on the far side of the Jordan.” The answer was no, and at the end God names the reason: Moses had broken faith with him at the waters of Meribah. He does not hide his grief, but he does not let it sour his last words, which are all blessing.

Then he climbs Mount Nebo, and God shows him the whole land, and he dies there in the land of Moab. Someone, perhaps God himself, buries him, and no one knows where. The book’s last words are his epitaph:

— Deuteronomy 34:10–12

How to read Deuteronomy

Read it aloud if you can. It is a book of sermons, and its long sentences and repetitions are the rhythms of a preacher. Read it after Numbers, so that you hear it as Moses’ people did, a generation whose parents had died in the wilderness. And read it with the New Testament open, because it is one of the books the New Testament quotes most often.

Listen for the words the book repeats. “Today” occurs again and again: the covenant is not a memory but a choice to be made now, by whoever is hearing. The phrase “with all your heart and with all your soul” runs through the book like a refrain. The phrase “the place that the LORD your God will choose” looks forward to a sanctuary not yet named, which later books will find in Jerusalem. And “the outsider, the fatherless child and the widow” keep appearing together, as though Moses cannot speak of the land’s harvest without thinking of those who own none of it.

It helps, too, to notice how much of the rest of the Old Testament is written in Deuteronomy’s accent. The books from Joshua to Kings measure Israel’s kings by its standards; of Josiah the writer says that he turned to the LORD “with his whole heart, with his whole being, and with all his strength”, the very words of the Shema. The prophets call Israel back to its covenant and threaten its curses. And when the exiles prayed for a way home, they could lean on its promise that even from the farthest edge of the sky, God would gather them.

The book says that Moses spoke these words and wrote down “this law” (31:9, 24), and Jewish and Christian tradition has received it as his; its account of his death has long been read as added by another hand. Many scholars connect it instead with “the book of the law” found in the temple in King Josiah’s reign, in 622 BC, whose reforms follow its laws closely (2 Kings 22–23), and think it was completed later still. Others hold that it preserves Moses’ own covenant, shaped for later generations. Christians hold a range of views on these questions, while hearing in it the voice of God to his people.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Deuteronomy are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”.

The Shema. The Hebrew of 6:4 has no verbs and can be read in more than one way. The translation keeps close to it: “Hear, Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one.” In 6:5 the word often rendered “soul” is “your whole being”, the whole living person.

“Faithful love.” The Hebrew hesed, God’s covenant love, is “faithful love”: God “keeps his covenant and his faithful love for a thousand generations”.

“The outsider.” The foreigner who lives among Israel, whom the book so often names beside the widow and the fatherless, is usually “the outsider”.
