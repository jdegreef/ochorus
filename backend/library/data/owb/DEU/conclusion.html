So the five books of Moses end, as Genesis ended, with a death outside the land. Genesis closed with Joseph’s coffin in Egypt; Deuteronomy closes with Moses’ grave in Moab, somewhere no one knows. The promise to Abraham is still not fulfilled. The people are still on the wrong side of the river. Everything is ready, and nothing is finished.

The question the book leaves with you

Moses has set the choice before Israel as plainly as words allow: “I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. So choose life”. But he is not naive about what they will choose. He tells them that “up to this day the LORD has not given you an understanding mind, seeing eyes or hearing ears.” In his last days God tells him that the people will “abandon me and break my covenant”, and the song he teaches them is written as a witness against them for when they do.

So Deuteronomy leaves its readers with its own deepest question. If love for God must come from the whole heart, where is such a heart to be found? The book’s answer is the promise of chapter 30: “The LORD your God will circumcise your heart.” The command of chapter 10 becomes a gift that God himself must give. The rest of the Old Testament, from Joshua to the prophets, is the long story of a people who could not keep the covenant, and of a God who would not let it go.

Where the book points

The last chapter says that no prophet like Moses had arisen, and Israel kept waiting for the one Moses had promised. The New Testament says that he came. When Jesus was tempted in the wilderness for forty days, as Israel had been tested for forty years, he stood where they had fallen, and answered from this book: “No one lives on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God.” When he was asked for the greatest commandment, he gave the Shema. And on the mountain of the transfiguration Moses appeared at last in the promised land, “talking with Jesus.”

The curses at the end of Deuteronomy are not the last word either. Paul writes that “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse on our behalf”, quoting Deuteronomy 21:23, so that the blessing of Abraham might reach the nations. And he hears Moses’ words about the nearness of God’s word as the voice of the gospel itself:

“The word is close to you, on your lips and in your heart.” This is the message of faith that we proclaim.

— Romans 10:8

Where to go from here

Deuteronomy has been preached in every age of the church. A few places to begin in this library:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Deuteronomy: Consecration” reads the book as God’s people giving themselves afresh to him on the border of the land.
  • On the Ten Commandments (Deuteronomy 5). Thomas Watson’s The Ten Commandments; and Martin Luther’s Small Catechism and Large Catechism, which teach the commandments one by one.
  • On loving God with all the heart (Deuteronomy 6 and 30). Andrew Murray’s The Two Covenants, whose chapter “Entering the Covenant: With All the Heart” sets the promise of Deuteronomy 30:6 beside the new covenant.
  • On the temptation of Jesus. The Gospel of Matthew in this library’s Open Word Bible edition, where Jesus answers the devil three times from Deuteronomy (Matthew 4:1–11).
  • On the open hand (Deuteronomy 15). Jonathan Edwards’s Christian Charity (Deuteronomy 15:7–11), on giving to the poor.
  • On the prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18). John Henry Newman’s sermon “Moses the Type of Christ”, in his Parochial and Plain Sermons, Volume VII, on Deuteronomy 18:15.
  • On the song and the blessing of Moses (Deuteronomy 32–33). Jonathan Edwards’s Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God (Deuteronomy 32:35); Alexander Maclaren’s The Eagle and Its Brood (Deuteronomy 32:11) and At the Bush (Deuteronomy 33:16); and Charles H. Spurgeon’s As Thy Days, So Shall Thy Strength Be (Deuteronomy 33:25).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Deuteronomy in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
