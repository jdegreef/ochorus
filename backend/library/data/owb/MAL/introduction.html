{{OWB:MAL.3.1.s1}}

— Malachi 3:1

Malachi is the last book of the Old Testament in Christian Bibles, and it ends looking forward. Its final verses promise a messenger, the prophet Elijah, who will come before the great day of the LORD. Then the prophetic voice falls silent for some four hundred years, until a man dressed like Elijah appears in the wilderness by the Jordan. The Gospels open by saying that Malachi’s promise has begun to come true.

The name Malachi means “my messenger”, the very words used in the promise of 3:1. Some think it was the prophet’s name; others think it is a title, taken from that verse, for a prophet whose name we do not know. The ancient Greek translation reads the first verse as “by the hand of his messenger”, and one Jewish tradition identified the author with Ezra. Either way, the book is about a messenger, and about the God who sends him.

Its place in the Bible gives it extra weight. In the Hebrew Bible, Malachi closes the Book of the Twelve, the single scroll that holds the Minor Prophets, and the Writings follow, ending in most printed editions with Chronicles. Most Christian Bibles put the prophets last, so that Malachi stands at the very end of the Old Testament, its promise of a coming messenger facing the first page of Matthew.

The time

Malachi gives no date, but the book’s world is clear. The temple has been rebuilt, for priests are offering sacrifices on its altar. Judah is ruled by a “governor”, as it was under Persia. And the problems Malachi addresses are the very ones that Ezra and Nehemiah faced in the middle of the fifth century BC: careless priests, Jewish men marrying women who worshipped other gods, and tithes not brought, so that the Levites had to leave the temple to work their fields (Nehemiah 13). Most scholars date Malachi to that time, perhaps around 460–430 BC.

It was a time of disappointment. Haggai and Zechariah had promised that the glory of the new temple would be greater than the old, that the nations would come, that the LORD would live in Jerusalem. Two or three generations later, the temple stood, but nothing seemed to have changed. The people were not rebellious so much as tired. Worship had become a chore; faithfulness seemed to bring no reward. “What a burden this is!” they said of the altar, and sniffed at it.

A book of arguments

Malachi is built as a series of disputes. Again and again God makes a statement, the people answer with a question, “Yet you ask, ‘In what way…?’”, and God replies. It reads like a court case, or a quarrel within a family, and both are fitting.

  • 1:2–5: “I have loved you.” “In what way have you loved us?”
  • 1:6–2:9: priests who despise God’s name. “In what way have we despised your name?”
  • 2:10–16: faithlessness. To one another, and to “the wife you married in your youth”.
  • 2:17–3:5: “Where is the God of justice?” The Lord will come to his temple.
  • 3:6–12: “Return to me.” “In what way shall we return?”
  • 3:13–4:3: “It is useless to serve God.” The book of remembrance and the coming day.
  • 4:4–6: Moses and Elijah. A closing charge and a promise.

“I have loved you”

The book begins where all God’s dealings with his people begin:

{{OWB:MAL.1.2.s1}} {{OWB:MAL.1.3.s1}}

— Malachi 1:2–3

The people doubt God’s love because their lives are hard. God answers by pointing to Edom, the nation descended from Jacob’s brother, now in ruins. In the language of covenant, to “love” Jacob and “hate” Esau means to choose one to carry the promise and not the other, as Genesis tells; in Malachi it concerns two peoples, Israel and Edom, rather than God’s feelings toward individuals. Paul quotes the line in Romans 9:13 as part of his argument that God’s promises rest on his choosing, not on human birth or effort. Christians have long disagreed about how far that argument reaches, but Malachi’s own point is plain: Israel’s existence is evidence that God has loved it.

Blind and lame offerings

The longest dispute is with the priests. They bring blind, lame and sick animals to the altar, things they would never dare offer a human ruler:

{{OWB:MAL.1.8.s1}}

— Malachi 1:8

God would rather someone shut the temple doors than go on lighting “useless fires on my altar”. And then, in the middle of the rebuke, comes one of the widest horizons in the Old Testament:

{{OWB:MAL.1.11.s1}}

— Malachi 1:11

The early church read this verse as a promise of worship among the Gentiles; the Didache and Justin Martyr both applied it to the Christian Eucharist. Malachi sets it beside a picture of what a priest should be: his covenant with Levi was “one of life and peace”, “True instruction was in his mouth”, and “he turned many back from sin”, “For a priest’s lips should preserve knowledge”.

The verse goes on to give the priest a title: “he is the messenger of the LORD of Armies.” It is the same word as the prophet’s name. The priests were meant to be God’s messengers, carrying his instruction to the people. Because they have “caused many to stumble”, God will send another messenger of his own.

Faithful to one another

The third dispute begins with two questions: “Do we not all have one father? Was it not one God who created us?” Then why break faith with one another? Malachi names two ways. Men of Judah were marrying women who worshipped other gods, “the daughter of a foreign god”, and some were divorcing the wives of their youth. They wept at the altar because God no longer accepted their offerings, and asked why:

{{OWB:MAL.2.14.s1}}

— Malachi 2:14

The Hebrew of the next two verses is among the hardest in the prophets. This translation reads verse 16 as “For I hate divorce, says the LORD”; others read it as a saying about the man who divorces his wife out of dislike, who “covers his garment with violence”. Either way, the passage stands firmly with the wife who has been wronged, and calls marriage a covenant of which God himself is witness.

The messenger and the refiner

The people complain that God does not care about justice: “Where is the God of justice?” He answers with the promise at the head of this introduction. A messenger will clear the way, and then the Lord they are seeking will come suddenly to his temple. But they should be careful what they ask for:

{{OWB:MAL.3.2.s1}} {{OWB:MAL.3.3.s1}}

— Malachi 3:2–3

He comes first to purify the priests, and then to judge everyone who wrongs the weak: those who cheat hired workers of their wages, oppress widows and orphans, and deny justice to foreigners. Mark’s Gospel opens by quoting Malachi’s promise together with Isaiah’s, and Jesus himself said of John the Baptist, “This is the man of whom Scripture says, ‘I am sending my messenger ahead of you’” (Matthew 11:10).

“I do not change”

The fifth dispute rests on a single verse:

{{OWB:MAL.3.6.s1}}

— Malachi 3:6

The people have changed, again and again, since the days of their ancestors. God has not, and that is the only reason they still exist. So he calls them back, “Return to me, and I will turn to you again”, and when they ask how, he names their tithes. “Will a mortal rob God?” Then he makes a rare invitation: bring the full tithe, “Put me to the test in this”, and see if he will not “open the windows of heaven for you”.

The book of remembrance

The last dispute is the saddest. Some had decided, “It is useless to serve God.” The arrogant prosper and evildoers get away with it. But not everyone thought so:

{{OWB:MAL.3.16.s1}}

— Malachi 3:16

God calls them “my special treasure”. And he promises a day that will make the difference between the righteous and the wicked plain again: a day “blazing like a furnace” for the arrogant, but for those who revere his name, a sunrise:

{{OWB:MAL.3.20.s1}}

— Malachi 4:2

Christians have sung of Christ as that rising sun ever since: “Hail the Sun of Righteousness”, in Charles Wesley’s Christmas hymn.

How to read Malachi

Malachi is short, and it reads quickly. Notice the pattern of the disputes, and listen for your own voice in the people’s questions: “In what way have you loved us?” “What have we said against you?” They are the questions of people who have not left God, but have stopped expecting much from him. Then read it with the opening chapters of Matthew, Mark and Luke beside it, where the messenger finally arrives.

When Malachi is read in synagogues, verse 4:5 is read again after the last verse, so that the book, and the prophets, do not end on the word “destruction” but on the promise of Elijah. It is a good instinct. Malachi’s last word is a warning, but its last hope is a coming.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Most of Malachi is set out here one line per line, as poetry. A few of the translation’s choices are worth knowing.

“The LORD of Armies.” This title, which older Bibles render “the LORD of hosts”, appears twenty-four times in Malachi’s fifty-five verses, almost always as the signature on God’s words: “says the LORD of Armies”.

The people’s questions. The repeated question of the disputes is rendered “In what way…?”, so that the pattern can be heard each time it comes.

Chapter 4. In Hebrew Bibles, Malachi has three chapters, and the last six verses are numbered 3:19–24. This edition follows English Bibles and prints them as chapter 4.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
