— Malachi 4:4–6

Malachi ends with two names. Moses stands for the law that God had given, which the people are told to remember. Elijah stands for the prophet who called Israel back to God on Mount Carmel, and who will come again before the great day. Between them they sum up everything the Old Testament has said: what God requires, and what God has promised to do. And the promise is about families, hearts turned toward one another, as if the healing of the nation had to begin at home.

The question the book leaves with you

Malachi was written for people who had not abandoned God but had grown weary of him. They still came to the temple, still offered sacrifices, still called themselves his people. But they gave him their leftovers, kept back what was his, broke faith with their wives, and said, “It is useless to serve God.” The book’s question is whether that weariness is ours. Do we still go through the motions while asking, under our breath, “In what way have you loved us?”

Malachi’s answer is not a new argument but a reminder. God has loved them. He does not change. He keeps a book of remembrance for those who fear him and talk with one another about him. And he is coming, both to refine and to heal.

Where it points

The silence after Malachi lasted some four hundred years. Then an angel told an old priest in the temple that his son would go ahead of the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of fathers back to their children” (Luke 1:17). That son was John the Baptist, and Jesus said of him, “And, if you will receive it, John is Elijah, whose coming was foretold” (Matthew 11:14).

The Lord whom John prepared for came suddenly to his temple, and on a high mountain Moses and Elijah, the two names of Malachi’s last verses, “appeared before them, talking with Jesus”. When the disciples asked about Elijah coming first, Jesus answered both that “Elijah is indeed coming” and that “Elijah has come already” (Matthew 17:11–12). Christians have understood the promise as fulfilled in John, and some expect a further fulfilment before Christ returns. Jewish families still set a cup for Elijah at Passover. The book that closes the Old Testament leaves its door open.

Where to go from here

A few places in this library to take Malachi further:

  • On the God who does not change (3:6). Charles H. Spurgeon’s sermon The Immutability of God, on Malachi 3:6, and his Morning by Morning, November 2, on the same verse.
  • On the refiner’s fire (3:2–3). A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, chapter “The Last Message of the Holy Ghost to the Old Dispensation”; Spurgeon’s Morning by Morning, October 15, on “But who may abide the day of his coming?”; The Key Teachings of Jeanne Guyon, an Ochorus Original, chapter “The Cross and the Refiner’s Fire”; and Hannah Whitall Smith’s The God of All Comfort, chapter “Things That Cannot Be Shaken”, which draws on Malachi 3:3–4 and 3:12.
  • On bringing the full tithe (3:8–12). Faithful with Little, an Ochorus Original, chapter “Firstfruits”.
  • On the messenger and Elijah (3:1; 4:5). A. W. Tozer’s sermon He Must Increase (John 3:30), and R. A. Torrey’s Three Fires (Matthew 3:11), both on John the Baptist.
  • On Malachi in the early church. Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, chapter 117, on Malachi’s pure offering among the nations (1:11), and chapter 49, on Elijah as forerunner; and Augustine’s The City of God, Book XX, chapters 25 and 29, on Malachi’s prophecy of the judgement and the coming of Elijah.

And read the opening chapters of Mark and Luke in this same edition, where the messenger arrives.
