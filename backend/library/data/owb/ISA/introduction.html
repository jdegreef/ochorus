— Isaiah 6:3

Isaiah is the grandest of the prophets. Its sixty-six chapters range from the temple in Jerusalem to the ends of the earth, from the armies of Assyria to new heavens and a new earth. It holds some of the fiercest judgments in the Bible and some of its tenderest comfort. It gave the church the child whose name is Immanuel, the voice crying in the wilderness, the servant who was pierced for our rebellions, and the promise of a world where “None will hurt and none will destroy”. Jerome, who translated it into Latin, said Isaiah should be called an evangelist rather than a prophet, because he told the gospel of Christ so clearly.

No prophet is quoted more often in the New Testament. In Luke’s Gospel Jesus begins his ministry at Nazareth by reading from Isaiah, and the apostles reached for it again and again to explain who he was. And no book of the Bible has come down to us in an older complete copy: the Great Isaiah Scroll, found in a cave by the Dead Sea in 1947, was copied more than a century before the birth of Christ, and contains all sixty-six chapters.

“The LORD saves”

The prophet’s Hebrew name, Yeshayahu, means “The LORD saves” or “The LORD is salvation”, and salvation is the word that runs through his book. “Look, God is my salvation!” sings the redeemed people in chapter 12. “Turn toward me and be saved, every corner of the earth!” calls the LORD in chapter 45.

The opening verse tells us who Isaiah was: “The vision of Isaiah son of Amoz: what he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem in the days when Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah were kings of Judah.” He lived in Jerusalem in the second half of the eighth century BC, had ready access to the royal court, and was married to a woman he calls “the prophetess”. He gave his sons names that were messages: Shear-jashub, “A remnant will return” (7:3; 10:21), and Maher-shalal-hash-baz, “Spoil hurries, plunder hastens” (8:1–4), a sign that Judah’s enemies, Damascus and Samaria, would soon be stripped bare. His call came “In the year King Uzziah died”, around 740 BC, and he was still prophesying in 701 BC, when Assyria besieged Jerusalem. A later Jewish tradition says he was put to death under Hezekiah’s son Manasseh.

Isaiah’s world

The great power of Isaiah’s day was Assyria. Its armies pressed west, and the small kingdoms of the region scrambled for safety. In the 730s BC the kings of Syria and of northern Israel tried to force Judah into an alliance against Assyria, and when King Ahaz refused they marched on Jerusalem (chapter 7). Ahaz turned to Assyria for help. In 722 BC Assyria destroyed northern Israel and its capital, Samaria. In 701 BC Sennacherib invaded Judah, “marched against all the fortified cities of Judah and captured them” (36:1), and laid siege to Jerusalem; but the city was spared (chapters 36–37).

Behind Assyria, Isaiah saw another power rising. When envoys from Babylon came to King Hezekiah, Isaiah warned that everything in his palace would one day “be hauled off to Babylon” (39:6). From chapter 40 the book speaks to a people who are in that exile, comforting them, mocking the idols of Babylon, and naming the Persian king Cyrus, who would conquer Babylon in 539 BC and let the exiles go home, as the LORD’s “shepherd” and “anointed” (44:28; 45:1).

The shape of the book

Isaiah falls into three great movements:

  • Chapters 1–39, set mostly in Isaiah’s own lifetime: warnings to Judah and Jerusalem, Isaiah’s call, the promise of a child on David’s throne, oracles against the nations, and the story of Hezekiah and the Assyrian siege (36–39), which forms a bridge to what follows.
  • Chapters 40–55, words of comfort to the exiles in Babylon: the LORD is coming to lead them home through the desert, the idols are nothing, and a servant of the LORD will suffer and bring salvation to the ends of the earth.
  • Chapters 56–66, addressed to a people back in the land but still waiting: calls to true worship and justice, the glory of a renewed Zion, and the promise of new heavens and a new earth.

Christians hold different views of how the book came to be. Jewish and Christian tradition received it all as the work of Isaiah son of Amoz, who foresaw the exile and Cyrus by the Spirit of God, and many still hold that view; the New Testament quotes passages from every part of the book under Isaiah’s name. Many scholars think chapters 40–66 come from later prophets who stood in Isaiah’s tradition and spoke to the exile and the return. Whichever view readers take, the book is one book, bound together by its themes, its picture of Zion, and above all its name for God.

The vision in the temple

Isaiah tells of his call in chapter 6. “In the year King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord sitting on a throne, high and towering, and the hem of his robe filled the temple.” A king of Judah had died; the true King was on his throne. Seraphim, fiery beings with six wings, called to one another the words at the head of this introduction, and the doorways shook. Isaiah’s response was terror:

— Isaiah 6:5

A seraph took a burning coal from the altar and touched his lips: “Your guilt is removed, and atonement is made for your sin.” Only then came the question and the answer:

{{OWB:ISA.6.8.s1}}

— Isaiah 6:8

The message he was given was hard: the people would hear and not understand, until the land lay desolate and only a stump was left. Jesus quoted these words to explain why many heard his parables without understanding (Matthew 13:14–15), and Paul quoted them at the very end of Acts. But the chapter ends with a seed of hope: “its stump is a holy seed.”

The Holy One of Israel

From that vision comes Isaiah’s favourite name for God, “the Holy One of Israel”. It appears some twenty-five times in Isaiah, in every part of the book, and only a handful of times in the rest of the Old Testament. The holy God is utterly other, high and lifted up, and yet he has bound himself to one people: he is Israel’s Holy One.

For Isaiah, that holiness demands trust. When Ahaz panicked at the armies of Syria and Israel, Isaiah met him at “the end of the water channel from the Upper Pool, on the road to the Fuller’s Field” (7:3) and told him, “If you do not hold firm in trust, you will not be held firm” (7:9). Ahaz trusted Assyria instead. Years later, the Assyrian commander stood at exactly the same spot (36:2) to demand Jerusalem’s surrender. This time King Hezekiah took the enemy’s letter into the temple, “opened it out in the presence of the LORD” (37:14), and prayed; and the city was saved. The lesson is summed up in one of Isaiah’s great sayings: “Your strength lies in staying calm and trusting” (30:15).

Justice, judgment and mercy

Isaiah begins not with comfort but with an accusation. The heavens and the earth are called to listen: the LORD raised children, “but they have rebelled against me” (1:2). Their worship is busy and empty, their hands are full of blood, and the LORD is weary of their sacrifices. What he wants is plain: “Learn to do good. Go after justice. Set the oppressor straight. Give the fatherless child a fair hearing; stand up for the widow in court” (1:17). And then, in the middle of the indictment, comes an invitation:

{{OWB:ISA.1.18.s1}}

— Isaiah 1:18

In chapter 5 Isaiah sings a love song about a vineyard that its owner tended with every care, which still produced only rotten grapes. The vineyard is Israel. The song ends with a play on words that is lost in translation: the LORD looked for mishpat, justice, and found mispah, bloodshed; for tsedaqah, righteousness, and heard tse‘aqah, a cry. Later the book will say that the fast God chooses is to “loose the bonds that wickedness has tied” and “to break your bread with someone who is hungry” (58:6–7). Holiness, in Isaiah, always has to do with how the weak are treated.

The child and the king

When Ahaz refused to ask for a sign, Isaiah gave him one anyway:

{{OWB:ISA.7.14.s1}}

— Isaiah 7:14

The sign was for Ahaz’s own day: before the child was old enough to know good from evil, the two kings he feared would be gone (7:16). But the promise of a child does not stop there. Two chapters later the darkness lifts over Galilee, “The people walking in the dark have seen a great light” (9:2), and the reason is a birth:

{{OWB:ISA.9.5.s1}}

— Isaiah 9:6

Chapter 11 adds that “A shoot will grow from Jesse’s stump”, a new David from the felled tree of David’s line, on whom the Spirit of the LORD will rest, who will judge the poor with justice, and in whose kingdom “The wolf will stay as a guest with the lamb” (11:6).

Matthew’s Gospel reads Isaiah 7:14 as fulfilled in the birth of Jesus to the virgin Mary, quoting it in the ancient Greek translation, which renders the Hebrew word for “young woman” with a word that usually means “virgin”. Readers have long discussed whether Isaiah himself foresaw a virgin birth, or whether God’s promise was larger than the prophet knew. Christians agree with Matthew that the child whose name means “God is with us” has come, and that the names of chapter 9 belong to him.

The mountain of the LORD

Isaiah’s hope is never for Israel alone. In chapter 2 the nations stream to Jerusalem to learn God’s ways:

{{OWB:ISA.2.4.s1}}

— Isaiah 2:4

In chapter 25 the LORD spreads a feast on his mountain “for all the peoples”, and does what no one else can do: “He will swallow up death for all time. The Lord GOD will wipe every face clean of tears” (25:8). Paul quotes those words when he writes of the resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:54), and Revelation echoes them in its last vision (Revelation 21:4).

“Bring comfort, bring comfort”

Chapter 40 opens a new movement with words that have been set to music more often than almost any in the Bible:

{{OWB:ISA.40.1.s1}} {{OWB:ISA.40.2.s1}}

— Isaiah 40:1–2

A voice calls for a road to be built in the wilderness for the LORD, the voice that all four Gospels hear in John the Baptist. People wither like grass, “but the word of our God holds firm for all time” (40:8). The God who measures the seas in his hand comes “Like a shepherd” and gathers the lambs in his arms (40:11). And the chapter ends with one of the best-loved promises in Scripture:

{{OWB:ISA.40.31.s1}}

— Isaiah 40:31

Chapters 40–48 also mount the Bible’s sharpest attack on idolatry. A man cuts down a tree, warms himself with half of it and bakes his bread, and “What is left he makes into a god” (44:17). Against such gods the LORD speaks: “I am the first and I am the last; there is no god but me” (44:6). And because there is only one God, his salvation is for everyone: “to me every knee will bow” (45:23), words Paul applies to Jesus (Philippians 2:10–11).

The servant of the LORD

In these chapters a figure appears called the servant of the LORD. Sometimes the servant is plainly Israel: “But you, Israel, are my servant” (41:8). Yet in four passages, often called the Servant Songs (42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12), the servant is one who has a mission to Israel as well as to the nations. He is gentle: “He will not snap off a split reed or smother a wick that is barely burning” (42:3). He is a light for the nations, “so that you may be my salvation to the farthest edge of the earth” (49:6). He is beaten and spat upon and does not turn away (50:6). And in the last song he dies for others:

{{OWB:ISA.53.5.s1}} {{OWB:ISA.53.6.s1}}

— Isaiah 53:5–6

He goes silent like a lamb to the slaughter, is buried, and yet afterward “he will see, and he will have his fill” and “make the many righteous” (53:11).

Who is this servant? An Ethiopian official reading this chapter in his chariot asked the same question: “who is the prophet talking about here? Is he speaking of himself, or of another person?” (Acts 8:34). Jewish interpreters have often understood the servant as Israel, or a faithful remnant within Israel, suffering among the nations. Philip, “Starting from that very Scripture,” told the official about Jesus (Acts 8:35), and the church has always read Isaiah 53 as the clearest picture in the Old Testament of the cross. Matthew applies the first song to Jesus (Matthew 12:18–21); Peter writes, “By the bruises he bore you have been healed” (1 Peter 2:24).

New heavens and a new earth

The last chapters widen the invitation again. “Listen, all who thirst: come down to the water!” (55:1). “Rise up and shine, for your light has come” (60:1). In chapter 61 an anointed messenger announces good news to the lowly and freedom for captives. In the synagogue at Nazareth Jesus read that passage aloud, stopped in the middle of a sentence before “a day when our God takes vengeance”, rolled up the scroll and said, “Today, as you have been listening, these words of Scripture have come true” (Luke 4:21).

And at the end Isaiah’s vision reaches as far as any in the Old Testament:

{{OWB:ISA.65.17.s1}}

— Isaiah 65:17

Yet the book does not end with a garden. Its last verse looks out on the bodies of those who rebelled, whose “worm will not die” (66:24), the words Jesus used for the final judgment (Mark 9:48). Isaiah holds together what we are tempted to separate: the holiness that judges and the love that saves. In the synagogue, by long custom, the verse before the last is read again at the end, so that the book closes on all humanity coming to worship.

How to read Isaiah

  • Read it in its three movements. Chapters 1–39, 40–55 and 56–66 each have their own voice. Many readers begin with 40–55.
  • Keep the history close. The stories of Ahaz and Hezekiah in 2 Kings 16–20 make chapters 7–12 and 36–39 come alive; chapters 36–39 are told almost word for word in 2 Kings 18–20.
  • Read the poetry aloud. Isaiah is one of the great poets of the world, and his book is meant to be heard.
  • Watch for the threads. The Holy One of Israel, Zion, the highway in the wilderness, the nations streaming to the light, the remnant, the servant.
  • Read it with the New Testament open. Follow the passages the Gospels and the apostles quote, and see how they read them.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Most of Isaiah is poetry, and this edition prints each line of the poetry on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out; the stories of chapters 36–39 are printed as prose. A few of its choices are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD of Armies.” LORD in capitals stands for God’s personal name, YHWH. Isaiah’s favourite title for God, older Bibles’ “the LORD of hosts”, is rendered “the LORD of Armies”: the hosts are the armies of heaven. Where the Hebrew joins the word “Lord” to God’s name, it reads “the Lord GOD”.

The names of the child. In 9:6 each of the four names is translated by what it means: “Planner of Wonders, God the Mighty Warrior, Father for All Time, Ruler of Peace”, rather than the familiar “Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace”.

“The young woman.” In 7:14 the Hebrew word for a young woman of marriageable age is translated “the young woman”, with a note on the Greek translation’s “virgin” and Matthew’s use of it.

Numbering. The Hebrew numbers the verses of chapter 9 one behind most English Bibles, and those of chapter 64 as well. The references in this edition follow the English numbering.
