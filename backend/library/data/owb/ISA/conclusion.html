Isaiah began with a rebellious people and a land in ruins. It ends with new heavens and a new earth, and with a promise that God’s people, and their name, will stand as firm as the new creation itself. Between the two lie the throne room and the burning coal, the child and the shoot from Jesse’s stump, the highway in the wilderness, the servant pierced for others, and a feast for all the peoples on the mountain of the LORD.

But Isaiah is honest to the very end. Its last verse looks at those who rebelled and would not come. The book will not let its readers forget that the Holy One of Israel is holy, or that his invitation can be refused.

The question the book leaves with you

Two questions in Isaiah stay with the reader long after the last page. The first is the one Isaiah heard in the temple: “Whom am I to send? Who will go on our behalf?” (6:8). The second is the question that opens the song of the suffering servant:

— Isaiah 53:1

The good news that reached Israel was strange news: that God would save the world through a servant who was despised and rejected, who had “no fine looks to make us long for him”, and whose death would bring healing to those who had turned their faces away. Paul quotes this question when he writes about those who had heard the gospel and not believed (Romans 10:16). Isaiah asks it of everyone who reads him. The book is full of invitations — “Come, let us take this matter to court together”, “Listen, all who thirst: come down to the water!”, “Turn toward me and be saved” — and each of them waits for an answer. “Look for the LORD while he can still be found; call to him while he is close by” (55:6).

Where Isaiah points

The New Testament reads Isaiah as a book about Jesus. The Gospels open with Isaiah’s voice in the wilderness, and Matthew with Isaiah’s child called Immanuel. Jesus reads Isaiah 61 in the synagogue and says it has come true. Matthew finds the gentle servant of chapter 42 in Jesus’s healing; Philip finds Jesus in chapter 53; Paul hears the good news of chapter 52 in every messenger of the gospel. John goes further still: after quoting Isaiah 6, he writes, “Isaiah said these things because he saw his glory and spoke about him” (John 12:41). The King on the high throne was the one who would be lifted up on a cross.

And the last book of the Bible ends with Isaiah’s last vision: “Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth” (Revelation 21:1). The one on the throne says, “See, I am making everything new!” The hope Isaiah saw from afar is the hope the church still waits for.

Where to go from here

Isaiah has been preached, sung and loved by the church in every century, and some of the best of that work is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the vision of the Holy One (Isaiah 6). A. W. Tozer’s sermon The Seraphim’s Worship, on 6:1–4.
  • On the texts of Handel’s Messiah. John Newton’s discourses Comfort Ye My People (40:1), Unto Us a Child Is Born (9:6) and Messiah Suffering and Wounded for Us (53:4–5).
  • On the prophecies of Christ. Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, the second-century conversation in which Justin argues from Isaiah, among other prophets, that Jesus is the Christ, with chapters on Isaiah 7:14.
  • On the gentle servant (Isaiah 42:1–3). Richard Sibbes’s The Bruised Reed, on the reed he will not snap and the wick he will not smother, as Matthew 12 quotes them.
  • On waiting and renewed strength (Isaiah 40). Alexander Maclaren’s The Secret of Immortal Youth (40:30–31), and Andrew Murray’s Waiting on God, which takes several of its thirty-one days from Isaiah (8:17; 25:9; 30:18; 40:31; 64:4).
  • On God’s care for his people. Charles H. Spurgeon’s Fear Not (41:14) and Maclaren’s The Writing on God’s Hands (49:16).
  • On the great invitation (Isaiah 55). Maclaren’s The Call to the Thirsty.
  • On the Spirit in Isaiah. The chapter “The Holy Spirit in the Book of Isaiah” in A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, Part I, which traces the Spirit from Isaiah’s own consecration to the Spirit resting on the promised Christ, on Israel and on each believer.
  • On Hezekiah’s prayer (Isaiah 37). The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “Opening the Letter Before the Lord” follows the king who spread the Assyrian letter before God, as 2 Kings 19 tells it.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Isaiah in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
