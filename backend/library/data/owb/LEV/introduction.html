{{OWB:LEV.20.26.s1}}

— Leviticus 20:26

Many plans to read the whole Bible come to grief in Leviticus. After the drama of Exodus, the reader meets chapter after chapter of sacrifices, priests, skin diseases and food laws, and wonders what any of it has to do with them. Yet an old Jewish custom had children begin their study of Scripture here, and the letter to the Hebrews cannot explain what Jesus has done without it. Leviticus is the book that teaches what it means for a holy God to live among his people. Once you see that, it becomes one of the most searching books in the Bible.

In Hebrew it is called Vayikra, “And he called”, from its first word. The name we use comes, through Latin, from the ancient Greek translation, and means “the Levitical book”, after the tribe of Levi, from which the priests came. Both names point to the same scene. Exodus ended with the glory of the LORD filling the tabernacle, so that even Moses could not go in. Leviticus begins with God calling to Moses from inside it: “From the tent of meeting the LORD called to Moses and spoke to him.” And the first thing he speaks of is how an Israelite may bring “an animal to the LORD as an offering”.

Notice that word “offering”. In Hebrew it comes from the verb “to bring near”. The God who has come to live among his people now tells them how they may come near to him. Everything in the book, the altar, the priests, the washings, the festivals, serves that one purpose.

The shape of the book

Leviticus has almost no story. The whole book is set at Mount Sinai, in the month after the tabernacle was finished, and it is nearly all speech: “The LORD spoke to Moses” is its constant refrain. But it is carefully built, and its plan is worth knowing before you begin:

  • offerings and sacrifices (chapters 1–7)
  • the priests ordained, and the first day of worship (8–10)
  • clean and unclean (11–15)
  • the Day of Atonement (16)
  • a holy people: life, worship and the land (17–27)

The Day of Atonement stands at the centre, and the book turns on it. Before it, the focus is on the sanctuary: how God’s house can be kept and cleansed. After it, the focus widens to the whole of life: how God’s people can be holy in their homes, their fields, their markets and their courts. Scholars often call chapters 17–26 the Holiness Code, from its great repeated command, “You must be holy, because I, the LORD your God, am holy.”

Offerings: coming near

The first seven chapters describe five kinds of offering. The burnt offering is given wholly to God, turned into smoke on the altar, “a soothing aroma to the LORD.” The grain offering is a gift of flour and oil, always with salt, “the salt of your God’s covenant”. The peace offering is a shared meal, in which part goes to God, part to the priests, and the rest is eaten by the worshiper and his household. The sin offering deals with sins done unintentionally, and with impurity. The guilt offering deals with wrongs that can be repaid, and requires that the worshiper pay back what was taken, “with a fifth of its value added”.

Again and again, the worshiper does something with his own hands:

{{OWB:LEV.1.4.s1}}

— Leviticus 1:4

The animal stands in for the person. Its life is given, and the one who brings it is accepted. Readers have long noticed, too, how carefully the law makes room for the poor: whoever cannot afford a lamb may bring two birds, and whoever cannot afford birds may bring flour. When Mary came to the temple after Jesus was born, Luke says, she brought “a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons”, the offering of a poor family.

Two things are easy to miss. First, sacrifice in Leviticus is not a way of buying God off. He is the one who gives it: “I have given the blood to you to make atonement on the altar for your lives.” Second, the sacrifices cover sins of weakness and ignorance, not deliberate defiance of God. They require confession: “Whoever is guilty in any of these ways must confess the sin they have committed.” The book never imagines that ritual can stand in for a changed heart.

Priests, fire and glory

In chapters 8–9 Moses ordains Aaron and his sons as priests, washing them, clothing them, anointing them and marking them with blood. On the eighth day Aaron offers sacrifices for the first time, and blesses the people, and God answers:

{{OWB:LEV.9.24.s1}}

— Leviticus 9:24

And then, on that very day, disaster. Two of Aaron’s sons, Nadab and Abihu, offer “an improper fire, one he had not commanded them to bring.” The same fire comes out from the LORD and devours them. The text does not say exactly what their sin was, and readers have guessed at it ever since. What it does say is what Moses says to his grieving brother: “Through those who come near me I will show that I am holy.” And it adds four of the saddest words in the book: “And Aaron said nothing.”

The story is placed here to teach the reader the stakes. The nearness of God is the greatest gift Israel has, and the most dangerous. That is why the priests are given a task that frames the next chapters: to “tell apart what is holy and what is common, what is unclean and what is clean.”

Clean and unclean

Chapters 11–15 deal with what makes a person unclean, and how they can be made clean again: animals that may and may not be eaten, childbirth, skin disease and mold, and the flows of the body. These chapters are the strangest in the book to modern readers, and it helps to know three things.

First, being unclean is not the same as sinning. A mother who has just given birth is unclean; so is anyone who has touched the dead. Uncleanness was part of ordinary life, and the law gave ways to deal with it. What mattered was that it should not be brought into God’s presence: “so that it does not bring death on them when they defile my tabernacle, which is among them.”

Second, many of the causes of uncleanness have to do with death: a corpse, the loss of blood, a disease that eats away the skin. The God of Israel is the living God, and the laws keep teaching that life and death cannot share the same space. Hardest of all was the life of someone with a serious skin disease, who had to live “outside the camp” and call out, “Unclean, unclean!” It is worth remembering that sentence when you read in the Gospels how Jesus stretched out his hand and touched such a man, and he was made clean.

Third, the food laws marked Israel off from the nations. Chapter 20 ties the two together: Israel must distinguish clean animals from unclean, because God has set Israel apart from the other peoples. Christians have generally read these laws as fulfilled in Christ, who, in Mark’s words, “made all foods clean”, and whose church brings the nations in.

The Day of Atonement

Chapter 16 opens by recalling the death of Aaron’s sons, and then tells Aaron how he may come into the Most Holy Place, where God appears above the mercy seat. Not “at any time he pleases”, but once a year, on the tenth day of the seventh month, after washing and dressing in plain linen, and only with blood.

Two goats are chosen by lot, one “for the LORD” and one “for Azazel”. The first is killed as a sin offering for the people, and its blood is sprinkled on the mercy seat, to cleanse the sanctuary from the impurities of Israel. Then comes the scene that has given the English language the word “scapegoat”:

{{OWB:LEV.16.21.s1}} {{OWB:LEV.16.22.s1}}

— Leviticus 16:21–22

No one knows for certain what Azazel means; the word occurs only in this chapter. It may be the name of a wilderness power, or a word for the remote place where the goat is sent. Either way, the picture is clear. Sin is not only covered over. It is carried away, as far as possible from the camp and from God. And the chapter ends with a promise for the whole people:

{{OWB:LEV.16.30.s1}}

— Leviticus 16:30

The letter to the Hebrews reads this day as a picture of Christ, who “entered the sanctuary once for all” with his own blood, not into a copy made by hands but “into heaven itself”.

The life is in the blood

Chapter 17 explains why blood matters so much in Leviticus, and why Israel must never eat it:

{{OWB:LEV.17.11.s1}}

— Leviticus 17:11

Blood stands for life, and life belongs to God. Atonement is made by a life given up. That is the thought behind the New Testament’s many sayings about the blood of Christ, and behind Hebrews’ summary of the whole law: “where no blood is shed there is no forgiveness.”

“You must be holy”

From chapter 18 onward, holiness moves out of the sanctuary into everyday life. Israel is not to copy “what is done in the land of Egypt, where you once lived, or what is done in the land of Canaan, where I am bringing you.” Chapter 19 shows what the alternative looks like, and it is one of the richest chapters in the Old Testament. It mixes laws about worship with laws about farming, business and the courts, as though they were all one thing, because in God’s eyes they are:

{{OWB:LEV.19.9.s1}} {{OWB:LEV.19.10.s1}}

— Leviticus 19:9–10

Pay workers the same day; do not curse the deaf or trip the blind; do not favour the poor or the powerful in court; keep honest scales. And then, in the middle of it all:

{{OWB:LEV.19.18.s1}}

— Leviticus 19:18

Jesus called this the second great commandment, Paul says that the whole law comes to its fullness in it, and James calls it “the royal law”. Sixteen verses later Leviticus applies the same words to the foreigner: “You are to love them as yourself, since you yourselves were outsiders in the land of Egypt.” Notice the refrain that closes so many of these laws: “I am the LORD.” Holiness here is not a mood or a feeling. It is a way of treating people, grounded in who God is.

Holy times: festivals, Sabbath and Jubilee

Chapter 23 sets out Israel’s year: the weekly Sabbath, Passover and Unleavened Bread in spring, the offering of the first sheaf, the Festival of Weeks fifty days later, and in the autumn a day of trumpet blasts, the Day of Atonement and the Festival of Booths, when the people live in shelters to remember the wilderness. Christians will recognise Passover, and the fiftieth day, which came to be known by its Greek name, Pentecost.

Chapter 25 widens the Sabbath to the land itself. Every seventh year the fields are to rest. And every fiftieth year is a Jubilee:

{{OWB:LEV.25.10.s1}}

— Leviticus 25:10

Debt, poverty and the loss of family land were not allowed to become permanent, because “the land belongs to me. You are only outsiders and residents living with me.” Whether Israel ever kept the Jubilee fully is not known, but its hope runs through the prophets. When Jesus began his ministry in Nazareth, he read from Isaiah of a messenger sent “to announce freedom to the captives” and “to announce the year when the Lord shows favor.”

Blessing, warning and hope

Chapter 26 sets before Israel the blessings of faithfulness and the consequences of rebellion, ending in exile. The blessings reach their height in a promise of presence:

{{OWB:LEV.26.11.s1}} {{OWB:LEV.26.12.s1}}

— Leviticus 26:11–12

Paul quotes these words to the Corinthians: “We, after all, are the temple of the living God.” And even the warnings do not end in despair. If the scattered people confess their sin, God will remember his covenant with Jacob, Isaac and Abraham: “I will not cast them off.”

How to read Leviticus

Read it with Exodus behind you and Hebrews beside you; chapters 9 and 10 of Hebrews draw again and again on Leviticus 16. Do not try to master every detail of every sacrifice on a first reading. Ask instead, in each chapter, what it shows about God: that he is holy, that he wants to be near, that sin and death are serious, and that he provides the way back.

Listen, too, for the refrains. “I am the LORD” closes law after law, as a signature. “Make atonement” and “they will be forgiven” return again and again in the opening chapters, like a promise repeated until it is believed. And notice how often the poor, the outsider, the deaf and the blind appear in a book that seems at first to be only about ritual. Leviticus never separates the worship of God from the care of the neighbour.

Christians have long debated how these laws apply to them. Many have distinguished moral laws, which still bind, from ceremonial laws, fulfilled in Christ, and civil laws, given for Israel as a nation; others prefer to say that the whole law is fulfilled in Christ and read through him. Either way, the New Testament treats Leviticus not as something to be thrown away but as a shadow of the good things to come.

The book itself says that its commands were given by the LORD to Moses at Sinai (27:34), and Jewish and Christian tradition has received it as one of the five books of Moses. Many scholars think it was shaped by Israel’s priests over a long period and reached its final form after the exile; others argue that much of it is very old. Christians hold a range of views on how it came together, while receiving it as Scripture.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Leviticus are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”.

Offerings and atonement. The five offerings are called the burnt offering, the grain offering, the peace offering, the sin offering and the guilt offering. The priest’s work for the worshiper is “make atonement”, kept in the same words throughout the book, so the reader can follow it.

“Serious skin disease.” The word older Bibles render “leprosy” covers a range of skin conditions, and the same word is used of marks on cloth, leather and walls. The translation calls the first “serious skin disease” and the second “mold”.

Azazel. The goat of chapter 16 is sent “to Azazel”, keeping the Hebrew word, whose meaning is uncertain, rather than the older “scapegoat”.

“The outsider.” The foreigner who lives among Israel, protected by the law, is usually “the outsider living among you”.
