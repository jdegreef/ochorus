Leviticus ends as quietly as it began. There is no battle, no journey, no death of a hero. The last chapter is about vows and tithes, the gifts people promise to God and how they may redeem them, and then a single sentence telling us where all of this was spoken. The camp is still at Sinai. The cloud still rests on the tent. But Israel now knows how to live with the God who lives among them.

The question the book leaves with you

Leviticus asks more of its readers than any book before it. “You must be holy, because I am holy.” It reaches into kitchens and fields, into marriages and markets, into the way a worker is paid and a blind man is treated. And it is honest about how far short people fall. The priests who stand nearest to God die for their carelessness on the first day. The high priest must make atonement for his own sins before he can make it for anyone else’s. And the great Day of Atonement has to come round again every year.

So the book leaves an ache. Can anyone really come near to God? Can sin ever be taken away for good, and not only covered for another year? Can holiness be more than a rule from outside? The letter to the Hebrews puts the question plainly: “For the law holds only a shadow of the good things to come, not the very form of the things themselves.” The sacrifices “bring sins back to mind every year, because the blood of bulls and goats cannot take sins away.”

Where the book points

The New Testament answers by pointing to Jesus as everything Leviticus describes. He is the priest: “We have, then, a great high priest who has gone through the heavens: Jesus the Son of God.” He is the sacrifice, the one John the Baptist called “the Lamb of God, who carries away the sin of the world”. He is the one who touched the unclean and made them clean. And by his death the curtain that hid the Most Holy Place is opened to all his people:

“So then, brothers and sisters, the blood of Jesus has given us confidence to go into the sanctuary. He has opened for us a way that is new and living, a way through the curtain, which is his flesh.”

— Hebrews 10:19–20

Leviticus does not become useless once Christ has come. Its command to holiness is quoted by Peter: “You are to be holy, because I am holy.” Its second great commandment, love of neighbour, is the one Jesus set beside the first. And its whole language of offering is taken up by Paul, who asks the church to “offer your bodies to God as a living sacrifice, holy and pleasing to him.” The altar has moved, but the call to come near has not.

Where to go from here

Leviticus is best read with company. Some of the best in this library:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Leviticus: Communion” reads the book as a guide to fellowship with God.
  • On the priesthood and the Day of Atonement. The Letter to the Hebrews in this library’s Open Word Bible edition, the New Testament’s own commentary on Leviticus; F. B. Meyer’s The Way into the Holiest, an exposition of Hebrews, with chapters on “The True Tabernacle” and “The Blood of Christ”; and Charles H. Spurgeon’s Salvation to the Uttermost, on Christ the priest who is “always alive to plead on their behalf” (Hebrews 7:25).
  • On the veil and the Most Holy Place. Horatius Bonar’s The Rent Veil, which moves from the veil of the tabernacle to the veil torn at the cross and the blood carried within it.
  • On the bread of God (Leviticus 21:22). Horatius Bonar’s The Divine Banquet, which reads the bread of the presence, eaten by the priests, as a picture of Christ.
  • On the covenant with Levi. Andrew Murray’s The Two Covenants, with its chapter “The Covenant of an Everlasting Priesthood”, on the tribe of Levi as a picture of the priesthood every believer is called to share.
  • On atonement. William Booth’s The Atonement of Jesus Christ and William J. Seymour’s The Precious Atonement, two sermons on what the blood of Christ accomplishes.
  • On being holy (Leviticus 19:2). J. C. Ryle’s Holiness, whose title chapter, on Hebrews 12:14, asks what practical holiness looks like.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Leviticus in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
