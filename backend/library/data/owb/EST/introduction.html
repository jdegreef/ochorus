— Esther 4:14

Esther is one of the great stories of the Bible, and one of the strangest. It has a beautiful orphan who becomes queen, a wise guardian at the palace gate, a villain who builds a gallows for his enemy and is hanged on it himself, a king who cannot sleep, and a sudden, glorious reversal. It is told with suspense, irony and even humour. And in all its ten chapters, the name of God is never mentioned.

There is no prayer in it, though there is fasting. There is no temple, no sacrifice, no prophet, no miracle, and no mention of the law of Moses or the covenant. Yet the Jewish people have read it every year for more than two thousand years at the feast of Purim, and the church has kept it among the Scriptures. Its silence about God is not an accident. It is the book’s way of asking where God is when he cannot be seen, and of answering, without saying so, that he is everywhere.

Jews who stayed behind

The story is set in Susa, one of the capitals of the Persian empire, in the reign of King Ahasuerus. Most scholars identify him with Xerxes I, who ruled from 486 to 465 BC, and whose name in Persian lies behind the Hebrew form. The book describes him as ruling “127 provinces stretching from India to Cush”, from the Indus to the upper Nile. Greek historians tell of Xerxes’ great banquets, his failed invasion of Greece, and his queen Amestris, and scholars have long debated how the book’s account fits with theirs.

That places the story between the two halves of the book of Ezra: after the temple was rebuilt in Jerusalem, and before Ezra came from Babylon to teach the law. It is a story about the Jews who did not go home. Decades after Cyrus had let them return, most remained scattered across the empire, living as a minority among other peoples, under foreign law, at the mercy of the powerful. Esther is the story of what happened when those powers turned against them.

A book of banquets

The book is built around feasts. It opens with the king’s display of his wealth, “180 days in all”, followed by a seven-day banquet for everyone in Susa, with wine served in gold cups. Esther’s coronation is marked by a banquet. When the decree to destroy the Jews has gone out, the king and Haman “sat down to drink”. Esther exposes Haman at a banquet of her own making. And the book ends with the feasts of Purim, kept every year ever after. Feasting frames the story, and the reversal at its centre is a reversal of feasts: the drinking of the powerful turned into the joy of the rescued.

Vashti, and a king’s law

Chapter 1 shows us the world of the story. At the end of his banquet, the king, “in high spirits from the wine”, orders Queen Vashti to be brought in, wearing her crown, so that he can show off her beauty to his guests. She refuses. The king is furious, and his advisers fear that every wife in the empire will follow her example. So a law is issued, in “the laws of Persia and Media, which cannot be revoked,” that “every man should be master in his own house”.

The narrator does not comment, but the irony is plain. The most powerful man in the world cannot command his own wife, and his answer is to send letters to every province in every language. The king of this book is a man of vast power and very little judgement. That will matter. And so will the law that cannot be revoked.

Esther and Mordecai

To replace Vashti, young women are gathered from across the empire into the king’s harem. Among them is Hadassah, that is, Esther, an orphan raised by her older cousin Mordecai, “a Jew in the citadel of Susa”, a Benjaminite whose family had been carried into exile from Jerusalem. Hadassah is her Hebrew name and means “myrtle”; Esther is the name she is known by in the Persian court. On Mordecai’s instructions, she keeps her people a secret.

Esther wins the favour of everyone who sees her, and the king makes her queen. Some readers admire her poise; others are uneasy that a Jewish woman should enter a pagan king’s harem and conceal who she is, and Christian interpreters have read her early chapters in both ways. The book itself passes no verdict. It simply shows how little power she has over her own life, and how she uses what she is given.

The chapter ends with what looks like a loose end. Mordecai, sitting at the king’s gate, uncovers a plot by two of the king’s eunuchs to assassinate him, and tells Esther, who tells the king. The plotters are hanged, and the matter is “recorded in the book of the annals in the king’s presence.” Mordecai receives no reward. The reader files it away.

Haman the Agagite

In chapter 3 the king promotes Haman son of Hammedatha, “the Agagite”, and orders everyone to bow to him. Mordecai refuses. The book does not say why, except that “he had told them that he was a Jew.” But the names tell an old story. Agag was the king of the Amalekites, Israel’s ancient enemy, whom King Saul, son of Kish the Benjaminite, was commanded to destroy and spared (1 Samuel 15). Mordecai too is “son of Kish, a Benjaminite”. The quarrel at the king’s gate is the latest round of a very long war.

Haman is not content to punish one man. He looks “for a way to wipe out all the Jews throughout the kingdom of Ahasuerus.” He casts lots, the pur, to choose the day, and the lot falls on the thirteenth of Adar, eleven months away. Then he goes to the king with a speech that has been heard, in other words, many times since:

“For a time like this”

Mordecai tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth and goes through the city “crying out loudly and bitterly.” Across the empire the Jews mourn and fast. Esther, in the palace, hears only that her cousin is in sackcloth, and sends him clothes. Through a servant, Mordecai tells her everything and asks her to go to the king and plead for her people.

Esther’s answer is reasonable. Anyone who goes to the king uninvited may be put to death, unless he holds out his gold scepter, and the king has not called for her in thirty days. Mordecai’s reply, the verse that opens this introduction, is the heart of the book. He is sure the Jews will be rescued: help “will come to the Jews from another place”. He does not say where from. But he warns Esther that silence will not save her, and then he asks his great question: “And who knows? Perhaps you have become queen for a time like this.”

Esther’s answer marks the moment the orphan girl becomes the leader of her people. For the first time she gives Mordecai orders:

A fast in the Bible goes with seeking God. The book does not say that anyone prays. But every reader knows what three days of fasting by a whole people in mortal danger means.

Two banquets and a sleepless night

On the third day Esther puts on her royal robes and stands in the inner court. The king holds out the scepter: “Even if it is half the kingdom, it will be given to you.” And Esther, instead of asking, invites the king and Haman to a banquet. At the banquet she invites them to another. The delay is maddening, and it is deliberate on the narrator’s part. Something is about to happen that Esther could not have arranged.

Haman goes home delighted, sees Mordecai at the gate, and boils over: “Yet none of this satisfies me as long as I see Mordecai the Jew sitting at the king’s gate.” His wife and friends advise him to build a gallows, about seventy-five feet high, and ask the king in the morning to hang Mordecai on it. Then comes the turning point of the whole book, in one short sentence: “That night the king could not sleep.”

The king has the annals read to him, and the reader finds that the loose end of chapter 2 was not loose after all. The record of Mordecai’s loyalty is read out, and the king learns that nothing has been done for him. At that very moment Haman arrives at the palace to ask for Mordecai’s death. The king asks him, “What should be done for the man the king delights to honor?” Haman thinks, “Whom would the king delight to honor more than me?”, and describes a royal parade. He then has to lead it himself, with Mordecai on the horse. Even Haman’s wife sees where things are going: “You will certainly fall before him.”

The reversal

At the second banquet Esther finally speaks. She asks for her life and the life of her people, “For I and my people have been sold to be destroyed, killed, and wiped out.” The king demands to know who has dared to do this, and Esther points across the table: “An adversary and an enemy! This evil Haman!” Within a few verses it is over:

— Esther 7:10

But the danger is not over. Haman’s decree, sealed with the king’s ring, cannot be revoked, and the thirteenth of Adar is still coming. So the king gives Mordecai the ring and lets him write a second decree, allowing the Jews in every city to gather and defend themselves against anyone who attacks them. Mordecai goes out from the king in royal robes, and the city that had been thrown into confusion now shouts for joy: “For the Jews it was a time of light and gladness, joy and honor.”

When the day comes, the book sums it up in two Hebrew words that could serve as its motto, “it was turned around”, translated here “the opposite happened”. The Jews overpower those who hated them. Chapter 9 records the killing of their enemies in Susa and the provinces, including Haman’s ten sons, and it is hard reading. Readers have responded in different ways. Some stress that the Jews act under the king’s decree, in self-defence against an attack the empire had authorised. Others are troubled by the numbers and by the second day of fighting that Esther asks for in Susa. The book itself repeats one detail three times, as if to make sure we notice it: “But they did not lay a hand on the plunder.” Saul had seized the plunder of the Amalekites, and lost his kingdom for it. This time, the war is not for gain.

Purim

The last part of the book explains the festival. The days of fighting become days of rest, “of feasting and joy, of sending gifts of food to one another and gifts to the poor”, to remember “the month that was turned for them from grief into joy and from mourning into a holiday.” They are called Purim, after Haman’s lots: “That is why these days are called Purim, from the word Pur.” The man who cast lots to choose the day of destruction gave his name, in a sense, to the day of deliverance.

Jews still keep Purim on the fourteenth of Adar, or on the fifteenth in Jerusalem and other anciently walled cities, following the book’s distinction between Susa and the villages. The scroll of Esther is read aloud in the synagogue, and the congregation drowns out Haman’s name with noise every time it is read. The day before is kept by many as the Fast of Esther. The book closes briefly, with Mordecai second only to the king, a man who “worked for the good of his people and spoke up for the well-being of all their descendants.”

The hidden God

Why is God never named? The book has been read in many ways. Some have thought it a purely national story that only later found a place in Scripture, and its place in the canon was questioned by some in both synagogue and church; Martin Luther was famously uneasy with it. The ancient Greek translation added six passages, more than a hundred verses in all, including prayers of Mordecai and Esther that name God freely. These additions are printed in Catholic and Orthodox Bibles; Protestant Bibles, like this one, follow the Hebrew.

But most readers, Jewish and Christian, have come to see the silence as the book’s point. The God of Esther does not part seas or send fire from heaven. He works through a queen’s courage and a king’s insomnia, through a forgotten record and a villain’s pride, through what looks like coincidence, until the coincidences become too many to be coincidences. The book does not tell us that God is at work. It lets us see it for ourselves. That is the way most believers experience God for most of their lives, and Esther is the book of the Bible for those times.

How to read Esther

Esther is a story, and it should be read the way a story is meant to be read: in one sitting, if possible, and preferably aloud. It takes less than an hour. Notice how carefully it is built: almost every scene in the first half has a mirror in the second, as the threats against the Jews come back on the heads of those who made them. Watch for the irony, which is everywhere, and for the small details that turn out to matter.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition credited it to the men of the Great Assembly, the council said to have followed Ezra; others have suggested Mordecai himself, since he is said to have “recorded these events” (9:20). Most scholars date it to the later Persian or early Greek period. Christians differ over whether to read it as straightforward history or as history told with the shaping and artistry of a great storyteller; its knowledge of Persian court life has impressed many readers.

As you read, keep asking the question the book never asks aloud: where is God in this? And then ask it of your own life.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices are worth knowing before you begin.

“From another place.” Mordecai’s words in 4:14 are kept as open as the Hebrew keeps them. Many readers hear in them a quiet hint of God’s care; the book never says so outright, and the translation does not say so either.

“Gallows.” The Hebrew word for what Haman builds is simply “tree” or “wooden pole”. The translation renders it “gallows”, the familiar term; the Persians also impaled their enemies on stakes, and that may be what is meant.

Months and measures. The months are named as the book names them, with their place in the year: Nisan is the first month, Adar the twelfth. Weights and sizes are given in modern equivalents, so Haman offers “about 375 tons of silver” and his gallows is “about 75 feet high”.
