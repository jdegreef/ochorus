Esther ends quietly. The enemies are gone, the feast is fixed in the calendar, and the last we hear is of a Jew who rose high in a foreign court and used his place for the good of his people. The book that began with a king showing off his riches ends with a servant spending his influence on others. That too is a reversal.

The question the book leaves with you

The book never asks where God is. It leaves the reader to ask, and to notice the answer. An orphan becomes queen just before she is needed. A forgotten good deed lies in the king’s annals. On the one night that matters, the king cannot sleep, and the book that is read to him is the one that mentions Mordecai. Haman is in the courtyard at that very moment, and is hanged on his own gallows. No one in the story says, “God did this.” But by the end, no reader can quite believe that no one did.

Wisdom had already put it in a single line: “The lot is tossed into the lap, but how it falls is decided by the LORD.” Haman cast his lots to choose a day for destruction. He did not know who decides how they fall.

The question Esther leaves, then, is really two questions. The first is whether you can trust a God you cannot see, in a world where his name is not spoken and his hand is not obvious. The second is Mordecai’s question to Esther, and it is put to every reader: “And who knows? Perhaps you have become queen for a time like this.” Esther could have kept silent and hoped. Instead she fasted, risked her life, and went in to the king. Providence, in this book, works through human courage, not around it.

Where the story points

The apostle Paul never mentions Esther, but he writes the sentence that sums up her book:

“We know that, for people who love God, all things work together and lead to good. They are the ones called in keeping with his purpose.”

— Romans 8:28

Christian readers have also seen in Esther a shadow of a greater rescue. A whole people stood under a sentence of death that could not be revoked. One of their own, raised to a place near the king, went in on their behalf at the risk of her life, saying, “If I perish, I perish.” The sentence was not cancelled; a second decree was given that turned the day of death into a day of life. And the enemy who had plotted their ruin fell into the very trap he had built.

The New Testament tells of one who did not only risk death for his people but died for them, and rose again. He did not need to wait for the scepter to be held out. The letter to the Hebrews says of him that “he is always alive to plead on their behalf.” And the greatest reversal of all is the one the church celebrates at Easter, when the cross, the instrument of death, became the means of life. The book of Esther says it in its own way: a month “turned for them from grief into joy and from mourning into a holiday.”

Where to go from here

Esther has comforted God’s people in many dark times, and some of what it has inspired is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On Esther and God’s faithfulness. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Esther: God’s Faithfulness” reads the book as a picture of God’s unseen care for his people.
  • On “a time like this” (Esther 4:14). Portraits of Courage – Corrie ten Boom, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “May 1940” opens with Mordecai’s words to Esther and tells of the German invasion of the Netherlands, when a watchmaker’s family in Haarlem faced their own time like this.
  • On the story of the return. The Big Story, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “Coming Home” tells the story of Ezra 1, Nehemiah 8 and Esther 4 together.
  • On providence. Thomas Watson’s All Things for Good, on Romans 8:28 and how even the worst things work for good to those who love God; and John Calvin’s Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book 1, Chapter 17, “Use to Be Made of the Doctrine of Providence”, on finding comfort in the God who governs all things.
  • For younger readers. Tell Me the Story – Book 3: Kings and Prophets, whose chapter “Queen Esther” tells the story for children to hear at bedtime; and Rooted – 30 Days with God for Youth – Book 6, whose “Day 21 — For Such a Time as This” is a devotion on Esther 4:14.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Esther in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
