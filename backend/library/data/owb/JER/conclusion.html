— Jeremiah 52:34

Jeremiah ends quietly, far from the prophet himself. Jerusalem is ashes, its people scattered, and Jehoiachin, the king of David’s line carried off before the end, has spent thirty-seven years in a Babylonian prison. Then a new king of Babylon lets him out, gives him a seat at his table and a portion for each day. It is only a thread, and the thread is still attached to David.

The question the book leaves with you

Jeremiah spent forty years telling people what they did not want to hear, and almost nobody listened. He was mocked, beaten, put in the stocks, thrown into a cistern and carried off to Egypt against his will. By any ordinary measure his ministry failed. And yet his words outlived the king who burned them.

So the book asks every reader the question Jeremiah asked at the crossroads: “ask about the age-old paths, which one is the good way, and walk in it; then you will find rest for your souls.” His hearers answered, “We will not go that way.” The book is honest about how hard it is to hear God’s word when it goes against everything we have planned. It is just as honest about the alternative. The people who trusted the temple, the alliances and the comforting prophets lost all of them. The prophet who trusted God lost nearly everything else, and kept God.

And the book asks what we will do with a God who grieves. Jeremiah’s tears and God’s are hard to tell apart. Judgement in this book is never cold. It is the sorrow of a husband betrayed and a father whose heart aches for his son, and it is always followed by “Return”.

Where the book points

Jeremiah does not end with the exile; it ends with promises that reach far beyond it. He spoke of a “righteous Branch for David” who would “do justice and righteousness in the land”, and whose name would be “The LORD Is Our Righteousness.” He spoke of a covenant written on the heart and of sins remembered no more. He said the seventy years would end, and a generation later Daniel, reading his book in Babylon, counted the years and prayed.

The New Testament hears Jeremiah everywhere. When Jesus asked who people said he was, some answered, “Jeremiah or another of the prophets.” He wept over Jerusalem as Jeremiah had, and quoted Jeremiah’s temple sermon as he cleansed the temple. At the Last Supper he gave his disciples the cup of “the new covenant in my blood”. And the letter to the Hebrews quotes the new covenant promise in full, and concludes that by calling it new, God has treated the first as old, and what grows old is “close to vanishing.” What Jeremiah could only promise, the church believes Christ has brought: forgiveness, and God’s law written on the heart by his Spirit.

Where to go from here

Some of the best preaching on Jeremiah is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the new covenant (Jeremiah 31–32). Andrew Murray’s The Two Covenants, whose chapters on the new covenant and the everlasting covenant are expositions of Jeremiah 31:31–34 and 32:38–40.
  • On the prophet’s call (Jeremiah 1). John Henry Newman’s sermon Jeremiah, a Lesson for the Disappointed (Jeremiah 1:8), and A. B. Simpson’s chapter “The Holy Spirit in the Life and Testimony of Jeremiah” in The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, Part I.
  • On false peace and the old paths (Jeremiah 6). George Whitefield’s The Method of Grace (6:14); J. C. Ryle’s chapter “The Good Way” in The Upper Room and Newman’s “Stedfastness in the Old Paths” in Parochial and Plain Sermons, Volume VII, both on 6:16.
  • On the heart (Jeremiah 17). John Wesley’s sermon “The Deceitfulness of the Human Heart” (17:9), in his Sermons on Several Occasions.
  • On the potter and the Branch (Jeremiah 18 and 23). Whitefield’s “The Potter and the Clay” (18:1–6), in Selected Sermons of George Whitefield, and his sermon The Lord Our Righteousness (23:6).
  • On prayer and the word (Jeremiah 14, 15 and 33). Robert Murray M’Cheyne’s Why Is God a Stranger in the Land? (14:8–9); Charles H. Spurgeon’s The Golden Key of Prayer (33:3); and Andrew Murray’s chapter “Feeding on the Word” in The Inner Chamber (15:16).
  • On Jeremiah among the prophets. Augustine’s The City of God, Book XVIII, chapter 33, on what Jeremiah foretold of Christ and the calling of the nations.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Jeremiah in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.

Then read Lamentations, the five poems of grief that follow Jeremiah in the Bible and have long been linked with his name, and Ezekiel, who was preaching the same message to the exiles in Babylon at the same time.
