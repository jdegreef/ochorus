{{OWB:JER.1.5.s1}}

— Jeremiah 1:5

No prophet lets us closer than Jeremiah. Isaiah sees the LORD high and lifted up; Ezekiel sees wheels full of eyes. Jeremiah tells us what it cost him to be a prophet at all: the nights of weeping, the friends who turned on him, the day he cursed the day he was born. He is called the weeping prophet, and he earned the name. But he is also the prophet of the new covenant, and the one who bought a field in a country about to be conquered because God told him that fields would be bought there again.

The book opens with a young man from a family of priests in Anathoth, a village a few miles north of Jerusalem, and a word that came to him “in the thirteenth year of Josiah’s reign”, about 627 BC. It runs for some forty years, through the last kings of Judah, the siege and burning of Jerusalem, and the scattering of its people. Jeremiah watched his nation die. He had spent his life telling it that it would, and asking it, in God’s name, to turn back before it was too late.

Jeremiah’s world

Jeremiah was born into a hopeful time. Josiah was the best king Judah had had in generations. In his eighteenth year a book of the law was found in the temple, and the king led a great reform: idols were torn down, the Passover was kept, and the covenant was renewed (2 Kings 22–23). Assyria, the empire that had swallowed the northern kingdom of Israel a century before, was crumbling.

Then everything went wrong at once. Josiah was killed in battle in 609 BC. His son Jehoiakim undid much of his father’s work and despised the prophet. Babylon, under Nebuchadnezzar, defeated Egypt at Carchemish in 605 and became the power of the age. In 597 it took Jerusalem, and carried off the young king Jehoiachin with the leading families, among them a priest named Ezekiel. Zedekiah, the last king, wavered between submitting and rebelling until Babylon lost patience. The city fell in 587 or 586 BC, after a siege of a year and a half; the temple Solomon had built was burned.

Through all of it Jeremiah said the same unwelcome thing. Babylon was coming as God’s instrument of judgement; resistance was useless; the only way to live was to submit, and the only hope was in God’s mercy beyond the disaster. To his hearers it sounded like treason, and they treated it so.

The shape of the book

Jeremiah is the longest book in the Bible by word count, and it is not arranged in order of time. Its pieces were gathered by theme and by collection, and a reader who expects a story told from start to finish will be lost by chapter 21. It helps to know the rough outline:

  • Chapters 1–25: the call, and the oracles against Judah and Jerusalem, mostly in poetry, with the prophet’s own laments woven in.
  • Chapters 26–29: Jeremiah against the false prophets: the temple sermon and his trial, the yoke on his neck, the letter to the exiles.
  • Chapters 30–33: the Book of Consolation, the great promises of restoration and the new covenant.
  • Chapters 34–45: the story of the last years, the siege and the fall, and what came after, much of it told by Jeremiah’s scribe Baruch.
  • Chapters 46–51: oracles against the nations, from Egypt to Babylon itself.
  • Chapter 52: a closing account of the fall of Jerusalem, close to the end of 2 Kings.

The book itself tells us how some of it was written. In chapter 36 Jeremiah dictates his messages to Baruch son of Neriah, who writes them on a scroll. There is also an older form of the book, preserved in the ancient Greek translation and among the Dead Sea Scrolls, that is roughly an eighth shorter and places the oracles against the nations in the middle. Scholars weigh the two forms differently; this translation follows the Hebrew text that most English Bibles use.

The call: to uproot and to plant

Jeremiah’s first words to God are an objection: “I do not know how to speak; I am a mere youth.” God does not argue with the description. He answers with a promise, “for I am with you to rescue you”, and a commission:

{{OWB:JER.1.10.s1}}

— Jeremiah 1:10

Count the verbs: four to tear down, two to build. That proportion is the shape of Jeremiah’s ministry, and the pair returns through the book as a refrain. Most of his work will be demolition. But demolition is not the last word, and the last verb is “plant”.

Two visions follow. Jeremiah sees “a rod cut from an almond tree”, and God says, “I am keeping watch over my word until I carry it out.” In Hebrew the almond is shaqed and “keeping watch” is shoqed: the almond, the first tree to wake in the spring, becomes a pledge that God is awake to his word. Then a boiling pot tilted from the north: the disaster will come from that direction, and it will be Babylon.

Broken cisterns and a temple of words

The first oracles are a lawsuit. God remembers Israel’s first love, “how you loved me as a bride”, and asks what fault their ancestors found in him that they went so far away. Then comes one of the great images of the Bible:

{{OWB:JER.2.13.s1}}

— Jeremiah 2:13

Idolatry, in Jeremiah, is not only wicked; it is foolish. It trades a living spring for a tank cut in rock that leaks. And the people have done it while going to church. In chapter 7 Jeremiah stands at the temple gate and tells the worshippers not to trust in the chant “This is the LORD’s temple, the LORD’s temple, the LORD’s temple!” They steal, murder, commit adultery and burn incense to Baal, and then come and stand in God’s house and say they are safe. God asks whether this house has become “a hideout for robbers in your eyes”, and tells them to go and look at Shiloh, where the sanctuary once stood and stood no longer. Six centuries later Jesus quoted these words as he drove the traders out of the temple courts (Mark 11:17).

For that sermon Jeremiah was nearly put to death. Chapter 26 tells how the priests and prophets demanded it, and how some of the elders remembered that Micah had said much the same in Hezekiah’s day and lived. A prophet named Uriah, who preached the same message, was not so fortunate. He fled to Egypt, and Jehoiakim had him brought back and killed.

The heart and its cure

Jeremiah’s diagnosis goes deeper than behaviour. The people say “Peace, peace” when there is no peace; they “have forgotten how to blush”; they cannot change. “Can a Cushite change his skin, or a leopard its spots?” And then the verse that has troubled and steadied readers ever since:

{{OWB:JER.17.9.s1}}

— Jeremiah 17:9

It is set beside a picture of the alternative. The one who trusts in mere human strength is “like a bare shrub in the desert”; the one who trusts in the LORD is “like a tree set out beside the water”, green in the heat and fruitful in drought (17:5–8). Jeremiah’s answer to a heart past healing is not a better heart of our own making. It is the promise, still fourteen chapters away, that God will write on hearts himself.

Meanwhile God pleads. The word that runs through these chapters is “return”. “Return, faithless Israel… I will not stay angry forever.” “Return, faithless children, and I will heal your faithlessness.” The judgement Jeremiah announces is real, but it is announced so that it need not fall.

The confessions of Jeremiah

Scattered through chapters 11–20 are passages unlike anything else in the prophets: Jeremiah’s own prayers, raw with anger and grief, often called his confessions. He learns that the men of his own village are plotting to kill him: “I was like a trusting lamb being led away to be slaughtered.” He asks God why the wicked prosper, and God answers that worse is coming: “If you have raced with runners on foot and they have worn you out, how will you keep pace with horses?” He wishes he had never been born. He accuses God of being “like a stream that deceives”.

The last of them is the fiercest. “You enticed me, LORD, and I was taken in.” Everyone laughs at him; his message has made him a joke. He resolves to stop speaking, and finds he cannot:

{{OWB:JER.20.9.s1}}

— Jeremiah 20:9

Within a few verses he moves from “the LORD is with me like a terrifying warrior” to a song of praise, and then straight to “A curse on the day I was born!” The book does not tidy this up. It leaves the prophet’s agony on the page, and in doing so gives every believer who has felt abandoned by God permission to say so to God. Jeremiah complains to God, not about him behind his back, and God answers him: “If you turn back, I will take you back, and you will stand before me.”

His life itself became part of the message. He was told not to marry or have children, because of what was coming on the children of that land (16:1–4). He was sent to the potter’s house, where a spoiled pot was reshaped on the wheel, and heard God say, “As the clay is in the potter’s hand, so you are in my hand, house of Israel.” He smashed a clay jar before the elders (19), wore a yoke on his neck to tell the nations to submit to Babylon (27), and watched the prophet Hananiah break it, only to be told that the wooden bars would be replaced with iron (28).

A letter to the exiles

After the first deportation in 597, Jeremiah wrote to the exiles in Babylon. Prophets there were promising a quick return. Jeremiah told them to settle in: “Build houses and settle down in them. Plant gardens and eat what they produce.” Marry, have children, increase. And more surprising still:

{{OWB:JER.29.7.s1}}

— Jeremiah 29:7

The famous promise of 29:11 belongs here, and its setting matters. “I have planned peace for you, not disaster, so as to give you a future and a hope” was spoken to people who would not see home, after the words “When Babylon’s seventy years have been completed, I will come to your aid.” It is not a promise that trouble will be short. It is a promise that God has not forgotten his people in it, and that those who seek him with all their heart will find him there, in Babylon.

The Book of Consolation

In chapters 30–33, as the city goes under, the tone changes. Jeremiah is told to write down words of restoration: the captives brought home, the city rebuilt, Rachel weeping for her children and told to hold back her tears, because “they will come back from the land of the enemy”. God speaks of Ephraim as “my dear son” and says, “my heart aches for him.” Then comes the passage for which the book is most remembered:

{{OWB:JER.31.31.s1}} {{OWB:JER.31.32.s1}} {{OWB:JER.31.33.s1}}

— Jeremiah 31:31–33

The old covenant failed not because God’s instruction was faulty but because it was written on stone and the people broke it. The new one will be written inside. Everyone will know the LORD, “from the least to the greatest”, and the reason is the last line of the promise: “For I will forgive their guilt, and I will remember their sin no more.”

No Old Testament passage is quoted at greater length in the New. The letter to the Hebrews sets out the whole of it (8:8–12) and returns to it again (10:16–17). And on the night he was betrayed, Jesus took the cup and called it “the new covenant in my blood” (Luke 22:20).

Chapter 32 turns the promise into a deed. With the Babylonian army outside the walls and Jeremiah himself in custody, his cousin Hanamel comes to ask him to buy the family field at Anathoth. Jeremiah buys it, signs the deed before witnesses and has it sealed in a clay jar to last, because God has said, “People will once more buy houses, fields and vineyards in this land.” Then he prays, a little uncertainly: “There is nothing too hard for you.” And God answers him with his own words turned into a question: “Is there anything too hard for me?”

The scroll, the cistern and the fall

The last part of the book tells the story of Jeremiah’s sufferings with a plainness that makes it hard to read. In chapter 36 King Jehoiakim sits by the fire in his winter house while the scroll of Jeremiah’s words is read to him, cutting off each few columns with a knife and throwing them into the fire. Jeremiah simply dictates it again, “and many other words like them were added”.

Under Zedekiah he is beaten, imprisoned, and at last lowered by ropes into a cistern where “there was no water in the cistern, only mud, and Jeremiah sank down into the mud.” He is rescued by a foreigner, Ebed-melech the Cushite, an official of the palace, who lets down old rags for him to put under his arms so the ropes will not cut him. Zedekiah sends for the prophet in secret, again and again, to ask whether there is any word from the LORD. There is, and it does not change.

Then the wall is breached. Zedekiah is caught fleeing, sees his sons killed and is blinded; the city is burned. Jeremiah is freed by the Babylonians and stays with the remnant under the governor Gedaliah, until Gedaliah is murdered and the survivors, against Jeremiah’s pleading, flee to Egypt and take him with them (40–44). There, among the Judeans scattered through Egypt, his last recorded words are a protest against their worship of “the queen of heaven”. We do not know how he died.

A short chapter is addressed to Baruch, who has been complaining of his own sorrows: “Are you seeking great things for yourself? Do not seek them.” He is promised his life, and only that. Then come the oracles against the nations, ending with the longest of all, against Babylon, the empire that had seemed to hold the world. “Here the words of Jeremiah end.”

How to read Jeremiah

Jeremiah is long and not in order, and it is best not to expect to read it like a story. Read the call (1), the temple sermon (7 and 26), the confessions (11–20), the letter to the exiles (29), the Book of Consolation (30–33) and the narrative of the end (36–45) first, and the rest will begin to make sense around them.

Keep the history beside you: Josiah, Jehoiakim, Jehoiachin, Zedekiah, and the three blows of 605, 597 and 587. Many chapters begin by naming the king, and knowing who is on the throne tells you what Jeremiah is facing.

Notice how much the book is about speaking and hearing. Words are put in the prophet’s mouth, eaten as food (15:16), burned and rewritten. God’s word is “like fire” and “like a hammer that smashes rock to pieces”. False prophets say what people want; the true one is known when his word comes to pass. And listen, as you read, for Jeremiah’s tears, because they are God’s too. Again and again it is hard to tell whether it is the prophet or the LORD who is grieving: “If only my head were all water and my eyes a fountain of tears.”

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. Much of Jeremiah is poetry, and the poetry is printed one line per line, as it is laid out in the Hebrew. A few of its choices are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”. Where the Hebrew has “Lord” and the name together, as in Jeremiah’s first protest, it reads “Lord GOD”.

“The LORD of Armies.” Jeremiah’s favourite title for God, traditionally “the LORD of hosts”, is rendered “the LORD of Armies”: the God who commands the armies of heaven, and before whom Babylon’s army is only a tool.

“Declares the LORD.” The phrase that punctuates almost every oracle, older Bibles’ “saith the LORD”, is printed as “declares the LORD”, marking the prophet’s words as God’s own.

“Loyal love.” The Hebrew hesed, God’s faithful covenant love, is “loyal love”: “I have loved you with an everlasting love, so I have kept on showing you loyal love.”

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
