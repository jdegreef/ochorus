Joshua ends with three graves. Joshua himself is buried at Timnath-serah, in the hill country he was given. Eleazar the priest, Aaron’s son, is buried in the hill country of Ephraim. And between them, almost in passing, the book records the burial of a man who had died centuries before: “The bones of Joseph, which the Israelites had brought up from Egypt, were buried at Shechem.” The last request of Genesis has been honoured. The promise that kept Joseph’s bones waiting in Egypt has come true.

The question the book leaves with you

And yet the book ends with a sentence that has a shadow in it. Israel served the LORD as long as Joshua lived, and as long as the elders lived who had seen what God had done. What happens when they are gone?

Joshua had seen the danger. He set up stones at the Jordan so that children would ask what they meant. He warned the people that they would not be able to serve a holy God by their own resolve. He wrote the covenant in a book and raised a stone to witness it. The very next book gives the answer he feared: “after them another generation grew up who did not know the LORD or what he had done for Israel.”

That is the question Joshua leaves with every reader. Faith cannot be inherited like a field. Each generation has to choose, and each generation has to be told. Joshua’s “My household and I will serve the LORD” is not only a vow for himself. It is a charge to every parent, every church, every elder who has seen the works of God, to make sure the next generation sees them too.

The rest still waiting

The book says that “the LORD gave them rest all around them”, and that not one of his good promises failed. But it also says that much of the land was never taken, and the books that follow tell how Israel lost even what it had. The rest Joshua gave was real, and it did not last.

The New Testament reads that as a signpost. The letter to the Hebrews, noting that long after Joshua, God still says through David, “Today, if you hear his voice”, draws the conclusion:

“If Joshua had brought them into rest, God would not afterward speak of a different day. So a Sabbath rest is still waiting for God’s people.”

— Hebrews 4:8–9

There is a deeper Joshua, and a deeper rest. The Christian church has always heard something in the fact that Jesus bears Joshua’s name, “the LORD saves”, and that he too leads his people across a river, through death, into an inheritance. And it has noticed who was waiting on the far side: Rahab, the Canaanite who trusted the God of Israel, is named in Matthew’s first chapter among the ancestors of Jesus.

Where to go from here

Joshua has been read in the church as a story of faith, of courage, and of the Christian life, and some of the best of that reading is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Joshua: Heavenly Places” reads the entry into the land, beside Ephesians, as a picture of the believer’s victories “in heavenly places” in Christ; and Justin Martyr’s Dialogue with Trypho, whose chapter “Joshua was a figure of Christ” is one of the earliest Christian readings of the book.
  • On Rahab (Joshua 2 and 6). Clement of Rome’s The First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians, whose twelfth chapter tells her story and sees the scarlet cord as a sign of redemption through the Lord’s blood; and the chapter “Rahab” in Gareth Evans’s He Holds My Tomorrows, on Hebrews 11:31.
  • On Jericho (Joshua 6). Hannah Whitall Smith’s chapter “The Shout of Faith” in The God of All Comfort, on Joshua 6:5.
  • On Achan and Ai (Joshua 7). Andrew Murray’s chapter “Restraining Prayer—Is It Sin?” in The Ministry of Intercession, which reads the defeat at Ai as a lesson in hidden sin and restored blessing.
  • On Caleb (Joshua 14). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon Caleb—A Green Old Age (Joshua 14:6–14), and Gareth Evans’s sermon Caleb: A Different Spirit, which moves from Numbers 14:24 to Caleb’s request for Hebron.
  • On the choice at Shechem (Joshua 24). George Whitefield’s sermon “The Great Duty of Family Religion”, on Joshua 24:15, in Selected Sermons of George Whitefield.
  • On the rest still waiting. Richard Baxter’s The Saints’ Everlasting Rest, a whole book on Hebrews 4:9.
  • For reading with children. “Into the Land”, on Joshua 1–6 and 24, in The Big Story, an Ochorus Original.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Joshua in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
