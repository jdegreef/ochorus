— Joshua 1:9

The book of Joshua opens at a graveside. Moses, who had led Israel out of Egypt and through forty years in the wilderness, has died on a mountain looking across the Jordan into a land he would never enter. The people are camped on the eastern bank. The river is in flood. And the first words God speaks are blunt: “My servant Moses is dead. Now get up and cross this Jordan.”

What follows is the story of a promise coming true. Four centuries earlier God had told Abraham that his descendants would have this land. Joshua tells how they crossed into it, how they fought for it, how it was shared out among the tribes, and how, at the end of his life, Joshua gathered the people and asked them to choose whom they would serve. It also raises one of the hardest questions in the Bible, which this introduction will not pretend is easy.

“The LORD saves”

The book is named after its central figure. Joshua son of Nun first appears in Exodus as Moses’ young assistant, fighting the Amalekites and waiting on the mountain while Moses meets with God. He was one of the twelve men sent to explore Canaan, and one of only two, with Caleb, who came back saying that the LORD could give them the land. Numbers records that Moses changed his name: “Moses gave Hoshea son of Nun the name Joshua.” Hoshea means “salvation”; Yehoshua adds the name of God, and means “the LORD saves”.

In Greek, Yehoshua becomes Iēsous, Jesus, and the letter to the Hebrews says that the rest Joshua gave was not the last word: “If Joshua had brought them into rest, God would not afterward speak of a different day.” Christians from the second century on (the Epistle of Barnabas, Justin Martyr) have seen in Joshua a picture of the greater Joshua, who leads his people into an inheritance no one can take away.

The shape of the book

Joshua falls into three parts.

  • Entering and taking the land (chapters 1–12). The charge to Joshua, Rahab and the spies, the crossing of the Jordan, the camp at Gilgal, the fall of Jericho, Achan’s sin and the defeat at Ai, the covenant renewed at Mount Ebal, the treaty with Gibeon, and the campaigns in the south and north, ending with a list of thirty-one defeated kings.
  • Dividing the land (chapters 13–21). The boundaries and towns given by lot to each tribe, Caleb’s request for Hebron, the cities of refuge, and the towns for the Levites.
  • Holding the land (chapters 22–24). An altar beside the Jordan that nearly starts a civil war, Joshua’s farewell to the leaders, and the great covenant at Shechem.

The middle section, with its lists of borders and villages, is the part most readers skip. But it is the point of the whole: the promise to Abraham was not a war but a home.

Be strong and courageous

The first chapter sounds one phrase four times. God says it to Joshua three times, and at the end of the chapter the people say it back to him: “Only be strong and courageous!” It is the courage of a man taking up a task too big for him, and it rests on a promise: “I will be with you just as I was with Moses. I will not let go of you or abandon you.” The letter to the Hebrews would later give that same promise to every believer.

The courage is also tied to a book. He is told to keep the law Moses gave:

{{OWB:JOS.1.8.s1}}

— Joshua 1:8

Success in the land will come, not from military skill, but from listening to God’s word and doing it. The book ends on the same note.

Rahab and the scarlet cord

The first person in the land to confess faith in Israel’s God is a Canaanite: Rahab, a prostitute of Jericho, who hides Joshua’s two spies under flax stalks on her roof and sends the king’s men off on a false trail. Then she tells the spies why:

{{OWB:JOS.2.11.s1}}

— Joshua 2:11

She has heard what the LORD did at the Red Sea, and she has drawn the right conclusion. She asks the spies to swear that, since she has shown them “faithful love”, they will show it to her family. They tell her to tie a scarlet cord in the window, and when the city falls, everyone inside her house is spared.

Rahab’s story is told with remarkable care, at the very moment the book is describing a people under judgement. She is the first sign that the line between those who are saved and those who are not runs through faith, not through race. The New Testament remembers her three times: Hebrews names her among the heroes of faith, James as an example of faith shown in deeds, and Matthew’s Gospel places her in the family line of Jesus, as the mother of Boaz. Many Christian writers, from Clement of Rome onward, have seen in the scarlet cord a sign of the blood of Christ, as the Passover blood once marked the doors in Egypt.

Crossing the Jordan

Israel had left Egypt through the sea. Now they enter Canaan through a river, and the book tells it deliberately as a second crossing. The priests carry the ark of the covenant ahead of the people; as soon as their feet touch the water, the flow from upstream stops and rises “in a single heap”, and the whole nation crosses “on dry ground”.

Joshua has twelve men take twelve stones from the riverbed and set them up at Gilgal, the first camp in the land. The reason is the next generation:

{{OWB:JOS.4.21.s1}} {{OWB:JOS.4.22.s1}}

— Joshua 4:21–22

Faith in Joshua is something handed on, to children not yet born, and beyond them, “so that all the peoples of the earth may know how mighty the hand of the LORD is.”

Gilgal: before the battle

Before a single battle is fought, the book slows down. At Gilgal the men born in the wilderness receive circumcision, the covenant sign, and the LORD says, “Today I have rolled away from you the disgrace of Egypt.” They keep the Passover. And the manna, the bread from heaven that had fed them every morning for forty years, stops: “that year they ate from the crops of the land of Canaan.” The wilderness is over.

Then, near Jericho, Joshua meets a man with a drawn sword and asks the question any general would ask: “Are you on our side, or on the side of our enemies?” The answer turns the question round:

{{OWB:JOS.5.14.s1}}

— Joshua 5:14

The stranger tells him to take off his sandals, as Moses was told at the burning bush. The question is never whether God is on Israel’s side, but whether Israel is on God’s.

Jericho and Ai

The fall of Jericho is the most famous story in Joshua, and the strangest military plan in history. For six days the army marches once around the city in silence, with seven priests blowing ram’s horns before the ark. On the seventh day they go around seven times, the horns sound, the people shout, “and the wall collapsed where it stood.” The city is not taken. It is given.

Then Israel is beaten at Ai, a small town, because of one man. Achan has taken from Jericho what was set apart for God, and his confession is the oldest pattern of temptation there is: “I saw… I coveted them and took them, and now they are buried in the ground in the middle of my tent.” The whole people suffers for one man’s hidden sin, and Achan and his household are put to death in the Valley of Achor, “Trouble”. It is a hard chapter: the people of God are bound together, and what one member hides affects them all. Centuries later the prophet Hosea heard God promise to “make the valley of Achor a door of hope.”

When Ai is finally taken, Joshua does something unexpected. He leads the whole nation to Mount Ebal and Mount Gerizim, as Moses had commanded, builds an altar, and reads the law aloud: “There was not a single word of all that Moses had commanded that Joshua did not read aloud before the whole assembly of Israel, including the women and children and the outsiders who lived among them.”

The hard question: “devoted to destruction”

No honest reader can pass over the violence in Joshua. Again and again the book says that a city and its people were “devoted to destruction”: men and women, young and old. The Hebrew word, herem, means something set apart wholly to God, given up to him and not to be kept for human use. In war it meant total destruction. Many readers find it the most troubling thing in the Old Testament.

Christians have answered it in more than one way, and it is fair to set them side by side. Many have stressed that the Bible presents this as God’s judgement on the long-standing wickedness of the peoples of Canaan, delayed for centuries because, as God told Abraham, “the Amorites’ wickedness has not yet run its full course”; and that Deuteronomy insists Israel was given the land not because it was righteous. Some have noted that the language of total victory was common in ancient war reports, and that the book itself tells, in the same breath, of Canaanites who remained, so that its sweeping summaries may not be meant as a literal count. Others have pointed out that the same judgement turns on Israel when Achan sins, and that Rahab and the Gibeonites, Canaanites both, are spared.

On one point Christians today broadly agree, though the book has sometimes been misused to justify violence. The book describes a particular event in the history of God’s promise, under a particular command, at a particular time. It gives no warrant to anyone, ever, to take up arms in the name of God against a people. The New Testament reads its battles as pictures of a different warfare, and its promised rest as one that Christ gives.

The Gibeonites and the long day

Chapter 9 tells of a different kind of defeat. The people of Gibeon, a Canaanite city, disguise themselves as travellers from far away in worn-out sandals with dry, crumbling bread, and ask for a treaty. Israel’s leaders inspect the bread, “but they did not seek the word of the LORD”, and they swear an oath. When the trick is discovered, Israel keeps its word, and the Gibeonites become woodcutters and water carriers for the sanctuary. A promise made in God’s name, even to the wrong people, must be kept.

And it is kept at once, at some cost. When five kings attack Gibeon for making peace with Israel, Joshua marches all night to defend them. That is the setting for the long day, when Joshua prays, “Sun, stand still at Gibeon, and moon, in the valley of Aijalon!” The book quotes an old poem from the Book of Jashar and then gives its own verdict:

{{OWB:JOS.10.14.s1}}

— Joshua 10:14

Readers differ over what happened in the sky that day: a day miraculously lengthened, or the language of battle-song. Either way, the book’s wonder is not the sun but the God who listened.

Dividing the land

From chapter 13 the battles give way to boundaries, shared out by lot at Gilgal and then at Shiloh, where the tent of meeting is set up. Some things stand out.

The first is Caleb. At eighty-five he comes to Joshua and reminds him that forty-five years earlier the two of them alone had trusted God when the other spies spread fear. “Today I am just as strong as on the day Moses sent me,” he says, and asks for the hill country of Hebron, where the giants lived, because “Perhaps the LORD will be with me, and I will drive them out.” Three times the book says that Caleb followed the LORD “with all his heart”.

The second is the tribe of Levi, which receives no territory at all: “The LORD, the God of Israel, is their inheritance.” The third is the six cities of refuge, where someone who had killed a person by accident could flee and be safe from revenge until a fair hearing.

The fourth is a quiet, persistent refrain: “But the people of Judah could not drive out the Jebusites”; “they never drove them out completely.” The conquest is real, but it is not finished. God tells the aging Joshua that “very much of the land still remains to be taken.” And yet the section closes with one of the great summary sentences of the Bible:

{{OWB:JOS.21.45.s1}}

— Joshua 21:45

Both are true at once: God has kept every word, and his people have not yet taken hold of all he has given. Believers have lived between those two sentences ever since.

An altar called Witness

In chapter 22 the tribes east of the Jordan build a large altar by the river, and the other tribes, fearing apostasy, gather for war. But the altar was never meant for sacrifice. It was a reminder, so that their children would never be told “You have no share in the LORD.” War is avoided because someone asked before they struck.

Choose today

The book ends with two farewell speeches. In the first, Joshua, “old and well along in years”, reminds the leaders that “not a single word has fallen short of all the good words the LORD your God spoke about you”, and warns them that the God who has kept his promises of good will keep his warnings too.

In the second, at Shechem, where Abraham had built his first altar in the land, Joshua tells the whole story again, from Terah serving other gods beyond the River, through the patriarchs and Egypt and the wilderness, to the gift of “a land you had not worked for and towns you had not built”. Then he puts the question to them:

{{OWB:JOS.24.15.s1}}

— Joshua 24:15

The people answer at once that they will serve the LORD. Joshua replies: “You will not be able to serve the LORD, because he is a holy God, a jealous God.” He is making sure they know what they are promising. Three times they insist, and Joshua writes the covenant down, sets up a great stone under the oak by the sanctuary, and sends them home, each to their inheritance.

How to read Joshua

Read the story chapters (1–12, 22–24) straight through first, then go back to the land lists in chapters 13–21 with a map beside you. Watch for the phrases that recur: “be strong and courageous”, “to this day”, “the LORD fought for Israel”, the stones set up as memorials at the Jordan, at Achor, at Ai and at Shechem.

The book does not name its author. An old Jewish tradition holds that Joshua wrote most of it, with the account of his death added by others. Many scholars see it as one part of a longer history running from Deuteronomy to Kings, shaped over time and completed later; its repeated note that things remain “to this day” shows a writer looking back. Christians also differ over the date of the events, some placing them in the fifteenth century BC and others in the thirteenth, and over how to read the archaeology of Jericho and other sites. None of these questions changes what the book is about.

Read Joshua as the hinge between Moses and the kings, between the promise and the history of a people in the land. And read it, as the New Testament does, as part of a larger story: a God who keeps his word, a rest that is given and then still awaited, and a leader named “the LORD saves” who points beyond himself.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Joshua are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”. Where it uses the ordinary word for God, it writes “God”.

“Devoted to destruction.” The Hebrew herem, which older Bibles render “accursed” or “utterly destroyed”, is translated “devoted to destruction”, and the plunder that belonged to God “the devoted things”. The phrase keeps both sides of the word: something set apart for God, and something not to be kept.

“Faithful love” and “outsiders.” When Rahab asks the spies to show her family the kindness she has shown them, the word is hesed, translated here “faithful love”. And the foreigners who lived among Israel and heard the law read at Mount Ebal are called “outsiders”, people living as guests in a land not their own, as Israel itself had once lived in Egypt.
