— Numbers 9:18

Numbers is the story of the longest detour in the Bible. The journey from Sinai to the edge of the promised land should have taken weeks. It took forty years, and a whole generation died on the way. In Hebrew the book is called Bemidbar, “In the wilderness”, from its first verse, and that is where nearly all of it happens. The name we use comes, through Latin, from the ancient Greek translation, and refers to the two counts of the people that frame the book, in chapters 1 and 26.

The two names, put together, tell you what the book is about. The people are counted, ordered and ready; then they spend a lifetime in the wilderness. Numbers is a book about what God does with a people who are rescued but not yet home, who complain, rebel and lose their nerve, and whom he still will not abandon. That is why the New Testament turns to it so often. Every Christian lives in the wilderness between the exodus and the promised land.

Two generations

The simplest way to follow Numbers is by its two censuses. The first, at Sinai, counts the generation that came out of Egypt. Chapters 1–25 tell its story: the camp set in order, the journey begun, and then complaint after complaint, until at Kadesh the people refuse to enter the land and are sentenced to die in the wilderness. The second census, on the plains of Moab, counts their children. Chapters 26–36 belong to them, a generation standing on the border, receiving laws for life in the land.

The journey itself falls into three stages: at Sinai (1:1–10:10), from Sinai to Kadesh and the long years around it (10:11–20:13), and from Kadesh to the plains of Moab across the Jordan from Jericho (20:14–36:13). Stories and laws are woven together throughout, sometimes in ways that seem abrupt. But the laws are usually answers to the stories: after the people despair of ever entering the land, chapter 15 begins with instructions for “When you enter the land where you will live”.

The camp around the tent

The first ten chapters show Israel as God meant it to be. Every man of fighting age is counted, tribe by tribe. The tribes camp in a great square, three on each side, “all around the tent of meeting”, with the Levites closest to it. The Levites belong to God in place of Israel’s firstborn, whom he spared on the Passover night. Everything has its place, and at the centre is God.

Then come laws for keeping the camp pure, the vow of the Nazirite, who sets himself or herself apart for the LORD for a time, and the blessing that the priests are to speak over the people, still spoken in synagogues and churches around the world:

“May the LORD bless you and watch over you. May the LORD make his face shine toward you and show you favor. May the LORD lift up his face to look on you and grant you peace.”

— Numbers 6:24–26

“So they will put my name on the Israelites,” God says, “and I myself will bless them.” Chapter 9 describes the cloud over the tabernacle, rising and resting, and the people moving or staying as it moves or stays, “for as long as the cloud remained”. And in chapter 10 the cloud rises, the silver trumpets sound, and for the first time the people set out. Whenever the ark moved, Moses prayed, “Rise up, LORD! Let your enemies scatter.”

Complaining in the wilderness

Three days later the trouble begins. The people complain about their hardships, and fire breaks out at the edge of the camp. Then they crave meat, and look back to Egypt with a strangely selective memory: “We remember the fish we used to eat in Egypt at no cost, and the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions and the garlic.” They do not remember the whips. “There is nothing at all, and all we ever see is this manna!”

Moses, for his part, reaches the end of himself. “I cannot carry this whole people on my own. The load is too heavy for me.” God’s answer is to share his Spirit with seventy elders, and when two of them prophesy in the camp instead of at the tent, and Joshua wants them stopped, Moses gives one of the most generous answers in the Old Testament:

— Numbers 11:29

The wish was granted at Pentecost. Meanwhile, God sends the quail Moses doubted he could provide, with a question that echoes through Scripture: “Is the reach of the LORD too short?” Then even Moses’ own brother and sister turn against him, and God defends him. Moses, we are told, is the humblest man on earth, and the one to whom God speaks “mouth to mouth, openly and not in riddles”.

The scouts and the refusal

The turning point of the book comes in chapters 13–14. Twelve scouts, one from each tribe, explore Canaan for forty days. They bring back a cluster of grapes so large that two men carry it on a pole, and a report that the land “does flow with milk and honey”. But ten of them add that the people are giants and the cities fortified: “To ourselves we looked like grasshoppers, and that is how we looked to them.”

Only Caleb and Joshua speak up. “The shelter over them has been taken away, while the LORD is with us. So do not fear them!” The people talk of stoning them, and propose to choose a new leader and go back to Egypt. It is the exodus in reverse. God threatens to destroy them, and Moses prays again, quoting back to God the words God spoke at Sinai: “The LORD is slow to grow angry and rich in faithful love, forgiving wrongdoing and rebellion.” God answers, “I have pardoned them, as you asked.”

Pardoned, but not excused. The generation that refused the land will not enter it. They will wander for forty years, a year for every day the scouts spent in the land, until they have died in the wilderness, and their children, whom they said would be carried off as spoil, will go in instead. One man is singled out:

— Numbers 14:24

The next morning the people change their minds and attack anyway, without the ark and without Moses, and they are routed. Faith that comes too late, on its own terms, is not the same as obedience.

Rebellion and the priesthood

Chapters 15–19 are full of challenges to the order God has set up. Korah the Levite and his allies accuse Moses and Aaron: “You claim too much! Every member of the congregation is holy.” The ground opens and swallows them. When the people blame Moses for their deaths, a plague breaks out, and Aaron runs into the middle of the crowd with incense to make atonement:

{{OWB:NUM.17.13.s1}}

— Numbers 16:48

To settle the question of the priesthood, God has a staff from each tribe laid in the tent before the testimony, and the next morning Aaron’s has budded, blossomed and borne ripe almonds. Life from dead wood is God’s sign of whom he has chosen. The Levites receive no land, because God himself is their share: “I am your portion and your inheritance among the Israelites.” And the ashes of a red heifer are prepared to cleanse those who have touched the dead, in a book where there is a great deal of death.

The waters of Meribah

Chapter 20 opens with Miriam’s death and closes with Aaron’s. Between them comes the saddest moment in Moses’ life. At Kadesh there is no water, and the people quarrel with him again. God tells Moses to speak to the rock. Instead Moses says, “Listen, you rebels! Are we to bring water out of this rock for you?” and strikes it twice. The water comes anyway; God does not let the people go thirsty for their leader’s anger. But the verdict follows:

— Numbers 20:12

Readers have long asked why so severe a sentence fell on so faithful a servant. The text points to unbelief and to a failure to show God’s holiness before the people. Perhaps the point is that the greater the leader, the more his example matters. Either way, Moses will see the land only from a mountaintop.

The bronze snake

As the people travel around Edom they lose patience once more, and speak against God and against Moses. Fiery snakes come among them, and when they confess their sin, God gives a strange remedy:

{{OWB:NUM.21.8.s1}} {{OWB:NUM.21.9.s1}}

— Numbers 21:8–9

The cure is not medicine but looking, a trust in God’s word that a bronze image on a pole can save. Centuries later the snake had itself become an idol, and King Hezekiah smashed it to pieces (2 Kings 18:4). But Jesus took it as a picture of himself: “Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, and in the same way the Son of Man must be lifted up, so that everyone who believes will have eternal life in him.”

Balaam: the blessing no one could curse

Now the story moves, unexpectedly, to the other side of the hill. Balak king of Moab is terrified of Israel and sends for Balaam, a famous seer from the east, to curse them. God tells Balaam not to go, then lets him go, and on the way sends an angel to block his path, whom Balaam’s donkey can see and he cannot. It is a comic scene with a serious point: the great seer is blinder than his donkey.

Three times Balak takes Balaam up to a height to curse Israel, and each time Balaam opens his mouth and blessing comes out. “How can I curse a people God has not cursed?” And in the second oracle:

A new generation

The next chapter is a shock. What no curse could do, sin does. At Peor Israel’s men join the women of Moab in the worship of their gods, “on Balaam’s advice”, as chapter 31 later reveals, and a plague follows until Phinehas the priest acts. Then comes the second census, and its verdict:

— Numbers 26:64–65

The rest of the book looks forward. Five sisters, the daughters of Zelophehad, ask for their dead father’s share of the land, since he had no sons, and God answers, “The daughters of Zelophehad are right in what they say.” Moses asks God to appoint a leader after him, “so that the congregation of the LORD will not be like sheep with no shepherd”, and Joshua is commissioned. There are offerings for the festivals, laws on vows, a war with Midian, the eastern tribes settled with a warning that “your sin will catch up with you”, a list of the forty years’ stopping places, the borders of the land, and cities of refuge, where someone who has killed by accident can be safe.

How to read Numbers

Read the stories in order and let the laws interrupt them; the interruptions are part of the book’s meaning. Keep a map nearby, because the journey matters. And read it alongside the New Testament passages that read it for us: Paul in 1 Corinthians 10, who says “These events became examples for us”, and Hebrews 3–4, which takes up Psalm 95’s warning, “Today, if you hear his voice, do not harden your hearts”.

Notice, too, how often the book shows Moses on his face before God, praying for the very people who want to stone him. Numbers is full of rebellion, but it is just as full of intercession, and of a God who answers it. Again and again the people deserve to be destroyed, and again and again they are carried on.

Christians have always found their own lives in this book. The church, too, has been brought out of slavery and is not yet home; it, too, is tempted to look back to Egypt, to grumble at the manna, to measure the giants instead of the promise. The wilderness in Numbers is a hard place, but it is also the place where God feeds his people every morning, guides them by day and night, and teaches them to trust him. The question it puts to every reader is the one Caleb answered: will you follow with all your heart?

Some things in Numbers are hard. The punishments are severe, and the war against Midian in chapter 31 troubles many readers. The book does not soften them, and Christians have wrestled with such passages in different ways; it helps to read them within the whole story of a God who is patient for forty years, and whose purpose, from the first chapter of Genesis, is blessing.

The numbers themselves raise questions. The census gives 603,550 men of fighting age, which would mean a people of two million or more. Some take the figures as they stand; others note that the Hebrew word for “thousand” can also mean a clan or military unit, which would make the numbers far smaller. The book says that Moses recorded the stages of the journey (33:2), and tradition has received it as one of the five books of Moses; many scholars think it gathers old lists, laws and stories that reached their final form later. Christians hold a range of views on these questions.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few things are worth knowing before you begin.

“The LORD.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, the four letters often written Yahweh, this translation follows long Jewish and Christian custom and writes “the LORD”.

Numbers as numbers. The census totals are given as figures, as the Hebrew gives them: “The total number enrolled was 603,550.”

Chapter and verse. In a few places the Hebrew text numbers its verses differently from English Bibles: the Hebrew 17:1–15 is the English 16:36–50, and the Hebrew 30:1 is the English 29:40. This edition follows the English chapters, and all the references in this introduction use English numbering.

“Faithful love.” The Hebrew hesed, God’s covenant love, is “faithful love”, as in Moses’ prayer: “as befits the greatness of your faithful love”.
