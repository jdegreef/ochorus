— Numbers 36:13

Numbers ends where it has been heading for forty years: on the plains of Moab, by the Jordan, across from Jericho. The land is in sight. But almost everyone who set out from Sinai is gone. Miriam is buried at Kadesh and Aaron on Mount Hor, and Moses has been told that he will see the land only from a mountain. The people camped by the river are their children, counted, organised and ready, waiting to cross.

The question the book leaves with you

Why did a whole generation, who had seen the sea split and eaten bread from heaven, never reach the land? Numbers answers plainly, and so does the New Testament when it looks back: “So we see that it was unbelief that kept them from entering.” They believed the giants more than the promise. They remembered Egypt’s cucumbers and forgot its whips. And when the moment came, they would not go.

That is the question Numbers puts to every reader. Not whether God is faithful, because the book shows that again and again: the manna every morning, the cloud every night, the pardon at Moses’ prayer, the blessing that Balaam could not turn into a curse. The question is whether we will trust him at the border. Caleb is the book’s answer, the man with “another spirit in him”, who “followed me with all his heart”. And behind Caleb stands a God who keeps his word to the children even when the parents have failed.

Where the story goes next

The next book, Deuteronomy, is Moses’ farewell to this new generation, spoken on these same plains. After it, Joshua will lead them across the Jordan. But the wilderness was never only a place. The prophets remembered it as the time of Israel’s first love, and the psalmists as a warning. And the New Testament finds the whole book retold in Jesus.

The Spirit led him into the wilderness for forty days, where he was tempted as Israel had been, and he answered each temptation with words Moses spoke to the wilderness generation. He called himself the true manna: “Your ancestors ate the manna in the wilderness, and yet they died”, but “I am the living bread, come down from heaven.” He said that he would be lifted up as the bronze snake was lifted up. And Paul tells the Corinthians that the rock that gave water in the desert was Christ, and that the whole story was told for us:

“What happened to them was meant as an example, and it was written down to warn us, who live at the time when the ages reach their end.”

— 1 Corinthians 10:11

The letter to the Hebrews draws the same lesson and adds the hope: “So a Sabbath rest is still waiting for God’s people.” The wilderness is not the end of the road.

Where to go from here

Numbers has been a favourite of preachers, who have found the pilgrim church on every page. A few places to begin in this library:

  • On the whole book. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Numbers: Wilderness Wanderings” reads the wanderings as a picture of the Christian who has been redeemed but has not yet entered into the fulness of salvation.
  • On the cloud and the voice (Numbers 7–9). Alexander Maclaren’s The Guiding Pillar (Numbers 9:16); and Andrew Murray’s The Inner Chamber, whose chapter “Moses and the Word of God” turns on Numbers 7:89, Moses hearing the voice from above the mercy seat.
  • On the scouts and Caleb (Numbers 13–14). Gareth Evans’s Caleb: A Different Spirit (Numbers 14:24).
  • On the tassels and the priests’ portion (Numbers 15 and 18). Hudson Taylor’s A Ribband of Blue (Numbers 15:38–39); and A. W. Tozer’s God’s Best Is Himself (Numbers 18:20).
  • On the rock in the wilderness (Numbers 20). Christmas Evans’s The Smitten Rock, on 1 Corinthians 10:4, which dwells on Moses striking the rock twice.
  • On Balaam (Numbers 22–24). Horatius Bonar’s The Vain Wish, on Balaam’s prayer to die the death of the upright (Numbers 23:10).
  • On Phinehas and the priesthood (Numbers 25). Andrew Murray’s The Two Covenants, with its chapter “The Covenant of an Everlasting Priesthood”.
  • On the wilderness as a warning and a hope. A. W. Tozer’s The Sin of Murmuring (1 Corinthians 10:1–13); John Wesley’s sermon “The Wilderness State”, in his Sermons on Several Occasions, on the believer who has come out of Egypt but lost his way; R. A. Torrey’s The Day of Golden Opportunity (Hebrews 3:7); and Charles H. Spurgeon’s Heavenly Rest (Hebrews 4:9).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Numbers in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
