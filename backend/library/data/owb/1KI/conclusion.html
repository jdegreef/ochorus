1 Kings does not end; it breaks off. Its last sentence describes a new king doing exactly what his father did, and the story runs straight on into 2 Kings. The book that opened with the glory of Solomon closes with the son of Ahab bowing to Baal. Somewhere between the two, the golden age slipped away.

The question the book leaves with you

Almost every king in 1 Kings fails. Solomon, the wisest of them, ends with a divided heart. Jeroboam, given ten tribes by a prophet’s word, makes golden calves to keep them. Ahab, warned by drought and fire and a voice at his own vineyard, goes into battle in disguise rather than listen. The best of Judah’s kings, Asa and Jehoshaphat, do what is right, yet leave the high places standing. The book’s verdict on the monarchy is not that it lacked power, but that it lacked a whole heart.

And so it leaves a question that is older than the monarchy: whom will you follow? Elijah put it to a crowd on Carmel, “How long will you go on hobbling between two choices?”, and the people said nothing. The book will not let its readers stay silent. Every reign ends with a verdict, and readers are meant to ask what the verdict on their own lives would be.

Yet the book also leaves two small lights burning. One is the lamp God promised to keep for David in Jerusalem, “so that my servant David may always have a lamp before me”, a promise that outlasts every bad king of Judah. The other is the seven thousand who had not bowed to Baal, unknown even to Elijah. God’s purposes did not depend on the kings. They never had.

Where the story goes next

2 Kings carries the story on: Elijah taken up in a whirlwind, his cloak falling to Elisha, the end of Ahab’s house, the fall of Samaria and at last of Jerusalem itself. The man of God’s word against Jeroboam’s altar comes true when Josiah burns bones on it, three centuries after it was spoken.

But the New Testament reaches back into 1 Kings again and again. Jesus pointed to the queen of Sheba, who travelled so far to hear Solomon, and said:

— Matthew 12:42

Solomon prayed that God would hear those who prayed toward the house he had built; Jesus, standing in the temple, spoke of a temple that would be torn down and raised in three days, “But the temple he meant was his own body.” Stephen, before the council, remembered that “it was Solomon who built a house for him”, and added, as Solomon himself had, that the Most High does not live in houses made by hands (Acts 7:47–48).

Elijah too steps out of this book into the Gospels. John the Baptist came “in the spirit and power of Elijah, to turn the hearts of fathers back to their children” (Luke 1:17). On the mountain of the transfiguration Elijah and Moses appeared with Jesus and spoke of “the exodus he was about to complete in Jerusalem” (Luke 9:31). The prophet who fled to Horeb, sure he alone was left, stood at last beside the one he had been waiting for.

Where to go from here

The preachers of the church have loved these chapters, Elijah’s above all. A few places to begin:

  • On Solomon and his kingdom (1 Kings 1–11). Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “1 Kings and 2 Chronicles: The Kingdom of Peace” reads Solomon’s reign of peace as a picture of the rest Christ gives; Hudson Taylor’s sermon Coming to the King (1 Kings 10:13), on the queen of Sheba’s journey as a pattern for coming to Christ; and Book XVII of Augustine’s The City of God, which argues that the promises made to David’s son are not fulfilled in Solomon but in Christ.
  • On Elijah and the drought (1 Kings 17–18). E. M. Bounds’s Prayer and Praying Men, with its chapter “Elijah, the Praying Prophet”; and “Fire from Heaven, Rain on the Earth”, a chapter of The Prayers Jesus Learned, an Ochorus Original, on Elijah’s prayers on Carmel.
  • On Elijah at Horeb (1 Kings 19). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon Elijah’s Weakness, and Its Cure (1 Kings 19:1–18), on God’s gentle care of a burnt-out servant; A. B. Simpson’s sermon The Power of Stillness (1 Kings 19:12); and the chapter “The Still, Small Voice” in Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High.
  • For younger readers. Tell Me the Story – Book 3: Kings and Prophets, an Ochorus Original, which retells Solomon’s request for wisdom, Elijah and the ravens, the fire on the mountain and the still, small voice.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to 1 Kings in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
