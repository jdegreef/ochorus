— 1 Kings 8:27

1 Kings opens with an old man who cannot get warm. King David, the shepherd who faced a giant and sang the psalms, lies in bed under heaps of clothes while his sons manoeuvre for his throne. It closes, twenty-two chapters later, with a king of Israel bleeding to death in his chariot, dogs licking up his blood, and a son on the throne who “served Baal and worshiped him”. Between those two scenes lies a golden age and its undoing.

In Hebrew the book is called Melakhim, “Kings”, and it is the first half of a single work. The ancient Greek translation counted Samuel and Kings together as four books of “Kingdoms” and cut Kings in two, which is why 2 Kings begins in the middle of a reign. The story it tells runs from about 970 BC, when Solomon came to the throne, to about 850 BC, in the days of Ahab’s sons.

It is a book about kings, but its real subject is a King. Every ruler in it is measured by one question: did he do what was right in the sight of the LORD? And its most powerful figures are not the men on thrones but the prophets who walk in and say, “The LORD says this.”

Three movements

1 Kings falls into three parts. Chapters 1–11 belong to Solomon: his rise, his wisdom, the temple he built and the heart that turned away. Chapters 12–16 tell how the kingdom tore in two and follow both halves, king by king, through half a century of short reigns and violent ends. Chapters 17–22 slow down to follow one prophet, Elijah, in his long contest with King Ahab and Queen Jezebel.

The pace changes sharply. Solomon’s forty years fill eleven chapters; then kings rise and fall in a sentence or two, and Zimri reigns for seven days. Then the book all but stops to watch a man on a mountain, praying for fire.

A heart that listens

Solomon does not come to the throne gently. His older brother Adonijah declares, “I will be king,” Nathan and Bathsheba hurry to remind David of his promise, and Solomon is anointed while the people shout, “Long live King Solomon!” Once David is dead, the new king removes his rivals one by one, and the narrator notes without comment, “So the kingdom was made secure in Solomon’s hand.”

Then, at Gibeon, the LORD appears to the young king in a dream and makes an astonishing offer: “Ask for whatever you want me to give you.” Solomon calls himself “only a little boy” who does not know “how to go out and come in as a leader”, and asks:

{{OWB:1KI.3.9.s1}}

— 1 Kings 3:9

“It pleased the Lord that Solomon had asked for this.” He is given wisdom, and riches and honour besides. The next story shows the wisdom at work: two women, one living baby, and a king who calls for a sword and lets a mother’s love reveal the truth. His understanding is “as vast as the sand on the seashore” (4:29). And the old promise to Abraham seems to have come true at last: “Judah and Israel were as many as the sand by the sea; they ate and drank and were glad.”

A house for the name

The centre of Solomon’s reign is the temple. It was begun, the book says, in the 480th year after Israel came out of Egypt, and it was built in reverent quiet: the stones were dressed at the quarry, “so the sound of hammer, axe or any iron tool was never heard at the temple during the building.” When the ark is carried into the inner room, the cloud of God’s presence fills the house so thickly that the priests cannot stand to do their work, “since the house of the LORD was full of the glory of the LORD.”

Then Solomon spreads out his hands toward heaven and prays one of the great prayers of the Old Testament (8:22–53). Its heart is the verse that heads this introduction. The king who has just built the finest house in Israel knows that it cannot hold its Owner. What the temple can be is a place toward which people pray, and so the prayer becomes a long plea that God will listen: “Hear in heaven, your dwelling place; and when you hear, forgive.”

Solomon imagines every kind of trouble: drought, famine, defeat, the foreigner who comes from far away because of God’s name, and finally the worst of all, the people carried off into exile, since “there is no one who never sins.” Even then, he asks, if they turn back and pray toward this land, hear and forgive. For the exiles who first read the finished book, this prayer was a lifeline. Its goal is wider than Israel: “so that every people on earth may know that the LORD is God and that there is no other.”

When the queen of Sheba comes to test Solomon with hard questions, she leaves overwhelmed: “I was not told even half.” For a moment Israel is what it was meant to be, a light that draws the nations.

The heart that turned

But the shadows are there for anyone who reads closely. Deuteronomy had warned that a king must not build up horses from Egypt, or take many wives, “or his heart will be led astray”, or pile up silver and gold. Chapter 10 lists Solomon’s gold and his horses from Egypt; chapter 11 counts his wives. The sentence that follows is one of the saddest in the Old Testament:

{{OWB:1KI.11.4.s1}}

— 1 Kings 11:4

The wisest man in the world was not kept safe by his wisdom. The LORD tells him, “I will certainly tear the kingdom from you and give it to your servant.” Yet even in judgement there is mercy: one tribe will be left to his son “because of my servant David and because of Jerusalem, which I have chosen.” The prophet Ahijah tears a new cloak into twelve pieces and hands ten to Jeroboam, one of Solomon’s officials. The one left is kept for David’s sake, “so that my servant David may always have a lamp before me in Jerusalem”. That small lamp will burn through the rest of Kings.

The kingdom torn in two

When Solomon dies, his son Rehoboam goes to Shechem to be made king, and the northern tribes ask him to lighten the heavy labour his father laid on them. The old advisers tell him to be a servant to the people; his young friends tell him to show them who is master. He follows the young men: “My father made your yoke heavy, but I will make your yoke heavier still.” The north answers with an old cry of rebellion: “What part do we have in David?”

From here on there are two kingdoms. Israel, in the north, has ten tribes and, later, its capital at Samaria; Judah, in the south, has Jerusalem, the temple and David’s line. Jeroboam, the north’s first king, fears that if his people keep going up to Jerusalem to worship, their hearts will go with them. So he gives them shrines of their own:

{{OWB:1KI.12.28.s1}}

— 1 Kings 12:28

The words are almost exactly those spoken over Aaron’s golden calf at the foot of Sinai (Exodus 32:4), and the book means us to hear the echo. “This thing became a sin.” It is worship arranged for the safety of the state, and it becomes the north’s original sin. King after king of Israel is condemned for walking “in the way of Jeroboam and in his sin, by which he had led Israel to sin.”

Weighed against David

From chapter 14 on, the book settles into a pattern. Each king is introduced by the year of his neighbour’s reign in which he came to the throne, his age and the length of his reign are given, and then comes the verdict. Kings of Israel are measured against Jeroboam; kings of Judah against David. Asa “did what was right in the sight of the LORD, as his ancestor David had done”, though, the book adds, “The high places, however, were not taken away.” Of others the book says only that they did evil.

This is not the history a court chronicler would have written. Omri, who founded Samaria and was so powerful that Assyrian inscriptions went on calling Israel “the house of Omri” long after his line had ended, gets six verses. For his battles the reader is sent elsewhere, to “the Book of the History of the Kings of Israel”. What Kings wants to know is not how strong a king was but whether he was faithful.

And it watches for the word of the LORD coming true. A man of God from Judah cries out against Jeroboam’s altar at Bethel that a son of David named Josiah will one day burn human bones on it (13:2), and three hundred years later, in 2 Kings, he does. When Jericho is rebuilt at the cost of the builder’s sons, the narrator adds, “as the word of the LORD had said through Joshua son of Nun.” Kings make their plans, but the prophets’ word is what stands.

Elijah

Then Ahab comes to the throne, who “did what was evil in the sight of the LORD, more than all who were before him”. He marries Jezebel, a princess of Sidon, and builds a temple of Baal in Samaria. Baal was worshipped as the storm god, the giver of rain and harvest. And into that world, with no introduction and no ancestry, walks a man whose name means “My God is the LORD”:

{{OWB:1KI.17.1.s1}}

— 1 Kings 17:1

The drought is aimed at Baal on his own ground. For three years there is no rain, and God feeds his prophet by unlikely means: ravens bring him bread and meat by a stream, and then he is sent to a widow in Zarephath, in Sidon, Jezebel’s own country. She has “only a handful of flour in the jar and a little oil in the jug”, and is gathering sticks to cook a last meal for her son and herself. She shares it, and “the jar of flour was not emptied, and the jug of oil did not give out”. When her son dies, Elijah stretches himself over the boy and cries to the LORD, and the child lives. Jesus would later remind his home town that of all the widows in Israel, Elijah was sent to this foreigner (Luke 4:25–26).

Fire on Carmel

In the third year Elijah confronts Ahab and calls the people and the prophets of Baal to Mount Carmel. There he puts the question that the whole book has been asking:

{{OWB:1KI.18.21.s1}}

— 1 Kings 18:21

The prophets of Baal call on their god from morning until noon, “But no sound came, and no one answered.” Elijah mocks them: perhaps their god “has fallen asleep, and must be woken up!” Then, at the time of the evening offering, he rebuilds the LORD’s broken altar with twelve stones, drenches it with water, and prays a short, plain prayer: “Answer me, the LORD, answer me, so that these people may know that you, the LORD, are God and are turning their hearts back again.” The fire falls and consumes even the stones.

{{OWB:1KI.18.39.s1}}

— 1 Kings 18:39

The day ends in blood, with the prophets of Baal put to death at the Kishon River. It is a hard scene. It carries out the sentence the law of Moses laid on prophets who led Israel to other gods (Deuteronomy 13:5), and Christians have not taken it as a pattern for the church: when James and John later wanted to call down fire from heaven, Jesus “gave them a stern rebuke” (Luke 9:54–55). Then Elijah climbs the mountain again, bows to the ground and prays for rain, sending his servant to look out to sea seven times until “a cloud as small as a man’s hand” rises out of it. The letter of James takes the lesson: “Elijah was a human being just like us.”

A faint sound, thin as silence

The next chapter is one of the most human in the Bible. After the greatest victory of his life, a single threat from Jezebel sends Elijah running for his life into the wilderness. He sits under a broom tree and asks to die. “It is enough,” he says. “Now, LORD, take my life, for I am no better than my ancestors.” God’s first answer is not a rebuke but sleep and food, twice: “Get up and eat, for the journey is too much for you.”

He walks forty days to Horeb, the mountain where God gave the law to Moses, and there God asks him, “What are you doing here, Elijah?” Elijah pours out his complaint: he alone is left. Then the LORD passes by. A great wind splits the mountains, but the LORD is not in the wind, nor in the earthquake that follows.

{{OWB:1KI.19.12.s1}}

— 1 Kings 19:12

The God who answered by fire on Carmel now comes in something almost too quiet to hear. He does not argue with his tired servant. He gives him work to do, among it the anointing of Elisha to take his place, and corrects him gently: he is not alone. “Yet I will leave 7,000 in Israel, everyone whose knees have not bent to Baal.” Paul would recall those seven thousand when he wrote that there is always a remnant, “chosen by grace” (Romans 11:4–5).

A vineyard and a vision

The last chapters show what kind of king Ahab is. He wants Naboth’s vineyard; Naboth refuses to sell his family’s inheritance. Jezebel has him falsely accused and stoned, and when Ahab goes down to take the vineyard, Elijah is waiting. “Have you found me, my enemy?” says the king. “I have found you,” comes the answer. And yet, when Ahab tears his clothes and humbles himself, God delays the judgement. There is mercy even for Ahab.

The book ends with a prophet who will not say what the king wants to hear. Four hundred court prophets promise Ahab victory at Ramoth-gilead; Micaiah son of Imlah, whom Ahab hates “for his prophecies about me are never good”, says, “whatever the LORD says to me is what I will speak.” His vision of a lying spirit sent among Ahab’s prophets has puzzled readers; many take it to mean that God gave Ahab over to the falsehood he preferred, and notice that Micaiah tells him so to his face, a last warning. Ahab goes into battle in disguise, and “a man drew his bow at random”. No disguise can hide a king from the word of the LORD.

How to read 1 Kings

Read it with 2 Kings, which picks up the story mid-sentence. Do not worry about remembering every king of the north: notice the verdict on each, and watch for the prophets, who appear at every turning point. The books of Chronicles tell much of the same story, from Judah’s side and with different emphases; reading the two together is like hearing two witnesses.

The book does not name its author. Jewish tradition, recorded in the Talmud, credited Kings to the prophet Jeremiah. Many scholars see it as the last part of a long history running from Deuteronomy through Kings and shaped by Deuteronomy’s teaching, given its final form during the exile, since 2 Kings ends in about 561 BC. The writer names his sources, royal annals now lost, and Assyrian and Moabite inscriptions name Omri, Ahab and Jehu. The reign lengths and cross-dates are famously hard to fit together; overlapping reigns and different ways of counting a king’s first year explain much of the difficulty, and Christians hold a range of views on the details.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain, printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in 1 Kings are worth knowing.

“The LORD” and “the Lord.” Where the Hebrew uses God’s personal name, this translation follows long custom and writes “the LORD”. Where it uses the title Adonai, “Lord”, as it does when “It pleased the Lord” that Solomon asked for wisdom, it writes “Lord”. The old title “LORD of hosts” appears as “the LORD God of Armies”.

Hobbling. At Carmel the same Hebrew verb describes the people “hobbling between two choices” and the prophets of Baal “hobbling in a dance around the altar”. The translation keeps the echo: a people that will not choose ends up limping like the priests of a god who does not answer.

The voice at Horeb. The famous “still small voice” of older translations is, in Hebrew, something like “a sound of thin silence”. This translation renders it “a faint sound, thin as silence”.

“Rested with his ancestors.” The phrase older translations gave as “slept with his fathers” closes each reign; here it reads “rested with his ancestors”.
