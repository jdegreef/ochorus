— John 1:1

Every other Gospel begins somewhere on a map. Mark begins at the Jordan, Matthew with a family tree, Luke in the temple at Jerusalem. John begins before there was a map at all. His first three words are the first three words of the Bible, “In the beginning”, and before he has told us a single thing Jesus did, he has told us who Jesus is: the Word who was with God, and was God, and through whom everything that exists was made.

That is the key to the whole book. John is not less interested in what happened than the other Gospel writers; he is often more precise about times, places and festivals than any of them. But he will not let us watch a single scene without knowing who is standing in it. When Jesus turns water into wine, or gives sight to a blind man, or stands weeping at a friend’s grave, the reader already knows what the people in the story are only beginning to guess. The tension of the book lies in watching them find out, or refuse to.

Christians have loved this Gospel above the others for as long as there have been four. It has been called the “spiritual Gospel”, and it is the one most often put into the hands of someone reading the Bible for the first time. Both are fitting. It is simple enough that a child can follow it and deep enough that no one has ever reached the bottom of it. Its vocabulary is tiny, a few dozen ordinary words used over and over: light, life, love, truth, world, glory, believe, remain. But John turns those words slowly, like a jeweller turning a stone, until each one catches a different light.

Why a fourth Gospel?

Readers who come to John from Matthew, Mark and Luke notice at once how much is missing. There is no birth in Bethlehem, no baptism scene, no temptation in the wilderness. Jesus tells no parables of the kind the other Gospels are full of: no sower, no prodigal son, no good Samaritan. He casts out no demons. At the last supper there is no bread and cup; instead, Jesus kneels with a towel and washes his disciples’ feet.

And readers notice how much is new. Roughly nine-tenths of John has no parallel in the other three. Only John tells of the wedding at Cana, the conversation with Nicodemus by night, the woman at the well, the man who had been ill for thirty-eight years, the man born blind, the raising of Lazarus, the long farewell in the upper room, Thomas putting his hand into the wounds, and breakfast on the beach. Where the others give us mostly short sayings, John gives us long conversations and speeches, in which a single image (bread, water, light, a shepherd, a vine) is turned over until it has given up its meaning.

The early church explained the difference simply. Around AD 200, Clement of Alexandria wrote that John, seeing that the outward facts had already been set down in the other Gospels, composed “a spiritual Gospel”. That is not quite right, since John is full of outward facts, but it points in the right direction. John writes for readers who already know the story and need to understand it.

The book says why it was written

Most books leave their purpose to be guessed. John states his near the end, plainly:

— John 20:31

Everything in the book serves that sentence. The word to watch is believe. John never once uses the noun “faith”; he uses the verb, nearly a hundred times, more than Matthew, Mark and Luke together. For John, faith is not something you have so much as something you do: a movement of the whole person toward Jesus. People in this Gospel come to Jesus, follow him, remain in him, eat and drink him (in his own startling words in chapter 6). Each is another way of saying believe.

And belief, in John, is rarely instant. One of the quiet pleasures of reading the book through is watching people move toward Jesus by stages. Nicodemus comes “at night” in chapter 3, cautious and puzzled. In chapter 7 he speaks up, carefully, in Jesus’s defence before the council. In chapter 19, after the crucifixion, when the disciples have scattered, he comes out in daylight carrying about seventy pounds of myrrh and aloes to bury the body. The man born blind in chapter 9 calls Jesus first “the man named Jesus”, then “a prophet”, then a man “from God”, and at last falls down to worship him. John is showing his readers that it is possible to start a long way off and still arrive.

Seven signs

John never calls Jesus’s miracles “miracles” or “mighty works”. He calls them signs, deeds that point past themselves to something greater. The first half of the book is built around seven of them:

  • Water into wine at a wedding in Cana (2:1–11): the old purification jars filled to the brim with the best wine, kept until last.
  • An official’s son healed at a distance, by a word (4:46–54).
  • A man ill for thirty-eight years made to walk at the pool of Bethesda (5:1–15), on a Sabbath.
  • Five thousand fed from five barley loaves and two fish (6:1–15), followed by the teaching on the bread of life.
  • Walking on the water to his frightened disciples (6:16–21).
  • A man born blind given sight (9:1–41), after which the blind man sees and the sighted religious leaders are shown to be blind.
  • Lazarus raised after four days in the tomb (11:1–44).

The order is not accidental. The signs grow larger as they go, from a household embarrassment at a wedding to the undoing of death itself, and each is paired with words that explain it. Jesus feeds a crowd and then says he is the bread; he opens blind eyes and then says he is the light; he calls Lazarus out of the grave and then says he is the resurrection. The sign is the picture; the saying is the caption. The last sign is also the one that sets the authorities on the path to the cross: from the day Lazarus walks out of his tomb, John tells us, they planned to kill Jesus. Giving life costs Jesus his own.

Many readers have seen an eighth sign standing behind the seven: the empty tomb, on “the first day of the week”, which no one witnesses and to which the whole book points.

“I am”

Alongside the signs runs the other great thread of the book, the sayings in which Jesus describes himself with the words “I am”:

  • “I am the bread of life” (6:35)
  • “I am the light of the world” (8:12)
  • “I am the door” (10:9)
  • “I am the good shepherd” (10:11)
  • “I am the resurrection and the life” (11:25)
  • “I am the way, the truth, and the life” (14:6)
  • “I am the true vine” (15:1)

Each takes something people could not live without (food, light, safety, guidance, life itself) and says that what they were really reaching for is a person. But John also records Jesus saying “I am” with nothing after it at all, and these are the moments when his hearers react most violently. In chapter 8, arguing about Abraham, he says, “before Abraham was, I am”, and they pick up stones. Any Jewish hearer would have caught the echo of the name God gave Moses at the burning bush, “I am who I am”. In the garden in chapter 18, soldiers come to arrest him; he asks who they are looking for, answers “I am he” (in Greek, simply “I am”), and they step backward and fall to the ground. In John, Jesus is never really the prisoner. He goes to his death, as he says himself, because he lays down his life of his own accord.

Light and darkness, night and noon

John’s prologue announces a contest: “The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it.” The rest of the book stages it, often through the simple detail of what time it is.

Nicodemus, a respected teacher of Israel, comes to Jesus at night, and leaves still in the dark about what Jesus means. In the very next chapter, a Samaritan woman with a damaged past meets Jesus at a well about noon, in the full light of day, and goes back to her town to tell everyone she has found the Messiah. The insider stays confused; the outsider becomes a witness. When Judas leaves the last supper to betray his master, John adds three words that are among the most chilling in Scripture: “And it was night.”

And on the morning of the resurrection, Mary Magdalene comes to the tomb “while it was still dark”, and leaves it having seen the Lord.

The festivals

Much of John is set in Jerusalem during the great feasts of Israel, and Jesus repeatedly takes the meaning of a festival into himself. At Passover he feeds the crowd with bread in the wilderness, as Israel was fed with manna. At the Festival of Tabernacles, when water was poured out at the temple and great lamps lit its courts, he stands and cries out that anyone thirsty should come to him and drink, and says, “I am the light of the world.” At the Festival of Dedication, which remembered the cleansing of the temple, he speaks of himself as the one the Father set apart and sent.

Above all, John frames the story with the Passover. At the start, John the Baptist points to Jesus and says, “Look, there is the Lamb of God, who carries away the sin of the world!” At the end, John notes that Jesus was condemned about noon on the day of Preparation for the Passover, the day the lambs for the feast were slaughtered; that a sponge of sour wine was lifted to him on hyssop, the plant used to mark the doorposts with blood in Egypt; and that not one of his bones was broken, as the law required of the Passover lamb. John does not stop to explain these details. He trusts his readers to notice.

The hour and the glory

Early in the book, Jesus keeps saying that his “hour” has not yet come. At Cana he says it to his mother; in Jerusalem the authorities try to seize him, but cannot, “because his hour had not yet come.” Then, in chapter 12, when some Greeks come asking to see him, he says, “The hour has come when the Son of Man is to be glorified”, and begins at once to speak of a grain of wheat falling into the ground and dying.

This is perhaps the most profound thing about John’s Gospel. The other Gospels show the cross as the depth of Jesus’s humiliation, followed by the glory of the resurrection. John sees glory in the cross itself. Jesus speaks of being “lifted up”, a phrase that means both raised on a cross and exalted to a throne. His last word from the cross is not a cry of abandonment but a single Greek word of completion:

— John 19:30

The love that John says Jesus had for his own, the love with which he “loved them to the end”, is shown nowhere more fully than there. In John, the cross is not where the glory of God is hidden. It is where it is seen.

What are you looking for?

Some of John’s meaning is carried by quiet repetitions that are easy to miss. The first words Jesus speaks in this Gospel are a question to two men who have started to follow him: “What are you looking for?” Near the end, standing outside his own empty tomb, he asks a weeping woman almost the same question: “Who is it you are looking for?” She supposes he is the gardener, until he says her name. The whole Gospel lies between those two questions.

Or take the fire. On the night of the arrest, Peter stands warming himself by a charcoal fire in the high priest’s courtyard, and that night he denies three times that he knows Jesus. The Greek word for that kind of fire appears only twice in the New Testament. The second time is in the last chapter of John, on the shore of Galilee, where the risen Jesus has a charcoal fire burning with fish laid on it, and asks Peter three times, “Do you love me?” Three denials on the night of one fire; three declarations of love beside another. Jesus does not merely forgive Peter. He takes him back to the place of his failure, and makes it the place of his calling: “Feed my sheep.”

The disciple whom Jesus loved

The Gospel never names its author. It points instead to a figure it calls “the disciple whom Jesus loved”, who leans against Jesus at the last supper, stands at the foot of the cross where Jesus entrusts his mother to him, outruns Peter to the empty tomb, and is the first to recognise the risen Lord on the shore. The closing verses say that his testimony stands behind the book: “This is the disciple who is testifying about these things and who has written them, and we know his testimony is true.”

From the second century onward, the church identified this disciple as John the son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve, writing late in a long life at Ephesus, probably toward the end of the first century. Many readers still hold that view; others think the beloved disciple was a follower of Jesus from Jerusalem outside the Twelve, or that his own disciples gave the book its final shape (the “we” of that closing verse). Either way, it is striking that the writer never puts himself at the centre. He is not named; he is simply loved. Perhaps that is the point. Every reader of this Gospel is invited to stand where he stood.

How to read John

John can be read in an afternoon, and it is worth reading that way at least once, straight through, to feel its shape: the prologue (1:1–18); the book of signs, in which Jesus reveals himself to the world and the world largely turns away (1:19–12:50); the farewell, in which he turns to his own and prepares them for his going (13–17); the hour of glory, his arrest, trial, death and resurrection (18–20); and the epilogue by the lake (21).

But it is also a book to read slowly. Notice who comes to Jesus and who goes away. Notice when it is night. Notice how often the people in the story misunderstand Jesus in exactly the way that lets him say more: Nicodemus wondering how a grown man can be born again, the Samaritan woman asking where the living water is kept, the disciples supposing Lazarus is only asleep. And notice the questions Jesus asks, because John means them for the reader too. “What are you looking for?” “Do you want to be well?” “Do you believe this?” “Do you love me?”

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in John are worth knowing about before you begin.

“The Jews.” John uses the phrase more than seventy times, in several senses: the Jewish people as a whole, the people of Judea, and very often the religious authorities in Jerusalem who opposed Jesus. Where the authorities are meant, this translation says “the Jewish leaders”. Jesus, his mother and every one of his first disciples were Jews, and nothing in this Gospel should be read as setting him against his own people.

John 3:16. The most famous verse in the Bible reads a little differently here: “For God’s love for the world was such that he gave his one and only Son.” The Greek word often translated “so” (“God so loved the world”) means “in this way”, not “so much”: the verse tells us not how much God loved but how, by giving.

The disputed passage. The story of the woman caught in adultery (7:53–8:11) is not found in the oldest manuscripts of John, and later manuscripts place it in different spots. Like most Bibles, this edition prints it, set apart and in brackets, with a note.
