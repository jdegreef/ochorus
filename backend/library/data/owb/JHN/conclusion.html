John ends by admitting that he has left almost everything out. It is a disarming last sentence for a book so carefully made, and an honest one. He has not tried to tell us everything Jesus did. He has chosen, from a life too large for all the books in the world, the handful of days and words that would let a reader see who Jesus is, and believe, and live.

The question the book leaves with you

Look back over the Gospel and it is full of people deciding what to do about Jesus. Andrew goes to fetch his brother. Nathanael, who doubted anything good could come from a place like Nazareth, ends the first chapter calling Jesus the Son of God. The Samaritan woman leaves her water jar at the well. A crowd that wanted bread walks away when Jesus offers himself instead, and he turns to the Twelve to ask whether they will go too. Peter answers for them all:

Others decide the opposite way. Some who see Lazarus walk out of his tomb go straight to the Pharisees, and from that day the council plots to kill the man who called him out. Pilate, who asks “What is truth?”, has the Truth standing in front of him and hands him over to be crucified. John does not soften this. Light has come into the world, and people respond to it.

The last person in the Gospel to come to faith is Thomas, who had said he would not believe unless he could touch the wounds. When Jesus offers them to him, he does not need to. He says the greatest words of faith in the book, “My Lord and my God!”, and Jesus answers with a blessing that reaches beyond the room and down the centuries to every reader of the Gospel: “Blessed are those who have believed without having seen.” That blessing is the book’s hope for you.

Life, not only information

John’s stated purpose has two halves: that you may believe, and that by believing you may have life. He means a life that begins now. In John, eternal life is not only life that lasts forever; it is the life of God himself, given to people while they are still on this earth. “Eternal life is this,” Jesus prays on his last night: “that they know you, the only true God, and the one you sent, Jesus Christ.”

That is why the farewell chapters (13 to 17) are so full of the word remain. Having shown us who Jesus is, John shows us what it is to live with him: to remain in him as a branch remains in the vine, to keep his word, to love one another as he has loved us, to receive the Spirit he promised, the Advocate who would be with his people when he had gone. The Gospel ends on a beach with a meal and a commission, not with a farewell. The risen Jesus says to Peter what he says to every disciple: “Follow me.”

Where to go from here

Readers have been preaching, praying and writing about John for nineteen centuries, and many of the best of them are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On abiding in Christ (John 15). Andrew Murray’s The True Vine is a month of short readings on the vine and the branches; his Abide in Christ takes the single word “remain” and spends a whole book on it.
  • On being born again (John 3). J. C. Ryle’s sermon Are You Born Again? and D. L. Moody’s The New Birth both take up Jesus’s words to Nicodemus; Charles Finney’s God’s Love for a Sinning World preaches on John 3:16.
  • On the cross. John Newton’s Behold the Lamb of God (John 1:29), and two sermons on Jesus’s promise to draw all people to himself when he is lifted up (John 12:32): Spurgeon’s Christ Lifted Up and R. A. Torrey’s The Great Attraction.
  • On living water (John 4 and 7). Hudson Taylor’s Unfailing Springs and William J. Seymour’s River of Living Water.
  • On the promise of the Spirit (John 14). Martin Luther’s The Office of the Holy Spirit and A. W. Tozer’s How to Cultivate the Holy Spirit’s Companionship.
  • On faith and following. Alexander Maclaren’s Grace and Truth (John 1:17), Tozer’s He Must Increase (John 3:30), Ryle’s To Whom? (John 6:68), Horatius Bonar’s Faith in an Unseen Christ (John 20:29), and Spurgeon’s Lovest Thou Me? on the conversation by the lake (John 21:15–17).

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to John in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this Gospel.

But the best next step may simply be to read John again. It is a book that rewards a second reading more than almost any other, because on the second time through you know, from the very first verse, what the people in the story are still finding out. And you may find that the question Jesus asked at the beginning has been waiting for you all along:

“What are you looking for?”

— John 1:38
