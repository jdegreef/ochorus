— Matthew 28:18–20

Matthew ends where Jesus began his teaching, on a mountain in Galilee. There is no ascension scene, no farewell, not even an “Amen”. The last word is a promise, and the book stops while it is still sounding. The Gospel that began with a family line reaching back to Abraham closes with a family about to reach out to every nation on earth.

The question the book leaves with you

At the turning point of the Gospel, Jesus asks his disciples two questions. The first is easy: who do people say the Son of Man is? Some say John the Baptist, some Elijah, some Jeremiah or another prophet. The second is not:

{{OWB:MAT.16.15.s1}}

— Matthew 16:15

Matthew has been answering that question since his first verse. Jesus is the son of David and the son of Abraham; Immanuel, God with us; the one who will save his people from their sins; the teacher with authority of his own; the Son in whom the Father delights; the king of the Jews on a Roman cross. But the question is put to the disciples, not to the crowds, and the book puts it to its readers in the same way. It is not enough to know what others say.

And the answer is meant to be lived. The Sermon on the Mount ends with a storm and two houses. In Matthew’s great picture of the judgment, the King says to those on his right that whatever they did for the least of his brothers and sisters, “you did it for me.” Neither the sheep nor the goats knew whom they were serving or neglecting. Matthew’s Jesus cares about what we confess and about what we do, and he will not let us have the one without the other.

Where the book points

It points, first, to the nations. The command to “make disciples of all the nations” has sent the church to the ends of the earth, and the book shows what that means. Disciples are made by baptism into the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, and by teaching people to keep everything Jesus commanded, the commands this Gospel has so carefully gathered into its five great sermons.

It points, second, to the church: a community gathered in Jesus’s name, where two or three are enough for him to be among them, and where forgiveness has no ceiling.

And it points to the end. Matthew’s Jesus speaks often of the close of the age, of a harvest, a wedding feast, a master returning from a journey. He also says plainly that no one knows the day or the hour, not the angels, not even the Son. The right response is not to calculate but to be ready, and to be found faithful.

Where to go from here

Matthew has been preached and expounded more than almost any book of the Bible, and some of its best interpreters are in this library. A few places to begin:

  • Through the whole Gospel. John Chrysostom’s Homilies on Matthew, ninety sermons preached at Antioch, the fullest commentary from the early church; and J. C. Ryle’s Expository Thoughts on Matthew, plain and warm, chapter by chapter.
  • On the Sermon on the Mount (5–7). Thomas Watson’s The Beatitudes (5:3–12) and his The Lord’s Prayer (6:9–13); Alexander Maclaren’s The First Beatitude (5:3); and three sermons by Martyn Lloyd-Jones, The Salt of the Earth (5:13), The Light of the World (5:14–16) and God or Mammon (6:19–24).
  • On Jesus’s invitation (11:28–30). Maclaren’s The Rest Giver and John Newton’s Messiah’s Easy Yoke.
  • On weak faith. Charles H. Spurgeon’s Sweet Comfort for Feeble Saints, on the bent reed and the wick that is barely burning (12:20), and Augustine of Hippo’s Peter on the Waves (14:25–31).
  • On Peter’s confession (16:17). Jonathan Edwards’s A Divine and Supernatural Light, on what it means that “No human being showed you this”.
  • On the resurrection (28:6). Christmas Evans’s The Resurrection of Jesus.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Matthew in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this Gospel.

But the best next step may be to read Matthew again, with the end in view. Knowing how it finishes, you will notice how often the nations were already at the door: the magi, the centurion, the Canaanite woman, the soldiers at the cross. You will notice how often Jesus promised to be present. And you may hear the last words of the book as they were meant, not as a farewell, but as a promise for every day of your own life:

“I myself am with you every day, until the close of the age.”

— Matthew 28:20
