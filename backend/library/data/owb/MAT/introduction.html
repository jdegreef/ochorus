{{OWB:MAT.1.1.s1}}

— Matthew 1:1

Open the New Testament and the first thing you meet is a list of names. It is not a false start. Matthew’s first words, in Greek, are the words that open the account of heaven and earth and the list of Adam’s descendants in the Greek Old Testament (Genesis 2:4; 5:1). Before he has told us a single thing Jesus did, he has told us that this story is the next chapter of the old one, and its fulfilment.

The list says so too. It runs from Abraham, to whom God promised that all nations would be blessed, through David, to whom God promised a king whose kingdom would never end, to “Jesus, who is called Christ”. Matthew arranges it in three sets of fourteen generations: from Abraham to David, from David to the exile in Babylon, and from the exile to the Messiah. Israel’s whole history, its rise, its fall and its long waiting, has been leading to this child.

That is the key to the whole book. Matthew is the Gospel of the promises kept. It was written by a Jew, steeped in the Scriptures of Israel, for readers who needed to see that Jesus was not a break with everything God had said before but the point of it all. And it ends, as the old story never quite could, with the nations of the world drawn in.

The first Gospel

Matthew has stood first among the four Gospels for as long as there have been four, and for most of the church’s history it was the most read, the most quoted and the most preached of them. It is the Gospel of the Sermon on the Mount and the Lord’s Prayer as most Christians pray it, of the wise men and their star, of Peter walking on the water and the parable of the sheep and the goats. It reads like a book made to be taught.

Like the other Gospels, it does not name its author. From the second century the church gave it to Matthew, one of the Twelve, the tax collector whose calling it tells in a single verse:

{{OWB:MAT.9.9.s1}}

— Matthew 9:9

Mark and Luke tell the same story of a tax collector named Levi; only this Gospel calls him Matthew. Papias, a bishop in Asia Minor early in the second century, is quoted by the historian Eusebius as saying that “Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language, and every one interpreted them as he was able”, and Irenaeus, around 180, says that Matthew issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect. What Papias meant is still debated, since the Gospel we have reads like a book composed in Greek.

Most scholars today think Matthew drew on Mark’s Gospel, which he follows closely and nearly all of which he contains, along with a collection of Jesus’s sayings that he shares with Luke. Others hold, as Augustine did, that Matthew came first. Many date the book to the decades after the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70; others put it earlier, in the 60s. On any view its author knew the Jewish world from the inside, and the questions it was asking about Jesus.

Five great sermons

Matthew gathers Jesus’s teaching into five long speeches, each ending with almost the same words: “When Jesus finished these words”, “When Jesus had finished giving these instructions”, “When Jesus had finished telling these parables”, and so on. Between them come stories of what Jesus did. The book moves back and forth, deed and word, like a weaver’s shuttle:

  • The Sermon on the Mount (5–7): the life of the kingdom, from the beatitudes to the house built on rock.
  • The sending of the Twelve (10): how the disciples are to go out, and what they will suffer.
  • The parables of the kingdom (13): the sower, the weeds, the mustard seed, the treasure, the pearl and the net.
  • Life together (18): little ones, lost sheep, a brother who sins, and forgiveness without limit.
  • The end of the age (24–25): the fall of the temple, the coming of the Son of Man, and three parables of readiness.

Many readers have heard in the five an echo of the five books of Moses, and seen Jesus as a new Moses who teaches from a mountain. Matthew does not say so, and the echo should not be pressed. But the parallel with Moses is there from the start: a wicked king who kills the baby boys, a child saved by flight, a return from Egypt, forty days in the wilderness, and then a mountain and a teaching. The difference is that Moses received the law from God. Jesus speaks it in his own name: “But I tell you.”

Promises kept

About ten times Matthew stops the story to say that something happened “so that the word the Lord had spoken through the prophet would be fulfilled”, and quotes the verse. The virgin conceives; the child is born in Bethlehem; he is called out of Egypt; he settles in Galilee, “where the Gentiles live”; he heals the sick, rides into Jerusalem on a donkey, and is sold for thirty silver coins. No other Gospel quotes the Old Testament so often or so deliberately.

These quotations can puzzle modern readers, because the verses often meant something in their first setting that does not look like a prediction. Hosea’s “Out of Egypt I called my son” is about Israel leaving Egypt in the exodus. But that is exactly Matthew’s point. Jesus does not only fulfil a handful of forecasts; he lives Israel’s story over again, and gets it right. Israel was God’s son, called out of Egypt, tested in the wilderness and found wanting. Jesus is God’s Son, called out of Egypt, tested in the wilderness for forty days, and faithful. At his baptism he tells John that this is the fitting way “to fulfill all righteousness”, and he says the same of the whole of Scripture:

{{OWB:MAT.5.17.s1}}

— Matthew 5:17

The kingdom of heaven

John the Baptist’s message and Jesus’s are the same, word for word: “Repent, because the kingdom of heaven has come near.” Where Mark and Luke say “the kingdom of God”, Matthew almost always says “the kingdom of heaven”, a phrase found nowhere else in the New Testament. It is not a different kingdom, and it does not mean a place we go when we die. Devout Jews often spoke of “heaven” to avoid saying the name of God too freely, and Matthew follows their custom. The kingdom of heaven is God’s own rule, breaking into the world in the person of Jesus.

The parables of chapter 13 explain what kind of kingdom it is. It starts small, like a mustard seed. It grows unseen, like yeast in the dough. For now it grows alongside the weeds, and the sorting is left to the harvest. And it is worth everything: a man finds treasure buried in a field and, “full of joy”, sells all he owns to buy that field; a trader finds a single pearl of very high price and does the same. The chapter ends with a picture that may be Matthew’s own portrait of himself, a scribe trained for the kingdom, who brings out of his storeroom “both new things and old”.

The Sermon on the Mount

The first of the five speeches is the most famous sermon ever preached. It opens with a list of the happy that turns the world’s list upside down:

{{OWB:MAT.5.3.s1}} {{OWB:MAT.5.4.s1}} {{OWB:MAT.5.5.s1}}

— Matthew 5:3–5

Then it goes deeper than any law court can reach. Six times Jesus quotes what “our ancestors were told” and goes beyond it: not only murder but anger, not only adultery but the look that wants, not only fair revenge but turning the other cheek, not only love for a neighbour but love for an enemy. He calls for a righteousness that “goes far beyond that of the scribes and Pharisees”, and he ends that part of the sermon with words that have humbled every reader since: “You, then, are to be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect.”

Christians have read the sermon in many ways: as a law for every disciple, as a counsel of perfection for the few, as a mirror that shows us our need of grace, as the ethic of the kingdom still to come. Most have found some truth in more than one of these. But the sermon itself does not let anyone admire it from a distance. Its centre is the Lord’s Prayer, a prayer to a Father who already knows what his children need. Its rule of life is short enough to memorise: “treat others the way you would want them to treat you.” And it ends not with an idea but with two houses, one on rock and one on sand, and a storm that comes to both. The difference is whether we listen to Jesus’s words and act on them.

God with us

Near the beginning of the book an angel tells Joseph to name the child Jesus, “because he is the one who will save his people from their sins.” Then Matthew adds a second name, from Isaiah:

{{OWB:MAT.1.23.s1}}

— Matthew 1:23

Matthew returns to that name at the middle of his Gospel and at the end. In chapter 18 Jesus promises that wherever two or three come together in his name, “I am there among them.” And the last words of the book are a promise: “I myself am with you every day, until the close of the age.” The Gospel is framed by God’s presence. Jesus is not only the teacher of Israel and the fulfilment of its Scriptures. He is the one in whom God has come to be with his people, and he does not leave.

For Israel, and for the nations

Matthew is the most Jewish of the Gospels. It assumes its readers know about the Sabbath, the temple tax and the washing of hands. Jesus is the son of David, the king of the Jews, the Messiah of Israel. When he first sends out the Twelve he tells them to stay off the roads that lead to the Gentiles and go “to the lost sheep, the people of Israel”, and he says the same of himself.

And yet it is also the Gospel that opens most widely to the world. Four women appear in the family line, all of them, it seems, outsiders in one way or another: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and “the wife of Uriah”. The first people to worship the infant king are magi from the east, Gentile stargazers, while Jerusalem’s king tries to kill him. A Roman centurion shows faith such as Jesus has found nowhere in Israel, and Jesus says that many will come from east and west to eat with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. A Canaanite woman, whom the disciples want sent away, will not be put off, and goes home with her daughter healed and these words ringing in her ears: “Woman, your faith is great.” At the cross it is a centurion and his soldiers who say, “This man truly was God’s Son!”

So when the risen Jesus sends the eleven out to “all the nations”, he is not changing his mind. He is completing what was promised to Abraham at the head of the family list: that through his offspring all the peoples of the earth would be blessed.

Peter and the church

Matthew is the only Gospel in which Jesus speaks of the church, and he does so twice. The first time is at Caesarea Philippi, at the turning point of the book. Jesus asks his disciples who people say he is, and then who they say he is:

{{OWB:MAT.16.16.s1}}

— Matthew 16:16

Jesus answers that no human being showed Peter this, but the Father, and then plays on his name, which means rock: “you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church.” Christians have long differed over what the rock is: Peter himself, as the first of the apostles; the faith he has just confessed; or Christ, the only foundation. The second time is in chapter 18, where the church is a small and very practical thing, a gathering of brothers and sisters who must learn to deal honestly with sin among them and forgive one another, not seven times but seventy-seven.

Matthew does not flatter the church’s first leader. A few verses after his great confession Peter tries to talk Jesus out of the cross and is called Satan. He walks on the water and sinks. He swears he will never desert Jesus and denies him three times before a rooster crows. The rock on which the church is built is a man who needs to be caught by the hand.

The king on the cross

The last chapters of Matthew follow Mark closely, but add details of their own: Judas’s remorse and death, the dream of Pilate’s wife, Pilate washing his hands, the earthquake at Jesus’s death and the guard at the tomb. Over the cross hangs the charge, “This is Jesus, the King of the Jews”, and Matthew wants his readers to see that it is true. The king of Israel is enthroned between two criminals.

One verse in these chapters needs a careful word. When Pilate washes his hands of Jesus’s death, the crowd gathered before him shouts, “Let the guilt for his death fall on us and on our children!” For centuries that cry was used to blame the Jewish people as a whole for the crucifixion and to justify hatred and violence against them. The text does not support that use. Matthew reports what a crowd, stirred up by its leaders, shouted on one morning. Jesus, his disciples and the women at the cross were all Jews; and in this Gospel Jesus’s blood is “poured out for many to bring forgiveness of sins”.

The resurrection in Matthew comes with an earthquake and an angel like lightning, who rolls back the stone and sits on it. The guards become like dead men; the women run off with fear and great joy, and meet Jesus himself on the road. And the book ends on a mountain in Galilee, where the eleven worship him, “but some had doubts”. It is an honest touch, and a hopeful one: Jesus sends them out anyway.

How to read Matthew

Read it first for its shape. The birth and childhood (1–2) and the beginning of Jesus’s work (3–4) lead into the alternation of speeches and stories (5–25), and then into the passion and resurrection (26–28). Notice the five speeches and the words that close each one. Notice the turning point at 16:21, where Jesus begins to show his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and suffer.

Then read it slowly, with the Old Testament open. When Matthew says something was fulfilled, look up the verse and ask what it meant in its first setting, and why he thought it came true in Jesus. Notice how often Jesus says “Truly I tell you”, and how often he says “something greater” is here: greater than the temple, greater than Jonah, greater than Solomon.

And read the Sermon on the Mount as Jesus meant it, not only as teaching to admire but as words to build a life on.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Greek and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Matthew are worth knowing about before you begin.

“The kingdom of heaven.” The translation keeps Matthew’s own phrase rather than changing it to “the kingdom of God”, so that readers can follow it through the book.

The beatitudes. Each is printed in two halves, the blessing and the promise, joined by a colon: “Blessed are the merciful: mercy will be shown to them.” Where older Bibles say “the meek”, this one says “the gentle”, since “meek” can now suggest weakness.

The Lord’s Prayer. It asks God not to lead us “into testing” and to “rescue us from the evil one”. The familiar closing words of praise, “For yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory”, are not in the earliest manuscripts; the church added them in its worship, and this translation ends the prayer where Matthew did.

Verses not found in the oldest manuscripts. Matthew 17:21, 18:11 and 23:14, which older Bibles print, are missing from the earliest copies and were probably added from Mark and Luke. They are not in the text, and the verse numbers are skipped. In 27:16–17 the prisoner whom Pilate offers the crowd is called by his full name, Jesus Barabbas, as some early manuscripts have it: Pilate asks whether they want “Jesus Barabbas, or Jesus who is called Christ”.

“Christ.” The Open Word Bible lets a reader choose whether Christos reads “Christ” or “the Messiah”. This edition prints “Christ”, but in Matthew above all it is worth remembering that the word means Israel’s anointed King, the son of David.
