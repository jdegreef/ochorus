— Job 42:16–17

Job ends with a long life and a peaceful death, his children’s children around him, and the friends who wronged him forgiven through his prayers. It is a gentle ending to a terrible book, but it is not the answer. The answer came earlier, in the storm, and it was not an explanation. It was God himself.

The question the book leaves with you

The book began with a question asked in heaven: “Does Job fear God for nothing?” By the end, it has been answered. Job lost everything God had given him, and he did not curse God. He complained, wept and demanded a hearing, but he never let go, and when God finally spoke, Job bowed down. The Accuser was wrong. A human being can love God for God’s sake.

But the question does not stay in heaven. It comes down to every reader. Would my faith survive if the hedge came down? Is God, to me, a means to a comfortable life, or is he worth trusting when he gives no reasons? Job does not make that question easy, but it shows that faith is allowed to be honest, that lament is a form of prayer, and that God would rather be argued with than lied about. “You have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has.”

And Job leaves a second question, one he asked himself. He longed for someone to stand between him and God: an umpire who “could lay his hand on us both”, a witness in heaven, a Redeemer who would stand up for him at the last. Who could that be?

Where the book points

The New Testament gives the answer Job could only reach for:

“For there is one God, and one mediator between God and humanity, Christ Jesus, himself human, who gave himself as a ransom for all.”

— 1 Timothy 2:5–6

Job wanted a go-between who was not “a human being like me” on the one side and yet could understand a human being on the other. In Jesus, God became the mediator Job asked for. More than that, Jesus became the innocent sufferer. He was the one truly blameless man, and he suffered not for his own sins but, as the church has always confessed, for ours. On the cross he cried out to God in the words of a psalm, “My God, my God, why have you abandoned me?”, and Job’s lament became the prayer of the Son of God. The letter to the Hebrews says that we have a high priest who can “share the feeling of our weaknesses”.

Job asked, “If a mortal dies, will they live again?” The empty tomb is God’s reply. Job hoped to see God in his flesh; Christians believe that hope will be fulfilled in the resurrection of the body. And when Paul came to the end of his own long wrestling with God’s ways in Romans 11, he could only break into praise, and he borrowed words from the LORD’s speech to Job to do it: “Or who has given him anything first and must be paid back?”

Where to go from here

Job has been a companion to suffering believers in every age, and some of the best writing on him is in this library. A few places to begin:

  • On the Redeemer (Job 19). John Newton’s sermon I Know That My Redeemer Liveth (Job 19:25–26).
  • On Job’s complaint and longing (Job 10, 14, 23, 29). Charles H. Spurgeon’s The Sweet Uses of Adversity (Job 10:2), Order and Argument in Prayer (Job 23:3–4) and Comfort for the Desponding (Job 29:2); and George MacDonald’s Unspoken Sermons, whose long sermon “The Voice of Job” begins from Job 14:13–15.
  • On the counsel of the friends and Elihu. Alexander Maclaren’s The Peaceable Fruits of Sorrows Rightly Borne (Job 5:17–27) and What Life May Be Made (Job 22:26–29), and Spurgeon’s Songs in the Night (Job 35:10).
  • On the book as a whole. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, whose chapter “Job: The Death of Self” reads the book as the story of a good man brought to the end of himself; and Andrew Murray’s Divine Healing, whose chapter “Job’s Sickness and Healing” begins with Job 2:7.
  • On asking God why. The Key Teachings of Elisabeth Elliot, an Ochorus Original, whose chapter “On Asking God Why” turns to Job; and The Key Teachings of Hudson Taylor, whose chapter “Blessed Adversity” follows Taylor’s address on Job.
  • On the hard places of providence. Thomas Boston’s The Crook in the Lot, on bearing what God has sent, which returns again and again to Job; and Boethius’s The Consolation of Philosophy, written in prison awaiting execution, long loved by Christian readers as a book on suffering and the good that cannot be taken away.

You can also go the other way, from the text to the classics. Every reference to Job in this library can be tapped to read the verse, and the Scripture pages gather, chapter by chapter, what the great writers of the church have said about each passage of this book.
