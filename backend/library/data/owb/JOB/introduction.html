— Job 1:9

Everyone has heard of Job. His patience is a proverb, and his name has become shorthand for anyone who suffers much and complains little. But people who actually read the book are often surprised. The Job of chapters 1 and 2 is patient indeed. The Job of the next forty chapters curses the day he was born, accuses God of hunting him down, demands his day in court, and tells his friends that they are “miserable comforters”. And at the end of the book, God says that Job, not his pious friends, has spoken rightly of him.

Job is the Bible’s great book about suffering, but it is not a book that explains suffering. It is a book about what happens to faith when suffering cannot be explained: whether a person can go on trusting God, and arguing with him, and still be his servant. Its question is asked in the first chapter, by the Accuser in the court of heaven: “Does Job fear God for nothing?” Is faith only a bargain that lasts as long as God keeps his side of it, or is God worth trusting for his own sake?

A man in the land of Uz

The story is set far from Israel, in “the land of Uz”, probably in Edom or the desert east of the Jordan, and in a world that feels very old. Job counts his wealth in flocks and herds, offers sacrifices for his family himself, as the patriarchs did, and lives to a great age. He is not an Israelite, and the book never mentions the exodus, the law of Moses or the temple. It is a story about a human being before God, and it speaks to anyone.

The book does not name its author or its date. One ancient Jewish tradition credited it to Moses; scholars have proposed dates from the time of Solomon to after the exile, and the question cannot be settled. The prophet Ezekiel names Job with Noah and Daniel as a byword for righteousness (Ezekiel 14:14), and James in the New Testament speaks of “Job’s endurance” and of the outcome the Lord gave (James 5:11). Its Hebrew is among the most difficult in the Old Testament, rich in rare words, and its poetry among the greatest ever written.

The shape of the book

Job is built like a great arch, with a prose story at either end and poetry in between:

  • The prologue (chapters 1–2), in prose: Job’s goodness, the two scenes in heaven, his losses, and his friends’ arrival.
  • Job’s lament (chapter 3), in which he curses the day of his birth.
  • The debate (chapters 4–27): three rounds of speeches, in which each of Job’s friends speaks in turn, Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar, and Job answers each one. The rounds grow shorter as the friends run out of things to say; in the third, Bildad’s speech is only six verses long and Zophar does not speak at all.
  • A poem on wisdom (chapter 28).
  • Job’s final defence (chapters 29–31), ending, “The words of Job are ended.”
  • The speeches of Elihu (chapters 32–37), a young man who has been listening.
  • The LORD’s speeches from the storm (38:1–42:6), with Job’s two brief replies.
  • The epilogue (42:7–17), in prose: the friends rebuked, Job restored.

The question in heaven

The book opens on a man who is “blameless and upright; he feared God and turned away from evil.” He has seven sons and three daughters and great wealth, and he is so careful that after his children’s feasts he offers sacrifices for them, in case they have “sinned and cursed God in their hearts.”

Then the scene moves to heaven, where “the sons of God”, members of God’s council, present themselves before the LORD, and among them is the Accuser. It is the LORD who raises Job’s name: “Have you considered my servant Job?” The Accuser answers with the question that drives the book. Of course Job is good, he says; God has put “a protective hedge around him”. Take away what he has, “and he will surely curse you to your face.”

The LORD allows the test, within limits: “Only do not lay a hand on the man himself.” In a single day Job loses everything. Four messengers come, each arriving before the last has finished, each with the same terrible refrain: “I am the only one who has escaped to tell you.” His oxen, donkeys, sheep and camels are gone, his servants killed, and all ten of his children crushed when a great wind brings down the house where they are eating. Job tears his robe, shaves his head, falls to the ground, and worships:

{{OWB:JOB.1.21.s1}}

— Job 1:21

A second test takes his health. He sits among the ashes, scraping his sores with a piece of broken pottery, and his wife says what the Accuser had predicted: “Curse God and die!” Job answers, “Are we to accept good from God and not accept trouble as well?”

Everything that follows depends on one fact: the reader knows what Job never learns. We have seen the scene in heaven. We know that Job is not being punished, that the LORD himself has called him blameless, and that he suffers, as the LORD says, “for no reason”. Job and his friends know none of this. They must make sense of his suffering from below, and that is exactly what they cannot do.

Seven days of silence

Three friends hear of Job’s troubles and come to comfort him. When they see him, they do not recognise him. They weep, tear their robes, and sit with him on the ground for seven days and nights: “No one said a word to him, because they saw how great his suffering was.” It is the best thing they do in the whole book. Readers who have sat with the grieving know why.

Then Job speaks, and what he says is not patient at all. He curses the day he was born: “Let the day I was born perish”. He asks why he did not die at birth, why light is given to those in misery. It is one of the darkest chapters in Scripture, and it is in Scripture. The Bible does not ask the suffering to pretend.

The friends’ answer

The friends are not fools, and they are not villains. They are devout men who believe, with most people in every age, that the world is morally ordered: God rewards the righteous and punishes the wicked. Eliphaz appeals to experience and to a vision in the night: “Think back now: who that was innocent has ever perished?” Bildad appeals to the wisdom of tradition: “Does God twist justice?” Zophar is blunter still, and tells Job that God has overlooked part of his guilt.

Much of what they say is true in general, and some of it is quoted elsewhere in the Bible. Eliphaz’s “How blessed is the one whom God corrects!” could stand in Proverbs. But applied to Job, it is false, and the reader knows it. As the debate goes on, the friends grow harsher. Since Job suffers, he must have sinned; since he will not confess, he must be proud as well. By the third round Eliphaz is listing the sins Job must have committed. Their theology has no room for an innocent sufferer, so they must make Job guilty to keep it.

Job’s replies are sometimes bitter: “No doubt you are the people who matter, and wisdom will die with you!” He calls them “worthless physicians”, and says their greatest wisdom would be silence. But his real argument is not with them. It is with God.

From despair to hope

Job does not doubt that God exists or that God is in control. That is precisely his problem. He believes, as the friends do, that God governs everything, and so he can only conclude that God has turned against him. He wants to take God to court, but how can a mortal argue his case with God?

— Job 16:19

And in chapter 19, abandoned by his family, mocked by children, having “escaped with only the skin of my teeth”, he rises to the most famous words in the book:

{{OWB:JOB.19.25.s1}} {{OWB:JOB.19.26.s1}} {{OWB:JOB.19.27.s1}}

— Job 19:25–27

The Redeemer, in Hebrew go’el, was the nearest relative, whose duty was to stand up for a family member in trouble: to buy back his land, free him from slavery, and defend him in court. Job’s relatives have deserted him, yet he is sure that his Redeemer lives and will stand up for him at the last. Most interpreters think he means God himself: the God who seems to be his enemy will in the end be on his side. Christians have long heard in these words, set to music in Handel’s Messiah, a hope that only the resurrection of Christ fulfils. Whatever Job himself saw, he saw further than anyone around him.

Where can wisdom be found?

Chapter 28 is a quiet interlude, a poem that may be spoken by Job or by the narrator. It describes miners tunnelling deep into the earth for silver, gold and precious stones, going where no bird of prey has looked. Human beings can find almost anything. But then it asks:

— Job 28:12

Wisdom cannot be dug up or bought. Only God knows where it is, and the poem ends with his word to humanity:

{{OWB:JOB.28.28.s1}}

— Job 28:28

It is exactly how the book first described Job: a man who “feared God and turned away from evil.” The poem tells us that, whatever his friends say, Job has lived the wise life all along.

Job’s last words

In chapters 29–31 Job makes his final defence. He remembers the days “when God watched over me”, when he was honoured at the city gate because “I was eyes for the blind and feet for the lame.” He describes his present humiliation. And then he swears a long oath of innocence, listing sins he has not committed, beginning with “I made a covenant with my eyes”, and calling down curses on himself if he is lying. He signs it, as it were, and waits: “Here is my signature; let the Almighty answer me!”

Elihu

The answer does not come at once. Instead a young man named Elihu, who has held back out of respect for his elders, bursts in, angry with Job “because Job made himself out to be in the right rather than God”, and angry with the friends for finding no answer. For six chapters he speaks. He adds an important thought the friends had not: that God may use suffering not only to punish but to teach, that “He rescues the afflicted through their suffering and opens their ears through distress.” As he speaks, a storm seems to gather, and he ends with God’s majesty.

Readers have disagreed about Elihu. Some see him as a brash young man who repeats the friends’ errors at greater length; others see him as preparing the way for God, and value his insight into suffering as discipline. Neither Job nor God answers him, and the epilogue does not mention him.

The LORD answers from the storm

At last “the LORD answered Job from the storm”. Job had asked for a hearing, and he gets one. But God does not answer Job’s questions. He asks his own:

{{OWB:JOB.38.2.s1}} {{OWB:JOB.38.3.s1}} {{OWB:JOB.38.4.s1}}

— Job 38:2–4

What follows is a tour of creation unlike anything else in Scripture: the foundations of the earth, laid “while the morning stars were singing together”, the sea shut in behind doors, the dawn given its orders, the storerooms of the snow, the stars of the Pleiades and Orion. Then the animals: the lioness and the raven, the mountain goat, the wild donkey, the wild ox, the ostrich, the war horse, the hawk. Most of them are wild creatures, useless to human beings and beyond their control, and God cares for every one.

Job is silenced: “I am so small; how can I answer you? I put my hand over my mouth.” But God speaks again, and asks, “Would you condemn me to prove that you are right?” He shows Job two great creatures, Behemoth and Leviathan, beasts of overwhelming power that no human can tame, and that God made and rules. “Everything under the whole heaven is mine.”

God never tells Job about the scene in heaven. He never explains why Job suffered. Yet Job is answered. He has met God, and that is enough:

— Job 42:5–6

The ending

The prose epilogue brings a surprise. The LORD is angry with Eliphaz and his two friends, “because you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has.” The men who defended God are rebuked; the man who argued with him is vindicated. Honest protest addressed to God is better than pious falsehood spoken about him. The friends must bring sacrifices, and Job must pray for them. “When Job prayed for his friends, the LORD restored his fortunes”.

Job receives twice the flocks and herds he had before, and seven sons and three daughters. The daughters are named, Jemimah, Keziah and Keren-happuch, are said to be the most beautiful in the land, and receive an inheritance with their brothers, which was unusual. Some readers find the happy ending too neat, as if it undid the book’s hard lessons. But it does not say that Job’s faith was a bargain after all; the test is over, and his faith had held when he had nothing. And it does not pretend that his first children are replaced. Some interpreters have noticed that the animals are doubled but the children are not, and suggested that his first ten were not lost to him for ever. Job dies “old and satisfied with his days.”

How to read Job

Job is long, and much of it is argument, so it helps to know the shape before you begin. Read the prologue and the epilogue first, to see the frame, and then read the poetry in large portions, a whole speech at a time, listening for how each speaker answers the last. Do not treat every sentence as Scripture’s last word on its subject: the friends say many things that God later calls wrong. The book is a debate, and its meaning comes from the whole, not from any one speech.

If you are suffering, you may find you need Job’s laments more than its answers. They are there to be prayed. If you are trying to help someone who suffers, take the friends’ seven days of silence as your model, and their speeches as a warning.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices are worth knowing before you begin.

Poetry, line by line. Almost all of Job, from chapter 3 to 42:6, is poetry, and this edition prints each line on its own, as the Hebrew sets it out, so that you can hear the parallel lines answering one another.

“The Accuser.” The figure in the heavenly court is called in Hebrew the satan, with the article, a title meaning the adversary or accuser rather than a personal name. This translation renders it “the Accuser”. Many readers identify him with Satan, God’s great enemy, as later Scripture names him; the book of Job itself presents him as the accuser who can act only as far as the LORD allows.

“The Almighty.” Job’s favourite name for God is Shaddai, one of the oldest names of God, which appears more often in Job than in any other book. It is translated “the Almighty”.

“Curse.” In 1:5, 1:11, 2:5 and 2:9 the Hebrew uses the word “bless” where it means “curse”, out of reverence, rather than write that anyone cursed God. The translation gives the meaning.

“I take back what I said.” The Hebrew of Job’s last words (42:6) is brief and has been read in several ways. The translation takes Job to be withdrawing what he said without knowledge, not confessing the hidden sins his friends had charged him with, since the LORD at once declares that Job has spoken rightly.
