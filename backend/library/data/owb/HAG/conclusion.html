— Haggai 2:23

Haggai ends with a promise to one man, and with three words, “I have chosen you”. The people who heard it were a small community of returned exiles in a province of the Persian empire, rebuilding a modest temple on a ruined hill. Nothing about them looked chosen. That is exactly why they needed to hear it.

The question the book leaves with you

Haggai’s first question is still the sharpest: “Is it a time for you yourselves to live in paneled houses, while this house lies in ruins?” Every generation has its own reasons why the time has not yet come to put God first. Haggai does not argue with the reasons. He asks his hearers to look at what their own priorities have produced, a purse with holes, and to think carefully.

His second question is for those who did obey and were disappointed by the results: “Does it not look like nothing at all to you?” Here the book is very gentle. God does not tell them the work is greater than it looks. He tells them that he is with them, that his Spirit stands among them, and that the glory of the house does not depend on its stones. Faithfulness in small things, done in his presence, is not small.

Where it points

The promises of Haggai’s second chapter are larger than anything that happened in 520 BC. The shaking of heaven and earth, the wealth of the nations, the glory greater than Solomon’s, peace in this place, the chosen heir of David: the New Testament gathers them up in Jesus, who came to this temple, and in the “kingdom that nothing can shake” that he gives his people (Hebrews 12:28).

Where to go from here

A few places in this library to take Haggai further:

  • On courage for the work (2:1–9). Alexander Maclaren’s sermon Brave Encouragements, on Haggai 2:1–9; and the chapter “The Abiding of the Spirit the Glory of the Church” in Spurgeon on the Holy Spirit, on Haggai 2:4–5.
  • On the Spirit in the rebuilding. A. B. Simpson’s The Holy Spirit, or Power from on High, chapter “The Holy Spirit in the Days of the Restoration”, and his sermon Words for Discouraged Workers, which begins with Haggai and goes on to Zechariah’s visions.
  • On coming back to God. Hannah Whitall Smith’s Old Testament Types and Teachings, chapter “Ezra: Restoration from Backsliding”.
  • On putting God first (1:9; 2:8). Charles H. Spurgeon’s Morning by Morning, October 26, on Haggai 1:9, and Evening by Evening, August 4, on Haggai 2:17; and Faithful with Little, an Ochorus Original, chapter “It All Belongs to God”.
  • On what cannot be shaken (2:6). Hannah Whitall Smith’s The God of All Comfort, chapter “Things That Cannot Be Shaken”, on Hebrews 12:27.
  • On the greater glory (2:9). Augustine’s The City of God, Book XVIII, chapters 35 and 48, which argue that Haggai’s promise was fulfilled not in the rebuilt temple but in the church of Christ.

And read Zechariah 1–8 in this same edition, spoken in the same months to the same builders, and Hebrews 12.

Every reference to Haggai in this library can be tapped to read the verse. But the best next step may be to take up whatever work has been waiting, and to hear the word that was spoken to the builders:
