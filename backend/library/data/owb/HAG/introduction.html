Haggai is the second shortest book in the Old Testament, and one of the most precisely dated. It holds four messages, each fixed to the day, all given within four months of the year 520 BC. And it records something rare in the books of the prophets: the people listened. Twenty-three days after Haggai first spoke, they were back at work on the house of the LORD.

The time

In 539 BC Cyrus of Persia took Babylon, and the next year he allowed the Jewish exiles to go home and rebuild the temple that Nebuchadnezzar had burned in 586. Some went. They rebuilt the altar and laid the foundation, and the old people who remembered Solomon’s temple wept at how small the new one would be (Ezra 3:12). Then opposition from their neighbours, hard times and plain discouragement brought the work to a halt. For about sixteen years the site lay unfinished.

Haggai spoke “In the second year of King Darius”, Darius I of Persia, to two leaders: Zerubbabel, the governor, a grandson of Jehoiachin, the last king but one of Judah; and Joshua, the high priest. The book of Ezra names him with his younger contemporary: “Now the prophets Haggai and Zechariah son of Iddo prophesied to the Jews in Judah and Jerusalem” (Ezra 5:1). Encouraged by them, the people finished the temple in 515 BC.

Four messages

  • 1:1–15: “Think carefully about how you are living.” The first day of the sixth month: a call to rebuild, and the people’s response.
  • 2:1–9: “Be strong.” The twenty-first day of the seventh month, the seventh day of the Festival of Booths: the glory of the new house.
  • 2:10–19: “But from this day on I will bless you.” The twenty-fourth day of the ninth month: a question for the priests about holy and unclean.
  • 2:20–23: “I have chosen you.” The same day: a promise to Zerubbabel.

“Is it a time?”

The people had a reason for waiting. “The time has not come”, they said, “the time to rebuild the LORD’s house.” Haggai answers with a question:

Then he asks them to look honestly at their lives:

{{OWB:HAG.1.5.s1}} {{OWB:HAG.1.6.s1}}

— Haggai 1:5–6

A purse with holes: it is one of the Bible’s most vivid pictures of a life that works hard and gets nowhere. Haggai’s diagnosis is not that they were lazy. They were busy, “each of you hurries off to your own house”, but their priorities were upside down. God had been put last, and nothing else had prospered.

Then something happened that the prophets rarely saw. Zerubbabel, Joshua and “all the rest of the people” obeyed, “And the people feared the LORD.” Haggai’s next word is the shortest message in the book, and perhaps the most important: “I am with you, declares the LORD.” The LORD stirred up their spirit, and on the twenty-fourth day of the month they came and set to work.

The glory of the latter house

A month later, discouragement returned. The second message is spoken to those who could remember the first temple:

— Haggai 2:3

God does not pretend the new house is impressive. He tells them to be strong and work, because he is with them, as he promised when they came out of Egypt, and his Spirit still stands among them. Then he looks ahead: once more, and soon, he will shake heaven and earth and all the nations, and fill this house with glory.

— Haggai 2:9

The temple they built was enlarged by Herod into one of the wonders of the ancient world. But Christians have long seen the greater glory in something else: that Jesus himself came into that temple, taught in its courts and called it his Father’s house. The letter to the Hebrews quotes Haggai’s promise to shake heaven and earth “one more time”, and reads it as a final shaking, after which only “a kingdom that nothing can shake” will remain (Hebrews 12:26–28).

Holy and unclean

The third message begins with a question of ritual law. If holy meat touches other food, does the food become holy? No. If someone made unclean by a dead body touches it, does it become unclean? Yes. Holiness, in other words, does not rub off; uncleanness does. “So it is with this people”, says Haggai. A rebuilt altar did not make a people holy whose hearts were turned away, and their harvests had shown it. But now that they had turned, God makes a promise with a date on it: “But from this day on I will bless you.”

The signet ring

The last message is for Zerubbabel alone. God will shake the heavens and the earth and overturn the thrones of kingdoms, and on that day he will make Zerubbabel “like a signet ring, for I have chosen you”. A signet ring was a king’s seal, worn on the hand. Jeremiah had told Zerubbabel’s grandfather that even if he were the signet ring on God’s right hand, God would pull him off (Jeremiah 22:24). Haggai puts the ring back on. Zerubbabel never became a king; but his name stands in the family line of Jesus in both Matthew and Luke, and Christians read the promise as kept in the Son of David to whom it pointed.

How to read Haggai

Haggai can be read in ten minutes. Read it with Ezra 1–6 beside it, and with Zechariah 1–8, which was spoken in the same months. Notice the dates, and the call to “think carefully”, repeated again and again. And notice the order of things: first the honest look at one’s life, then obedience, then the promise “I am with you”, then the courage to keep going when the results look small.

About this translation

The text in this edition is the Open Word Bible, a new translation made directly from the Hebrew and given freely to the public domain. It is printed here at its Balanced level, which aims to be accurate and to read naturally aloud. A few of its choices in Haggai are worth knowing.

“Think carefully.” The Hebrew says literally “set your heart on your ways”, which older Bibles render “consider your ways”. The translation gives the sense: “Think carefully about how you are living.”

“The LORD of Armies.” Haggai’s favourite title for God, used more than a dozen times in two chapters, renders what older Bibles call “the LORD of hosts”: the LORD who commands the armies of heaven.

“The wealth of all the nations.” In 2:7 the Hebrew word means what is precious or desired. The verb that goes with it is plural, so the translation reads it as the nations’ wealth, which verse 8 explains: “The silver belongs to me, and the gold belongs to me.” The Latin translation read it as a person, “the desired one of all nations”, and from it came the traditional “desire of all nations”, long sung of Christ.

For the reasoning behind every sentence of the translation, with the Hebrew beside it, see the Open Word Bible itself at openwordbible.org.
